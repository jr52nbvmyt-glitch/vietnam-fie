# Theater Needs Assessment

*COMUSMACV to CINCPAC · Saigon · 1 January 1964*

## Purpose

You asked me for an honest statement of what this command lacks before anything is sent to fill it. This is that statement. It ranks the deficits facing the Military Assistance Command by one test only: how directly each connects to the Republic of Vietnam failing to become a state that can survive on its own. It does not rank them by cost, by how readily American equipment could reach them, or by how much each embarrasses this headquarters. Ranked that way, the first four deficits on this list are ones that almost nothing in an inventory of weapons will touch, and I have put them first anyway. A command that ranks its problems by what its own tools can reach will spend the war polishing the problems it can solve and lose it on the ones it cannot.

Each deficit below is set out in four parts: the problem as it stands today; why it matters to the survival of the state; what closing it would actually take, which is my guide to what kinds of provision would change the war and what kinds would only change its statistics; and what I will measure to know whether it is closing, chosen wherever possible from indicators the enemy cannot help generating rather than from reports written by the people being rated on them. The assessment ends with my judgment of which end state is reachable with what I hold today, and with the gap register against which every later memorandum and checkpoint will report.

## Basis

The situation on this date is taken from the record of the closing weeks of 1963: the Defense Intelligence Agency's assessment of 13 December [S4], the survey of Long An Province of 20 December [S2], the Secretary of Defense's report to the President of 21 December [S1], the Director of Central Intelligence's letter of 23 December [S3], and National Security Action Memorandum 273 of 26 November [S17]. Where the historical record gives a better figure for a condition that already exists on this date, or for the year now opening, than the figure my staff can give me today, I use the better figure, tag it to its source, and say so where the difference between the two is itself a finding. The course of the war as it ran historically, held in the baseline chapters, is the reference against which I assess what the present force can and cannot do; I do not restate it. Where a figure is my own judgment it is tagged as a judgment of this exercise [X-TNA], and it stays a judgment.

The order matters, and it is not intuitive. The deficits most of the force I could be sent would address — finding the main force, air power, the Trail — sit in the middle and lower half. The deficits at the top are political and institutional, and I can reach them only indirectly. That is not a counsel of despair. It is a statement of where the leverage lies: a provision that touches G-1 through G-4 even modestly can be worth more than one that transforms G-12, and I will say so when it happens.

### G-1 · A state without a foundation

**The problem.** Two months ago the officers of the Republic's army overthrew and killed its President and his brother. What replaced them is a Military Revolutionary Council of generals under Duong Van Minh which, in the Secretary's words, is "indecisive and drifting", and whose members are not experienced in political administration [S1]. The administrative continuity the state possessed — such as it was — went with the old regime. The province chiefs who were Diem's men have largely been replaced; the Strategic Hamlet Program, which was the regime's only concept for the countryside, has been suspended and is collapsing on the ground (G-2) [S7]; the networks of informants and political control that Ngo Dinh Nhu ran through the Can Lao party, whatever their abuses, have been dismantled and nothing has replaced them [H].

The deeper problem predates the coup and will outlast this junta. In the countryside the government of the Republic is, for most peasants, three things: the tax collector, the conscriptor, and the landlord's agent. The Viet Minh distributed land in large parts of the South during the war against France. The Diem government reversed much of that distribution: landlords returned, and farmers who had worked land for years were required to give it back and pay arrears of rent [S19]. Ordinance 57 of 1956 set a retention limit of 100 hectares of rice land — generous to the landlord by any standard in Asia — acquired 422,000 hectares and distributed 244,000 of them between 1958 and 1961, under a tenth of the country's cultivated land, and made the beneficiaries pay for it [S19]. Military operations to clear insurgents have often let landlords reclaim land the insurgents had distributed [S19]. The Viet Cong offer the peasant the land he works. The government offers him the return of the man he used to pay rent to, escorted by the army.

And the junta's own cohesion is the first thing I doubt. Officers who have now learned that a coup succeeds, is accepted by the United States, and leads to promotion will make another. I judge it more likely than not that the present council is overthrown by some of its own members within months [X-TNA], and that the pattern — generals spending more of their energy on each other than on the countryside, province and district chiefs changed with each turn of the wheel — continues until one faction is strong enough to end it.

**Why it matters.** The end state ladder this exercise measures is the viability of a state, not the territory an army holds. Every other deficit on this list is a deficit of the instruments of a state: its army (G-4), its police (G-3), its local security (G-2), its administration (G-13). An army cannot create the political foundation those instruments rest on; at best it buys time and space for one to form. If the government in Saigon cannot hold together, cannot appoint and keep competent province chiefs, and cannot offer the rural population a reason to prefer it, then any military improvement is borrowed against the American clock (G-5) and repaid with interest when that clock runs out. This is why the historical war ended where it did. The army that collapsed in 1975 was not out-gunned to the end; it belonged to a state whose foundation had never been laid.

**What closing it would take.** Most of what can be sent to this command does not touch this deficit at all, and I will not pretend otherwise. What can touch it, indirectly, falls into four kinds. First, anything that makes the government's presence in the countryside continuous and competent rather than episodic and predatory: trained province and district administrators, paid on time, resident in their districts, with communications to Saigon and the means to deliver what they promise. Second, anything that changes the land question in the peasant's favour on a scale he notices — a real land-to-the-tiller programme, financed so that the landlord is compensated and the tenant is not charged, executed in the provinces where the Viet Cong distributed land rather than in the ones where it is easy. Third, anything that makes another coup harder or less rewarding: an officer corps selected and promoted on competence (G-4), and an American posture that stops treating each new junta as a fact to be accommodated. Fourth, security for the political process itself — the space for a government with some claim to represent the country to form. None of these is a weapon. Several of them are things a capable military force can enable, protect or execute, and I will judge every provision partly by whether it does.

**What I will measure.** Coups and coup alerts, and the number of times ARVN units are withdrawn from operations to guard against one. The tenure of province and district chiefs. The share of the rice harvest sold to government markets rather than taxed by the Viet Cong. Voluntary enlistment in the Self-Defense Corps. Volunteered intelligence from the population — tips that arrive unprompted and turn out to be true. Not the number of hamlets the government says it controls; see G-10.

### G-2 · Who holds the countryside after dark

**The problem.** The Strategic Hamlet Program reported 8,600 hamlets complete by the end of its life [S9]. American officials now judge that about one in five met minimum standards of security and readiness [S9]. In Long An Province, on the doorstep of Saigon, 219 hamlets had been reported complete; the survey of 20 December found no more than 35 capable of their purpose, nine of them in the provincial capital or district towns, and 167 extensively damaged by the Viet Cong [S2]. The province had reported 147 armed hamlet militia squads; no more than 50 exist [S2]. Three-quarters of Long An's hamlets have been destroyed since the summer, by the Viet Cong, by their own inhabitants, or both [S9]. In the Delta as a whole, the government controls perhaps a fifth of the hamlets [S9]. In Vinh Long the Civil Guard crosses the river only by day [S9].

That last detail is the whole problem in one sentence. The government of the Republic governs the countryside by day, from district towns and from posts along the roads. The Viet Cong govern it by night, from inside the hamlets. A hamlet whose chief sleeps in the district town, whose Self-Defense Corps squad sits in a mud-walled post with a radio that may or may not work, and whose nearest reinforcement is a Civil Guard company that will not move until daylight, belongs to whoever walks into it after dark. The hamlet's people know this better than any province chief, and act accordingly: they pay the tax, give the recruit, keep silent, and wait to see who is still there in a year.

The posts are worse than useless when they fall. The Defense Intelligence Agency counts about 2,400 modern weapons lost by government forces in 1963, 900 of them in November alone, while the Viet Cong's own losses of weapons decline [S4]. The isolated post, held by underpaid militia who have no confidence that anyone will come for them, is the enemy's quartermaster. Each one that falls arms a guerrilla squad and teaches every other post in the district the same lesson.

**Why it matters.** Night control is where the enemy's state lives. It is what lets the Viet Cong infrastructure (G-3) collect its taxes, conscript its recruits, run its courts and kill the government's hamlet officials; it is where the main force (G-7) draws its replacements, its rice and its intelligence. No victory over a main-force battalion survives contact with a countryside the government does not hold at night, because the battalion will be rebuilt from that countryside. And no government can claim legitimacy (G-1) over people it cannot protect while they sleep. Of all the deficits on this list, this is the one where military capability most directly meets the survival of the state, and that is why it stands second.

**What closing it would take.** Security that lives in the hamlet and stays there at night, and that knows help will come if it is attacked. That has several parts, and the force I hold today has almost none of them.

- *Numbers and permanence.* A population of about 15 million [E: standard estimate of the RVN population in the early 1960s] in something over 12,000 hamlets [E: order of magnitude from the hamlet counts in the strategic hamlet reporting] cannot be held by mobile forces that sweep through by day and leave by dusk. The Self-Defense Corps' 104,000 [S10] are, on paper, roughly one squad per hamlet; in practice they are fewer, worse armed, unpaid for months at a time, and posted where the province chief wants them rather than where the hamlet needs them. The rule of thumb from other insurgencies — on the order of 20 security personnel per thousand inhabitants [E: the conventional counterinsurgency planning ratio] — implies roughly 300,000 men in local security, resident [D], before any main-force war is fought.
- *Communications.* A post that cannot call for help at night will not fight at night. Every hamlet post needs a radio that works, batteries that are delivered, and someone at the other end who answers.
- *A reaction that arrives in the dark.* Reinforcement by daylight is no reinforcement. The force that answers a post under attack at 02:00 — artillery that can fire on call into a pre-registered perimeter, aircraft that can illuminate and strike at night, a reaction platoon that will move on foot or by helicopter in the dark — is what turns a post from a weapons depot into a trap.
- *Status for the men who hold the hamlets.* The Self-Defense Corps and Civil Guard are the despised end of the RVNAF: paid least, armed last, promoted never. Their pay, rations, weapons and families' welfare are military problems with political consequences, and they are cheap.
- *Police, not only soldiers.* Identity cards, a family census, a resident policeman. This connects directly to G-3.

The provisions that would matter most here are not the most impressive ones: reliable small radios in tens of thousands, night-capable fire support and reaction, protected and supplied posts, and anything that raises the quality and confidence of the hamlet militia. A provision that destroys main-force battalions does not, by itself, change who holds a hamlet at night, and I will say so every time.

**What I will measure.** Whether hamlet chiefs and officials sleep in their hamlets. Self-Defense Corps night ambushes and patrols actually conducted. Weapons lost at posts, against weapons captured. Viet Cong tax collection, as prisoners and documents report it. Assassinations and abductions of hamlet officials. The number of hamlets reported "secure" is not on this list.

### G-3 · Identifying the infrastructure inside the population

**The problem.** The Viet Cong are not an army with a political wing. They are a political organisation — the party apparatus of the Lao Dong in the South, fronted since 1960 by the National Liberation Front — with an army attached [H]. In the villages it controls, and in many it only contests, the infrastructure is a government: a party chapter, a village committee, a tax collector, a recruiter, a security section that keeps lists and kills informers, front associations for farmers, women and youth [H]. Its members are not in the jungle. They are in the hamlet, by day, in the same clothes as their neighbours, and many of them are their neighbours' sons.

I have no reliable count of them. That absence is itself the finding. This headquarters' intelligence effort is built to produce an order of battle — battalions, regiments, their strength and location — and it is thin even at that (G-7). The capacity to identify the political infrastructure belongs, in principle, to the Vietnamese police and intelligence services and to the Central Intelligence Agency. In practice the police barely exist outside the towns; the intelligence networks of the old regime have been dismantled with it; and the Director of Central Intelligence tells the President that what his agency has learned has mostly come through military channels, and that he is now sending officers to build sources of his own [S3]. The enemy's communications in the South are largely by courier, so signals intelligence yields little about the infrastructure [H]. And the enemy has penetrated the government and the army: operations are compromised before they are launched, and every list we build is a list he may read [H].

**Why it matters.** The infrastructure is the enemy's state, competing with ours for the same population. It is what makes the main force regenerable, because it supplies the recruits and the rice; it is what makes the countryside dark, because it decides who in the hamlet is afraid of whom. A main force that is defeated can be rebuilt by an intact infrastructure within a year or two [X-TNA]. An infrastructure that is broken cannot be rebuilt by a main force at all, because soldiers cannot administer a hamlet. Of the two halves of the find-and-fix problem, this is the one on which the survival of the state depends more directly, and it is the one for which the instruments of American power are least suited.

The danger runs both ways. Identification done badly is worse than identification not done. A blacklist built from denunciations, rumours and quotas will be used to settle private scores, extort the innocent and fill the prisons with the wrong people, each of whom has a family. Every man wrongly taken is a recruiting argument for the enemy and a lesson to the hamlet that the government's justice is no better than the Viet Cong's terror.

**What closing it would take.** A rural police presence that is resident, literate and honest enough to be trusted with records. A national identity-card and family-census system that lets a stranger be recognised as one. A judicial process that convicts on evidence, so that an arrest means something and a release means something. Interrogation that produces truth rather than confessions. A defector programme — the Chieu Hoi programme began last year [H] — that treats a defecting cadre as an asset to be debriefed rather than a number to be counted. And fusion: one place, at province and district level, where the police, the military, the Americans and the Vietnamese services pool what they know about the same names. A provision that improves our sensors in the jungle does not touch this. A provision that improves records, communications, biometric or documentary identification, interrogation by trained people, or the integrity of the police might.

**What I will measure.** Cadres identified by name and position and confirmed by documents or by a second independent source. The infrastructure's tax take, as documents and prisoners report it. Assassinations and abductions of government officials, which fall when the infrastructure's security sections are broken. The proportion of arrests that lead to conviction, and the proportion of those convicted who were what we said they were. Not "neutralisations", which will be counted by the people rated on them.

### G-4 · The officer corps: selection, leadership, desertion, corruption

**The problem.** The RVNAF's officers come from the educated urban middle and upper classes: a few from the old aristocracy, many commissioned or trained by the French, a rising number from the commercial classes of Saigon [S12]. Educational qualifications rather than demonstrated leadership open the road to a commission and to promotion [S12]. The result is an officer corps socially distant from the peasants who fill its ranks and from the hamlets it is meant to protect, and in which a capable sergeant has almost no road upward.

Selection has been political as well as social. Under Diem, loyalty was the first qualification for command, and commanders who lost men were reprimanded [S8]. The consequence was a doctrine of not closing with the enemy. At Ap Bac on 2 January 1963, about 1,400 men of the ARVN 7th Division with 13 M113 armoured personnel carriers and 15 American helicopters failed to destroy about 350 men of the Viet Cong 261st and 514th Battalions [S8]. The division's former commander had refused to block the enemy's line of retreat; the commander of the M113 squadron refused to take direction from American advisers and advanced only under threat; the ARVN lost 83–86 killed, three American advisers died, five helicopters were brought down, and the Viet Cong lost 18 killed and walked away that night [S8]. This headquarters reported it as a success because the government's troops held the ground afterwards [S8]. I record that here because it belongs to this deficit and to G-10 equally: the senior American adviser on the spot called it "a miserable damn performance", and the record bears him out [S8].

The coup has made selection worse, not better, for the time being. Promotion now follows participation in the coup and loyalty to its winners; commands change hands with each shift of the council; commanders are kept close to Saigon to guard against the next attempt [S12]. Command tenure is short, and short tenure prevents commanders from earning their men's confidence [S12]. Some officers seek province and district posts to escape training and combat [S12]. Advisers cite poor leadership as the first reason for unit ineffectiveness, and note that unsatisfactory commanders are seldom relieved because there are no replacements [S12].

Desertion follows from the same roots. The soldier's loyalty is to his family and his village before it is to a government in Saigon; discipline is weak and penalties are seldom enforced; desertion rises before Tet [S11]. On the record's count the armed forces will lose about 73,000 men to desertion across all components in the year now opening, and about 113,000 the year after [S11]. Divisions are not moved far from their home areas for fear of desertion, so the RVNAF has no mobile strategic reserve beyond the Airborne and Marine battalions [S11].

Corruption completes the picture: ghost soldiers on the pay rolls whose pay goes to the commander, the sale of appointments, the diversion of rice, fuel and American supplies to the market, and the province chief whose post is an investment to be recovered [H]. I cannot quantify it, and I will not pretend to. I can say that every American dollar and every American rifle that enters this system loses part of its value on the way to the soldier, and that the soldier knows it.

**Why it matters.** This is the deficit that decides the distance between rung (c) and rung (d), and that distance is the whole war. A state survives without American combat forces only if its own army can defeat a conventional offensive and its own territorial forces can hold the countryside, and both depend on officers who will close with the enemy, stay with their men at night, be promoted for doing so and relieved for failing. The equipment can be supplied; the firepower can be supplied for a time; the leadership cannot be supplied from outside, and no amount of American support makes an army whose officers are chosen for loyalty into one that can stand alone. The officer corps is also the political class of the Republic (G-1): the men who command divisions are the men who make and unmake governments. Reform of the officer corps is therefore reform of the state, and it will be resisted as such.

**What closing it would take.** Selection and promotion for demonstrated competence in combat and in the protection of the population, with a road from the ranks to a commission for men who have earned it. Relief for cause, and a pool of trained replacements deep enough that relief is possible. Officers' pay sufficient to live on without stealing, and soldiers' pay delivered to the soldier. Families provided for, so that a soldier posted away from home does not desert to feed them. Training that produces leaders, not only technicians. An end to the use of division commands as coup insurance. Most of these are matters of Vietnamese law and politics; some of them — training at scale, pay systems that bypass the commander, the provision of dependants' housing, medical care that tells a soldier he will be evacuated if hit — are things that resources can buy. A provision that improves the RVNAF's equipment without touching its leadership improves a metric and not the war, and I will say so.

**What I will measure.** Commanders relieved for cause, and what happened to them. Desertion per thousand by unit, against the unit's leadership and its casualty rate. The proportion of ARVN operations that make contact, and who initiated it. Night operations conducted by ARVN units. The proportion of a battalion's authorised strength actually present for duty, counted by an American and not reported by the battalion.

### G-5 · The American political clock

**The problem.** Every plan this command makes runs against a clock I cannot read. The commitment of the United States to the Republic of Vietnam rests today on the policy of the President — NSAM 273 restates that the central object of the United States is to assist the people and Government of the South, and in the same document reaffirms the objectives for the withdrawal of American military personnel announced in October [S17]. It does not yet rest on any act of Congress, on any mobilisation of the reserves, or on any broad public understanding of what the commitment may come to cost. The President is new in office and faces an election in November. About 120 Americans died in this country in 1963 [S7]. Today the war is a small thing in American politics. It will not stay small if it is to be won.

The clock is not a date. It is a reserve of tolerance — in the Congress, in the press, in the public and in the administration itself — that is spent by American casualties, by draft calls, by taxes, by years, and above all by the visible gap between what officials say and what the public comes to see. It is replenished, if at all, only by evidence of progress that the public believes. Its size is unknown and its rate of expenditure is variable, and that is what makes it a strategic constraint rather than a planning factor: I do not know how long I have, I know that it is finite, and I know that the actions most likely to save the Republic in the short run — committing American forces in strength — are also the actions that start the clock running fastest.

**Why it matters.** The survival of the state is a race between two processes: the building of a Vietnamese state and army that can stand without American combat forces (G-1, G-2, G-4), and the expenditure of American patience. If the first is not complete when the second runs out, the Republic is left at rung (b) or below when the Americans go, and the historical record shows what follows. This is why the clock ranks fifth, above every purely military deficit. It is not the enemy's main force that decides how the war ends; it is whether the Republic is ready before the clock expires. Every year of American commitment spent on the wrong problem — on attrition that does not attrit (G-8), on statistics that do not measure (G-10) — is a year that cannot be recovered.

The clock interacts with almost every other deficit. A provision that lowers American casualties lengthens it. A provision that improves a reported metric while the war does not improve shortens it, because the gap will be found, and a gap once found poisons every later report. A large American ground commitment buys time in the field and spends time at home at the same moment. The war Hanoi has chosen to fight — protracted, patient, willing to absorb losses we would not — is a war aimed at this clock, and the enemy understands it better than we do.

**What closing it would take.** The clock cannot be stopped. It can be slowed and it can be used well. It is slowed by lower American casualties per unit of progress; by a commitment whose costs are carried honestly rather than hidden and then discovered; by reporting that the public has no reason to disbelieve; and by evidence of Vietnamese progress that is real. It is used well by spending the years it gives on the deficits at the top of this list rather than the ones at the middle. Provisions that reduce American casualties — through protection, medicine, or doing with machines what would otherwise be done with men — have value under this heading far beyond their tactical effect, and I will weigh them at that value.

**What I will measure.** American casualties per month and their trend. Draft calls and whether the reserves are called. The proportion of the American force in combat roles against support. Congressional and press treatment of official claims — not public opinion polls alone, but the frequency with which an official statement is publicly contradicted by events. The gap between what this headquarters reports and what its own officers say in private.

### G-6 · Advisory leverage and its limits

**The problem.** American advisers advise. They do not command, and they cannot relieve, promote, pay or punish. The adviser to an ARVN battalion is a captain with an interpreter, a radio and a one-year tour, attached to a Vietnamese major who may have commanded troops for fifteen years, who owes his position to a general in Saigon, and who can make the adviser's life easy or impossible [H]. If the adviser reports his counterpart's failures honestly, he damages the relationship that is his only instrument; if he does not, the reporting chain learns nothing. Ap Bac showed both halves: a commander who would not take advice, and an adviser whose honest account the command did not want to hear [S8].

The quality of the advisory effort has fallen. The Secretary of Defense reports that the standard of personnel has dropped considerably from the high standards of the original selections in 1962, and that we must have our best men here [S1]. Most advisers do not speak Vietnamese. Most rotate out just as they become useful. The structure is incomplete: advisers reach the battalions and the provinces, but not the districts, the Self-Defense Corps or the police, where the war in the hamlets is fought [H]. And the American mission itself is divided: the country team, in the Secretary's words, "lacks leadership, has been poorly informed, and is not working to a common plan", and the ambassador sends reports with major military implications to Washington without showing them to me [S1]. The Military Assistance Advisory Group is still a separate headquarters from this one [H]. We advise a government with several voices.

The deeper limit is the leverage dilemma. The instruments that would give us real leverage — joint command on the Korean model, aid conditioned on performance, American control of promotions, American officers encadred in Vietnamese units — would each make the Republic's army and government more effective in the short run and less legitimately its own in the long run. A government that visibly takes its orders from Americans proves the enemy's central argument for him. A government that takes no direction from Americans may fail. There is no position on this line that is free.

**Why it matters.** Advice is the only instrument I hold that reaches G-1 and G-4 at all. If it does not work, then improving the RVNAF depends entirely on Vietnamese leaders choosing to improve it, and the record of the last two months does not encourage me. If it works too well, the state we build is ours and not theirs, and it will not outlive our departure. The question of how much leverage to use, and through what channel, is one of the decisions that will set the shape of the war, and it is not one I can settle by myself.

**What closing it would take.** Better men, chosen and trained for the job, with language, and kept long enough to matter. Advisers extended down to district level and into the territorial forces and police. An adviser rating system that rewards honest reporting of the counterpart's failures rather than penalising it. One American voice in Saigon, so that the government cannot play the embassy, the agency and this headquarters against one another. And a decision, at the level of the President, on how far American leverage is to be pressed and with what instruments: conditional aid, a combined command, control over the appointment and relief of senior commanders. I will propose such measures where a provision makes them possible, and I will state their political cost.

**What I will measure.** Commanders relieved on American recommendation and the time it took. The proportion of advisers' adverse reports that lead to action. Advisers' tour lengths and language qualifications. Whether advice is followed in planning, not only acknowledged in conference.

### G-7 · Finding and fixing the main force

**The problem.** This headquarters carries the Viet Cong main force at 21,000–23,000 men in 37 confirmed battalions, with five regimental headquarters accepted this year, and the irregulars at 60,000–80,000 [S4]. With few exceptions, government operations have been unable to compress the enemy into manageable areas and destroy him [S4]. Large-scale attacks average about twelve a month, and incidents and armed attacks reached an all-time high in November [S4]. The enemy fights when he chooses, where he chooses, and for as long as he chooses, and he leaves when the cost begins to rise.

He can do this because I cannot find him reliably before he moves, and cannot hold him in place once found. My means of finding him are those of the historical baseline. Photographic reconnaissance does not see through triple canopy. The infrared and side-looking radar on the OV-1 Mohawk see heat and movement in open country and little under forest [H]. Signals intelligence against an enemy whose communications in the South are largely by courier yields fragments [H]. Ground reconnaissance by ARVN units is announced by their movement, and by Special Forces is limited to the border camps' patrol radius. Human intelligence comes from a population that will not volunteer it while the Viet Cong hold the hamlet at night (G-2). And what intelligence we have is compromised: the enemy has agents in the ARVN staffs, and an operation planned in a corps headquarters is often known in the target area before the helicopters lift [H].

Fixing him is the second half and it is no easier. A Viet Cong battalion that is found by day disperses into the paddy and the forest, or holds a prepared position until dark as it did at Ap Bac, then walks away [S8]. The heliborne assault that is the government's main offensive technique announces itself by its noise, lands on empty fields, and cannot be sustained after dark. Blocking forces arrive late or not at all. Firepower can punish a unit that stands; it cannot make a unit stand.

**Why it matters.** Finding and fixing the main force is not the whole war, but it is the part that can lose the war quickly. The main force's purpose is to destroy the government's mobile forces in detail and so strip the countryside of anything that could reinforce a hamlet; on the historical course, the Viet Cong move to regimental and divisional operations within the year and destroy ARVN battalions faster than they can be rebuilt. A Republic that cannot defeat the enemy's main force cannot protect its hamlets, and a Republic that can only defeat it with American forces stands at rung (b). The main-force war is also the arena in which the enemy controls his own losses (G-8), and therefore the arena where attrition is least likely to work. That is why it ranks seventh and not higher: it is the necessary condition for holding the countryside, not a sufficient one, and a command that treats it as the war will win battles and lose the war.

This deficit addresses the main-force half of the A6 problem only. Nothing that finds a battalion under canopy will find a cadre in a hamlet (G-3).

**What closing it would take.** Persistent, wide-area surveillance that sees through foliage and at night, so that a battalion's movement is detected before it chooses the battlefield. Intelligence that can be acted on in hours rather than days, which means fusion at the level of the unit that will strike. Mobility and firepower that can arrive by night and stay. Blocking forces that can be placed on the enemy's line of withdrawal faster than he can walk it. Operational security that the enemy's agents cannot penetrate, which means fewer Vietnamese staffs knowing more than they must. And in the end, units — Vietnamese units — whose commanders will close with the enemy when they find him (G-4). Provisions in ISR, night capability and mobility can transform this deficit, and if one does I will say so at full weight. Even transformed, it moves capability inside a rung unless G-1 through G-4 move with it.

**What I will measure.** The proportion of contacts initiated by friendly forces rather than the enemy. The proportion of operations that make contact at all. The time from detection to engagement. Prisoner and document yield, which an enemy cannot fake as he can a body. Whether enemy units that are hit regenerate, and how fast. Not the reported enemy dead (G-10).

### G-8 · Sanctuary, and what it does to any theory of attrition

**The problem.** The enemy's rear lies in territory I may not enter. The Laotian panhandle, through which the infiltration corridor runs, is barred to American and ARVN ground operations [H]; NSAM 273 directs planning for operations up to 50 kilometres inside Laos [S17], and the Secretary of Defense has judged the cross-border operations proposed so far neither politically acceptable nor militarily effective on the scale proposed [S1]. Cambodia has broken relations with Saigon and ended American aid [S16]; its border provinces adjoin the war zones north and west of Saigon and the Delta, and the enemy's base areas lie along and across that border. North Vietnam is closed to ground operations entirely and, for now, to bombing. The DMZ is bound by its conventions. Every one of these constraints is MODELLED and binds as it did.

**Why it matters.** Sanctuary defeats any theory of victory by attrition, and the reasoning is short. Attrition works only if the enemy's losses exceed his replacement for long enough to break his forces or his will. The enemy decides whether to fight (G-7); when the cost rises, he withdraws across a border I cannot cross, rests, retrains, receives replacements and returns. His loss rate is therefore a variable he controls, not one I impose. His replacement pool is twofold: the southern population, from which the Defense Intelligence Agency already concludes he is replacing most of his losses [S4], and behind it the manpower of North Vietnam, a country of about 17–18 million [E: standard estimate of the DRV population in the early 1960s], in which something on the order of 200,000 young men reach military age each year [E: demographic share of an 18-million population; the figure commonly used in US planning of the period]. No crossover point exists while he can choose his losses and regenerate beyond reach; a strategy that waits for one is waiting for something the geometry does not permit.

Sanctuary also shapes what pressure on the countryside achieves. Every main-force unit driven out of a province is a unit preserved in Cambodia or Laos, to return when the forces that drove it out have moved on. Clearing operations that are not followed by holding (G-2) move the enemy rather than destroy him.

**What closing it would take.** One of three things, or a combination. First, relief from the constraints — authority for ground or air operations against the base areas — with an honest statement of the political cost, which is high: in Laos, the Geneva Accords of 1962 and the neutralist settlement; in Cambodia, a neutral government that would be pushed toward Hanoi; in the North, the risk of Chinese intervention. I will argue for relief where a provision makes it decisive, and will say what asking costs. Second, a barrier: a means of denying passage across the border without crossing it, which would have to be continuous, persistent, and able to see and strike at night along hundreds of kilometres of forested frontier. Third, a strategy that does not depend on attrition at all: one that takes the population from the enemy (G-2, G-3) so that his units, wherever they rest, have nothing to come back to. The third is the only one entirely within my authority, and the one I judge most likely to work.

**What I will measure.** The time enemy units take to regenerate after heavy losses, from prisoner interrogation and document capture. The movement of enemy base areas. The proportion of enemy losses replaced by southern recruitment against infiltration. Whether the enemy accepts or declines battle, and when.

### G-9 · The Trail: throughput required against capacity

**The problem.** The infiltration system through Laos has run since 1959 under the PAVN's Group 559 [S13]. It moved its first trucks in December 1961 [S13]. Today it is largely a foot and porter system: groups walk south through the Laotian panhandle along what our own study will call the Main Corridor, from way station to way station, a journey of weeks [S5]. More than a hundred stations employing up to 6,000 men — possibly about 10,000 — run it [S5]. Its capacity for materiel in 1964 is of the order of 20–30 tons a day [S13]. The personnel it has moved since 1959 are, on our own best later reckoning, slightly over 34,000 to August 1964 and possibly as many as 45,000 [S5].

The figure that matters is not the capacity but the requirement. The enemy's forces in the South need very little from the North. The later reckoning of the Department of Defense is that about 20 tons a day of supplies from North Vietnam sustain the enemy's 1964 level of activity [S6]. Food comes from the southern harvest, taxed or bought. Most small arms come from the government: 2,400 modern weapons lost in 1963 [S4]. What the Trail carries is the margin — specialists, cadres, heavier weapons, ammunition for them, radios, medicine — and at today's tempo the margin is small enough to be carried on men's backs and bicycles.

**Why it matters.** Interdiction is not destruction. A provision that destroys trucks, kills porters or cuts roads matters only to the extent that it reduces the tonnage arriving in the South below the tonnage the enemy needs, and the Trail is a system built to defeat exactly that: it has redundant routes, it moves at night under canopy, it repairs itself with labour the North has in abundance, and its capacity is elastic — the North can widen it as fast as it decides to. At today's requirement, to make the Trail decisive I would have to cut its arrivals to a fraction of an already small flow, against an enemy who can reduce his own requirement simply by fighting less. The enemy controls the requirement as well as the supply.

It will not stay small. On the historical course Hanoi commits regular PAVN regiments within the year, and its requirement and the Trail's capacity grow together, the capacity faster; by January 1966 the Department of Defense judges the system able to deliver an average of about 200 tons a day, depending on the season, well above the 40–140 tons a day the enemy then needs [S6]. The Trail matters most at the moment Hanoi decides to escalate, because that is the moment when the requirement jumps and the system is still being built. A means of contesting the Trail that is in place before that decision is worth far more than the same means arriving after it.

**What closing it would take.** Measurement first: an honest estimate of tonnage arriving in the South, from captured documents, cache sizes, prisoner reports of shortages, and the enemy's tempo of operations — not trucks destroyed. Then a means of reducing arrivals below the requirement at the time the requirement is high: persistent surveillance of the corridor by night; strike that can arrive while a target is still there; and, if it is ever authorised, ground interdiction of the corridor itself (G-8, `laos_ground` MODELLED). A provision that raises the toll on transit without reducing arrivals below the requirement is an expensive way of keeping Hanoi's labour force busy. That is not nothing — every porter on the Trail is a man not in a rifle company — but it is not interdiction, and I will not call it that.

**What I will measure.** Tonnage arriving, estimated from the evidence the enemy cannot help generating. Road and way-station repair tempo. Infiltration groups identified through prisoners, against our own estimates. Enemy shortages of ammunition and medicine reported in documents. The trucks destroyed figure, when it exists, will be cited as a reported figure only.

### G-10 · The measurement problem and the corruption of the statistics

**The problem.** In the last three weeks every senior American who has looked closely at this war has found that the numbers describing it were false. The Secretary of Defense has told the President that the situation deteriorated further than we realised "because of our undue dependence on distorted Vietnamese reporting" [S1]. The Director of Central Intelligence has told him that the statistics received from government officials over the past year or more were misleading, and that the Viet Cong control more of the population and territory than we believed [S3]. The Long An survey found 35 working hamlets where 219 had been reported, and 50 militia squads where 147 had been reported [S2]. During his visit the Secretary was shown a district that claimed 18 strategic hamlets, and none of them existed [S7]. This headquarters called Ap Bac a success [S8].

The errors run in a consistent direction, and that is the diagnostic. Figures that flatter us are inflated; figures that alarm us are undercounted, because finding them takes work nobody is rewarded for. The Defense Intelligence Agency records about 27,000 Viet Cong casualties through November 1963, including 20,000 killed, and notes that they may be overestimated [S4]. In the same year the number of confirmed Viet Cong battalions rose from 30 to 37 [S4]. If 20,000 of a main force of 21,000–23,000 had been killed in a year, that force would not have grown by a fifth; either most of the dead were not main-force soldiers — irregulars, porters, or villagers who were in the wrong field — or there were far fewer of them. I believe both. My own judgment is that the enemy's actual dead in 1963 were perhaps half the reported figure, and that a substantial share of those were irregulars and civilians rather than main-force soldiers [X-TNA]. Body count is cited in this command, from today, only as a reported figure with its inflation stated, beside my own estimate, and never as evidence of progress.

The same Defense Intelligence Agency paper records 914 infiltrators known to have entered the South in 1963 [S4]. Our own interrogation programme, once it is complete, will put the figure for 1963 at about 6,200, and will warn that even that may be 30% short [S5]. The number that alarms us was low by a factor of six or seven. That is not a failure of diligence by one office; it is the predictable result of a system in which every figure is produced by someone with an interest in it, and no one has an interest in finding the bad news.

**Why it matters.** I ranked this deficit tenth because its connection to the survival of the state is indirect: false numbers do not themselves lose hamlets. But they multiply every other failure on this list. A command that cannot see whether it is winning will continue to do what is failing because the reports say it is working; will spend the American clock (G-5) on activity that generates good figures; and will, when the gap between figures and reality is discovered — as it has just been discovered in Long An — lose the confidence of the people whose patience is the clock. The measurement problem is the mechanism by which a losing war is reported as a winning one until it is lost.

**What closing it would take.** Prefer indicators the enemy cannot help generating and the reporter cannot easily invent: the rate at which the enemy accepts or declines battle; prisoner and document yield; weapons — physical objects that can be counted — lost against captured; rice prices and rice procurement; road traffic and market activity; tonnage arriving (G-9); the taxes the infrastructure collects, as its own documents record them. Never rate a commander, Vietnamese or American, on a figure his own unit reports. Inspect by sample, unannounced, with Americans who do not belong to the chain being inspected. Reward the officer who reports bad news early. Keep two sets of judgments distinct: what is reported, and what I believe. Provisions that give independent, physical evidence — imagery, sensors, records that do not pass through the reporting chain — are worth more here than their military value alone suggests.

**What I will measure.** The gap between reported and independently verified figures, sampled each quarter, province by province. The direction of revisions: if they all run one way, the system is still corrupt.

### G-11 · The sea and Sihanoukville

**The problem.** The Republic has a long and open coastline — about 1,900–2,300 kilometres [E: figures in common use for the RVN coast fall in this range] — crowded with tens of thousands of fishing junks and coastal craft [E: order of magnitude of the coastal junk population in the early 1960s], patrolled by a small Vietnamese Navy and the Junk Force [H]. The North has had a seaborne infiltration organisation, Group 759, since 1959, with steel-hulled vessels built for the purpose [S13]. I have no American surveillance of the coast, and almost no knowledge of what crosses it. A single trawler can land of the order of 100 tons of arms and ammunition in one night [S15] — on a day's arithmetic, five times the enemy's present daily requirement from the North (G-9) [D]. Later estimates will put the share of the enemy's external supply arriving by sea, before the Cambodian route opens, as high as 70% [S14]. I cannot confirm or refute that today; that I cannot is the deficit.

Cambodia is the second half. Prince Sihanouk ended the American aid programme in November, charging us with backing his enemies, and broke relations with Saigon in August [S16]. NSAM 273 calls the situation in Cambodia "of the first importance" for South Vietnam [S17]. Sihanoukville is a deep-water port with a road to the frontier opposite the enemy's base areas north and west of Saigon and above the Delta. Today it carries nothing for the enemy that I know of. On the historical course, it begins to carry arms for him from October 1965, under a cover company in Phnom Penh, and captured records will show 29,000 tons moved between December 1966 and April 1969 [S14] — enough to supply the enemy's forces in the southern half of the country by a route I can neither bomb nor patrol nor enter.

**Why it matters.** The sea and Sihanoukville make the Trail (G-9) only one of three supply systems, and make interdiction of any one of them insufficient. They feed the Delta and the provinces around Saigon — the most populous and the most contested parts of the country, and the ones NSAM 273 tells me to concentrate on [S17]. And they are the routes where the political constraints bite least at sea and most on land: the coast is mine to patrol if I have the means, but the port and the road from it are in a neutral country.

**What closing it would take.** For the sea: persistent surveillance of the coastal approaches — aircraft, radar and ships able to identify and board — and enough patrol craft to inspect the junk traffic without strangling the fishing economy. This is a deficit that the right provisions can close almost completely, because the sea is open water and the constraint is only means. For Sihanoukville: intelligence on what enters the port and where it goes, and a political effort to keep Phnom Penh from agreeing to the traffic in the first place. Its closure by force would require crossing a constraint that is MODELLED (`cambodia_ground`, `cambodia_air`), and I would argue for that only with a statement of what asking costs.

**What I will measure.** Trawler sightings and interceptions; arms caches found on the coast and their origin; the proportion of captured weapons of bloc rather than American manufacture, by region; shipping into Sihanoukville and truck traffic toward the border.

### G-12 · Air power against a dispersed, night-moving, low-signature enemy

**The problem.** The air power I hold is small: the 1st Air Commando Squadron's B-26s, T-28s and SC-47s, flown under a training fiction with a Vietnamese required aboard [S18]; the Vietnamese Air Force's A-1H Skyraiders [H]; and the helicopters of the Army and Marine Corps. Its effectiveness against this enemy is limited less by numbers than by the target. A Viet Cong battalion moves at night, rests by day under canopy or among the hamlets, has no vehicles, emits almost nothing that a sensor of today can detect, and concentrates only when it attacks — at a time of its choosing, usually at night. Strikes in daylight against areas where the enemy is "suspected" hit forests he has left and hamlets he has not. Approval for a strike passes through Vietnamese province and corps authorities, and the time from a request to bombs on target is commonly measured in hours [E: the clearance chain through province chief and corps]. Forward air controllers are few. At night, flares from an SC-47 are the extent of my capability.

Air power against the North is not mine to command, and is not yet authorised. When it comes, as NSAM 273's call for planning suggests it may [S17], it will strike a country whose contribution to the war in the South is measured in tens of tons a day (G-9) and whose will is not a function of its industry (G-14).

**Why it matters.** Air power is the arm in which American superiority is greatest and its translation into results in this war the most uncertain. Its value is real where the enemy must concentrate: against a battalion attacking a post at night, against a convoy, against a base area once found. Where the enemy is dispersed, it destroys little of value, and every strike that kills villagers instead of soldiers recruits for the infrastructure (G-3) and drives people off the land and into the towns, which is depopulation, not pacification. That is why air power ranks twelfth: its potential is large, but its direct connection to whether the state survives runs through the deficits above it. Its most important contribution may be the one least counted: the night strike or illumination that arrives in minutes over a hamlet post under attack (G-2).

**What closing it would take.** Sensors that see a dispersed, night-moving enemy, so that there are targets worth striking (G-7). A clearance process that is quick and still discriminating — which means knowing who is in a hamlet before striking it. Forward air controllers in numbers, with the language and the knowledge of the ground. Night capability: illumination, night-capable strike aircraft and gunships, munitions that can be delivered close to friendly troops in the dark. And a Vietnamese Air Force able to do this itself, because rung (d) requires it. Provisions here can be transformative within their arm; whether they move the war depends on whether they are aimed at the enemy's concentrations and the hamlets' nights, or at suspected areas.

**What I will measure.** Time from request to effect, by day and night. The proportion of strikes against confirmed rather than suspected targets. Civilian casualties from air, honestly counted, and refugees from struck areas. Posts that survived a night attack with air support, against those that fell without it.

### G-13 · No single manager of the war

**The problem.** No one runs this war. On the American side, the embassy, the aid mission, the information service, the Central Intelligence Agency, the Military Assistance Advisory Group and this headquarters each run their own programmes on their own reporting lines to their own departments in Washington [H]; the Secretary of Defense finds that the country team is not working to a common plan, and that the ambassador and I do not see each other's reporting [S1]. On the Vietnamese side, the corps commander commands the divisions and, in practice, the province chiefs, who are both civil administrators and military commanders of the territorial forces; the ministries in Saigon run parallel channels; and the council at the top is a committee of rivals (G-1). Pacification — the work of holding the hamlets, which is the war — belongs to no one on either side.

**Why it matters.** The deficits at the top of this list are interlocking: night security, the infrastructure, the police, administration and land cannot be fixed separately, and every agency that owns a piece of them has its own measure of success. Without a single manager on each side and a single plan between them, resources go to what each agency can count, and nobody answers for the hamlet.

**What closing it would take.** One American in Saigon with authority over the whole American effort in the provinces, civil and military, and one plan, with one set of measures (G-10); a Vietnamese counterpart with the authority to make province chiefs answer for pacification. This is a decision for the President, not a provision, but a provision can make it easier or harder — and a large American force arriving without it would make the problem worse by adding another strong voice.

**What I will measure.** Whether there is one pacification plan per province, written jointly and followed. Whether the American agencies in a province report the same facts.

### G-14 · No lever on Hanoi's will

**The problem.** The war is directed from Hanoi, and its continuation is a decision the Politburo takes and can take again. I have no instrument that reaches that decision. Ground operations against the North are not authorised and will not be; bombing of the North is not yet authorised, and when it is, the restricted and prohibited zones around Hanoi and Haiphong and the prohibition on mining the ports will bind as they did (`bombing_north`, `haiphong_mining` MODELLED). The covert raids of the OPLAN 34A programme are pinpricks [H]. Behind the North stand the Soviet Union and China, whose aid replaces what is destroyed and whose possible intervention — above all China's — sets the ceiling on how hard the North can be pressed [H].

**Why it matters.** Rung (e) — Hanoi abandoning armed reunification — is reachable only through Hanoi's will. Short of that, every lower rung leaves the Republic facing an enemy who will try again whenever its defences weaken. And Hanoi's will is not chiefly a function of its industry or its losses; it is a function of its judgment of whether the South can be taken. The North has paid, and shows every sign of being willing to pay, a price in lives that no American planner would accept as tolerable.

**What closing it would take.** Probably no provision closes this deficit directly. The most effective lever on Hanoi's will is a South that is visibly not collapsing — a state that holds its countryside and an army that can defend it — because that is what changes Hanoi's estimate of what armed reunification will cost and how long it will take. Pressure on the North may contribute at the margin, through cost and through the North's allies' willingness to underwrite it, and it carries the risk of widening the war. I will assess every provision aimed at the North against that standard, and against `INTL.chinese_intervention_risk`.

**What I will measure.** Hanoi's commitment of its own regular forces to the South, and when. Shifts in its public and captured doctrine between protracted war and general offensive. Soviet and Chinese aid. Any willingness to negotiate on terms other than our withdrawal.

### G-15 · The theater base: ports, airfields, roads, helicopters, engineers

**The problem.** The Republic has one port of consequence, Saigon, river-borne and congested [H]. Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Nha Trang are minor ports and Cam Ranh Bay is an undeveloped anchorage [H]. Route 1 and the coastal railway are cut by the enemy at will and repaired by day [H]. The airfields that exist were built for a small air force. Helicopter hours are scarce and are rationed among the corps. Engineer capacity — to build ports, airfields, roads and protected posts — is small. Every provision of any size will arrive through these ports and move on this road net, and the in-country distribution problem will often bind before the trans-Pacific one does (A2).

**Why it matters.** This is a deficit of capacity rather than of strategy, and that is why it is ranked fifteenth: it does not itself decide whether the state survives. But it decides how fast anything else can be done. A large American force, or a large injected capability, that arrives before the base can receive it will be idle, will clog the ports, will draw troops from operations to guard its own lines, and will feed the inflation that is already a political problem in Saigon.

**What closing it would take.** Deep-water port capacity at Cam Ranh Bay, Qui Nhon and Da Nang; airfields able to take jet and heavy transport aircraft; engineers in numbers; protected lines of communication; and helicopter lift in numbers that make mobility routine rather than rationed. Provisions that build the base are force multipliers for every later provision, and I will credit them as such even when their direct effect on the war is nil.

**What I will measure.** Port throughput and ship turnaround time. Days Route 1 and the railway are open. Helicopter hours available against demanded. Piaster inflation in Saigon.

### G-16 · Personnel spaces, and what the absence of a ceiling does and does not open

**The problem.** Washington has set no ceiling on my strength. That is a real asset, and I record it at full weight: no personnel limit forecloses any course of action this command might recommend, and the historical war's long arguments over each increment of strength do not bind me. What remains is the policy objective of withdrawal stated in October and reaffirmed in November [S17], which is a political commitment, not a ceiling, and belongs to the clock (G-5).

What the absence of a ceiling does not remove is the cost of each space. Every American in this country needs a training pipeline, a rotation that replaces him after twelve months, quarters, food, protection and a share of the port and road capacity (G-15). Every American is a visible sign that the war is becoming ours rather than the Republic's (G-1, G-6) and a casualty the clock will count (G-5). And men are not interchangeable: the quality of the advisers I already have has fallen [S1], and an expansion drawn from the same pool will dilute it further.

**Why it matters.** It is ranked last because, with no ceiling set, it is not the binding constraint on any course of action. It is listed because "no ceiling" is easily mistaken for "no cost", and that mistake would spend the clock and the Republic's legitimacy faster than any other.

**What closing it would take.** Not more spaces, but better use of them: provisions that substitute machines and systems for men where men would be exposed; that raise the quality of the men I have; and that place Americans where they enable the Vietnamese rather than replace them. Spaces will be counted and stated in every memorandum.

**What I will measure.** American strength in the Republic, its combat-to-support ratio, and the proportion of it engaged in work the RVNAF could be doing.

## The end state on the opening date

**What happens with what I hold.** The Secretary of Defense judges that current trends, unless reversed in the next two or three months, will lead to neutralisation at best and more likely to a communist-controlled state [S1]. I agree, and I do not believe the force I hold today can reverse them. Sixteen thousand advisers and their helicopters and aircraft, a junta that has just seized power, an army whose officers were chosen for loyalty, a countryside held by the enemy at night, and an enemy whose main force is growing while we report it bleeding: there is no combination of these that holds the Delta and the provinces around Saigon. Left to itself, I judge that the Republic loses the countryside around the capital and most of the Delta during 1964 and 1965, that its army loses its mobile reserve battalion by battalion to the enemy's main force as that force moves to regimental and divisional operations, and that the state is overthrown, neutralised or collapses during 1965 or early 1966 [X-TNA] — faster than on the historical course, where the commitment of American ground forces in strength from mid-1965 saved it for ten years.

**The rung.** With what I hold on the opening date, the reachable end state is **rung (a), Collapse** — on the historical timeline at best, and faster without the historical American commitment. The record's end state stands at (a), and this assessment does not change it.

**Why the next rungs are not reachable today.**

- *Rung (b), dependent survival,* requires American combat forces in strength — enough to defeat the enemy's main force in the field and keep it from destroying the RVNAF piecemeal — and I hold none. Historically the United States reached it only by mid-1965, at the cost of starting the clock (G-5).
- *Rung (c), assisted viability,* requires a Vietnamese state and army that can handle the insurgency themselves, with American air power and materiel only. That requires closing G-1 through G-4 substantially: a government with a claim on the countryside, night control of the hamlets, a broken infrastructure, and an officer corps that leads. None of those is within reach of what I hold, and none of them is directly within reach of equipment.
- *Rung (d), self-sustaining defence,* requires all of (c) and an RVNAF that can defeat a conventional PAVN offensive without American combat participation — its own air power, its own logistics, its own leadership at division and corps level. The distance between (c) and (d) is the whole war, and it is measured almost entirely in G-4.
- *Rung (e), strategic resolution,* requires Hanoi to conclude that armed reunification cannot succeed (G-14), which requires the South to be visibly unconquerable and the routes from the North to be unable to change that (G-8, G-9, G-11).

**What this means for what is sent.** Most provisions will move capability inside a rung without moving the rung, and I will say so when it is true. The provisions that can move rungs are those that reach the top of this register — directly, or by enabling something that does. A capability that finds the main force (G-7) or strikes it from the air (G-12) can be transformative within its arm and still leave the Republic at (a) if the hamlets stay dark and the officers stay chosen for loyalty. A capability that holds the hamlets at night, identifies the infrastructure, or changes how the RVNAF's leaders are made can move the war even when it moves no metric that this headquarters is used to counting. I will weigh each provision by that standard, at full weight in both directions.
