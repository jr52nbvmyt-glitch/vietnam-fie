# CP02 — Part 1: The state of the war after ten memoranda

Ten memoranda have given this command twenty-four provisions. The five since the last checkpoint all act on the RVNAF's and the American army's people, or on what they are given to work with: Phung Hoang, unit rotation and a selected advisory corps (006); officer education and an officer board (007); a domestic industrial base (008); basic training for every soldier (009); and NCO education for every sergeant, regular and territorial (010). Together they make the South Vietnamese battalion and outpost of 1966–68 the best-led, best-trained and best-equipped that the Republic ever fielded, and they make it so through the whole chain of command below the regiment. The war they have made is still the war that ends on 30 April 1975. The reasons are the same as at CP01 and MT01, and the five new memoranda have sharpened them. Every gain below the regiment shows up as fights the enemy declined, not fights he lost. Every institution built behind the American lever is captured when the lever lapses in 1970–71. Nothing in the force reaches the corps commanders, the air arm or the 1974 appropriation, which decided 1972 and 1975.

## 1. The state of the war

### 1964–65: a state built under a collapse, and an army taught from the bottom

The first eighteen months are still the historical collapse at the top and something new beneath it. Khanh's coup of 30 January 1964 (EV000-1), the Vung Tau Charter crisis and the failed September coup (EVCP02-1), and the governments of Saigon from December to June (EVCP01-1) run on their dates. Below them, from 1 January 1964, five institutions start that the historical Republic never had at once. These are the land decree and the Commission (EV001-1), the police and province decrees (EV002-1), the Phung Hoang centres and the selected advisers (EV006-1), the Officer Selection Board (EV007-1), and the Directorate of Defence Industry with its domestic share (EV008-1). The September coup was the first to announce a policy for the countryside, and that policy was the landlords'. When it failed, the reforms became the cause of the Young Turks who had beaten it (EVCP02-1).

Under all of this, the army is educated from the private upward. Since 1957 every recruit has passed ten weeks at Quang Trung or a division centre, and every territorial six weeks at his province's centre (EV009-1). Every NCO has passed his grade's course at the academy at Dong De, a division school or the RF/PF centres (EV010-1). Every lieutenant commissioned since 1957 has passed his branch course. The fighting of 1964–65 shows the effect. At Binh Gia on the last day of 1964 and at Dong Xoai in June 1965, the battalions that historically broke when their officers fell were held by sergeants who had been taught to take over (EV000-9, EV000-18). The general reserve was still spent as it was historically. The posts of the held belt went on their radios and held about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2). The Front answered by killing the best chiefs and constables, and at Can Giuoc by killing the families who had taken titles (EV001-3).

Above the posts the air war is measured and refused. A folder complete since August 1964 put the Thanh Hoa bridge down in one night with no aircraft lost on paper, and Washington refused it twice. Every SA-2 site was on a map before it fired, and the proposal to strike them was refused as well. The first Arc Light flew without tankers and found what history found (EVCP02-2). The sea route closed in 1964 and the Sihanoukville route opened in its place (EV003-2). The escalation of February–July 1965 came on its historical dates for its historical reasons (EVCP01-2).

### 1965–67: the main-force war and the better army beneath it

The main-force war runs as history's. The frontier battles of 1966–67, Masher/White Wing, the Hill Fights, Con Thien and Dak To, are each won on the ground, and none changes who chooses the next battle (EVCP02-3). What changed is at the edges of those battles: no Marine on 881 South died beside a jammed rifle; Con Thien became the first siege measured every night and lifted three weeks early; and the refugees of the Bong Son plain came home to parcels held in their names. The air war over the North and the barrier were measured from their first day and changed no rule (EVCP02-4).

Beneath the main-force war, the ARVN of 1966–67 is a different army. By the end of 1966 about three battalion commanders in four were chosen by the board, and by the end of 1967 four in five. They served eighteen-month commands with relief for cause (EV007-2). Every private was trained and every NCO schooled for his grade. When the field commissions emptied a squad, a corporal first class who had passed the leader's course took it. The enemy saw this and did what he does against every better unit: he declined to fight it. The 272nd Regiment left its ambush on Route 13 on 4 May 1966 rather than meet a battalion that walked its flanks, and killed about forty Regional Force soldiers at Bau Bang instead (EV007-3). Where the enemy did accept a fight, it went differently. At Hau My in Cai Be on 14–19 November 1966, a company of the 7th Division lost its officers in two minutes and held its base of fire under its sergeants. It lost fourteen men where history's company would have lost forty to sixty (EV010-3).

Taken together, the chapters now put the battalions broken in 1966–67 at about half of history's. Memorandum 007 made it about a third fewer, 009 a further one in eight, and 010 a further one in six to one in five (§5). No return shows any of it, because the enemy declined the fights that would have shown it.

The territorial war is also better and also bounded. The radio plant put a PRC-25 in every post of the held belt. The Front stopped assaulting the posts that could call, and at My Phuoc Tay on 9–10 March 1967 it attacked a post in order to ambush the relief its call brought (EV008-4). From then on the post's first call was for fire. The rearmed and trained Regional Forces were good enough to be borrowed by IV Corps in 1966, and the posts of Chuong Thien called and nobody came (EV004-2).

Phung Hoang broke the Front's district committees in the held belt at Hoc Mon and Can Giuoc in 1965–66, three years before the historical Phoenix (EV006-2, EV002-4). Its own success then pushed the method into the contested belt, and a raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 made "Phoenix" a press scandal (EV006-4). The enemy read all of this from the inside. An agent in the board's secretariat gave COSVN every command list (EV007-4). The academy's manuals reached the B-2 Front's staff in December 1964, and by 1966 the Front's companies attacked the posts that kept the routine least (EV010-1).

The war also leaked back through the people the Republic had trained. The devaluation of June 1966 cut the private's rice by a third. The trained deserters of the 25th Division took their course home, and a few hundred took it to the guerrillas of Cu Chi and Duc Hoa (EV009-3). In the lowlands south of Da Nang the trained soldier won the firefight, so the Quang Da Special Zone moved its war to the mine. By 1967 mines killed two-thirds of a Marine battalion's dead (EV009-2).

### 1968: Tet with a stronger floor

Tet is decided where it was decided historically: in Saigon, Hue, at Khe Sanh and in Washington. In the Delta, the posts of Cai Lay show the five memoranda working together. The 247th RF Company stayed off Route 4 on the night of 30–31 January because a listening post's report stopped the relief, as Major Liem's rules required after My Phuoc Tay. The district lost no relief column. The one post that fell was the one whose acting leader kept no listening post (EV010-4). The mobilization of June 1968 met a training base that would not send men untrained and that held the basic course at ten weeks, at the cost of a month (EV000-51).

The new PF platoons were then led by men who had held rank: one platoon in fifteen lacked a schooled leader, against one in three on the course memorandum 007 alone would have produced (EV000-51). COSVN's August offensive found the old posts holding and the new territorials not yet in the hamlets. It went instead for the self-defence groups, armed with Garands, that no course had reached. About 600–900 of their members died in Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong (EV009-4). That is the enemy's adaptation in its plainest form, and it recurs through every memorandum since 004.

### 1969–71: the inheritance and its capture

The drawdown hands over what the provisions built. In the Delta districts the call for fire passed to Vietnamese guns: Long My's posts were answered on the night of 23 February 1971 by a chain with no American in it (EV008-7). The American NCO corps was schooled for its grades and too thin to hold the army it led. Its platoons with schooled sergeants had about a third fewer drug cases and refusals than those with deferred ones, and the decay came all the same (EV010-5).

In 1970–71 the palace captured every institution that had stood behind the American lever: the Board, the list, the file, the Commission and the Directorate (EV002-6, EV007-5). The NCO gate was broken by the National Day decree of 1 November 1970. Within a year the training command half restored it by barring the next promotion instead of contesting the stripes the decree had given (EV010-6). The colonels' petition and Thieu's reshuffle sent Lam Son 719 into Laos with commanders who had met their staffs in January (EV000-71).

### 1972–75: the war the provisions do not reach

The conventional war is history's with small differences. At An Loc and Kontum the 1957 classes' staffs used American air precisely (EV000-77), and the PAVN lost about a tenth more men than historically, which cost its 1974–75 divisions quality and not time (POSMT01-2). The arsenal stretched the 1974 aid dollar, so the squad guns stayed fed and the radios stayed on the air (EV000-89). The Dalat seminar's withdrawal plan was taken without its method on 14 March 1975 (EV000-91, EV000-95). The corps dissolved toward the soldiers' families (POSMT01-6). Saigon fell on 30 April 1975, and Phung Hoang's file was captured whole in one building (EV000-98).

### What the cumulative force has and has not done

The five memoranda since CP01 complete one line of effort. They give the Republic a soldier, a sergeant, a lieutenant, a battalion commander, an adviser and a factory, all better than history's. That line of effort is now as complete as it can be made below the regiment. ARVN effectiveness has sat at the cap of +3 in Phases II and III since memorandum 007, and the last three memoranda added inside a band that cannot rise. Three things stand outside it, and none of the twenty-four provisions has reached them:

- who commands the corps and the divisions;
- who owns the air power and the fire on which the improved battalion's tactics rest;
- what the soldier's pay buys and where his family lives.

Those three are where 1972 and 1975 were decided. The force has made the Republic's army better at the war of 1966–68 and has left it as it was for the war of 1975.

## 3. Interaction sweep

**GVN.corruption.** Sixteen entries. It is pushed down by the Commission (P001b), the province reform (P002b) and the officer board (P007c), and up by the industrial base's contracts (P008a). The pairs that matter for the last five memoranda are in the record:

- the Muster Service puts each NCO's course number on the roll, and the Commission pursues bought passes (P010b × P001b, in 010's interactions);
- the training centres are policed as places where rations and seats can be sold (P009b);
- the Phu Cat lot is the industrial base's corruption case (EV008-5).

One pair was missing: **P008a pushes corruption up, and P010b depends on it.** The arsenal's contracts and the NCO gate meet at the training command, which buys rations, uniforms and ammunition for the courses from the Directorate's suppliers. I looked for a mechanism by which contract graft would reach the gate and found none in the record or in the logic. The training command's corruption is in rations, which 009 already treats. The gate is enforced by promotion boards, which the contracts do not touch. **The pair has no interaction to write.** I record that so the next sweep does not look for it again.

**RFPF.effectiveness.** Eleven pushes and three dependencies. The territorial post is now rearmed (004), titled (001), commanded by a merit chief (002), advised (006c), led by an educated lieutenant (007b), radioed (008b), trained (009c) and led by a schooled NCO (010b). The missing interaction is **P010b × EV004-2, the corps borrowing the RF companies.** The 1966 borrowing happened because the companies were rearmed and trained. Schooled company NCOs made them better still, and on the logic of EV004-2 more attractive to IV Corps. I checked whether this changes the war. It does not change the number borrowed, which IV Corps set by the season's needs, not by the companies' quality. Nor does it change the rule of 1967 that ended the borrowing. What it changes is which companies were borrowed. The NCO schooling makes the "fought best" companies of the advisers' reports slightly more numerous, so the borrowed thirty-four took slightly more of the provinces' best NCOs with them. That deepens the cost to the posts that called and got no answer, by an amount inside the band. **No chapter edit is warranted.** I record the interaction here, and it supports F010-17: the best schooled territorial sergeants are drawn away from their hamlets.

**US.personnel_demand.** Thirteen entries, all pushes or depends of one sign. Each provision since 006 adds Americans out of the line:

- overlap at every relief (006b);
- selected advisers, who come from the units' best captains and NCOs (006c);
- officers in school (007a);
- a larger drill-sergeant cadre (009a);
- NCOs in school, instructors and the Sergeants Major Course (010a).

Memorandum 010 wrote the triple drain on the senior NCO pool (006c, 009a, 010a: 5,000–8,000 NCOs in 1964–65, F010-11). The record lacks the fourth drain. **Unit rotation's overlap (006b) also draws senior NCOs.** It does so in the six-week staggered relief, when two battalions' platoon sergeants are in theater at once. I put this at a few thousand men in 1967, judged on the overlap's 15,000–25,000 and a platoon sergeant's share of a battalion. It makes the shortage of sergeants first class that 010 dated three to six months early (F010-12) deeper in 1967, not earlier. Against a ceiling of none it does not change the war, but it is the American counterpart of POSCP01-3, and I record it as a position (POSCP02-3).

**POP.night_control.** Thirteen entries. Every one of the night's dependencies says the same thing: a post holds the night only where its platoon stays and help comes (POSTNA-2). The missing pair is **P010b × P002a: the schooled PF leader and the resident constable at the same post.** The record never says who commands at night when both are present. The 1964 police statute and the PF's chain of command give the answer. The PF platoon leader commands the post's defence; the constable keeps the census and the arrest file and answers to the district police chief. The schooled leader's listening-post routine (F010-2) and the Special Branch's informant reports are two streams of warning that reach the district chief by different radios. At Cai Lay in Tet the listening post's report was the one that mattered (EV010-4). Nothing in the record shows the two streams joined. **This is a gap of method, not of the war.** It changes no event, but it is the reason the posts' warnings stopped at the post's wire in 1967 (POS008-4), and I name it in Part 2's bill.

**ARVN.effectiveness.** Eleven pushes; the band has been capped at +3 in Phases II and III since 007. Every pairing among 004, 007, 009 and 010 is in the record:

- the carbine and the course (009 with 004);
- the board's battalions and the trained private (009 with 007);
- the field commissions and the leader's course (C007-3, C010-2);

The pair that matters now is not a missing interaction but a measurement problem. **The cap hides every further gain.** Memoranda 009 and 010 each moved the battalions-not-broken figure (one in eight; one in six to one in five) without moving a band. Any provision that further improves the ARVN battalion will look like nothing on the vector. The running sum of DIRMT01-2 is the only place such a gain can be seen. I treat this in calibration (§5) and as a position (POSCP02-1).

## 5. Calibration

The five memoranda since CP01 added about 140 tagged judgments to the chapters. Those on headline variables are the following.

**ARVN battalions broken in 1966–67: about half of history's** (P2 ending; 010 §4.N) [X-010]. The figure is a product of three judgments:

- about a third fewer from the officer board (007);
- a further one in eight from basic training (009);
- a further one in six to one in five from NCO education (010).

Two-thirds × seven-eighths × four-fifths to five-sixths gives 0.47–0.49, so the arithmetic in the chapter is right. The mechanism is traceable for each step: the declined ambush on Route 13 (EV007-3) for 007, the trained platoon at Bau Bang and Thuan Loi for 009, and Hau My (EV010-3) for 010. Under A13 the figure is weak, because it counts battles that did not happen, and no return of any army can confirm that. The record says this every time, and that is the right treatment. **Two problems.** First, the factors are not independent. A battalion with a board-chosen commander is also the one most likely to have schooled sergeants and trained privates, so the steps overlap, and multiplying them overstates the joint effect. I judge the honest range at about two-fifths to a half fewer broken, not "about half". The next memorandum that carries the figure should write it so. I do not edit the chapter, because the chapter's sentences state each memorandum's increment, which is correct; it is the summed "about half" in 010's §4.N that should be read as an upper bound. Second, the figure sits on a variable capped at +3. Its movement is real and invisible on the vector, which POSCP02-1 records.

**American lives saved: about 3,700–7,800 across ten memoranda** (010 §4.N; POSMT01-5) [X-010]. This is six to thirteen percent of the historical dead. Each component is a judgment on a rate. DIRCP01-2 requires each to be shown in two dated events:

- **009's tourniquet** is shown at Albany and in the first wave of Tet (EV000-23, EV000-42). Met.
- **006's unit-rotation saving** is shown only at Dien Ban, where unit rotation cost lives rather than saving them (EV006-3). POS006-6 already says so.
- **010's 300–900** is shown in no dated event in which an American squad lives that would have died. EV010-2 and EV010-5 are about promotion and discipline.

**Under DIRCP01-2 the 010 component may not be repeated as a figure** until a chapter shows it. Until then it is a judgment on a rate of one to three percent of small-unit deaths. The running sum should carry it bracketed, as 2,500–5,000 established plus 1,200–2,800 still to be shown. That changes no band; US.casualties is 0 in every phase. But the upper end of the sum is near enough to a band that the next memorandum adding to it must show its own increment before the sum is quoted as a whole.

**South Vietnamese soldiers' lives saved: about 8,000–23,000** (010 §4.N) [X-010]. 009's part is shown at Binh Gia, Dong Xoai and Tet. 010's 3,000–9,000 is shown at Hau My and Cai Lay. Both meet DIRCP01-2. The range is wide because the rate is applied to a base, RVNAF killed in small-unit actions, that history records badly. Under A13 the figure is reliable to its order of magnitude and no closer.

**Posts lost to night attack: a tenth to a fifth fewer than 008 and 009 left** (P2 ending; F010-25) [X-010]. This rests on My Phuoc Tay and Cai Lay (EV008-4, EV010-4) and on the advisers' logs. The mechanism is traceable: the listening post's report and the relief's rule. POP.night_control holds at +1 in Phase II, where 002 and 008 already put it. The increment is inside that band, and the chapter does not claim more.

**Phung Hoang's audited count: about 18,000 by the end of 1967 and 63,000 by 1971** (P2 ending, P4 ending) [X-006]. These are consistent across chapters. They rest on the audit that the provision itself defined, so A13 rates them as the programme's own count, held honest by an outside audit until 1970. After 1970 the audit is the palace's, and the 1971 figure should be read as less reliable than the 1967 one. P4 does not say so. That is a calibration note for the next memorandum revising Phase IV, not a contradiction.

**The arsenal's 1974 stretch: about a third more from the last aid dollar, less a tenth to a fifth for duds and diverted brass** (EV000-89; C008-4) [X-008]. Chapters and memoranda agree. The deduction rests on Phu Cat as its only dated case. It is plausible as a mechanism and weak as a figure, and nothing turns on it: the rung does not move by days.

**One figure differs between chapter and memorandum.** Memorandum 010's Part 3 §4.N sums the American lives from 001–008 as "about 2,500–5,000 (POSMT01-5)". POSMT01-5 says that figure covers the provisions to MT01, that is, 001–007. Memorandum 008's American saving is not stated separately anywhere, and the sum treats it as zero. Since 008's provisions are industrial, zero is defensible, but the record should say "001–008, of which 008 adds none". The chapters do not carry the figure, so no chapter needs fixing. I note it here instead of filing an erratum, because the sum is right and only the label is loose.

## 6. The experience of the war

**Corporal First Class Huynh Van Sau, acting leader of a Popular Forces platoon, Ap Binh Phu post, Cai Lay District, Dinh Tuong Province, the night of 11–12 August 1967.** *Tests POS010-1 and F010-19: that the schooled post keeps its routine and is passed by an enemy who reads it.* Sau has held the platoon since its sergeant was commissioned in March. He passed the leader's course at the Dinh Tuong centre in 1965 and keeps its routine: a listening post of two men on the canal path from dusk, a stand-to at four, and the report to the district every two hours whether or not anything has moved. Tonight the listening post hears a column of about thirty men pass on the far dike at about one. He reports it, the district logs it, and the column goes on to somewhere else. Sau does not know that he was passed because he keeps the routine. He thinks he was lucky, and so does the district. The next morning the post at Ap My Hiep, four kilometres east, whose leader sleeps through the stand-to, has two men dead and its radio gone. **The claim is visible from where Sau stands, but not as a cause.** What he sees is chance, and what the district sees is one post hit and one not. Nobody sees the enemy's choice. That supports the analysis's repeated statement that the gain appears as attacks not made. It also means that no one in the Republic will ever defend the routine on evidence, which matters when the decree of 1970 comes.

**Mrs Le Thi Thu, wife of a private of the 25th Division, Duc Hoa district town, Hau Nghia Province, 2 December 1966.** *Tests POS009-3 and DIRMT01-9: that training does not reach desertion, which follows the soldier's pay, rice and family.* Her husband passed ten weeks at Quang Trung in 1964 and is, by his company's reckoning, a good soldier. His pay bought a gia of rice for every two days in May. Since the devaluation it buys one for every three. She has three children and sells cigarettes outside the district headquarters. On 2 December he comes home on a three-day pass and does not go back. His training is visible to her in one way only: he knows how to put a tourniquet on, and he shows the neighbour's boy. **The claim is visible, and it is the whole of what she sees.** No provision of the ten reached the price of rice at Duc Hoa. G-17 is open, and her husband's company will be short one trained man until the next intake. The force cannot see this from the battalion, because the battalion counts him as a deserter and not as a man feeding his family.

**Ba Thanh, military affairs cadre of the Cai Be district committee of the Front, the Plain of Reeds edge, Dinh Tuong Province, January 1967.** *Tests F010-20 and C010-1: that COSVN reads the academy's manuals and fights the posts that keep the routine least.* Ba Thanh has two copies of the ARVN platoon leader's manual, printed at Nha Trang, one captured and one bought in My Tho. He has made his district's company commanders read the chapter on the night defence of a post. They no longer ask whether a post is strong; they ask whether its leader is the kind who keeps the listening post out. After the battle at Hau My in November his main force lost a company's worth of men to a government company that did not break when its officers fell. He has told the province committee that the government's sergeants are now the enemy's best soldiers, and that the district must fight the people they do not protect: the self-defence groups, the roads, the families. **The claim is visible from his side more clearly than from any American's.** That supports the analysis, and it supports the counter-claim the record makes less often: the provisions' best effect was to redirect the enemy's war onto the unprotected population. The record counts that redirection in 1968 (EV009-4), and it began here.

**Major Robert E. Hanlon, selected adviser, Long My subsector, Chuong Thien Province, 14 September 1966.** *Tests C006-4 and EV004-2: that the selected advisers carry what the American battalions forget, and that the borrowed RF companies left the posts with no one to come.* Hanlon speaks Vietnamese well enough to argue, has been in the district fourteen months, and has four more to serve. Since April the district's three best RF companies have been with IV Corps' mobile groups at the U Minh edge. In five months the district's posts have called eleven times at night, and six times nothing came. He has written this in every monthly report. The province senior adviser has forwarded it, and the corps adviser has answered that the mobile groups are killing more of the enemy than the companies did in the district. Both are true. **The claim is visible from where Hanlon stands, and so is its limit.** The selected adviser sees exactly, reports honestly and changes nothing above the province. C006-4 says that the advisory corps' gain is concentrated in the war of the hamlets. Hanlon is the gain, and the corps commander who borrowed the companies is the limit.

**Mr Dang Van Phu, chief of the pay section, Directorate of Finance and Audit, Ministry of Defence, Saigon, 20 December 1966.** *Tests DIRMT01-11 and POS009-3: that the provisions do not reach the piaster or the soldier's pay.* Phu has the figures: the private's base pay, the family allowance per child, and the index of rice prices from the National Institute of Statistics. Since June they show the pay losing about a third of its value in rice. He has drafted a memorandum proposing a rice ration in kind for soldiers' families, paid from American aid commodities. The Directorate has sent it to the Joint General Staff, and the American comptroller's office has asked what it would do to the piaster. It will not be decided this year. **The claim is visible, and the missing provision is visible beside it.** None of the ten memoranda touched pay or family rations. The record's soldier is better trained, led and armed than history's and is paid exactly what history paid him. That is the bill's first item in Part 2.

**Sergeant Pham Van Lanh, platoon sergeant, a battalion of the ARVN 42nd Regiment, the ridges south of Dak To, Kontum Province, 18 November 1967.** *Tests POS010-1 at the main-force battle: that the schooled sergeant changes how the fights the enemy accepts go, never which fights he accepts.* Lanh's battalion was sent into the Dak To fighting on two days' notice, under a commander from the board's list. His platoon passed the course for its grades, and its acting squad leaders hold their sectors when they are hit. For three days the battalion has climbed toward bunkers whose occupants leave before it arrives. It has lost six men to mortars and one to a mine, and it has seen the enemy twice. **The claim is visible as nothing at all.** At Dak To the enemy chose the battle, the ground and the hour of leaving. Nothing in Lanh's schooling reached any of those choices, and every claim of the five memoranda about the main-force war is the same claim (POS010-1, POS009-1, POS007-1).

Five of the six passages see the claims the analysis makes. Each also sees the edge of those claims, and the edges are the same three as in §1: what the soldier's pay buys, who commands above the province, and who chooses the battle. The claims stand. The last five memoranda strengthened the floor of the war and left its ceiling where it was.
