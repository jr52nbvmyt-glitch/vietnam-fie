# MT01 — Part 2: Unexplored terrain

The audit's first part ended on a question it could not answer from the record as it stood: whether the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 is a conclusion of this war or a fixed point it has carried. This part answers it, and the answer is that the date holds, for a reason the record had never stated. Saigon fell on the day it fell because Hanoi resolved on 25 March to take it before the rains, and because the 1st Corps could not reach the capital from the North before the third week of April. Nothing any provision built in the Army of the Republic stood on that road. The mechanisms in this war that pulled the fall later acted on fights the PAVN chose to go round; those that pulled it earlier acted on decisions the palace took on the historical days for its own reasons. The date is now derived, and the analysis can say what it would have taken to move it: a delay of about three weeks in the collapse of I and II Corps, which only the President who gave the orders of 13 and 14 March could have supplied.

I return here to the voice of the command, because the terrain this part searches is the war, not the analysis of it. The search found more than the date. It found a countryside in 1972 that the record had moved four bands without a sentence, and which on examination did not move as far as the record said. It found that the single most important reason the ARVN's corps dissolved in March 1975, which was where its soldiers' wives and children lived, appears nowhere in seven memoranda, and that every reform of the army this exercise has made was absorbed at the end by it. It found an enemy who is one mind in the record when he was several, a Delta that is a different war written as a footnote to the main one, and an economy that the record mentions eleven times in Phase II and once in the three years in which it decided the end.

## What this part read

This part read its own Part 1 in full, the meta-checkpoint pack (the causal chains, the Trajectory Log, the settled positions, standing directives and gap register, the end state, and the digest of the enemy's decisions), and the Phase IV, V and VI chapters in full. It read the Phase I–III chapters only by search, for the terms the dimensions below concern: the Montagnards and FULRO, the Hoa Hao and Cao Dai, inflation and the piaster, rice, the Korean and Australian forces, Binh Dinh, Chuong Thien, the monsoon, COSVN and the Soviet Union. Those counts are quoted where they matter. It did not read the cards of memoranda 002–007 beyond the pack's lines. Where an item rests on a chapter it did not read whole, it says so.

## 1. The calendar as strategy

The calendar is where the war was decided at its end, and the record had treated it as a list of dates rather than a set of constraints. The monsoon is mentioned twice in Phase II, once in Phase VI, and never as the thing it was in 1975: the limit on Hanoi's time.

### The PAVN's losses of 1972

The directive the first part issued requires the PAVN's 1972 losses to be stated before 1973–75 is written (DIRMT01-6), and this is the first work to revise Phase V since. The answer resolves the first contradiction the audit found, and it resolves it by the distinction the audit proposed.

The measured sieges were the enemy's worst battles of the year, as memorandum 005 said, and they did not change the year by much. The record already holds the pieces. At An Loc the GBU-38 cells added perhaps a fifth to the losses of the B-2 Front's divisions (EV000-76) [X-005]; at Kontum the siege box destroyed about a third of the guns before the assault and broke the 320th Division's assault short of the wire (EV000-77) [X-005]; on the DMZ the radar-bombing destroyed some 20–30 armoured vehicles before they crossed (EV000-74) [X-003]. The fighting for Quang Tri, which was the largest single drain on the PAVN in 1972, ran on the historical course: the Marines' counter-offensive and the bombardment of the Citadel are unaltered (EV000-79). Put together, the PAVN's killed and wounded in 1972 come to about 7,000–10,000 above the historical figure of about 100,000, a tenth more, concentrated in five divisions: the 5th, 7th and 9th at An Loc, the 320th and 2nd at Kontum [X-MT01].

A tenth is real, and it is the wrong unit for asking what it did to 1975. The North made good its 1972 losses in men within its flow of replacements to the South in 1973 [E: one to two months of a replacement flow of the order of 100,000 men a year or more in 1973–74]. Its corps were formed on their historical dates, the 1st in October 1973 and the 4th in July 1974, because what formed them was the Politburo's resolution of October 1973 and its reading of the American political collapse, not the strength of the B-2 Front's companies (EV000-87). What the North could not make good in a season was the cadre: the company commanders and political officers killed in measured assaults on An Loc and Kontum. The 9th Division that attacked Rach Bap in May 1974, and the 7th that went into Phuoc Long in December and Xuan Loc in April, had fewer officers who had fought in 1972 than history's, and their first assaults in each battle were cruder [X-MT01].

So both memoranda can stand, with the distinction drawn. Memorandum 005's sieges were the PAVN's worst battles where they were fought; the year as a whole cost it a tenth more than history's; and the cost was paid in the quality of particular divisions, not in time. The chapter now says this (EV000-87 and the close of Phase V). The strongest-positive finding of memorandum 005 is not overstated about the sieges; it would be overstated if anyone used it to argue that the North's last campaign came later or weaker, and nobody should.

### The calendar of 1975

The date of the fall was set in Hanoi between 25 March and 14 April 1975, and it was set by two things: the rains and the road.

<!-- initiative -->
On 25 March, with Hue abandoned and the Highlands column already destroyed on Route 7, the Politburo resolved to take Saigon before the rainy season [H]. In the southern provinces the rains begin in May; they turn the paddy around the capital to water and the tracks of the rubber country to mud, and they had governed the rhythm of every campaign in this war since 1965 [H]. On 31 March, with Da Nang fallen, the Politburo decided to do it in April, and on 14 April it named the operation the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H]. This was the enemy's own decision, on his own clock, and nothing this command or any provision did provoked it: it was taken because his army had broken two corps faster than his plan had dared assume, and because Le Duan and Van Tien Dung would not let a dry season they had not expected to have go to waste.

Between those dates the whole of the PAVN's effort went into moving its corps. The critical one was the 1st Corps, the strategic reserve, which came south from the North by road, more than 1,500 kilometres, and reached its assembly areas north of Saigon only in the third week of April [H] [E: road distance from the Thanh Hoa region to Binh Duong by Route 1 and the strategic road]. The 2nd Corps came down the coast behind the collapse; the 3rd and 4th closed from the Highlands and the east. Dung set the assault for the last days of April because that was when the last corps would be in place and the rains still about a fortnight off (EVMT01-4) [H].

That is the critical path, and the date lies on it. Three nodes determined it: the palace's orders of 13 and 14 March that dissolved I and II Corps in a fortnight; the 1st Corps' march and the season; and General Minh's surrender on the morning of 30 April. Every mechanism the first part listed can be tested against those three nodes.

The pulls toward a later fall act on fights the PAVN chose to bypass. At Xuan Loc the 18th Division's squads, fed for the first time since January 1974, broke the 341st Division's assaults of 9–11 April more expensively than history's, and the consequence was that Dung's headquarters told Hoang Cam to stop assaulting the town three days earlier than he did historically and go round it (EV000-96) [X-MT01]. The town was held by a division the PAVN no longer needed to take until the Joint General Staff withdrew it on 19–21 April. The 22nd Division came out of Qui Nhon with more battalions than history's and fought at Tan An on Route 4 until the end; the 2nd Airborne Brigade held Phan Rang until 16 April; Ly Tong Ba's 25th Division held Cu Chi and Route 1 west of the capital. Each fought better than history's and none was on the critical path: each was a fight the PAVN went round, or took with the weight it was bringing to Saigon anyway. The PAVN's greener divisions were given the roles in which greenness cost least. The Delta's stronger held belt changed which units cut Route 4 south of the capital and not the day it was cut. And the IV Corps that was more intact on 30 April than history's obeyed Minh's surrender as history's did; its commander, Major General Nguyen Khoa Nam, and his deputy, Brigadier General Le Van Hung, took their own lives as they did historically, because the order to lay down arms came from Saigon and the Republic it might have fought for no longer existed above them [H].

The pulls toward an earlier fall act on decisions the palace took on the historical days. Thieu refused the general reserve to Phuoc Long on 18 December "with more conviction" (EV000-92), and gave the order to abandon the Highlands on 14 March with the Dalat study's conclusion in his hand (EV000-95); both decisions are dated as history dates them, because both were triggered by the enemy's blows, at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, which came on Hanoi's schedule. A President more certain that his army could not hold alone did not decide sooner; he decided with less hesitation on the same day. The ammunition hoards of I and II Corps were lost in the collapse and did not cause it (EV000-94). The Airborne's heavier losses in 1974 left the general reserve weaker, and the reserve was committed and withdrawn on the historical dates.

What would have moved the date is now statable. A delay of a few days anywhere in April was absorbed by a plan waiting for its last corps. A delay of about three weeks or more in the collapse of I and II Corps, enough to keep the 1st Corps' road or the coast closed into May, would have put the assault into the rains, and forced Hanoi to choose between fighting in the mud and waiting for the dry season of 1976, which was its original plan [X-MT01]. The first choice would probably have cost it weeks and not the war; the second would have given the Republic the better part of a year, and a year in 1975–76 was a year in which the appropriation, the price of oil and the Politburo's patience could all have moved. That threshold is the most important number this audit produces. Nothing in the cumulative force approaches it, because nothing in the force reached the orders of 13 and 14 March, and the orders, not the fighting, broke the two corps in a fortnight.

The date of the Republic's fall in this war is therefore 30 April 1975, within a window of about 27 April to the first days of May that reflects only the PAVN's own margins of movement [X-MT01]. The rung stays at (a). And the series' seven identical dates were right for the wrong reason: they were carried, not derived, and they would have been carried even if a provision had reached the threshold. From now on they will not be (DIRMT01-7).

### The rest of the calendar

The one-year tour and the replacement system are the structural degradations the procedure names, and memorandum 006 addressed them for the American battalions and the advisers. It did not address the ARVN's calendar, which was worse. The ARVN had no tour at all: a soldier served until he was killed, crippled, deserted or the war ended, and his unit's rhythm was set by the harvest, when desertion rose, and by Tet, when units went on leave and came back short [H]. The record never mentions either. The Tet of 1968 is treated at length as a military event; the Tets of 1969–75, on which the ARVN's units emptied for a week and the Front's local forces went home to eat, are not treated at all.

The American fiscal year appears once, as the year of the 1974 appropriation (EV000-89). It is the clock the end ran on: the appropriation for the year from July 1974 was voted in August 1974 and spent by March 1975, and Ford's request of 10 April for $722 million was a request against a fiscal year with less than three months left in it [H77]. A provision that changed what the Republic needed from that appropriation would have changed the war's last eight months more than any provision that changed what it did with it, and the exercise has received none.

**Carriage: standing** (DIRMT01-7). The calendar joins the analysis's permanent attention, and every memorandum that revises Phase V or VI tests its effects against the critical path of 1975.

## 2. The geography the analysis forgot

### The countryside of 1972

Phase V had returned four countryside variables to history in a year without a sentence on the countryside, and the first part named it as the plainest unsupported claim in the record. On examination the record was half right. Some of the gain was lost in 1972 and some was not, and the parts that were lost were lost in particular places for particular reasons.

Where a PAVN division came into the peopled lowlands, the gains of 1964–71 were lost. The clearest case is northern Binh Dinh, which the record names eleven times in Phases I–II and not once since. In April 1972 the PAVN 3rd Division came down out of the An Lao valley and took Hoai Nhon, Tam Quan and Hoai An from the 22nd Division's regiments and the province's Regional Forces, and held them until the summer [H]. What happened in the three districts was different from history in a way the record had never imagined: the Front came back to a plain where the land had been titled to its tillers in 1964, much of it land the Viet Minh had given them first, and it had nothing to give. It governed as an occupier. It taxed the spring harvest, took about 1,800 young men for its local battalions and took or killed the hamlet chiefs and police it could find, and when the 22nd Division returned the hamlets had paid the Front more in four months than in eight years and had lost their young men (EVMT01-1) [X-MT01]. The night in northern Binh Dinh went back to history's because a division sat in it for four months and the posts had no answer to a division. Quang Tri north of the Thach Han is the same case, and it was never recovered.

Where the offensive came as regiments against the posts and not as a division in the hamlets, the gain survived. When the 21st Division left the Delta for Route 13 in April, the PAVN and Front regiments in the U Minh came out against the posts of Chuong Thien and Kien Giang, as they did historically. In this war the posts of Long My and Kien Thien had held beside the police since 1964 with the land titled behind them, and they held about nine attacks in ten; the regiments turned to the Regional Force companies sent north as convoy escorts, and to the road (EVMT01-2) [X-MT01]. Major Huynh Cong Tri kept all forty-one of Long My's posts through the summer and lost the road to Vi Thanh for seven weeks. His reports show the Front's tax collectors outside the post line collecting at about twice their 1971 rate and inside it not at all [X-MT01]. The held belt stayed held; the contested belt, which the offensive gave back to the Front for a season, is where the Front's taxes and recruits recovered.

And one variable returned to history for history's reason. At the end of June 1972 Thieu was given decree powers; in August a press decree closed most of the opposition papers; and in the late summer the election of hamlet chiefs was abolished and the chiefs were appointed through the province and district chiefs [H]. In this war the elected chiefs had sat on the councils that confirmed the land titles of 1964, and the appointed ones replaced them (EVMT01-3) [X-MT01]. The government's claim to the hamlets' consent fell to where it stood historically. The land, the posts and the files were untouched.

The Phase V end vector now says so. Night control and the Front's weakness in the held belt stay one band better than history's at the end of 1972; the government's legitimacy returns to history's, with the decrees as its mechanism; the Special Branch's knowledge of the infrastructure is one band better rather than two, because the historical police and Phoenix had largely caught up with it by 1972 (the close of Phase IV says the historical police closed the gap after 1969) [X-MT01]. That is consistent with what Phase VI already said and nobody had reconciled with Phase V: that the Front's district committees in Chuong Thien, Vinh Binh and Kien Hoa lost a fifth of their members in 1973, twice the historical rate (EV000-84). A countryside at history's state at the end of 1972 could not have produced that 1973. A countryside one band better in its held belt could.

None of this moves the date or the rung. The held belt did not fight the Ho Chi Minh Campaign, and it surrendered on Minh's order (POS001-4 stands). It matters because the end-of-1972 state is the base from which every later memorandum writes 1973–75, and the record had it wrong by a band in three variables.

### The Delta is a different war, written as a footnote

The Delta appears in the record mostly as the place where the political provisions worked best: the land titles, the posts, the district centres, NIGHT LANTERN in Can Giuoc. As a theatre of war it is barely there. Chuong Thien has seven mentions in Phase II and none in Phase IV; the U Minh, the Seven Mountains and the Plain of Reeds, the Front's base areas in IV Corps, are not treated as places where a main-force war happened. Yet IV Corps was the one corps the Republic still held whole on 30 April, with three divisions, and it had a different enemy (a Front that was southern for longer, PAVN regiments that came later and fewer), different terrain (water, not canopy) and different force requirements (boats and territorials, not air cavalry and B-52s) [H]. Every provision in this exercise has been assessed against the war of I, II and III Corps, with the Delta as the place where the political provisions' gains are counted. The silence cost the record its best evidence that the political provisions worked, because it is in the Delta, in 1973–75, that they were still visible when everything else had converged.

### The Highlands beyond the set pieces

The Montagnards appear twice in the whole record, once each in Phases I and II, and FULRO not at all. The Highlands are the Ia Drang, Dak To, Kontum and the retreat down Route 7: battles fought over a country whose people the record never names. That silence has a cost in exactly the place the war ended. Ban Me Thuot was the capital of the Rhade country, the town where the Rhade CIDG companies had revolted in September 1964 and from which FULRO took its claim to autonomy [H]. Phu's II Corps defended it with a regiment and territorials because he believed the blow would fall on Pleiku, and part of the reason the PAVN's preparations around Ban Me Thuot were not reported to him in time is that the population around the town did not report them [H]. The Montagnards had been promised autonomy by the French, by Diem's enemies, by the Americans' Special Forces and by the Front, and given it by none; in 1975 they told none of them anything.

This exercise gave the Republic land reform, a police, merit chiefs, an anti-corruption commission and Phung Hoang, all built for the Kinh villages of the lowlands. None of them addressed the Highland peoples' land, which was not rice land and was not titled, or their claim to govern themselves. Whether a provision that had done so would have changed the warning at Ban Me Thuot cannot be known from the record. What can be said is that the analysis never asked, and that the strategic surprise of the last campaign happened in the province the analysis had never looked at. **Episodic:** the next provision that touches the Highlands, the CIDG or the territorial forces in II Corps must treat the Montagnards as a population with its own claim, and FULRO as an actor.

### The coast between Da Nang and Qui Nhon

Quang Ngai, Quang Tin and the southern half of Quang Nam, the most Front-held lowlands in the country from 1945 to 1975, are named in the record mostly as the location of My Lai, the Duc Thanh raid and the files captured at Da Nang. Binh Dinh disappears after 1967. These were the provinces where the land programme confirmed the Viet Minh's distributions most widely and where it should have mattered most; they are also where the Korean divisions fought from 1965, about which the record has four sentences [H]. The coast is where the reforms met a population that had been the Front's for a generation, and the analysis treated it as the backdrop to the main-force war of the Americans in I Corps. **Episodic,** with the Binh Dinh event of 1972 as its first filling, and the geographical directive below as its carriage.

**Carriage: standing** (DIRMT01-8). Every memorandum touching the countryside states its effect separately for the four regions that were four different wars.

## 3. The war the Vietnamese fight

The record has a habit of saying "the ARVN" where it means one division, and of saying it about the division that suits the argument. Its best units are the Airborne, the Marines, the 1st Division under Truong and the 18th under Dao at Xuan Loc; its worst are the 3rd Division of 1972, formed from deserters and Regional Forces, and the 22nd at Tan Canh; the 25th, historically the worst division in III Corps, is made good in this war by a list commander in 1967 and then not mentioned again until Cu Chi. Each of these is correct. What is missing is the ARVN as eleven infantry divisions and two elite ones of very different quality, recruited from different regions, commanded by men of different factions, and fighting in 1975 for different things. The 21st and 9th Divisions in the Delta were recruited locally and fought near their homes; the 1st and 2nd in I Corps were recruited locally too, and their homes were in the path of the offensive; the Airborne and the Marines were national units whose families lived around Saigon, and who fought the last campaign further from their families than any other formation in the army [H].

That difference, where the soldier's family lived, is the most important thing about the Vietnamese war that the analysis never examined. It is taken up under the open search, because it is the mechanism by which every reform of the army in this exercise was absorbed at the end.

The population is the second monolith. The record speaks of the population being secured, displaced, taxed, enrolled in the census and protected by the posts; it rarely speaks of it choosing. Millions of people decided every night whom to feed, whom to tell, and whom to hide, and in this war the land reform changed one of their reasons. The Binh Dinh event above is the first place in the record where the population is shown asking a question, whether the Front would confirm the titles, and the Front having no answer. There should be more such places, and the directive on the soldier's family (DIRMT01-9) carries part of this; the rest is episodic.

## 4. The wars within the war

The palace has been the record's one Vietnamese political actor, and it is given one motive: to capture the institutions the Americans protected. Against that the record has named the generals who were its instruments and victims (Khanh, Ky, Thieu, Loan, Lam, Giai, Phu, Truong), the coups that happened, and the board's list that became advisory in 1970. It has not treated the corps commanders as what they were until 1972: regional powers with their own provinces, revenues and loyalties, appointed by the President for their loyalty and kept for it, whose quarrels with the Joint General Staff explain more of the orders of 1972 and 1975 than any doctrine does [H]. Lam at I Corps in 1972 and Phu at II Corps in 1975 were not simply bad commanders; they were the President's men in the two corps he trusted least, and he relieved neither until after the disaster. A memorandum that reached the choice of the corps commanders (POSMT01-1 names it as one of three things that could move the rung) would have to reach this.

The Hoa Hao and the Cao Dai appear twice in Phase I and never again. In the Delta the Hoa Hao provinces, An Giang and Chau Doc, were the most anti-Communist countryside in the Republic, governed in practice by the sect's own militia and its own notables, and they were among the quietest provinces of the war [H]. They are also where a land reform confirming the Front's distributions would have been least welcome, since the sect's notables owned land the Front had never touched. The record does not say what the 1964 programme did there. **Episodic:** the next provision touching land, the territorial forces or the Delta states its effect in the Hoa Hao provinces.

On the other side, COSVN is mentioned twelve times in Phase IV and not once in Phases V and VI, which is to say that the record's enemy in the South loses his southern command at exactly the moment the war becomes a northern army's. That is partly historical, since the B-2 Front's war in 1974–75 was directed from Hanoi through Tran Van Tra and the corps commanders, and partly an analytical convenience. Tra argued in late 1974 for the attack on Phuoc Long against the caution of the General Staff in Hanoi, and won the argument by making it a test of the American response [H]. That is the enemy's internal politics deciding a date, and the record tells it as Hanoi's single decision (EV000-92). The Sino-Soviet split, which shaped the quantity and kind of the North's aid throughout, appears in the record twice, as the denunciation of the mining in 1972; the Soviet increase in deliveries in 1974, which historically paid for the stocks that the last campaign consumed, is not in the record at all [H]. **Carriage: standing** (DIRMT01-10): the enemy is several minds.

## 5. The economic war

The record mentions rice eleven times in Phase II, four in Phase IV, none in Phase V and once in Phase VI. It mentions inflation once in each of three phases. That is the profile of an analysis that treated the economy as the setting of the early war and forgot it in the years when it decided the end.

Those years were decided partly by the piaster. The departure of the Americans in 1972–73 took away the spending that had employed a large part of urban South Vietnam, the oil shock of late 1973 multiplied the price of everything that moved, and the soldier's pay, fixed in piasters, lost most of its value between 1972 and 1974 [H]. By 1974 a private's pay could not feed his family, and the ARVN's desertion, its petty corruption at checkpoints, and its soldiers' second jobs were the result (EV000-89 says this in one sentence) [H]. None of the provisions touched the soldier's household economy, and every reform that made the army's officers better ran into men who were hungry.

The land reform, which the record credits at full weight as a political act, is never credited as an economic one. Owner-tillers in the Delta, freed of rent from 1964, had more rice to sell, and sold it in the government's markets; the Front's rice procurement in the held belt was lower from the mid-1960s, and in 1973–74, when Saigon and the Front ran rice blockades against each other's zones, the Republic's held belt was a larger and richer zone than history's [X-MT01]. Whether that changed anything for the PAVN in 1975 is doubtful, since the last campaign was fed from the North by the strategic road and the pipeline; but it changed the Republic's own position in 1974, when its government had more rice and less money, and the record has never said so. Land reform, the procedure says, is the most powerful pacification tool and the one most consistently sabotaged by the people it is supposed to benefit; in this war it was not sabotaged, and its economic effect was never measured.

The black market and the diversion of American supply appear in memorandum 001's Commission cases (the ration and fuel diversion of 1967) and nowhere after the Commission was curbed in 1971; the most important diversions of the war, of fuel and ammunition in 1974 when both were rationed, are not in the record [H]. **Carriage: standing** (DIRMT01-11): every memorandum touching the countryside or the RVNAF states its effect on rice and on the piaster, with at least one dated event.

## 6. The people the analysis forgot

The record names formations and their commanders well, and it names the reformers this exercise invented with care. It is weaker on the Vietnamese who made the decisions the provisions never reached, and on the enemy's commanders below Giap, Dung and Le Duan. This part names those it found deciding outcomes the record had attributed to systems.

Van Tien Dung set the date of the fall by his plan for the Ho Chi Minh Campaign, which waited for its last corps; the record had him once, as the director of Campaign 275 (EVMT01-4). Hoang Cam, commanding the 4th Corps, chose at Xuan Loc to stop assaulting a division his superiors told him to go round, and the twelve days of Xuan Loc were his schedule rather than Dao's defence (EV000-96). Tran Van Tra argued for the attack on Phuoc Long and so for the test that answered Hanoi's question about the Americans. On the Republic's side, Nguyen Khoa Nam held IV Corps whole to the end and laid down its arms on an order he could have refused, and did not; Ly Tong Ba, who held Kontum in 1972, commanded the 25th Division at Cu Chi and was captured; Pham Van Phu, whose misreading of the blow at Ban Me Thuot began the end, is in the record as a name and not as the man who discounted the warnings he was given. And two invented officers carry the countryside of 1972: Major Huynh Cong Tri, who held Long My, and Major Dao Van Luong, who burned the Bong Son files three hours before the town fell. The latter is not the template of the capture undone; it is the opposite, an officer who had learned from Hue, and the record should have more of them.

## 7. The information war

The credibility gap has been the record's whole information war, and it has been treated well: no Tonkin deception, honest counts of the Trail and the army, a Phoenix scandal in 1967 rather than 1970, no Bach Mai (POSCP01-1). What has not been treated is everything else. Radio Hanoi is not mentioned in the record at all, nor the Liberation Radio that reached every hamlet with a transistor set, nor the Front's propaganda inside the ARVN, which in this war had a new subject the record never gave it: the board's lists and the land titles, both of which the Front attacked as American impositions on Vietnamese custom [X-MT01]. The photograph as weapon appears once, as the pictures of Bach Mai that were not taken (EV000-82).

The first part left one question open here, and this part takes it up because the directive it issued requires it (DIRMT01-2): the American aid appropriation of 1974 and the refusal of April 1975, which the record has carried as history in every memorandum.

First the sum the first part asked for. Four memoranda estimated American lives saved: about 1,000–2,500 for the carbines (F004-13) [X-004]; perhaps 1,400–2,700 allied lives in the sieges, most of them American [X-005]; about 1,000–2,000 for unit rotation (POS006-6) [X-006]; and one to three percent of American small-unit deaths in 1965–69 for officer education (POS007-4) [X-007]. Taken at the American share and discounted by a third for the overlap between them, the sum is about 2,500–5,000 Americans who did not die, most of them in 1965–69, against more than 58,000 historically: of the order of five to nine percent [X-MT01]. It is an exercise judgment built on four rates, and under DIRCP01-2 it stays one. It does not cross a band. The American clock was spent in 1967–68 by a war that looked unwinnable at a price that was still rising, and a war that cost five to nine percent fewer American lives by the end of 1968 looked the same on the evening news of February 1968. POSCP01-1 is reaffirmed for 1965–72 with the sum now stated.

Then the vote. Congress cut the military aid for the year from July 1974 to about $700 million in August 1974, the week Nixon resigned, and refused Ford's request for $722 million in April 1975 (EV000-89, EV000-97) [H71] [H77]. Historically the vote was driven by Watergate and the collapse of the President who had made Thieu his promises, by the oil shock and the inflation that made every appropriation contested, by the end of the draft and of American casualties, which removed the constituency that had wanted the war ended by winning it, and by a campaign over the Republic's political prisoners that gave the Republic's friends in Congress nothing to defend [H]. The record contains changes that pull on it in both directions. Toward more aid: no Tonkin deception, honest counts of the Trail and the army from 1965, and no Bach Mai. Toward less: a police and a national file used by the palace against its domestic opposition from 1966 and consolidated for the 1971 election (POS002-3, POS006-4), a Phoenix scandal that began in 1967, and the decrees of 1972 (EVMT01-3), which are the material from which the political-prisoner campaign of 1973–74 was made. My judgment is that these roughly cancel and that neither moves the vote, which was decided by Watergate and the nation's verdict of 1968–73, not by the record of the Republic [X-MT01]. A provision could change it only by changing what the Republic asked Congress to pay for: an army that needed less American fire, fuel and ammunition to fight. That is the second of the three things POSMT01-1 says the rung waits for.

**Carriage: episodic.** The 1974 vote is now an examined event; DIRMT01-2 carries it.

## 8. Open search

### The soldier's family

The open search found one thing the structure of this exercise systematically missed, and it is the most important finding of this part after the date.

The ARVN soldier did not go to war alone. His wife and children lived in the dependants' quarters beside his unit's base or in the town behind it, because his pay could not keep two households and because the army, unlike the American one, had no rotation home; the regiments of I Corps were recruited in I Corps and their families lived there [H]. In 1974 this was a strength: the battalions on the ridges at Thuong Duc and Duc Duc fought with their families behind them, and the record shows them holding their companies together (EV000-91). In March 1975 it became the mechanism of collapse. When the President's orders gave up Quang Tri, Hue and then the Highlands, the families were in the path of the PAVN, and the men went to them; the 2nd Division's 6,000 deserters on 28 March were not running from the enemy but toward their children (EV000-94) [H75]. The retreat down Route 7 became the convoy of tears because 400,000 civilians, many of them soldiers' families, joined the column (EV000-95) [H74]. The Airborne and the Marines, whose families were in Saigon, fought on.

This is the mechanism that absorbed every reform of the army this exercise has made. A board-chosen battalion commander can hold a company under fire; no commander, chosen by anyone, holds a man whose children are on the road with the PAVN behind them (EV000-94 now says so). The officer education, the board, the selected advisers, the better rifles: each made the battalion better at the fight in front of it, and in March 1975 the fight in front of it was not the one that decided whether it stayed together. The Dalat study of November 1974 saw this and put it first: in its plan for a withdrawal from the Highlands, the families were to be moved first (EV000-95). It is the only document in the record that treats the soldier's family as a military fact, and the President took its conclusion and not that line.

Why did the structure miss it? Because every provision received has been equipment, an institution, or a programme, and the dossier asks what each does to the force, the enemy and the population; the soldier's family belongs to none of the three. It is neither the population the provisions protect nor the force they equip, and it was the hinge between them. **Carriage: standing** (DIRMT01-9), and a new gap in the register (G-17).

### Every provision arrived on the same day

Every provision this exercise has received arrived on 1 January 1964 or earlier. The analysis has therefore never had to treat a capability arriving into a war already in motion, with a force already shaped around its absence, or the transition cost of adopting it mid-campaign. This is not a fault of the analysis but of its inputs, and it has a consequence the audit should name: every effect in the record is a ten-year effect, compounded from the first day, and none of the chapters has had to show what a provision does in its first year in a war that did not plan for it. The first provision with a later arrival date will be the first test of that, and the analysis should expect it to be harder. **Carriage: episodic.**

### An enemy without initiative in 1969–71

The first part noted that the digest of the enemy's war shows no event in Phase IV in which the enemy held the initiative. Historically 1969–71 contained at least one enemy decision of the first importance taken for his own reasons: COSVN's turn to protracted war and economy of force in 1969, after the losses of 1968, which the record mentions as a fact of the overview and never as a decision with a date and an author [H]. **Episodic:** the next memorandum that revises Phase IV writes that decision as an event, with its initiative, under G3.

## Close

### The end-state call

The end state does not change, and it is now held for stated reasons. The rung is (a), collapse, on the mechanism the first part reaffirmed (POSMT01-1): every provision so far is American-owned and gone by 1973, or works below the level of command at which 1972 and 1975 were decided, or was captured by the palace when the American lever lapsed. To that this part adds a fourth reason no memorandum had named, the soldier's family, which absorbed in March 1975 whatever the reforms had made of the battalions.

The date of the fall is 30 April 1975, in a window of about 27 April to the first days of May, as this audit's own estimate and not as history carried [X-MT01]. It was set by Hanoi's resolution to finish before the rains and by the march of the 1st Corps. The mechanisms in the cumulative force that pull it later act on fights the PAVN chose to bypass; those that pull it earlier act on decisions taken on the historical days. The threshold that would have moved it, a delay of about three weeks in the collapse of I and II Corps, is beyond anything the force has reached. That threshold, and not the date, is what the next memoranda should be measured against. The end-state file is unchanged, because neither the rung nor the date moves; its summary, "on the historical timeline", is now a conclusion of this test.

The question the reader put, how the fall could not move by so much as a day, has its answer. Some of the provisions would have moved it by days, in both directions, and the days were absorbed by a plan that was waiting for a corps on the road and racing the rains. The war was decided at three points none of them touched: the palace on 13–14 March, the road from Thanh Hoa, and the morning of 30 April. That is a real result and not a style. It is also a narrow one: a Republic that had held I and II Corps three weeks longer would have met the rains, and the next year is a war this exercise has never written.

### How far the analysis can be trusted

The analysis is strongest where it has been most specific: the political provisions in the held belt, the measured siege, the Trail as a system, and the honest treatment of every metric the war corrupted. Its account of why the rung has not moved is sound, and survived this audit. It is weakest in three places, all of which this audit has now touched: the end of the war, where it carried the historical outcome without deriving it; the countryside after 1971, which it moved by changing numbers; and everything about the Vietnamese that is not an institution, the soldier's family, the corps commanders' politics, the Montagnards, the sects, the enemy's internal arguments, and the economy that ran under all of them. The weaknesses do not imply that the end-state call is wrong. They imply that it was, until now, less tested than it read, and that a provision reaching the corps commanders, the 1974 appropriation, an air arm the Republic owns, or the soldier's household would find the record less ready to measure its effect than the length of the memoranda suggests.
