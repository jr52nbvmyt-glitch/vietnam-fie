## 1. The state of the war

### 1964–65: the North's economy burned, the South's war unchanged

Memorandum 013 opened the air war on history's date, 2 March 1965, with an operation history never flew. HAMMER FALL struck the full target list in eight days (EV013-1):
- the fuel farms at Haiphong and Hanoi;
- the power stations;
- the jet fields, with the 921st Regiment's MiG-17s on Phuc Yen's revetments;
- the Long Bien and Thanh Hoa bridges and the northeast railway;
- Haiphong's port facilities, struck once under the user's rule.

Within a week the North had no bulk fuel and no central power [X-013].

The North did not answer as an economy. It answered as a society (EV013-2):
- The Politburo's meeting of 25–27 March put the North on a war footing. It dispersed every factory, depot and fuel store that could be moved, and raised a militia of the whole people for air defence and repair.
- It held the 308th and 312th Divisions and two regiments of the 320th in the delta and on the coast through the summer, against a landing that was not coming.
- Moscow sent SA-2s for four battalions instead of two, about 1,000 advisers, diesel generators, pumps and trucks.
- Beijing sent railway, engineer and anti-aircraft troops from June. It refused Moscow an air corridor, for its own reasons, which put the first SA-2s in Hanoi three to five weeks late.

By the end of July the North's fuel was in drums under the trees and its power came from about 700 village diesels. Its bridges were pontoons by night and its fighters lived in China. About 300,000–400,000 of its people were repairing and carrying full time, a year before history's figure reached that size [X-013].

**The first of the user's questions is answered here.** The opening strike was the most disruptive single act of the war against the North. It destroyed what history's campaign took three years to reach, and it reached it before the North had dispersed. It changed the North's economy, its air force and its relationship with Moscow and Beijing.

It did not change the war in the South, because the war in the South did not run on what the strike destroyed. The Theater Needs Assessment's settled position POSTNA-4 is the record's base fact here. The southern war needed about 20 tons a day from the North in 1964 and about 70–90 tons a day in 1966, and both figures were carried through Laos on a road whose capacity was set by labour, not by fuel or electricity. The North replaced lost fuel and power with drums and diesels within a season. It replaced its lost industry with the very imports that also fed the Trail.

Chain C013-1 turns the strike against itself. The trucks that Moscow sent in place of heavy plant went to Group 559 in 1965–66, and the panhandle had more trucks than history's when HARD CROSSING struck it in April 1966 (F013-14). The provision's own terms closed the other two routes. Haiphong was struck once and not mined until May 1972, so the sea route stayed open. Laos stayed with the ambassador, so the Trail was not part of the campaign.

**The one channel to the South was the divisions held at home, and the checkpoint tests it in §5.** It finds less there than memorandum 013 claimed.

The ground war of 1964–65 is the war of CP02's review, with memorandum 012's two organisations laid over it. The combined command was a channel, not yet a command. It survived Khanh's coup by passing every order through his office (EV012-1). Seoul and Canberra consented on conditions: a Korean countersignature on every order to a Korean formation, and an Australian casualty estimate sent to Canberra (EV012-3).

The task force against the infrastructure ended 1965 about 1,100 strong. Its first campaign found nothing for two months on files built by day, then broke the Can Giuoc security section from one notebook (EV012-2). Its second drove the Hoc Mon committee, already pushed out of its district by STILL WATER, from Cu Chi into the Ho Bo woods (EV012-4).

### 1966–67: the infrastructure broken in the held belt, the main-force war unchanged

By the end of 1967 the task force had broken the Front's district government of the Saigon belt from its edge. The Hoc Mon committee and the region's finance section governed from the Ho Bo woods. The region's collections from the posted villages were about an eighth of 1964's (EV012-4) [X-012]. With the police (EV002-4, EV002-5) and Phung Hoang (EV006-2), it had driven the resident infrastructure out of most villages with settled posts. The arrest count fell because there was little left in the held belt to arrest [X-002].

On the II Corps coast the record goes the other way. The task group took a Korean special-forces team into a high-value list built from ralliers, and the team killed a family at Hoa Thanh. The Korean commander refused the magistrate's referral under the same national right that had made combined command acceptable in Seoul. The Front had a story about the task force a year before Duc Thanh (EV012-5, C012-3).

**This is the record's answer to the user's second question, and the answer is less than the question expects.** The VCI campaign changed the infrastructure, and the record says so: VCI strength in Phase III was re-judged to −8..−6 in memorandum 012. It did not change night control, and the record says nothing about that, because nobody judged it.

Night control's end-vector entries for Phases I to V are still the mechanical conversion of R01 from the old bands. In Phase III the entry is +2..+4 "converted". No memorandum since R01 has re-judged it, and none of its corps facets has been judged. The checkpoint finds that the record has no judgment of the rate of securing at all, only a carried-forward band. It finds three reasons why, set out in §3. Part 2 re-judges it by corps in the units the user's new scale will give it. The evidence it will weigh is already in the chapters:
- the posted villages paying an eighth of their 1964 taxes;
- the hamlets of Hoc Mon empty of resident cadres;
- the coastal provinces of I and II Corps known thinly and held as history held them.

That evidence supports a substantial gain in III and IV Corps and a small one in II and I Corps.

The northern campaign of 1966–67 ran on the theater's own list and tempo (P013b). It restruck the jet fields on every repair. It reported its own failure to reduce the flow south in October 1966, with the radar's counts beside its claims (EV013-3, C013-3). It lost about 40–90 fewer aircraft in 1966–67 than history's campaign (POS013-3) [X-013]. Christmas passed without a pause (EV013-4).

The Stennis hearings of August 1967 were never held, because there were no restrictions to blame (EVCP02-4). Olds's trap of 2 January 1967 met four MiG-21s flying in from China instead of the regiment's full strength, and killed three rather than seven (C013-2).

The main-force war of 1966–67 is unchanged in this period by memoranda 011–013. The enemy still chooses where his regiments fight, and the American divisions still fight on the borders.

### 1968: Tet against a broken infrastructure

Tet is decided where it was decided historically: in Saigon, at Hue, at Khe Sanh and in Washington. This checkpoint's consolidated Phase III (§2) puts the record's changes in one place, and they all lie at the edges:
- In IV Corps the towns are cleared faster. The hamlets warned, the guides did not come, and a third of the uprising committees were taken (EV000-42).
- On the northwestern road to Tan Son Nhut the columns came through Hoc Mon with nobody to guide them, and they arrived late (EV012-8, C012-2).
- At Tan Son Nhut the sappers who attacked the combined compound carried a plan drawn by a captain in its own plans cell, and the compound held (EV012-7).
- At Hue the battle was one battle under Truong. The 3rd Brigade, given first call on the lift, broke the western reinforcement at La Chu, and the battle ended on 27 February rather than 2 March (EV000-44).

**The third question, whether the VCI campaign changed the ability to prepare for Tet, is tested in §3. The answer is: at corps level, a little; at national level, not at all.** The files held the political half of the offensive: the caches, the committees and the guides. They did not hold its date or its scale, which travelled through military channels. Two decisions above the level the files reached absorbed the warning. MACV read the main effort as the north. The Joint General Staff let its leave order stand.

In the north the year went as before. Khe Sanh was measured every night and held at a third fewer losses (EV000-41). The decision of 31 March had no partial halt to offer, because the campaign allowed none (EV000-45). The northern campaign went on above the 19th parallel through the summer, over the Hanoi and Haiphong gun belts, at a cost of about 40 aircraft history never risked (EV013-6). The talks did not open in May (EV000-48). They opened on 13 November, after the halt of 1 November, and the four-party conference met in January on history's date (EV000-53, C013-4).

### 1969–71: the inheritance

Memorandum 011's removal of the massacre at Son My runs through this period. There was nothing for Ridenhour to report or Hersh to publish, and the Army was never made to look at the Americal Division's command climate. MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 for a theater-wide inquiry into the body count died for want of a scandal. In July 1971 the House subcommittee asked whether Colby's audited Phoenix figures could be trusted, not whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means (EV000-72, C011-2).

Phung Hoang ended 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralised since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon (EV000-72) [X-006]. The combined command's transfer to Vietnamese command stopped at the palace's corps commander in the Delta (EV012-6). The task force's American half left with the Americans.

### 1972–75: the reprieve

Memorandum 011's other removal, of the break-in of 1972, is the strongest single cause of the end state. The President who wrote the letters to Thieu stayed in office through 1974 (EV000-86). The midterm of 1974 cost his party about 22 House seats rather than 49 (EV011-1). The RVNAF received about $950 million for fiscal 1975, with fuel and aircraft reached for the first time (EV000-89).

After Phuoc Long, Thieu read the half of the money he received as a promise kept. On 11 and 14 March 1975 he refused to give up any province. Without the orders of 13–14 March, I and II Corps collapsed three to five weeks late. The Politburo, with no double collapse to resolve on, put the assault on Saigon back to the dry season of 1976 (EV011-2, EVMT01-4, C011-1).

Congress voted about $150 million in March and $175 million in May. Thieu then took on 6 May the Dalat study's method he had refused in March, and SOUTHERN WALL brought the reserve home from Da Nang (EV011-3, C011-3). On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south. Its fall is expected between February and May 1976. The end state stands at rung (b).

Memoranda 012 and 013 do not reach 1975 in any way that moves it. The task force's national file survives in Saigon. The northern campaign's legacy is a North that learned dispersal in 1965 rather than 1967, and an argument that air power had been shackled that nobody in this war can make (C013-3).

### What the cumulative force has and has not done

Thirteen memoranda have changed the war in three places:
- **The held belt of the Delta and the Saigon region.** Land, police, Phung Hoang and the task force broke the Front's government there, and the posts held it through 1972.
- **The quality of the ARVN below the regiment.** Rifles, officers, training and sergeants made battalions that broke less often and lost fewer men.
- **The American political calendar of 1972–75.** This change came through a removed scandal, not through anything fought.

They have not changed four things:
- the enemy's control of where the main forces fight;
- the North's ability to supply the South;
- the ARVN's divisions and corps;
- the Republic's dependence on American air.

The end state is held at rung (b) by the third change alone. The first two make the reprieve usable, because the Republic of 1975 has a Delta and a capital belt worth defending and battalions able to defend them. Neither, without the third, would have moved the end state off rung (a).

## 6. The experience of the war

**A Popular Forces platoon leader, Tan Thoi Nhi, Hoc Mon District, 30 January 1968, 2100.** *Claim tested: the task force had emptied Hoc Mon's hamlets of the infrastructure by 1968 (EV012-8).*

Corporal First Class Lam Van Thuan has held the platoon for two years. For the last eighteen months his men have slept in the post instead of in their houses, because nobody in the hamlet comes at night any more to tell their wives which house to stay out of. Tonight there is no festival noise from the fields west of Ba Queo, where on any other night of the holiday the market gardeners would be burning incense. Instead there are men lying in rows in the dry paddy, a company or more, with nobody bringing them rice. That is how he knows they are not from here.

He reports a company moving south. The district takes forty minutes to answer.

The claim is visible from where he stands, and visible as an absence: what he sees is soldiers who have no one in his hamlet to hide them. What he cannot see is the warning. Nobody tells him that a battalion is expected, and nobody in Saigon knows that the company in the fields is part of the attack on the capital. From his post, the VCI campaign gave him eyes and gave the enemy none. It did not give the army a date.

**A PAVN truck driver, Route 15 below the Mu Gia pass, October 1966.** *Claim tested: the opening strike turned the North's imports into transport for the Trail (C013-1, F013-14).*

Le Van Ngoc drives a GAZ-51 that came by rail from Pingxiang in the spring of 1965. It had been one of a hundred meant for the textile combine at Nam Dinh, before the combine's power station went in the first week. His company has twice the trucks it had at its formation. Every driver knows the joke that the Americans built the 559th's motor pool by burning Nam Dinh.

The claim is visible from his cab exactly as stated. What he does not see is a road any harder to drive than last year's. The bombing that matters to him is in Laos, on the passes. HAMMER FALL ended for him in a factory he never worked in.

**A VC district cadre, the Ho Bo woods, July 1966.** *Claim tested: the task force broke the Front's district government of the Saigon belt from its edge (EV012-4), and night control in III Corps is substantially higher than history's (§3, Part 2).*

The Hoc Mon finance cadre has not slept in a Hoc Mon hamlet since the spring of 1965. He collects by courier from three villages. The posted villages along Route 1 pay nothing, because the men who used to collect there are dead, in Con Son, or rallied. The courier who carried the region's ledger to Cu Chi was taken with it. He keeps his own accounts in his head now.

The claim is visible from his hammock: the Front still governs Hoc Mon, from eighteen kilometres away, and governs less of it. What the claim cannot measure from here is the hamlets beyond the posted villages. Those still pay him, and the night control figure will have to show both kinds.

**An American district senior adviser, Tuy An District, Phu Yen Province, December 1966.** *Claim tested: in II Corps the infrastructure campaign changed little, and the Korean incident at Hoa Thanh hurt the government's standing (EV012-5, C012-3).*

The adviser, a major on his second year in the district, has a map with three colours: hamlets the district chief's PF hold at night, hamlets the Koreans clear by day, and the rest. The second colour has not shrunk since the summer. The story the province chief's interpreters bring back from the market is about Hoa Thanh. In it the Korean soldiers who killed the family are now heroes in Seoul, and nobody in Saigon can touch them.

The claim is visible: from Tuy An, II Corps's coast is held as history held it. The task force is known here mainly as the name in a story. Night control in II Corps should be judged near zero, and the legitimacy facet for II Corps slightly negative.

**A hamlet chief, Can Giuoc District, Long An Province, March 1965.** *Claim tested: the land programme returned to Can Giuoc's posted hamlets with no title holder killed, because the rebuilt security section did not know the hamlets (C012-1).*

The chief gives out the second round of titles in the hamlet office, with the PF platoon outside. Last June the section killed four of his title holders in a week. This February the families came for their papers in daylight. He does not know about the notebook taken in an ambush in September. He knows that the men who used to come at night knew which house was whose, and the new ones, when they come, ask.

The claim is visible, and it is the clearest of the five: the change is in local knowledge, and he can see it in the questions.

**An official of the Central Intelligence Organization, Saigon, 12 January 1976.** *Claim tested: the national file survives in Saigon at the end of 1975, while the province files of Quang Ngai and Quang Tin were taken in March (Phase VI; EV000-84, EV000-94, EV000-98).*

The official keeps the national file's filing room in the CIO's building. It holds about 230,000 names by 1971 and more since. He has read the reports of what the Front did with the province files of Quang Ngai and Quang Tin after March. The destruction plan of 1968 sits in the drawer, and the thermite with it. Every week he asks whether a copy should go south to Can Tho, and every week nobody above him answers.

The claim is visible, and it has a cost the record has never weighed: the file is a target for the enemy, and the end state's "fall expected between February and May 1976" decides which of the two plans in his drawer is carried out. **The checkpoint notes this as a gap for Part 2.** The record has no judgment of what the file's survival or destruction means for the Republic's last months, or for the people in it.
