# Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, offered negotiations, kept the bombing of the whole North, and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war [H40], and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end [H40].

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment, and in this war, with no partial halt to trade, the talks wait for the full halt in November (EV000-45, EV000-48, EV000-53). The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp [H39]. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone [H39].

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way. Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and Khe Sanh, and in Washington (EV000-45) [X-001]. What changes is the Delta, the hamlets and the army that met the offensive.

In the held belt the Front attacks from a smaller base. It recruits fewer men in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre in 1968 (EV000-52). The hamlets around the western Delta towns warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers. The Special Branch knows the Front's urban cadres. Its guides do not arrive. A third of the leaders it sends into the Delta towns to direct the uprising are taken in February, because the district centres had charted them for three years (EV000-42) [X-001] [X-002] [X-006]. On the northwestern road to Tan Son Nhut its columns cross Hoc Mon through hamlets that have no resident cadres left to guide them (EV012-8). In Hue its lists are better than elsewhere, because the Republic's own census fed them. The province centre's informant cards burn in the first hour; the district centres' cards do not (EV000-44).

The warning was in the files and did not change the night. By January the task force's corps groups held caches, couriers and committees that pointed at the towns around Tet. The combined estimate read them as local, and the leave order stood (EV000-42, EV012-8) [X-CP03].

The ARVN and the territorials go into Tet changed in four ways:
- every man carries a modern rifle;
- every man has passed the basic course;
- battalion commanders were chosen by the board;
- sergeants have been schooled.

In the district towns about a third of the attacks never get inside the compound walls. In IV and III Corps the battalions recall their men faster. The wounded of both allied armies are alive in larger numbers in the morning. At Cai Lay the posts that kept their listening posts out see the 514th Battalion coming and are left alone (EV000-42, EV010-4) [X-004] [X-007] [X-009] [X-010]. In the cities the PAVN and the main force fight block by block, and there all of this lowers the cost without shortening the battles. Hue, fought under one combined commander, ends five days early (EV000-44) [X-012]. The sappers who attack the combined headquarters carry a plan a traitor had drawn, and the compound holds (EV012-7).

When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled. Phoenix is only the name Washington learned for district centres that had run since 1964 without quotas and under an audit, and the Commission audits it against the land register (EV000-54) [X-001].

The mobilization of June finds rifles in stock, a training command that keeps the course at ten weeks, and platoon leaders who have passed the leader's course in about fourteen platoons in fifteen; its new units reach the field trained and a month late, and the academy and the sergeants' ranks pay for it (EV000-51) [X-007] [X-009] [X-010]. The Front's third offensive goes for what no course reached, the People's Self-Defense Force (EV009-4), and COSVN works on the officers the board had passed over (EV000-52).

Above all this the radar watches the border. At Khe Sanh it measures the siege every night and its trenches are destroyed before they reach the wire; the PAVN pulls back about two weeks early at a much heavier price, and the garrison loses about a third fewer men (EV000-41). It is the most complete American victory of the war on the ground and changes nothing in Washington, and the brigade pulled from Thua Thien's hamlets to win it is missing when the Front returns to them. Abrams refuses to make it a doctrine of border bases and closes Khe Sanh on the historical date (EV000-51). After the halt the radar measures how the North uses it (EV003-5), and Commando Hunt opens against a panhandle net built to be cut, with a second ledger beside the Air Force's claims (EV000-55).

The President's decision of 31 March has no partial halt in it, because the campaign he had authorized in 1965 allowed none (EV000-45). Hanoi will not talk while the bombs fall, so the May and August offensives are fought with no table in Paris. The northern campaign goes on above the 19th parallel through the summer. It costs about 40 aircraft over the Hanoi and Haiphong gun belts, which history's campaign never risked (EV013-6) [X-013]. The halt of 1 November comes on history's date and opens the talks themselves. The four-party conference meets in January, as it did historically: the six months lost in Paris were months history had spent on statements (EV000-53).

## EV000-41 · The siege of Khe Sanh · 21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province; Route 9 in Laos to Ban Houei Sane · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: enemy*

In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region [H46]. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion [H46].

In this war the concentration was watched from the day it began. From mid-December the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos and the trucks of Group 559 turning east from Tchepone toward Ban Houei Sane, about 20–25 a night rising to about 80 by mid-January [X-003]. The U-2S's signals payload fixed the headquarters of the 325C Division northwest of Hill 881 North on 2 January and that of the 304th near Co Roc, across the border, on 11 January, and from the volume and pattern of their traffic the J-2 analysts identified a front headquarters forming above them [X-003]. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration this command had ever held. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", read it as confirmation, and after Con Thien he read it as an opportunity: here, he told his staff on 13 January, the enemy would give us the battle we could win (F005-14) [H46] [X-005]. The radar plots went every morning to Saigon and every evening to the White House, where the President kept a terrain model of Khe Sanh in the Situation Room [H] [X-003]. The 1st Cavalry Division moved north to I Corps in January, as it did historically, and in this war a brigade of the 101st Airborne Division moved with it, drawn from the coastal districts of Thua Thien where it had been working with the Regional Forces (F005-14) [H] [X-005].

The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that would attack the cities on 30–31 January moved into them in small groups, on foot and by bus and sampan among the millions travelling home for Tet, under the eyes of their own people; nothing in the radar picture warned of Saigon, Hue or the provincial towns (F003-16) [X-003]. General Frederick Weyand's decision of 10 January to pull fifteen battalions back toward Saigon rested, as it did historically, on signals and ground intelligence about the enemy's units in III Corps [H] [X-003]. The radar deepened the American fixation on Khe Sanh, which history had without it; the siege box, by promising a victory there, deepened it again.

The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump [H]. The answer was Operation Niagara, and in this war Niagara had two halves. The first was the historical one: about 60 B-52s on an average day, sensors and reconnaissance, more than 158,000 artillery rounds in the first three months, and boxes on the valleys, the parks and Route 9 in Laos that the radar had seen fill during the night [H46] [X-003]. It fell as a carpet half as dense as the Big Belly's, because the J carries about half the D's unguided bombs, and in tonnage of unguided bombs Niagara delivered about two-thirds of its historical weight (F005-8) [M005] [X-005]. The second half was new. From 23 January a Global Hawk stood over the Khe Sanh plateau every night, and its radar imaged the ground within three kilometres of the perimeter each hour; Sorrell had moved a detachment of the coordinate cell to Da Nang, and from the night of 26 January cells of three Js arrived every ninety minutes, each carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s aimed at positions measured that night (F005-21) [X-005].

What the PAVN had come to do at Khe Sanh, it did by digging. Historically the trenches of the 304th and 325C Divisions crept toward the perimeter from mid-February and reached within a few dozen metres of the wire on the east, where the 37th Rangers held [H46]. Here the trench lines were imaged as they were dug. A trench opened at dusk was on the cell's board before midnight and under a GBU-38 before dawn, and from 15 February the cells were cleared to bomb as close as 400 metres to the Marines and Rangers, inside the kilometre that history's carpets kept [M005] [X-005]. The 304th Division dug three times toward the eastern perimeter, on 17, 22 and 27 February, and each time its trenches were destroyed at 500–800 metres [X-005]. On the night of 29 February, when a regiment of the 304th formed to assault the Rangers' sector, it formed in the open beyond the trenches it had not been able to keep, and the GBU-38 cells and the Marine artillery broke it before it reached the wire, as they did historically, at a higher cost to the attackers [H] [X-005]. The PAVN's guns at Co Roc across the border, 130mm and 152mm pieces rolled out of caves to fire, were struck at their firing positions by GBU-31(V)3/Bs within hours of each mission, and by mid-March they fired from Co Roc only at night and only a few rounds a gun [X-005].

On the night of 6–7 February the panhandle watch tracked a column of eleven or twelve slow vehicles leaving the Ban Houei Sane area eastward on Route 9, and the warning reached the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei about forty minutes before twelve PT-76s of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, attacked it at about 0030 on 7 February [H46] [X-003]. The defenders knocked out seven tanks; the camp still fell, its last fight a matter of metres in which no bomb could help, and of about 500 CIDG some 160 were killed or missing and of 24 Americans eight were killed [X-003]. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, and the air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters [H46]. Here the flak around the approaches, measured each night, was struck each night, and I estimate the air bridge lost about a third fewer helicopters [X-005].

The front command under Major General Tran Quy Hai pulled the 325C Division back from the hills in mid-March and the 304th from the eastern trenches before the end of the month, about two weeks before its historical withdrawal [H] [X-005]. Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April [H46]. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July [H46]. I estimate that the garrison lost about a third fewer killed than the historical one, about 180, and that the PAVN's dead were about half again its acknowledged figure, perhaps 3,500–4,000 to July; both are judgments built on the historical figures and the change in what the bombs could reach, not counts [X-005]. In this war Khe Sanh was the worst battle the PAVN fought in 1968, and the most complete American victory of the war on the ground. It was won at the place Hanoi had chosen, on the border, while the war was decided in the cities and in Washington; and the brigade taken from Thua Thien's hamlets to help win it was not in Thua Thien when the Front's local forces returned to them in February (F005-14) [X-005].

## EV000-42 · The Tet Offensive: the first wave · 30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,004,006,007,009,CP03
*Initiative: enemy*
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps [H39]. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet [H]. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country [H39]. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital [H39].

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns [H]. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed [H39]. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing [H39]. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed [H39].

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces [X-001]. The differences fall in IV and III Corps and in the district towns.

**The land and the police.** The battalions that attacked Can Tho and the Binh Thuy air base came up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh. GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left those villages in the hands of their own Popular Forces (EV001-6). PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, and two of them fought [X-001]. The 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault [X-001].

The attackers also waited for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal. The guides never arrived, because both villages had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier [X-002]. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters. Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, and far less of the town was destroyed than on the historical course [X-001]. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days [X-001].

Ben Tre in Kien Hoa was the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta. There the government had titled little outside the town, because it held little outside the town, and the battle and the destruction were as they were historically [X-001]. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964 (F001-4, F001-17).

In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced to lead the uprising. In the first two weeks of February they arrested about 1,700 of them, on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs [X-002]. The infrastructure driven from the villages had gone to the towns (EV002-5).

The PIOCCs at Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long had carried charts of the province committees since 1965. The charts held photographs from the biometric enrolment of the cadres' arrested relatives and ralliers (EC006-1). About 140 province- and district-committee cadres came into the three towns to form the "revolutionary committees". About 50 of them were taken by 15 February, most on the identifications of ward police who had their photographs, and the Can Tho committee never held its first meeting [X-006].

**The warning.** The checkpoint of memorandum 013 tested whether the files should have warned of Tet [X-CP03]. They held the pieces:
- the three Hoc Mon caches found in December from a courier's notebook (EV012-8);
- the province committees' preparations for the uprising in the Delta towns, which the district centres had been charting;
- the task force's national picture of the Front's political organisation (F012-25).

What the files did not hold was the date or the scale. The order for the general offensive went down the military channels and reached the cadres in the towns only days before it, and the file saw the political half of the enemy, not the regiments [X-CP03]. The combined estimate of January 1968 read the pieces as preparations for attacks on the towns of the Delta and the Saigon belt around Tet, local in scale and secondary to Khe Sanh.

Two decisions above the level the files reached absorbed the warning, and they kept the national surprise:
- MACV judged the main effort to be in the north (EV000-41).
- The Joint General Staff's order that sent half the army on Tet leave stood until the first attacks in I and II Corps (DIRMT01-4).

Below those decisions the warning showed. The III and IV Corps combined centres kept one reaction battalion in each divisional area at its towns over the holiday, and that is where the recall plans worked fastest [X-CP03].

**The army in the towns.** By January 1968 about four in five ARVN battalion commanders had been chosen by the board and had held their battalions for more than a year. The board's commanders obeyed the leave order, but in IV and III Corps they had recall plans written to the career course's pattern [X-007].

The 21st Division's desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966. It stood at about 90 percent of its authorised strength on the muster roll (F002-15) [X-002]. Of that, by the evening of 31 January, the battalions of the 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions had about 70 percent in the towns against about half historically, a little more where the centres had held a battalion back [X-007] [X-CP03].

Every ARVN soldier and territorial had carried a modern carbine for four years, and had passed the ten-week or the six-week course (F004-16) [X-004] [X-009]. The difference is clearest in the district towns. There the first wave was made by VC local-force battalions and companies against garrisons of an RF company or two, a PF platoon and the district police. I estimate that the attackers penetrated about two-thirds of the 64–72 district towns attacked, against nearly all historically [X-004]. The ones they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, as at Long Huu in 1964 (EV002-2). The companies fired as the territorial course taught, in pairs, with ammunition kept for the second rush, under lieutenants many of whom had been sergeants in the same companies two years before (EV007-4) [X-007] [X-009].

At Cai Lay on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st. It had one squad gun lent by the 7th Division and its own carbines. At 0600 it still had about a third of its basic load, and it was still firing at 0730, when the 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived. The battalion of the 261st had planned to be through the town by 0400 [X-004] [X-009].

In Can Tho, Captain Jerome Pike, adviser to the 1st Battalion, 31st Regiment, took the call of a PF corporal on the Xa No canal in Vietnamese at 2340 on 30 January. He moved the battalion's reaction company to the university before the attackers reached it (F006-2) [X-006]. The 9th Division's 2nd Brigade and the Mobile Riverine Force had been together since 1967, and they fought the streets of My Tho and Vinh Long as the units they had trained as [X-006].

**The wounded.** Historically the ARVN's wounded in the offensive were about three times its dead [H39]. In this war more of those torn by a B40 fragment or a burst of AK-47 fire were alive at dawn, because the man beside them had put on a tourniquet. In Hue the wounded of the 1st Division and the Marines lay in the Citadel's streets for a month, and the course saved men every day of it. The surgeons at Phu Bai complained, as Nha Trang's had in 1965, about tourniquets left on too long [X-009].

**The count.** Taking the rifles, the officers and the course together, I put the ARVN's killed in the first phase at about 2,150 against 2,788 historically [X-004] [X-007] [X-009]. The rifles account for about 290 of the difference, the officers for about 200 and the course for about 150. I put the American killed in the first phase at about 30–60 fewer, at the rate shown at the Ia Drang (EV000-23) [X-009]. The warning in III and IV Corps does not move the count, because the dead fell at Saigon, Hue and the cities, where it changed nothing [X-CP03]. I do not adjust the enemy's losses, which were never measured to a precision these changes would show in.

None of this touched the outcome in Saigon, Hue or the Highlands, where the attackers were main-force units no district centre had charted [X-002] [X-006]. At Hue the 1st Division under Truong, the list's first name since 1966 (EV000-26), fought as it fought historically.

## EV010-4 · The posts of Cai Lay at Tet · 30 Jan – 2 Feb 1968 · Cai Lay and Cai Be Districts, Dinh Tuong Province; the post at Ap My Loi, My Phuoc Tay; the posts along the canal and Route 4 east to My Tho · NEW · 010
*Initiative: enemy*

The Front's battalions came through Cai Lay to attack My Tho, and most of the posts on the way were left alone. ## EV012-8 · Tet without guides in Hoc Mon · 30 Jan – 4 Feb 1968 · Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province; the approaches to Tan Son Nhut from Ba Queo and Vinh Loc · NEW · 012

The battalions that attacked Saigon from the northwest at Tet came down the corridor the Hoc Mon committee had governed until 1965. In this war the Hoc Mon committee had governed from the Ho Bo woods since 1966, and its tax collectors had not lived in a Hoc Mon hamlet since (EV012-4) [X-012]. ## EV000-43 · The battle for Saigon and the attack on the US embassy · 31 Jan – 7 Mar 1968 · Saigon, Cholon and Tan Son Nhut · HIST · 000
At 02:45 on 31 January a 19-man team of the VC C-10 Sapper Battalion blew a hole in the wall of the new US embassy compound in Saigon and fought inside the grounds for more than six hours without entering the chancery; the compound was secured by 09:20, and five Americans were killed [H39] [H]. The sappers were all killed or captured [H]. Other VC units attacked Tan Son Nhut air base with three battalions, the Joint General Staff compound, the Presidential Palace and the naval headquarters, and held the national radio station for about six hours, though they could not broadcast [H39]. The image of the embassy under attack, broadcast in the United States hours after Westmoreland had declared the enemy's offensive capacity broken, did more political damage than any battle of the war [H].

The fighting in Saigon lasted weeks in Cholon, the Chinese quarter, where VC battalions held out around the Phu Tho racetrack. On 4 February residents were ordered out and the area declared a free-fire zone; the last battle in Cholon was fought on 7 March [H39]. On 1 February the national police chief, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shot a captured VC officer in the head in a Saigon street before a photographer and a film camera [H]. The image became, with the embassy, the American public's picture of Tet.

## EV000-44 · The battle of Hue · 31 Jan – 27 Feb 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · HIST-ALT · 000,002,004,006,012,CP03
*Initiative: enemy*
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River [H45]. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions [H45]. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division [H45].

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle [H45] [H]. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west; historically it did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February [H45]. Historically the ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel, the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March [H].

In this war Hue was one battle under one commander, and it ended about five days earlier (F012-5, F012-6). Historically it was three battles. Truong's division fought in the Citadel, the Marines fought south of the river under their own chain, and the 1st Cavalry Division's brigade fought to the west under the Army's, with no commander below Saigon over all three until MACV Forward was established at Phu Bai on 9 February [H]. Here the I Corps combined centre at Da Nang, under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam with Lieutenant General Robert E. Cushman of III Marine Amphibious Force as his deputy, named Truong commander of the battle of Hue at 1100 on 1 February [H] [X-012]. Brigadier General Foster C. LaHue of Task Force X-Ray became his deputy for the south bank, and the 3rd Brigade's commander became his deputy for the western approaches [H] [X-012]. One fire-support coordination centre in the Citadel took every call for naval gunfire, Marine and Army artillery and the aircraft the weather allowed, from Vietnamese and American units alike, through the switchboard the combined command had run in I Corps since 1966 (EC012-3) [X-012].

The change that shortened the battle was in the west. Truong's first request to the combined centre, on the afternoon of 1 February, was for the western routes to be cut before anything else, because the PAVN was reinforcing through La Chu and the hamlets of Thon Que Chu. It was the reinforcement, not the walls, that would make the Citadel long [X-012]. The combined centre gave the 3rd Brigade first call on the I Corps helicopter lift from 2 February and placed the ARVN 1st Division's 3rd Regiment under the brigade's command for the block. Historically the brigade was short of lift and fought its way overland from PK-17 [X-012]. The brigade reached the Citadel's west wall on 20 February rather than 25 February. The 803rd Regiment's battalion from the 324B Division, which historically entered the city through La Chu in mid-February, was caught in the open at Thon La Chu on 12–13 February by the brigade and the ARVN 3rd Regiment together, with naval gunfire from the cruiser and destroyers of the gunfire support group off the coast, called through the Citadel's centre, and broken [X-012]. The flag of the Front came down from the Citadel tower on 19 February, and the combined command declared the battle over on 27 February [X-012]. The weather and the walls still set most of the battle's length, and nothing in the combined command touched either.

The squad weapons made Hue somewhat less costly to retake and no shorter. On the first night the Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound with M4A1s and four squad guns against the 800th and 802nd Battalions' assault teams. The difference was small, because the Hac Bao was among the few ARVN units historically re-armed by then [X-004]. Across the river the Marines had two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman with an M110A2, who could put rounds into an upper-floor window at 200 metres from cover. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and the public health building between 4 and 6 February (EC004-5) [X-004]. What took the blocks was what took them historically: the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, tear gas and men going through walls. Against 458 ARVN and 250 American dead historically, the rifles alone would have made them about 410 and 215 [X-004]. With the battle about five days shorter and the western reinforcement broken at La Chu, I put them at about 370 and 200, and the destruction a little less in the Citadel's western quarter, which was the last to be cleared [X-012].

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded, on the historical course [H45]. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source [H45]. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed [H45]. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800 [H45] [H]. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war. The five days the combined command saved were not days on which the security sections' killing stopped. Most of the executions were carried out in the first two weeks, and the shorter battle saved, I judge, a few hundred of the city's civilian dead at most, from the shelling of the western quarter rather than from the lists [X-012].

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966 (EV000-26), and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household (F002-9) [X-002]. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one [X-002]. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor [X-002].

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred. Every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a quarterly rehearsal [X-002]. The order was written with the Huong Thuy file in front of it (F006-25).

The Thua Thien province centre was in the province headquarters south of the river, which the attackers held until the 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines retook it on 6 February [H45]. Its informant cards never reached the attackers. Against Hieu's rule, the PIOCC's chief, Major Ton That Dinh Lang, had kept the cards of the province's 340 informants in a single safe, because he did not trust his district centres' security. When the attack reached the compound wall at about 0330 on 31 January, he burned the cards in the safe with a can of gasoline. He was killed an hour later on the stairs [X-006].

The district centres of Huong Thuy and Phu Vang, outside the city, kept their copies and lost them when their offices were overrun on 31 January and 1 February. The security section's lists for the southern suburbs included about 60 informants and centre staff named from those files. About 40 of them were among the dead found in the graves at Phu Thu and Da Mai Creek [X-006]. The national file in Saigon held every one of those names and was never in danger in 1968. It was the first time the record showed what a fused file was worth to the enemy.

## EV012-7 · The attack on the combined headquarters · 31 Jan – 3 Feb 1968 · The Combined Operations Command compound and the Joint General Staff compound, Tan Son Nhut, Saigon · NEW · 012

## EV000-45 · The reinforcement request and the decision of 31 March · 20 Feb – 31 Mar 1968 · Saigon and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,013

On 12 February Westmoreland asked for 10,500 more troops; on 13 February a brigade of the 82nd Airborne Division and a Marine regimental landing team, already authorized, were sent [H39] [H]. On 20 February the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Earle Wheeler, went to Saigon, and returned with a request for 206,000 more men, framed by the Chiefs to force a mobilization of the reserves [H]. On 28 February McNamara left office and on 1 March Clark Clifford succeeded him, charged with reviewing the request [H39] [H]. On 10 March the *New York Times* reported it [H]. ## EVCP03-1 · The May fighting: Dai Do, the second wave in Saigon and Kham Duc · 30 Apr – 15 Jun 1968 · Dai Do and the hamlets north of Dong Ha, Quang Tri Province; Saigon, Cholon and towns throughout the South; Kham Duc and Ngok Tavak, Quang Tin Province · HIST-ALT · CP03
*Initiative: enemy*

These three battles are told together because they showed one thing three ways. Hanoi kept the offensive going after February at a price it chose to pay, and none of the provisions reached the places it chose.

**Dai Do.** At the end of April, as the second wave began, battalions of the PAVN 320th Division moved into the hamlets of Dai Do, Dinh To and their neighbours north of Dong Ha, threatening the Marine base and the Cua Viet supply route. Two of the battalions were identified as belonging to the 48th and 52nd Regiments [H49]. The 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines under Lieutenant Colonel William Weise attacked across open paddies from amphibian tractors and boats. With it were the 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines, ARVN units of the 2nd Regiment and a mechanized battalion, and from 1 May the US 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry [H49]. The Marines lost 81 killed and the ARVN 5. The Americans claimed more than 600 PAVN killed; the PAVN has not released its losses [H49]. By 3 May the PAVN had gone. Whether Dai Do was a main thrust or a diversion is not settled [H49].

**Saigon.** On 5 May the PAVN and VC launched the second phase of the general offensive, which the Americans called "mini-Tet". It began with attacks by fire on more than 100 towns and installations and a renewed assault on Saigon [H39] [H]. VC battalions fought into the southern and western districts and Cholon. They were destroyed or driven out over several weeks, with air strikes and artillery used inside the city. Much of District 8 was destroyed and tens of thousands were made homeless [H]. The phase lasted until 15 June [H39]. Its losses fell on the local forces and cadres who had survived February, and many units were filled out with northern replacements [H]. American deaths in May 1968 were the highest of any month of the war [H].

**Kham Duc.** On 10 May the 40th Battalion of the PAVN 2nd Division's 1st Regiment overran Ngok Tavak, held by an Australian-led company of the 11th MIKE Force and a battery of the 2nd Battalion, 13th Marines [H48]. The 1st and 21st Regiments then closed on Kham Duc, held by Detachment A-105, CIDG, a SOG detachment and the 2nd Battalion, 1st Infantry [H48]. On 12 May, under about 350 air sorties, roughly 1,500 soldiers and civilians were flown out. A C-130 flown by Major Bernard L. Bucher was shot down after take-off, killing all 183 Vietnamese civilians aboard, a Special Forces officer and the five crewmen [H48]. Lieutenant Colonel Joe M. Jackson landed a C-123 on the abandoned strip to recover a three-man combat control team, and received the Medal of Honor [H48]. The Americans lost about 42–46 killed, 31–33 missing and twelve aircraft. About 220 CIDG were killed or missing. The US claimed about 345 PAVN killed, and a later study estimates 1,500–2,000 [H48]. Kham Duc was one of the few clear PAVN victories over a US-defended position in 1968 [H48].

**In this war.** All three run as they did historically, and what absorbs the record's changes is the enemy's choice of ground (DIRMT01-4) [X-CP03]:
- Dai Do and Kham Duc lie in I Corps outside the titled and policed belt.
- The May assault on Saigon came from the south and west, through Cholon and District 8, not through the Hoc Mon corridor the task force had emptied (EV012-8).
- In May, as in February, the attackers were main-force units that no district centre charted.

The ARVN's share of Saigon's fighting is smaller in losses by the same rule as February's, which I do not count again.

## EV000-51 · Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned · 10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005,007,009,010,CP03

On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff [H50] [H]. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces [H50]. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy [H50].

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended [H62]. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969 [H62]. Historically the expansion outran the rifles. The M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better [H].

In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment. About 260,000 carbines were already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June, against the year's expected growth [X-004]. The old weapons went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law. Its members got a few days of instruction from the district chiefs, as historically, because no course reached them (F009-6, EV009-4) [X-004].

The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but leaders, and three reforms met it in turn; the figures below are the record's net result.

**Officers.** Historically the Thu Duc candidate course was cut from 37 weeks to 24 in June 1968 [M007·S2]. In this war it was cut to 32 weeks, and the field commissions filled the gap. About 3,000 sergeants had been commissioned since 1966, and about 2,500 more were commissioned in the second half of 1968 after the branch basic course (F007-12, F007-22) [X-007]. The board's lists governed the new RF companies from their first month, and the new PF platoons not at all. The price fell on the ranks the lieutenants came from: by the end of 1968 the army had made lieutenants of about 5,500 of its best sergeants (C007-3) [X-007].

**Training.** Historically the RVNAF sent some draftees to units untrained, and the basic course was cut from twelve weeks to nine [M009·S9]. In this war the Joint General Staff ordered the course cut to six weeks on 21 June. The Central Training Command, with Coyle's successor and the Muster Service's chief behind it, answered that six weeks would leave out the Forge and the field week. It proposed instead to run the 33 national and division centres in two daily shifts, which raised their capacity from about 65,000 to about 105,000 in cycle (EC009-7) [M009·S9] [X-009]. The Joint General Staff accepted on 28 June. About 40,000 of the 220,000 inductees waited three to six weeks for a cycle to open [X-009]. The new battalions and territorial companies reached the field trained, about a month later than history's, in September and October and after the third offensive had begun [X-009].

**Sergeants and corporals.** On the historical training base the field commissions would have left about a third of the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank of sergeant (F004-3) [X-004]. In this war the drill-sergeant corps had returned about 650–1,800 sergeants a year to the battalions since 1966, and in July 1968 the training command released about 1,100 more from the second shift to the new platoons (F009-4) [X-009]. That cut the share without a sergeant to about a fifth [X-009]. Where no sergeant could be found, the platoon was given a corporal first class who had passed the leader's course and served a year in an RF company. In 1968 about two in three corporals first class in the regular battalions, and about half in the RF companies, had passed it (EC010-3) [X-010]. About two in three of the platoons without a sergeant got such a man. Venn's successor reported in September that about one new PF platoon in fifteen had no leader who had passed the course (F010-25) [X-010].

The academy paid. The Joint General Staff ordered the leader's course cut to three weeks. The training command kept the field week and cut the classroom, and the course ran at four weeks from July 1968 to June 1969, its second shift taught by instructors who had been platoon sergeants in 1967 [X-010].

On Khe Sanh the decision was harder than it had been historically, because in this war the base had been a victory rather than an ordeal. Westmoreland, in his last weeks, argued for keeping it as the anchor of a line of border bases under the siege box, and the III Marine Amphibious Force's staff drew up a plan for three more such bases along Route 9 and the Laotian border, to be held by battalions drawn from the coastal districts (F005-14) [X-005]. Abrams refused it on 15 June. His reason, in a message to Wheeler that his staff kept, was that a base the enemy would not attack was a base that held nothing, and that after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one [X-005]. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July [H46]. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ. Here it was read in Hanoi correctly, as a decision by an American commander that the siege the PAVN had lost was not worth inviting again, and in the American press, less correctly, as a retreat from a battle won [X-005].

## EV000-52 · The third offensive · 17 Aug – 23 Sep 1968 · Provincial towns and bases, especially in III Corps and the Central Highlands · HIST-ALT · 000,001,007
*Initiative: enemy*

The third phase of the general offensive began in mid-August with attacks by fire and ground assaults on towns and bases, including attacks around Tay Ninh in III Corps [H39] [H]. It was weaker than its predecessors: the local forces that had carried the attacks of February and May were largely gone, and the main forces would not commit themselves against American firepower in the same way again [H39]. It ended in late September without lasting gains [H39]. Taken together, the three offensives of 1968 cost the VC about 50,000 dead by one estimate against about 4,000 US and 6,000 ARVN [H39]. Their recruitment in the South collapsed: in Region 5, 1,700 recruits in 1969 against 8,000 in 1968, in the Mekong lowlands 100 against 16,000 [H39]. By the end of the year about 70 percent of the main-force troops in the South are reported to have been northerners [H39]. MACV estimated total PAVN and VC strength at 287,000 in January 1968 and 251,000 at its end [H39]. The losses forced a reassessment in Hanoi that would carry into 1969 [H].

In this war the third offensive runs as it did historically, in a part of the country the provisions barely touched [X-001]. What changes is the arithmetic behind it. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968, 16,000 on the historical course [H39], is about 11,000 here, because about a third of the tenant families who would have supplied the recruits had been titled by the government in districts it held at night, and the Front's own conscription in those districts had become coercion that the PF platoons could resist (F001-8, F001-13) [X-001]. The 1969 collapse comes from a lower base and is no less complete. The consequence is that COSVN begins filling its Delta main-force battalions with northern replacements in the summer of 1968, several months earlier than on the historical course, and the northerners who arrive in Chuong Thien and Kien Giang are strangers in hamlets where the Front's strength had always been that its soldiers were local [X-001]. The proportion of northerners in the main forces at the year's end, about 70 percent historically [H39], is a few points higher here [X-001].

COSVN read the year's failure in the towns, among other things, as a failure of its proselytizing among the ARVN's officers, and it chose a new target. A military proselytizing directive of August 1968, captured in Tay Ninh in December, ordered the province committees to work among "the officers whom the puppet promotion board has humiliated": the commanders relieved for cause, the majors passed over three times, the colonels kept from the list (F007-19) [X-007]. It attributed the board, wrongly, to American selection of the ARVN's officers, and its propaganda leaflets of the autumn called the board's commanders "officers chosen by the Americans" [X-007]. About 30 relieved or passed-over officers were identified by the Military Security Service as contacted in 1968–69, and two were arrested as agents; the directive's yield was small and its cost to the Front was smaller [X-007]. In the third offensive itself the attacks on Tay Ninh met the 25th Division's battalions in positions they had held since 1967, and the town was not entered [X-007].

## EV009-4 · The self-defence hamlets · 17 Aug – 31 Oct 1968 · Duc Hue and Trang Bang Districts, Hau Nghia Province; Can Duoc and Rach Kien, Long An Province; Cai Be and Cai Lay Districts, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 009,010
*Initiative: enemy*

The third offensive went for what the course did not reach, and in the provinces around Saigon that was the People's Self-Defense Force. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee, planning the offensive of August after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, ordered its subregions to avoid the RF and PF posts that had held in February and May and to strike the new self-defence groups of the mobilization law, whose members were townsmen and farmers with a Garand and three days' instruction (F009-6) [X-009]. The Front's subregion commands around Saigon had a second reason that the Military Affairs Committee did not need to state: every self-defence group destroyed told the hamlet that the government's new law armed it without protecting it [X-009].

The attacks began on the night of 17–18 August and ran through September. In Duc Hue and Trang Bang the Front's local-force companies attacked about 30 self-defence groups in their hamlets, usually at night and from two sides, and overran about two-thirds of them; in Long An, around Can Duoc and Rach Kien, where NIGHT LANTERN had left the posts strong and the infrastructure weak, it attacked fewer and lost more, because the PF platoons beside the hamlets came out to the self-defence groups' calls (EV002-4) [X-009]. In Cai Be and Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong it went for the self-defence groups in the hamlets between the posts, where no platoon was near enough to come [X-009]. Across the three provinces, by the end of October, I estimate that about 600–900 members of the self-defence groups were killed and about 1,500–2,000 of their weapons taken, almost all of them Garands and carbines of 1950 that the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 (C004-4) [X-009]. In the same weeks the RF and PF posts of the three provinces were attacked about a third less often than on the historical course, and held about as often as they had in May [X-009].

The trained PF platoon could not give the self-defence group its own training, but at Rach Kien it showed what it could do for it. On the night of 2–3 September a company of the Front's 506th Battalion attacked the self-defence group of a hamlet a kilometre from the PF post at the Rach Kien canal bridge; the group's leader, a schoolteacher, fired a flare as the PF corporal had shown him, and the platoon came across the paddies in sixteen minutes, in pairs, firing and moving as the territorial course had taught it, and broke the attack at the hamlet gate [X-009]. Two self-defence members died and four were wounded, all four with tourniquets the PF soldiers put on. In the next month the district chief of Can Duoc sent his PF platoon sergeants, the men the drill-sergeant corps had released in July, to give the self-defence groups of the district three evenings each of the first ten minutes, which no order required [X-009]. It was the only place in the three provinces where anyone did, and the only one where the groups held through the autumn.

The PF platoon sergeants whom the Can Duoc district chief sent to the self-defence groups were men who had passed the platoon leader's course, which taught a sergeant to train his own platoon and anyone the district put under him (EC010-2). On 24 October Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, the province chief, made Can Duoc's practice the province's: every PF platoon leader in Long An gave the self-defence groups within two kilometres of his post three evenings of the first ten minutes and the flare signal, and was rated on it [X-010]. In November and December the Front's local companies attacked eleven self-defence groups in Long An and overran four, against about two in three in Hau Nghia in the autumn [X-010]. Hau Nghia's province chief did not follow until February 1969. The difference was a province chief's order and a platoon leader who had been taught to teach.

## EV000-53 · The bombing halt, the talks and the American election · 31 Oct – 13 Nov 1968 · Washington, Paris and Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,013
*Initiative: both*

In October Hanoi signalled that it would accept the Saigon government at the table if the bombing stopped [H]. On 31 October Johnson announced the end of all bombing of North Vietnam from 1 November, ending Rolling Thunder [H9] [H51]. In this war the same announcement opened the talks themselves, which history had opened in May: Harriman and Xuan Thuy met for the first time at the Hotel Majestic on 13 November, and the four-party conference with Saigon and the Front was agreed in principle for the new year (EV000-48) [X-013]. Thieu refused to join the talks on the terms agreed [H51]. Through Anna Chennault the Nixon campaign had encouraged him to wait for a better deal, and on 2 November she told the South Vietnamese ambassador to "hold on a while longer"; Johnson learned of it through intercepts and ordered the campaign watched, but did not make it public [H51]. Historians disagree on whether the channel changed anything, since Thieu had his own reasons to refuse [H51]. On 5 November Richard M. Nixon was elected president by a narrow popular margin [H]. The four-party talks did not meet in substance until the new year, after a dispute over the shape of the table was settled with a round table for the governments, on history's date [H51]. The six months lost in Paris cost the settlement nothing that anyone could later find: history's talks had spent them on statements (F013-10) [X-013].

Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder had flown about the same number of attack sorties as history's 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine sorties, but about a third of them against fixed targets on the restrike list rather than one in a hundred, and far fewer in daylight armed reconnaissance of the roads [H9] [X-013]. It had lost, by this command's count, about 810–880 aircraft over or near the North rather than history's 922: about 30–60 fewer in 1965, when the North's air defence was broken before it was built, about 40–90 fewer in 1966–67, and about 30–40 more in the summer of 1968 over Hanoi (F013-17, F013-18) [H9] [X-013]. It ended, as history's did, without having broken Hanoi's will or stopped the flow of men and supplies south, and it ended with nothing left in the North for it to destroy that the North could not rebuild in a night.

## EV000-54 · The Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix · 1 Nov 1968 – Jan 1969 · The countryside of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,006
*Initiative: allied*
On the historical course Phung Hoang, the Vietnamese programme to coordinate the attack on the VC infrastructure, had been established by decree in December 1967 out of the American ICEX programme of June 1967, and Thieu put his own decree behind it in July 1968 [H52]. Its instruments were district intelligence centres, the CIA-run Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the police; its method was to identify, capture, turn or kill the cadres of the parallel administration [H52]. On 1 November 1968, at the urging of Komer and his successor William E. Colby, the government launched the Accelerated Pacification Campaign, a three-month drive to raise about 1,000 contested hamlets to relative security before any ceasefire could freeze the map, using RF and PF, Revolutionary Development cadre and the new People's Self-Defence Force, with regular forces screening [H].

The campaign met its targets by the Hamlet Evaluation System, a scale filled in by American district advisers under pressure to show progress (A13), and in many places those hamlets did become more secure after dark, because the VC local forces that had protected the infrastructure had died in the offensives [H]. Phoenix's own figures, 81,740 people "neutralized" between 1968 and 1972, 26,369 of them killed, were produced against monthly quotas that encouraged false arrests and padding, and about 87 percent of the killings credited to it were by ordinary military operations [H52]. Communist cadres later described 1968–72 as their hardest period, and some American advisers concluded that Phoenix had been a mortal threat in some provinces and a paper programme in others [H52].

In this war the campaign is launched on the historical date under the same men, and Phoenix is not launched at all, because Phung Hoang has run in every district since 1965 without quotas (EV006-1) [X-006]. Thieu's decree of July 1968 is issued, as a reaffirmation that adds his name to Khanh's [X-006]. Three things are different. The district intelligence centres have worked from the land register since 1964, which by November 1968 records about 900,000 titled hectares and the households that hold them; for the first time a district knew who lived in each titled hamlet, and who had left it for the Front, from a record compiled for another purpose and not for the quota [X-001]. And the Commission Against Corruption's inspectors, under the decree of 1964 rather than an agreement Colby made, had sampled the neutralization reports of 40 districts in IV and III Corps each quarter since 1965 and checked each name against the register, the province prison rolls and the PRU and police reports [X-001]. The sample reported in February 1969, the sixteenth, found about one claim in seven unsupported across the 40 districts: fewer than on the earlier course, because there were no quotas to pad and the districts had learned since 1965 that a name would be checked (F001-1, F001-3, F006-13) [X-006]. False arrests for ransom were still cases before the Special Court, about 30 in the quarter [X-006]. The register had been made public in 1964 only as parcel numbers, after Can Giuoc (EV001-3); in 1968 it was used, for the first time, as a list of persons, and the Front's propaganda said so [X-001].

The third is that Phung Hoang was not a new instrument here but an old one with a new urgency. The centres had sat in the Special Branch's district offices since 1964 (EV006-1), and the Special Branch had been running target folders in the held belt since then (F002-7) [X-002]. In the held belt the unsupported share in the February sample was under a tenth; in the districts recovered after Tet, where the Accelerated Pacification Campaign pushed the centres into villages a few weeks old, it was about a quarter, and in the eleven districts where the province chiefs let the PRUs and the sector S2 build the files, about two-fifths [X-006]. Without quotas the contested belt did not become a paper programme, but where the files were thin it became the raid on a rallier's grudge, as at Duc Thanh, and the Commission's cases came mostly from there (F006-15) [X-006]. The audited count for 1968 was about 9,600 neutralized, with about 1,400 killed, of whom about 500 in the programme's own operations; on the historical course the claims for the year were larger and were never audited [X-006]. In the held belt it was a refinement of what the police had done for four years, and the Front's cadres there had left before it began (F002-11).

## EV003-5 · The halt measured · 1 Nov – 31 Dec 1968 · The coastal road and railway from Thanh Hoa to Vinh and Dong Hoi, Route 15 to the Mu Gia pass, and the ferries at Quang Khe, North Vietnam · NEW · 003
The bombing of the North stopped on 1 November 1968 [H]. The Gulf watch did not. Its orbit, 40–60 nautical miles off the coast and outside the SA-2 belt, was a reconnaissance flight over international waters, and it went on counting the traffic on Route 1A, the coastal railway and Route 15 below the 19th parallel through November and December (EC003-7) [M003] [X-003]. What it saw changed within a week. Trucks that had moved only at night and in rain, in convoys of five or six, moved by day in columns of forty and fifty; the ferries at Quang Khe ran in daylight; the railway, repaired south of Thanh Hoa within three weeks, carried trains to Vinh [X-003]. Southbound vehicle movements on Route 15 toward the Mu Gia pass in December were about three times the October level [X-003].

The count went to Washington twice a week, as the bombing halt's terms required this command to report the North's use of it [H] [X-003]. It did not change the halt, which the President had traded for the opening of the expanded talks in Paris and which the President-elect did not reverse [H]. It changed the next campaign. When Commando Hunt opened on 15 November, its planners knew from the radar how much more was coming toward the passes, and when and where, and the U-2S found the new truck parks and the new fuel dumps north of Mu Gia that the surge required [X-003]. The halt was Hanoi's to use, and it used it exactly as expected; the difference was that the use was measured as it happened, and the headquarters that would fight the dry season of 1968–69 knew the size of it on its first night.

## EV000-55 · Commando Hunt begins · 15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads [H53]. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night [H53]. In this war the Global Hawk's panhandle watch was the campaign's third sensor from its first night, and the AC-130s of the 16th Special Operations Squadron were sent to radar tracks as well as to sensor activations [X-003].

The B-52Js and the coordinate cell were its fourth instrument. Historically Commando Hunt's interdiction points, the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes and the Ban Laboy ford, were struck by B-52 boxes and fighter-bombers night after night to crater them [H53]. Here they were cut with GBU-31s on measured coordinates, and the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Kong below them were sown with Quickstrikes set for the steel pontoons the North had brought in since 1966 (EV005-2) [X-005]. The tankers freed by the J's range refuelled the F-4s working the passes, which flew about a quarter more sorties a night over the panhandle than the historical force could keep in the air (F005-10) [M005] [X-005]. The interdiction points did not close. The enemy had spent two and a half years building a net with no single point of failure (F005-4); at Ban Laboy the cell now counted fourteen fords and two underwater causeways, and on any night the J could cut the ones it had measured and the trucks crossed at the ones it had not [X-005].

Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons [H53]. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000 [H53]. Here they were not believed by this headquarters either. The radar counted the trucks entering the panhandle at Mu Gia and Ban Karai and leaving it toward the South on Routes 92 and 96, and by that measure Phase I destroyed or disabled about 2,600–3,200 trucks and reduced the tonnage reaching the South by about a sixth, not the third the claims implied [X-003]. The J's crossings added little to that sixth, perhaps a fiftieth part of the flow, because the net they cut had been built to be cut (F005-4) [X-005]. MACV J-2 sent the figure to Washington in May 1969 beside the Air Force's, and from then on the two numbers travelled together [X-003]. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it; this time the growth was counted.

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent [H40].

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that, and the American decision of 1968 is unchanged, as is its timing.

- **The land.** About 900,000 hectares have been titled. The Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier [X-001].
- **The infrastructure.** In the held belt it is weaker than anywhere since 1959. The Tet losses fell on a network already broken there, and the Front's district committees enter 1969 governing from further out than history's (EV012-8) [X-012].
- **The police.** The National Police numbers about 95,000, and every file it keeps is held under a destruction plan [X-002].
- **Phung Hoang.** It enters 1969 with five years of files, an audited count the press has learned to quote, and no quotas. The Front's Delta committees have lost a third of the leaders they sent into the towns (EV000-42, EV000-54) [X-006]. The Hue district files have shown what the national file would be worth to the enemy (EV000-44).
- **The combined command.** It found a traitor in its plans cell and has extended the two-layer plan to every corps (EV012-7). Hue was fought under one commander and ended five days early (EV000-44) [X-012].
- **The warning.** The files saw the towns' part of Tet and not its date (EV000-42) [X-CP03].

The RVNAF ends 1968 larger, with its battalions under commanders who held them through Tet and a board that has not stopped working [X-007]. It is armed throughout with weapons the historical army was still waiting for, a gap now closing from the historical side as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969 [X-004]. The mobilization's soldiers are trained to the regulars' standard and reached the field about a month late [X-009]. Its new platoons are led, in about fourteen cases in fifteen, by a man who passed the leader's course, against about two in three historically [X-010].

The mobilization's squads have no sergeants, its lieutenants were sergeants, and the academy's course is four weeks for the duration (EV000-51). The People's Self-Defense Force, which no course reached, has paid for the difference in Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong (EV009-4). COSVN works among the officers the board passed over, and the Military Security Service keeps a second file on the board's officers, this time as targets (EV000-52).

About 2,150 rather than 2,788 ARVN soldiers died in the first wave. The hospitals of both armies are full of men who would have died on the historical course and are alive without a leg (F009-16) [X-009].

The PAVN leaves 1968 having learned that it does not lay siege to a position the Americans can measure. That is the one way of choosing its battles it has lost, and it chooses all the others [X-005]. The enemy's rear enters Phase IV counted. The surge after the halt is measured, and the first dry season of Commando Hunt is scored against the radar as well as the claims (EV003-5, EV000-55) [X-003]. The panhandle is harder to cut than history's, because the bombers taught its engineers how (EV000-55).

The northern campaign ends on 1 November, as history's did. It has lost about 810–880 aircraft rather than 922, and nothing is left in the North for it to destroy that the North could not rebuild in a night (EV000-53) [X-013]. The talks that open on 13 November are the talks history opened in May, six months late and on the same terms, and Nixon inherits them on history's date (EV000-48, EV000-53).
