### Three chapter fixes that Part 1 left for this part

**EV000-76, the siege of An Loc (Phase V).** Part 1 recommended an erratum. On reading the record I fixed the chapter here instead, because the error is in no finished part. Memorandum 015's null for Phase V says what happened: the gunships worked between the bomber cells, and the SA-7 drove the Spooky IIs above 10,000 feet. The chapter, last revised before that memorandum, simply did not say it. An erratum corrects a finished record. A chapter that omits what a memorandum said is a coherence fault, and a checkpoint stages the chapter to fix it (CHECKPOINT steps 3 and 6). The event now says that history's AC-119s and AC-130Es did not exist in this war. The night shift over An Loc was flown by the 17th Special Operations Squadron's AC-130Js from Ubon and the 817th's AC-130Us from Bien Hoa. They worked on the sappers, the digging parties and the mortar crews 50–200 metres from Hung's lines, and from late April the SA-7 drove them above 10,000 feet. **EV003-3, the Mu Gia pass (Phase II).** This answers the air-war study's Q-3. Twenty hours later the orbit watched the first trucks cross again on a ford the engineers had reopened beside the broken bridges, and the next night's Js cut the ford [X-CP04]. ### The trajectory, phase by phase

**Phase I (1964–Jul 1965).** The held belt begins to be held, and from the first night of 1964 an AC-130J answers its posts on clear nights. Delta's first finished raid takes the Long An province committee with its ledgers in March 1965 (EV014-2). Nothing here moves.

**Phase II (Aug 1965–1967).** This is still the force's strongest phase, and memorandum 014 made it sharper. The Front's district government of the Saigon belt is broken from its edge, and its province government is driven into Cambodia (C014-2). The posts in the net are not assaulted on clear nights. The main-force war is history's, and the coast's posts fall on monsoon nights.

**Phase III (1968).** Tet keeps its turn, and is fought by guides who were not there and committees a border away. Delta spends its best window clearing streets (C014-4).

**Phase IV (1969–71).** This is the inheritance and its capture, now judged in units. The held belt's gain hides under the HES's ceiling. The night over the posts passes to Vietnamese gunships, and that is the one handover in the record that worked at both ends (EV015-6). The palace takes the institutions, and the senior cadre the file names live where nobody can reach them.

**Phase V (1972).** The sieges are held by the J's cells, with Vietnamese and American gunships between them. The gains are lost where a PAVN division came into the lowlands and kept where it did not.

**Phase VI (1973–75).** The reprieve of a year is memorandum 011's. The Republic that has it holds III and IV Corps with its own night fire over the Delta's posts, and cannot stop a corps.

### The end state

**The A9 call stays at rung (b), dependent survival.** The Republic stands past 30 April 1975, holds III and IV Corps on 31 December 1975 under Thieu, and is expected to fall between February and May 1976. No event in Phase VI changed at this checkpoint, so the rung test of memorandum 015 stands as written.

**Why rung (c) is not reached.** Rung (c) needs a Republic that holds without American air power, and this one does not. At Thuong Duc in August 1974 the 304th Division brought its own 37mm guns and SA-7s, the first Spooky II over the trenches went down with its crew of thirteen, and the town fell (EV000-91). At Pleiku the 130mm guns closed Cu Hanh from 27 kilometres, beyond anything the Republic owned (EV000-95). The corps were commanded by the palace's choices. Nothing in fifteen memoranda changed those three things.

**The date (DIRMT01-1).** The window stays February to May 1976. What pulls it later is Thieu's refusal of 11–14 March 1975, the money that let him refuse, and the rains (EVMT01-4). What pulls it earlier is a PAVN whose five corps formed on history's dates (POSMT01-2) and a Republic whose general reserve sat at Da Nang. The first set dominates, because it is the one that moved the Politburo's calendar. Nothing in this checkpoint touches either set.

**Running sums (DIRMT01-2), corrected.** Two of the record's sums need restating, because the memoranda carried them wrongly.

- **American dead saved.** Memoranda 014 and 015 began their sums from POSMT01-5's 2,500–5,000. That figure was reversed by REV011-2, which put the sum at 3,700–7,800, and memorandum 012 moved it to about 3,750–8,100 [X-012]. The sum is therefore about 4,100–9,500, a judgment built from rates and shown at work at the Ia Drang, Dien Ban, Albany and the first wave of Tet [X-CP04]. That is about 7 to 16 percent of the American dead of the war. It still does not move tolerance, because the clock was spent by casualties the provisions did not touch and by Tet (POSCP01-1). It is recorded as POSCP04-1 so that no later memorandum starts from a superseded figure.
- **ARVN battalions not broken in 1966–67.** Part 1 found three increments stacked in Phase II's ending. They are [X-007] at about a third, [X-009] at about one in eight beyond it, and [X-010] at one in six to one in five beyond both. Taken as a product, they come to about half. The record's settled figure is REVR01-1's: about two-fifths to a half fewer than history's, with "about half" as the upper bound. - **Territorial posts not lost.** Memorandum 015 opened this count at about 1,000–2,500 over the war, overlapping with the radio of P008b by perhaps a third [X-015]. It stands, with its upper bound marked.
- **Senior infrastructure taken.** Memorandum 014's count stands at 297 in 1966–67 and perhaps 700–1,400 across the war, of which perhaps a half to two-thirds would not have been taken anyway [M014]. ### 8b. The experience of the war, retold

Part 1's six passages on the experience of the war restated the analysis with a name attached. The user asked for something else: one person's night, told from inside it, so that the reader sees the change, or its absence, the way that person saw it. Part 1 is finished and is not edited. Three of its passages are retold here as they should have been written. The facts are the record's, and the people are this headquarters' illustrations.

**The post at Ap My Loi, Cai Lay district, Dinh Tuong. The night of 11 December 1967.**

Sergeant Tran Van Hai heard the firing at Ap Hau before the radio did. It came across the paddies to the north, short bursts and then the flat cough of a B40, and he had his platoon on the wall before the district net woke up. Ap Hau was the next post up the canal, two kilometres away, and it had twelve men in it and a corporal he had trained with.

Three years earlier he would have known exactly what came next, because there was only one thing it could be. The district chief would order the RF company out to relieve Ap Hau, the company would go up the canal road in trucks, and somewhere in the cane the 514th Battalion would be waiting for it, as it had waited at My Phuoc Tay in March. After My Phuoc Tay the rule had been fire first and men in the morning. Hai had kept that rule for nine months. He had listened to posts fight all night on the radio, and to the relief that did not come.

Tonight the sky was clear and the moon was down. At 2140 he heard the other sound, the deep drone from the south-east that the men called the old woman. It was not a sound that hurried. It came up the canal road slowly, and he knew from the way it moved that it was looking at the cane on both sides of the road before it looked at Ap Hau. Twenty minutes later the trucks came, their lights off, under it.

He watched them pass below his wall, the RF company's men crouched in the beds with their carbines up. Nobody fired at them from the cane. Up at Ap Hau the B40s stopped, and then the firing stopped, and the drone circled for an hour and went home.

In January the cloud came down for three nights running. On the first of them the radio called from Ap Hau again. Hai listened for the engine and heard nothing but the rain on the tin. When the district asked whether the road was open, he told them to wait for daylight, and the district waited [X-CP04].

*What this shows.* The relief rides again on clear nights and only on clear nights (C015-2). From the wall the change is a sound in the sky, and the platoon leader's judgment turns on whether he can hear it.

**The Cai Be district committee, at Memot, Cambodia. October 1967.**

The courier came in at dusk with mud to the knees, and Nam Thanh knew from his face that the bag was light. He untied it on the bench under the rubber trees while the man drank.

Inside were the tax receipts from his villages for August, eight weeks old. They had been carried across the border by three couriers in turn, each of them walking at night and lying up by day. With them came a note from the village secretary at Hau My. Two of his collectors had been taken from their houses in September. A third had been taken from a sampan at the mooring where the committee used to meet, before April. The note gave the names. Nam Thanh had appointed all three himself.

Since the instruction came down in April, he had been inside his own district six nights. The instruction said what everyone already knew: the Americans came at night, by helicopter, to the house a man was sleeping in, and they knew which house. So the committee slept in Cambodia. It went home four or five nights every second month, and it governed by paper.

He read the receipts by the lamp. The collections in the hamlets with posts had fallen again. In the hamlets the government held only by day they were steady, because there the people still saw his men. He knew why the posted hamlets were falling away. A man who sets a tax has to be there to argue it, to listen to the family whose son was conscripted and the family whose rice was short, and to give a little back. A tax set from Memot was a demand from a stranger.

He wrote his instructions for the next collection and gave them to the courier. They would reach Hau My in November and be eight weeks out of date when they arrived. He was still the government of Cai Be at night. He governed it as a guest [X-CP04].

*What this shows.* The record's claim (C014-2), seen from the man it describes, is sharper than the record makes it. The collections fall because the man who sets them cannot be there to argue them, not only because his collectors are taken.

**A porter of the 559th Group at the Ban Laboy ford, Route 912, Laos. The night of 30 October 1966.**

Luong Van Kha had carried stone for six weeks, and his hands had stopped bleeding in the second week.

The work was always the same. He carried flat stones from the hillside in a basket on his back, down to the river, and handed them to the men standing waist-deep in the Xe Bang Fai. They laid them one by one, a foot under the water, so that a truck's wheels found stone and the aircraft's camera found only water. When he had come in September there had been four fords at Ban Laboy. Tonight there were nine, linked by tracks through the trees that the engineers recut every few days so that no one track was worn.

He had never seen a bomber. He had seen what they did. The bridge at the old crossing was steel twisted into the riverbed, and there were craters on the bank so regular that the older men said a machine had measured them. They had come in April, all at once, and every crossing on the river had been down by morning. That was why he was here. The cadre had explained it on the first night: the Americans could hit anything they could see, so the road would be made of things they could not see.

At 2100 the first convoy came down to the water with its lights off. A man with a red lamp walked ahead of each truck, and the trucks crossed on his stone, one at a time, at walking pace. Kha counted eleven. In six weeks he had not seen the convoys stop for a single night.

Before dawn the platoon took the pontoon sections out of the water and hid them under the bank. Then Kha slept until noon and went back to the hillside for stone [X-CP04].

*What this shows.* The record's claim that HARD CROSSING built the enemy a road with no single point of failure (EV005-2) is visible from the riverbed. From there the bombing is labour, not loss, and its price is paid in porters' nights.

The other three passages, the hamlet chief at Hoai Nhon, the adviser at Long My and the official of the Directorate, keep their findings. They are left as Part 1 wrote them. Directive DIRCP04-4 below sets the standard for every later passage of this kind.
