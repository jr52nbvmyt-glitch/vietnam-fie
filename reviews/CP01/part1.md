# CP01 — Part 1: The state of the war after five memoranda

Five memoranda have given this command eleven provisions, all of them on the war's first day. The war they have made is better in its interior and identical at its end. Saigon still falls on 30 April 1975. Between the first day and the last, the countryside of the Delta and the coastal Centre is governed, policed and owned in a way the historical Republic never managed, the enemy's rear is counted rather than guessed, the soldiers who hold the hamlets are not outgunned, and every siege the enemy lays where an American radar can measure it becomes his worst battle. None of it touches the three things that decided the war: the enemy's control of whether and where to fight, the sanctuaries he fights from, and the American clock that ran out in 1968 and was spent by 1973. This part sets out the war as the chapters now hold it, reviews the first phase end to end, sweeps five crowded variables for interactions the memoranda missed, follows every causal chain the memoranda predicted, tests the exercise's own figures, and then goes to six places in the war to see whether any of it is visible from the ground.

## 1. The state of the war

### The first eighteen months: a state built under a collapse

The war still begins with two collapses. In Saigon the generals make and unmake five governments between January 1964 and June 1965; in the field the Front forms its first regiments around Saigon and the Politburo sends the 325th Division's regiments down the Trail (EV000-1, EV000-6, EVCP01-1). What is new is that the Republic acquires, on its first day, the instruments of a state that the historical Republic built only after Tet or never. The land decree of 1 January 1964 ends rent on tenanted rice land and confirms by name what the Viet Minh had already given; the same session creates an anti-corruption commission with an American lever behind it, a rural police with a Special Branch, and a board that chooses province and district chiefs by examination (EV001-1, EV002-1). Khanh's coup tests all four within thirty days and all four survive it, because Johnson's note of 2 February carries the condition that the United States will support no government that repeals them (EV000-1).

By the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the dawn musters have struck about 41,000 names from the RVNAF's rolls, about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, and two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list [X-001] [X-002]. The Front reads the police within weeks and starts killing constables in their first months; about 410 are dead by July 1965, nearly all in posts settled ahead of night security (EV002-2) [X-002]. The land programme learns its own lesson at Can Giuoc in June 1964, where 37 title holders are shot from a posted list, and thereafter the register records parcel numbers, not names (EV001-3) [X-001].

The rifles arrive the same January. By June 1964 about 310,000 M4A1 carbines are in Vietnamese hands, 180,000 of them with the Regional and Popular Forces, and on the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa a Popular Forces platoon whose fire did not pause while it reloaded turns back a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion at the bund (EV004-1, EV002-2) [X-004]. Posts with their platoon hold about nine attacks in ten; the Front turns to the posts without them, to constables on the road, to the CIDG camps and to the hamlet militia carrying the Republic's discarded Garands [X-004]. At Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai the enemy still chooses the field and still wins, but the 4th Marine Battalion comes out of the rubber at Binh Gia in two perimeters with most of its wounded, and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out in 1965 (EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18).

What this command sees changes more than what it can do. TIDE TABLE's Global Hawk catches the trawlers of Group 125 every time they run south, and by January 1965 Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville, about eighteen months early (EV003-1, EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 Major Harold Kessling's radar log shows no torpedo boats within forty nautical miles of the *Maddox*; Johnson cancels Pierce Arrow at about 1900, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the attack of 2 August alone (EV000-3) [X-003]. The coordinate cell under Major Edwin Sorrell has 1,400 measured targets on file by June 1965, and Washington will let the B-52J strike none of them in the North (EV005-1, EV000-15). The July decision comes on its date and for its reasons (EVCP01-2).

### 1965–67: the main-force war and the quiet war beneath it

The main-force war runs as history ran it. Starlite, the Ia Drang, Masher/White Wing, Hastings, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City win their battles and do not change the war, and the enemy decides when to fight (EVCP01-4, EV000-23, EV000-24). The rifles shave the cost: about 125 men of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry die at Albany against 155 historically, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed M16 because there is no M16 (EV000-23, EV000-34) [X-004].

Beneath it the provisions of 1964 grind. NIGHT LANTERN, from 15 November 1965 to 30 April 1966, settles fourteen posts in Can Giuoc and Can Duoc under Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan's rule that no post goes in without its platoon and a reaction within the hour; the district committee's security chief is taken on 11 February with his files, and the families whose titles had sat in the district office since the killings of 1964 receive them in their hamlets (EV002-4) [X-002]. GOLDEN SHEAF carries the titling line along the Xa No canal and stops, in June 1966, about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh, at the line the territorials can hold after dark (EV001-6) [X-001]. The Front answers by killing the chiefs who keep their platoons out at night, nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs between August 1965 and June 1966, and by moving its legal cadres to the towns (EV002-3, EV002-5) [X-002]. The arrest count in the held belt falls from 1966 because there is almost no one left to arrest, and Washington asks for quotas (EV002-5).

The costs arrive on schedule. Ky removes Director General Cuong in March 1966 and Loan takes the census of Hue's pagoda quarter to the Buddhist repression; several dozen student leaders take what they know of it to the Front (EV000-26) [X-002]. The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by IV Corps for the U Minh, and Chuong Thien loses twenty-three outposts while they are away (EV004-2) [X-004]. The Special Court convicts Brigadier General Phan Duy Khiem on 18 April 1967, and the American lever that made it possible is visible to every officer who reads the case (EV001-7) [X-001].

The enemy's rear is counted from December 1965. STEADY COUNT finds about 70–90 tons a day reaching the South through the panhandle at the dry-season peak, several times what the forces there need from the North (EV003-3) [X-003]. HARD CROSSING cuts every measured crossing in the panhandle from 12 April 1966 and teaches Group 559 to build a road net with no single point of failure; by October the trucks moving south of Tchepone are about 15 percent above their March level (EV005-2) [X-005]. In March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4) [X-003]. At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is imaged every night and struck every two to three hours, the great bombardment of 25 September comes at about half its historical weight, and the siege lifts about three weeks early (EV000-36) [X-005]. Westmoreland takes the wrong lesson from the right result.

### 1968: the same year with a different Delta

Tet runs on its dates and is decided in the same places: Saigon, Hue, Khe Sanh and Washington (EV000-42, EV000-45). The difference lies along the line of night security. At Can Tho the Popular Forces of the titled hamlets on the Xa No canal report the columns on the night of 30–31 January, the battalions wait for guides the night check had taken two weeks earlier, and the town is cleared in about four days (EV000-42) [X-001] [X-002]. In the district towns about a third of the first-wave attacks never get inside the compound walls, as at Cai Lay, where the 311th RF Company holds until the 7th Division's battalion arrives [X-004]. In Hue the Front's execution lists are drawn from the Republic's own census, and the police and their families are about a fifth of the identified dead (EV000-44) [X-002].

Khe Sanh becomes the PAVN's worst battle of 1968. The trenches of the 304th Division are imaged as they are dug and destroyed at 500–800 metres, the siege lifts about two weeks early, and the garrison loses about 180 killed against more than 274 (EV000-41) [X-005]. It is won at the place Hanoi chose, while the brigade of the 101st taken from Thua Thien to help win it is not there when the Front's local forces return to the hamlets around Hue. Abrams refuses to make the victory a doctrine of border bases and closes the base on its historical date (EV000-51). The mobilization of June arms its new territorials from stock within a month and finds it cannot make sergeants as fast (EV000-51) [X-004]. Phoenix starts here from a land register and a Special Branch four years old, and the Commission's first audit finds a third of its claims unsupported, a tenth where the Branch ran the folders and a half where the Provincial Reconnaissance Units did (EV000-54) [X-001] [X-002].

### 1969–71: the best years in the countryside, the worst on the frontier

The countryside is better placed in 1969 than history's and the frontier is the same. Resolution 9 tells the Delta's party committees to stop contesting the titles and to govern by visiting cadres, which is the Front's own admission of what the land and the police had done to it (EV000-61) [X-001] [X-002]. The Land to the Tiller law of 1970 is not needed (EV000-65). The incursion of May 1970 lands on depots five years of counting had mapped and finds the caches in ten days (EV000-66) [X-003]. Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against the radar, and the radar says air interdiction costs the Trail about 8–10 percent at its best (EV000-68) [X-003]. Lam Son 719 goes in against an enemy the radar measured at twice the planners' estimate, and fails for reasons no radar touches; the bases where the PAVN dug, Lolo and Sophia, hold under the siege box, and I Corps learns to build its defence around American bombs (EV000-71) [X-005].

The institutions of 1964 lose their American protection as the Americans leave. Thieu packs the Selection Board and subordinates the Commission in July 1971, when its licence inquiry reaches a relative of his staff (EV002-6, EV001-8) [X-001] [X-002]. Their work below the palace continues. Their reach upward ends.

### 1972–75: the conventional war the provisions never reached

The Easter Offensive is seen before it crosses the DMZ, and Quang Tri still falls, because the radar did not make the 3rd Division's regiments steady (EV000-74). Freedom Train drops the Thanh Hoa bridge on its first night, seven years after the request to drop it was refused (EV000-75) [X-005]. At An Loc and Kontum the siege box breaks the assaults short of or inside the wire, and Kontum is secure five days early (EV000-76, EV000-77) [X-005]. Linebacker II loses about six bombers rather than fifteen, and Bach Mai is not hit (EV000-82) [X-005]. The terms of January 1973 are the terms of October 1972.

Then the Americans go and take everything the provisions had built for the conventional war with them. The coordinate cell is disbanded in September 1973 with nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to (EV000-86). The ration of 1974 finds an army built around the belt-fed gun and silences the gun first; its divisions hoard about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm against the ration, and lose them in the collapse of I Corps (EV000-89, EV000-96) [X-004]. At Thuong Duc the 79th Rangers dig in and wait for a siege box that no longer exists (EV000-91) [X-005]. At Phuoc Long Thieu declines to send a division to a province he knows cannot be held without American bombers (EV000-92). Ban Me Thuot falls on its date, the Highlands are abandoned, and the Republic falls on 30 April 1975.

### What the cumulative force has and has not done

The cumulative force has done three large things. It has given the Republic a government in its own held belt four to six years early: land, police, chiefs and an honest muster, which together drove the resident infrastructure out of the villages with settled posts before 1968 (POS001-1, POS002-1). It has made the enemy's rear and his air defences measured facts from 1964, so that the bad news about the bombing and the Trail reached Washington in weeks, not years (POS003-1). And it has made every fixed target and every measured siege a place where the enemy dies in large numbers, from Con Thien in 1967 to Kontum in 1972 (POS005-1, POS005-3).

It has not touched the enemy's control of whether to fight except at the siege, his sanctuaries, Hanoi's will, or the American clock. And it has deepened, in three provisions out of eleven, the Republic's dependence on things only Americans could supply: American ammunition in volume, American bombers with American coordinates, and an American lever to protect its own honest institutions from its own government (POS004-3, POS005-2, POS001-2, POS002-2). The war's last three years are fought by a Republic that is better governed below the palace and more dependent above it. The end state stays at rung (a), and Part 2 will say why.

## 2. Coherence review: Phase I

I read the Phase I chapter end to end against Phase II, the end state and the positions register. It is coherent in substance: no enemy capability is silently restored, every provision's events follow from their causes, and the positions it carries (POS003-3 on Tonkin, POS004-1 on the outposts, POS005-1 on precision) are stated where they bite. The chapter was also over the size limit at 16,090 words, and I consolidated it. The fixes:

1. **Overview, the four battles.** The overview said that at "Binh Gia, Song Be, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai" the Front destroyed ARVN battalions in set-piece battles. Song Be was a government victory (EV000-16, now EVCP01-3), and in this war no battalion of the general reserve was destroyed at Binh Gia (EV000-9). The sentence now says the general reserve was beaten at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and that Song Be was held narrowly. The same contradiction stood in "How the phase ends" ("destroyed ARVN battalions"), and is corrected there to "broken ... though none of the general reserve has been wiped out".
2. **"How the phase ends", the countryside.** It said the government's pacification effort had "nothing like the stable administration or the local security forces it needs", in a phase where the chiefs, the posts and the platoons had begun to supply exactly that in the held belt. It now limits the sentence to the country outside the held belt.
3. **Overview, the five provisions.** The five paragraphs, one per memorandum, had each been written as if it were the only change ("One thing in this phase is new" was followed by a second, third, fourth and fifth). They are consolidated into one passage that names all five and says, once, that neither the coups nor the summer offensive turns on any of them; the overview also placed Binh Gia (December 1964 – January 1965) among "the battles of the summer of 1965", which is corrected.
4. **Song Be (EV000-16).** It stood as unchanged history although its garrison, the 36th Ranger Battalion of the general reserve, had carried the M4A1 and the M249 since February 1964 (EV004-1). The consolidated account in EVCP01-3 now says so and says why I do not adjust the losses: most were taken in the first hour to mortars, recoilless rifles and sappers inside the wire.
5. **Initiative.** Only three of the chapter's thirty-two events said who held the initiative, so the generated account of the enemy's decisions showed none in Phase I, the phase in which Hanoi decided to commit its regulars. Every live event in the chapter now carries its line, and Phase I shows the enemy's choices: the Can Giuoc killings, the commitment of the regulars, the move to Sihanoukville, Nam Dong, Bien Hoa, Song Be, Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai (EV001-3, EV000-6, EV003-2, EVCP01-3, EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18).
6. **Consolidation of Phase I (16,090 → about 14,970 words).** Eleven historical events that no provision had changed, and on which no memorandum or position relies beyond one citation, are consolidated into three summary events, each marked HIST-ALT and each keeping every fact the originals held that a later passage uses:
   - **EVCP01-1**, Saigon's governments and the war in the city, 19 December 1964 – 19 June 1965, consolidates EV000-8 (the Brink bombing and the High National Council), EV000-10 (the fall of Khanh), EV000-14 (the embassy bombing) and EV000-19 (Quat's resignation and the Thieu–Ky government). EV000-19 is cited by C001-3 for the Thieu–Ky succession, which EVCP01-1 carries.
   - **EVCP01-2**, the American escalation, 7 February – 28 July 1965, consolidates EV000-11 (Pleiku, Qui Nhon and Flaming Dart), EV000-12 (Rolling Thunder begins), EV000-13 (the Marines at Da Nang) and EV000-21 (the July decision).
   - **EVCP01-3**, the camps, the bases and the towns, 6 July 1964 – 12 May 1965, consolidates EV000-2 (Nam Dong), EV000-7 (the Bien Hoa attack, including the U-2S damaged there, which memo 003 recorded) and EV000-16 (Song Be).
   Each consolidated event is marked CANCELLED in place with a pointer to its summary, and says that the event itself still happens.
7. **Consolidation of Phase II (16,338 → about 14,960 words).** Phase II was also over the limit, and I consolidated it at the same time rather than leave it a further checkpoint. **EVCP01-4**, the big-unit war, 18 August 1965 – 14 May 1967, consolidates EV000-22 (Starlite), EV000-25 (the fall of the A Shau camp), EV000-28 (Hastings), EV000-29 (Long Tan), EV000-30 (Attleboro), EV000-32 (Cedar Falls) and EV000-33 (Junction City), and states, as their originals did not, that the American battalions in them carried the provision's weapons and that I do not adjust the historical returns, for the reason POS004-1 gives (the enemy chose these fights; the squad weapons did not decide their cost by an amount the counts could show). The Phase II overview's five provision paragraphs are consolidated in the same way as Phase I's. The Phase II coherence review proper falls to the next checkpoint in rotation.

One thing in Phase II I note for that review rather than fix now: EV004-2 says the rule that Regional Force companies belong to their province chief was written into CORDS's founding instructions (EV000-35), but EV000-35 itself does not mention the rule. The record holds the fact once, in EV004-2 and C004-2, which is enough for any later memorandum; the account in EV000-35 is thin, not contradictory.

## 3. Interaction sweep

The pack suggested five variables. For each I read the ledger and asked, for every pair of provisions on it, whether their interaction is in the record. Most are. Four are not, and I write them here.

### American political tolerance

Eight entries: four provisions depend on tolerance (P001a, P002a, P003a, P005a) and two push it up by one band each (P001b, P004a). Every pairing among the dependents is in the record as a cost each one pays separately. **What is missing is the sum of the pushes.** Five memoranda have each found a small gain on the American clock: the Tonkin resolution resting on one real attack rather than a false one (POS003-3); honest damage assessment from 1966 (EV000-27); no M16 scandal and no Ichord hearings (F004-13); a conviction instead of a scandal in the spring of 1967 (EV001-7); twenty-one fewer prisoners in Hanoi and no Bach Mai photographs in December 1972 (C005-6, EV000-82). Every phase chapter still holds US.political_tolerance at 0.

I have asked whether these add up to a band, and the answer is that they do not, for a reason the record should state once. The clock in this war was spent by three things: the casualties of the main-force war from 1965 to 1968, the shock of Tet against the progress campaign of 1967, and the long withdrawal under fire from 1969. None of the eleven provisions changed the first two by more than a few percent, and one of them made the second worse: the radar fixed the President's attention on Khe Sanh and sharpened the surprise of the cities (C003-3). The gains are all in the credibility gap, which is real and which narrowed, but the gap was not what ended the war's support; Tet was. The interaction is therefore a set of small gains in the gap, offset by one loss in the surprise, against a clock set by casualties and Tet. The band stays at 0 in every phase. I put it in a position below so that the next memorandum does not reopen it without new cause.

### The Republic's political stability

Eight entries, four pushes, all from the reforms of 1964: land and the Commission push it down (coup grievances), the police pushes it up (a coup is harder to hide from a Special Branch that watches the generals), and the Board pushes it down (patronage taken from the generals). Their pairing is fully in the record: the September 1964 coup announces a programme against three of the four, and its failure makes the Young Turks their defenders (EV000-4). Nothing is missing here except a judgment the record makes implicitly and should make explicitly: **the net effect on stability over the war is nil, and its sign changes with the American lever.** Until 1969 each reform is a coup grievance held in check by the Aid Compliance Board; after 1970 each becomes an instrument the palace captures (EV002-6, EV001-8), and the regime becomes more stable and narrower at once (EV000-73). The chapters' band of 0 in every phase is right.

### Advisory leverage

Eight entries. Two pushes (P001b's Board, P002b's Inspectorate) and six dependents. Every pairing between the Board and the reforms it protects is in the record. **What is missing is the pairing between leverage and the three provisions that the Americans never handed over.** Leverage in this war was used, successfully, to protect Vietnamese institutions from Vietnamese governments. It was never used to make the Republic own the instruments that made the conventional war winnable while the Americans stayed: the coordinate cell, the reconnaissance aircraft, the stock of linked ammunition. In 1973 the Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell, and the request was not pursued because no VNAF aircraft could carry a guided bomb (EV000-86); nobody had used the American lever in 1970 or 1971 to demand that such a cell and such an aircraft be built while the bombers were still there. The lever was spent on the palace and not on the army.

That is a gap in the record and also a finding about the war: **advisory leverage in this exercise has been directed entirely at governance and never at self-sufficiency**, and the dependence that P004 and P005 deepened (POS004-3, POS005-2) was never contested by the instrument that might have contested it. I do not change a chapter for it, because nothing in the force as held could have supplied the Republic a guided-bomb aircraft; but it belongs in the bill in Part 2.

### Finding the main force

Seven entries: two pushes (both aircraft of memo 003), five dependents (the rifles and the bombs). Every pairing is in the record and every one says the same thing: the aircraft find movement in the open and the measured position, and the rifles and bombs act only where the enemy has been found or has chosen to be found (POS003-1, POS005-1, POS004-1). There is no missing interaction. There is one consequence of all of them together that the record has not stated: **five memoranda of provisions have produced an enemy who is easier to see and exactly as hard to find.** The band on FIND.main_force is +1 from Phase I to Phase V and it has never moved ENEMY.engagement_control except through the siege. That is the strongest single indicator in the record that G-7 is open and binding.

### ARVN leadership

Seven entries. The Commission (P001b) and the Board (P002b) push it up; the police, the rifles and the marksman's rifle depend on it. The pairing of the Commission and the Board is in the record in one direction, that the list answers the Commission's replacement problem (F001-5, F002-5). **The other direction is missing: the two draw on the same few honest field-grade officers, and so does every other provision that needs a good leader.** The Board takes 80–120 able majors and lieutenant colonels from the divisions for the provinces (F002-6); the Commission needs a clean replacement for every officer it removes (F001-5); the squad marksman needs a squad leader who knows how to employ him (P004b). The record treats each as its own constraint. Together they are one: the Republic's pool of competent, honest officers in 1964–66, perhaps 900–1,200 by the Board's own count [M002], was spent three times over, and the divisions were the losers each time. It is visible in one place in the chapters already: the 21st Division in 1966 runs GOLDEN SHEAF on the Xa No canal while IV Corps borrows its province's Regional Forces for the U Minh (EV001-6, EV004-2), because the corps had more operations than commanders. I do not edit a chapter for this. It is the mechanism behind G-4 staying open, and Part 2 ranks it.

## 5. Calibration

The chapters carry about a dozen exercise judgments, tagged X, on headline variables. I take the ones that matter.

**Hectares titled** (330,000 by July 1965; 820,000 by the end of 1967; about 900,000 by November 1968; about 1,150,000 by the end of 1971) [X-001]. They rest on a titling apparatus whose rate is estimated from the 1970 programme's real rate and limited by security, and they move by a traceable mechanism: the titling line follows the held belt, and stops where it stops (EV001-6). They are consistent across all six chapters. Under A13 they are a programme output, the government's own count, and the record already says in four places that titles issued are not allegiance (F001-10). Reliable as a count of paper; never cited as a count of loyalty.

**Delta recruitment of about 11,000 against 16,000 in 1968** [X-001]. The arithmetic is traceable (about a third of the tenant families who would have supplied recruits titled in districts held at night), but the coefficient that turns titled families into recruits not taken is an assertion. I would carry it as a direction and an order of magnitude: a recruitment shortfall of a quarter to a half in the titled districts. The record should keep the figure with its tag and should not build on its precision.

**RVNAF ghosts, about 41,000 by December 1964** [X-001]. It rests on the 5–10 percent band the dossier set and sits near its middle. Traceable, and of no consequence to any later figure.

**Constables killed, about 410 by July 1965** [X-002]. Traceable to the posts settled ahead of security, and the chapters later hold it consistent with the 31 constables of EV004-2 and the 31 dead of NIGHT LANTERN. Reliable as a judgment.

**Tet in the district towns: about two-thirds penetrated against nearly all, ARVN killed about 2,500 against 2,788** [X-004]. The mechanism is traceable (local forces against RF companies firing from walls), and the case at Cai Lay shows it. The 2,500 is a modest adjustment and plausible. Reliable as a judgment.

**Lives saved by the rifles: 1,000–2,500 American, 10,000–25,000 South Vietnamese over the war** [X-004]. This is the figure I trust least, and it is the one most often repeated (the card, the Trajectory Log, the memo). The chapters carry the per-battle adjustments: Binh Gia, Ba Gia, Dong Xoai, the Ia Drang, the Hill Fights, Tet and Hue together account for perhaps 1,000–1,500 lives [D]. The remaining nine-tenths rest on the territorial war, on outposts that held in 1964–68 because their platoons were not outgunned, and that mechanism is asserted at the rate of "nine in ten" (EV002-2) without an event-level count of posts attacked. The figure is not wrong in direction, and its order of magnitude is defensible from the historical RF/PF death rate of 1964–68. But the record should carry it as a judgment resting mostly on one rate, and the next memorandum that touches the territorial war should either show that rate at work in a second district or narrow the figure. No erratum: the memoranda and the chapters agree on it.

**Siege lives saved: 1,400–2,700 allied** [X-005]. The chapters' per-siege adjustments (Con Thien a third fewer, Khe Sanh about 94 fewer, An Loc a fifth fewer, Kontum a quarter fewer) sum to a figure in that range [D]. Traceable.

**Linebacker II: about six bombers lost against fifteen** [X-005]. It rests on three mechanisms, the J's electronic-warfare suite, the higher release and the early destruction of two SA-2 sites, of which only the third is shown at event level. Reliable as a direction; the precision of "six" is more than the reasoning supports, and the chapter says it holds the estimate loosely. Leave it.

**Trail interdiction: Commando Hunt's net loss about a sixth in Phase I, about 8–10 percent in Phase V, the J adding one or two points** [X-003] [X-005]. Traceable to the radar's entry and exit counts, which are the exercise's best-measured figures. Reliable. POSTNA-4 holds: the tonnage arriving stays above the requirement in every phase.

**The bands on enemy engagement control (−1 in Phases III, IV and V)** [X-005]. One band needs comment. In Phases III and V the −1 rests on battles the enemy fought and lost (Khe Sanh; An Loc and Kontum). In Phase IV it rests on sieges the enemy declined to lay, which is control exercised, not control lost: from 1969 to 1971 he still chose every battle he fought, and chose not to fight the one kind he had learned he would lose. The mechanism behind Phase IV's band is POS005-3, which says he "loses that one way of choosing his battles", and that is defensible; but the band and the war it describes pull in opposite directions. I do not change it at this checkpoint, because the position is settled and the band is consistent with it. I flag it for the next memorandum that revises Phase IV: either the chapter should show what the lost option cost the enemy in those years, or the band should return to 0 with a reversal of the position's Phase IV clause.

**American political tolerance at 0 in every phase.** Addressed in the sweep above; it is right.

No figure differs between the chapters and the memoranda in a way that needs an erratum. Two internal differences are within their ranges and need no fix: memo 001's figure for the historical programme (1,007,217 hectares by March 1973) and the card's (1,198,000 hectares by the end of 1973) are different dates from different sources, and both are tagged.

## 6. The experience of the war

**Sergeant Lam Van Thoi, Popular Forces platoon leader, Thanh Hoa hamlet on the Xa No canal, Phong Dinh Province, the night of 2–3 October 1967.** *Tests F001-17: that in a titled hamlet the government's arrival became a guarantee, and the platoon defends something of its own.* Thoi's platoon has twenty-two men, sixteen of them sons of families that received land in the spring of 1966, and on this night it has nineteen present, which is the most it has had on any night since GOLDEN SHEAF left. He checks the bunker at the canal bend at 2300 and the boy on the radio says the district has called twice. At 0140 a sampan comes down the canal without a light; the boy at the bend fires a flare and two men in it jump into the water and swim for the far bank. Nothing else happens. In the morning Thoi finds in the sampan a bag of piasters and a list of eleven names, the hamlet's families, with a sum against each. He knows six of the sums are what those families paid the Front in 1965. The other five families are new, and the sums against them are smaller. The Front is still trying to tax them; it sent a collector by water at night because it could not send one on foot by day. *From where Thoi stands the claim is visible, and it is smaller than the analysis makes it: the platoon guards a hamlet that would like not to pay, and the collector still comes. The guarantee holds because the platoon is there at night, which is POS001-1, not because the titles changed what the families want.*

**Nguyen Thi Hoa, hamlet chief, Phu My district, Binh Dinh Province, 11 April 1967.** *Tests EV000-24: that refugees who fled the Bong Son plain found their parcels held for them.* She has been hamlet chief for five weeks, since the district chief appointed her because the man before her went to Qui Nhon and did not come back. Thirty-one families have returned from the camp at Phu My since January, and twenty-eight of them have gone to the land service's office in the district town and found their parcels recorded in their own names; the clerk shows them the register and they go home. Three families find their parcels recorded to someone else. All three had received their land from the Viet Minh in 1954 and had never been on any list the decree could confirm, because they had been in the North in 1955 and come back in 1959. She sends their cases to the land court on circuit, which will sit in Phu My in July. *The claim is visible, and its edge is visible too: the decree confirmed what had been recorded, and the families the Front had moved about the country fell outside it. The record should know that a confirmation of the Front's distributions confirms only the distributions someone wrote down.*

**Tu Hai, member of the Can Giuoc district committee of the Front, the Rung Sat fringe, Long An Province, December 1967.** *Tests POS002-1 and F002-2: that the infrastructure leaves the villages with settled posts, and that arrest counts fall because there is no one left to arrest.* Tu Hai has not slept in a village of Can Giuoc since February 1966. He lives in a hut on a mangrove creek with four other cadres and goes into the district two nights a month, to a different hamlet each time, and stays two hours. The district's agricultural tax for 1967 is a quarter of what it was in 1965, and most of it now comes not from the rice villages but from the people of Can Giuoc who have moved to the Saigon wards of District 8 and pay a cadre there who lives among them. He has two orders from the province for the Tet season: to provide forty guides for the battalions that will enter Saigon from the south, and to identify the houses of the district's constables. He can provide the second from memory. He can find nine of the forty guides. *The claim is visible from here, and so is its displacement: the infrastructure has left the villages and is alive in the city, where the police knows less (EV002-5). The arrest count in Can Giuoc is low for the right reason; the count in District 8 is low for the wrong one.*

**Private Do Van Khanh, Group 559 road engineer, 98th Engineer Battalion, the Ban Laboy ford on the Xe Bang Fai, Laos, 20 October 1966.** *Tests F005-4 and C005-1: that the bombers cut every crossing they could measure and built the enemy a net with no single point of failure.* Khanh's company has laid its fourth stone ford of the season below Ban Laboy, a foot under the water, and has learned the rules: never build anything the night after the bombs, because the cameras come in the morning; build three things when one is needed; never use the same ford two nights running after a strike. The bridge his battalion built in March is a set of broken piers, and nobody has been sent to rebuild it. His company has lost four men since April, two to a bomb on a causeway and two to fever; it lost nine the previous season to the fighter-bombers. He carries stone at night and sleeps by day under the trees, and on a good night his company moves forty trucks across the river. *The claim is visible to Khanh, exactly: the bombs take nights and labour, not tonnage, and his company is safer than it was when it owned a bridge. Hanoi has bought redundancy with men's nights, which it has.*

**Major Walter Ruane, US district senior adviser, Duc Hue District, Hau Nghia Province, March 1969.** *Tests EV000-54 and C002-4: that the Commission's audit cut Phoenix's padding.* Ruane's district has a Phoenix quota of eighteen neutralizations a month. It was not in the Commission's first sample of forty districts and is not in the second; the inspectors' list is published only after the quarter ends. The Provincial Reconnaissance Unit that runs most of Duc Hue's operations reports names Ruane cannot find in the district's land register or in the Special Branch's files, which in Duc Hue are thin, because the Branch's district section was formed only in 1967 and the district was contested until Tet. He reports the discrepancy in his monthly evaluation, as he is required to, and the province's Phoenix coordinator tells him the names come from the PRU's own sources. In the neighbouring district of Cu Chi, which the Commission sampled in December, the quota has been met with fewer names and two PRU men are before the Special Court. *Here the claim is half visible. The audit works where it reaches, and it reaches forty districts a quarter out of about 240. The headline that unsupported claims fell from a third to a sixth is an average over the sampled districts. The record should say so where it next uses the figure: the audit raised the cost of padding everywhere by making it possible, and lowered padding mostly where it actually looked.*

**Mr Phan Van Tuoc, deputy director of the Land Register, Ministry of Land Reform, Saigon, 26 March 1970.** *Tests EV000-65: that the decree of 1970 completed the programme quietly, and that Thieu's government could claim the land in 1971.* Tuoc drafted the decree converting provisional titles to final ones and has spent the morning at the palace while the President signs it. The cameramen of the Vietnamese Information Service are there and the American press is not. His office has 1.06 million parcels on its register, and about 140,000 of them are in hamlets the district chiefs rate as held by day only; the families in those hamlets have provisional titles that the decree now makes final, and Tuoc knows that a final title in a hamlet the Front visits at night is a better list of targets than a provisional one. He has asked that the conversion in those hamlets wait for a security rating, and the ministry has refused, because the President wants to announce the figure. *The claim is visible and has a cost the record has not yet written: the completion of the programme in 1970 made final the titles that the Can Giuoc rule of 1964 had kept in district offices for safety. Nothing in the chapters shows what that cost in 1972, when the Easter Offensive's local forces returned to the contested hamlets. It belongs to the next memorandum that touches Phase V's countryside.*

Five of the six passages see what the analysis claims. Every one of them also sees an edge the analysis has not drawn: the collector who still comes, the families the decree did not reach, the cadres who moved to the city, the districts the audit never sampled, the titles made final in hamlets not held. The claims stand. They stand narrower than the memoranda wrote them.
