# Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964 [H40], none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year [H40]. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon; at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve and its regiments in set-piece battles, and at Song Be the government holds a province capital only narrowly and with American firepower. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

Five things in this phase are new, and all of them begin on its first day. Neither the coups in Saigon nor the summer offensive turns on any of them.

The first two belong to the Republic itself. On 1 January 1964 the junta decrees land to the tiller, confirming by name what the Viet Minh and the Front had already given, and an independent Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it (EV001-1); the same session gives the Republic a resident rural police with a Special Branch, and province and district chiefs chosen by examination from a ranked list (EV002-1). Khanh's coup tests both within a month and both survive it (EV000-1). By the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the musters have shown the RVNAF to be about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls [X-001]; about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the Front has killed about 410 constables, nearly all in posts settled ahead of night security (EV002-2) [X-002]. The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

The third is what this command can see. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks fly from January 1964. The coastal picket closes the trawler route within a year, and Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months before it did historically (EV003-1, EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats near the destroyers, Pierce Arrow is not flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the real attack of 2 August alone (EV000-3).

The fourth is in the hands of the men who fight: modern carbines for the whole RVNAF and the territorials, and a belt-fed gun and a marksman in every regular squad (EV004-1). In the held belt a post with its Popular Forces platoon now holds about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2). Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy still chooses where and when to fight, but they are lost at a smaller price and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out (EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18). The outposts that fall give the Front the same carbines.

The fifth waits on Guam: B-52Js carrying GPS-guided bombs and, from March 1964, a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut that measures the U-2S's imagery into targets for them (EV005-1). Washington refuses every request to use them over the North (EVCP02-2), and the first Arc Light strike of June 1965 flies without tankers and finds as little as history's did (EV000-20).

A sixth thing begins on the same first day and does not show until the second year. The decree of 1 January 1964 puts a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch and orders a coordinating centre in every province and district, built on the posts and files the police decree creates; the centres are staffed by the Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the sector S2, forbidden quotas, held to warrants under the police statute, and audited by the Commission (EV006-1). About 100 district centres are open by the end of 1964 and all of them by the end of 1965. In the same month the Army begins to select its field advisers by board, to train them for eight months in Vietnamese and in advising, and to keep them eighteen months; the first of them reach the subsectors and battalions in October. In March 1965 the centre at Hoc Mon breaks the district committee above the hamlets the police had already cleared, and the Front learns that leaving a village no longer puts its leaders out of reach (EV006-2).

A seventh thing began before the first day, and it is the only one that reaches back past it. Since 1957 every new officer of the ARVN, and every new American officer, has passed a branch course after commissioning and the courses of the 2026 continuum before each promotion, so that on 1 January 1964 seven such classes, about 10,000–14,000 ARVN officers, hold most of the platoons and companies of the army [M007]. The session of 1 January gives that army an Officer Selection and Promotion Board with written criteria, a rated evaluation report, central command lists for battalions and regiments, relief for cause, a path to commission for proven sergeants, and a ranked list from which generals and division commanders must be chosen, behind an American aid condition (EV007-1). Khanh's coup tests it in its first month (EV000-1). Its effect in this phase is in the companies, where the educated officers already stand, and in the first battalions the board fills; Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy chose them and the generals planned the relief (EV000-17, EV000-18).

An eighth thing stood on the first day, and it was made of steel and copper. The Republic began the war with an industrial base a generation above its own: a steelworks, an arsenal and rebuild works at Bien Hoa, a radio plant, a battery works and a wire mill at Go Vap, placed under a Directorate of Defence Industry (EV008-1). For fourteen months it had more capacity than customers, until the Aid Compliance Board made the RVNAF buy a share of its ammunition bodies, batteries, wire, radios and steel from it from March 1965. Its first effects were small and real: the Popular Forces platoons beside the police posts carried their own radios from the spring of 1964, and the Front learned to attack the posts that could not call (EV002-2); the bridges the November floods took on the coast were back in six weeks rather than months (EV008-2). No battle of the phase turned on it.

A ninth thing stood in the ranks on the first day, and it was the plainest of them. Since 1957 every man who enlisted or was drafted into the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers and the Vietnamese Marines had passed a ten-week basic course on the 2026 American programme at Quang Trung or a division centre, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit a six-week abridgement of it; every American recruit since 1957 had passed his own service's 2026 course. On 1 January 1964 about 120,000–160,000 of the Republic's regular soldiers and about 60,000–100,000 of its territorials had been trained so, and the rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was put on the muster roll as a course number the Muster Service could check (EV009-1) [M009]. It changed no battle's choice of ground in the phase. It changed how the posts' first ten minutes went at Long Huu, how many of the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment came back from Thuan Loi, and how many wounded men in both armies were alive in the morning (EV002-2, EV000-18).

A tenth thing stood between the private and the officer, and it had been there since 1957 as well. Every NCO of the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers, the Vietnamese Marines, the VNAF and the VNN, and every sergeant and corporal of the Civil Guard and the Self-Defense Corps, had passed the course for his grade at the National NCO Academy at Nha Trang, a division school or a territorial centre before he could be promoted to the next, on a curriculum built on the American Army's of 2026; every American NCO had passed his own service's course for his grade under the same rule (EV010-1). It did not make more sergeants, and it took between 1,300 and 2,500 of the best ones to teach. It meant that the squad and the platoon, where the trained private met the educated lieutenant, were led by men schooled for the job, and that a corporal first class who had passed the leader's course could take an empty squad as a schooled man (F010-1, EC010-3) [X-010].

## EV001-1 · The Land-to-the-Tiller Decree and the Commission Against Corruption · 1 Jan 1964 · Saigon · NEW · 001,CP01
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh, with Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho's cabinet, promulgated two decree-laws drafted with the US Mission over the preceding weeks (P001a, P001b). The first ended the collection of rent on tenanted rice land from the coming main-season harvest, made every present tiller the provisional owner of the land he worked up to 3 hectares in the Delta and 1 hectare in the Central Lowlands, set the landlord's retention of tenanted land at zero and his compensation at 2.5 times the land's annual paddy yield, 20% in cash and the rest in eight-year bonds indexed to the paddy price, and confirmed by name every distribution the Viet Minh or the Front had made to the man now tilling the land [M001]. The second created the Commission Against Corruption under a Commissioner appointed for six years, Judge Truong Vinh Khoa of the Saigon Court of Appeal, a Special Court for Corruption, a Pay and Muster Verification Service, and a Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board whose American co-chair, the Deputy Chief of Mission, could suspend aid to a named command, importer or province and whose concurrence was needed to remove the Commissioner [M001].

Minh read the land decree on Radio Saigon that evening; its first sentence was the confirmation of the Front's distributions [X-001]. Within a week the Vietnamese Information Service's loudspeaker teams and leaflets had carried it to the district towns of the Delta and the coastal Centre. The landowners' associations of Saigon and Can Tho protested to the council within days, and the protest was carried by officers of landed families who sat on it [X-001]. Two USAF-contracted survey aircraft began photographing the rice lands from Tan Son Nhut on 13 January, tied to ground control laid by an Army topographic detachment from the old French triangulation; they finished the Delta in early April and the coastal Centre in May [X-001].

## EV002-1 · The Police and Provincial Administration decrees; the first list · 1 Jan – 27 Apr 1964 · Saigon · NEW · 002,CP01
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964, in the same session that promulgated the land and anti-corruption decrees (EV001-1), the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh promulgated two more decree-laws drafted with the US Mission and the aid mission's Public Safety Division (P002a, P002b). The first rebuilt the National Police around the village: a resident post of 8 to 12 constables in every village the government held, a Police Special Branch with sole charge of intelligence on the Viet Cong infrastructure, a Police Field Force to be raised at once rather than in 1966, a national identity card and family census, a pay and career statute, and magistrate review of every arrest within 72 hours, with detention without trial capped at two years and reviewed every six months [M002]. The Directorate General was given to Vo Dinh Cuong, a career inspector of the old Sûreté who had served in the police under the French and under Diem and had kept out of Nhu's political service, for a fixed term removable only for cause [X-002]. The second decree created the Provincial Administration Selection Board, seven members under Judge Dinh Quang Tuyen of the Court of Cassation, and an Inspectorate of Provincial Administration, and made unlawful the appointment of any province chief, district chief or deputy for administration who was not taken from the Board's ranked list [M002].

The two decrees were read on Radio Saigon on the evening of 1 January after the land decree, and drew less notice than it did; the landowners protested the land decree, and the officers who would lose their provinces said nothing yet, because there was nothing yet to lose [X-002]. The British Advisory Mission under R. G. K. Thompson, in Saigon since 1961, lent four Special Branch officers from the Federation of Malaysia to write the Special Branch's first manuals; the first 2,500 recruits a month entered the police training centres at Vung Tau and Da Lat from February [H] [X-002]. The Board's written examination was sat on 2 March at the National Institute of Administration by about 1,640 officers and civil servants, of whom about 1,000 were judged credible after the three-day assessments held through March and April [X-002].

On 27 April 1964 the Board published its first list in the official gazette: ranked candidates for 31 posts, the 6 province chiefs and 25 district chiefs whom the Inspectorate's first sweep had found worst, nine of them in Khanh's "acting" appointments of February (EV000-1) [X-002]. Khanh's government appointed 27 of the 31 from the first name on each list, 3 from the second and 1 from the third; it refused no list [X-002]. By 31 December 1964 about 95 of the Republic's roughly 290 chiefs had been appointed through the list, a third of the whole, and the Board's register of candidates had become the first record the Republic had ever kept of what its officers could actually do (F002-5) [X-002].

## EV006-1 · The Phung Hoang decree, the first centres and the first selected advisers · 1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Special Warfare School at Fort Bragg and the Defense Language Institute at Monterey; the district towns of the held belt in IV and III Corps · NEW · 006
*Initiative: allied*

The Phung Hoang decree was the third signed in the session of 1 January 1964, after the land decree and the police decree, and it was the shortest of the three. It created a national Phung Hoang Committee under the Minister of the Interior, a national coordinating centre of about 150 staff in the Directorate of National Police, and an intelligence and operations coordinating centre in every province and district as the Special Branch's sections opened there: a PIOCC of 25–40 in each province capital and a DIOCC of 10–15 in each district office [M006]. Each centre sat in the Special Branch's office, under the district or province chief, with a Special Branch officer as its head, an officer of the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and the census teams and the Chieu Hoi office feeding it [M006]. Three articles were this headquarters' and were fought for in the drafting: no centre would be given a monthly target or credited with a number; no person would be arrested on a centre's file except on a magistrate's warrant under the police statute, on the three-source rule; and the Commission Against Corruption would sample the centres' reports of persons neutralized, name by name, from the first quarter of 1965 [M006]. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the same statute as a police arm, about 44 units of 60–100 men under the province chiefs, with a rule that a raid's object was a prisoner [M006].

The national centre's director was Lieutenant Colonel Tran Dinh Hieu, a Military Security officer who had served in the Sûreté's political section before 1954 and had been passed over twice under Diem for refusing to work on the Can Lao's lists [X-006]. Beside him sat an American cell of about 60, and in Saigon an exploitation detachment of about 80 Vietnamese-speaking analysts and translators, drawn from the 500th Military Intelligence Group, the Agency's station and the language school, under Lieutenant Colonel Martin J. Ashby of the US Army [X-006]. The detachment's work was the method the 2026 practice calls F3EAD and the period had no name for: every arrest and every rallier was exploited within the week, every captured document translated, every name placed on a chart of the district committee, its sections, its couriers and its families, and the chart returned to the district centre with the next questions [M006]. About 1,500 SEEK II kits enrolled every detainee and rallier by fingerprint, iris and face against an offline watchlist [M006·S3]; the kits were the one instrument of 2026 the centres kept whole, and the Muster Service's technicians maintained them with their own (F006-3).

The centres opened behind the posts, not ahead of them. The first DIOCC opened at Phu Tan in An Giang on 16 March 1964, two weeks after the first village post; by the end of June there were about 40, all in districts of the Delta and around Saigon where the posts already stood, and by 31 December about 100 DIOCCs and 20 PIOCCs [X-006]. In the contested districts of the coastal Centre the province chiefs asked for centres before they had posts, and Hieu refused them; that refusal, which Ashby supported and this headquarters endorsed, kept the method out of the villages where a file could only be built from a rallier's grudge (EC006-12 abandoned) [X-006].

The advisers came by another road. In January 1964 the Department of the Army opened the Selected Advisory Corps to volunteers and convened its first boards in February: captains, majors and senior NCOs with a combat or command record, assessed over two weeks at Fort Bragg for temperament, judgment and aptitude for language, of whom about half passed [M006]. The first class of about 1,100 began the course on 3 February: six weeks of advising, about 24 weeks of Vietnamese at the Defense Language Institute, and four of weapons, combat medicine and the calling of fire and air [M006]. They reached the field from 1 October, replacing advisers on the old short tour as each left, and by the end of December about a third of the field posts below corps were held by men who had chosen to be there, could speak to their counterparts without an interpreter and would stay eighteen months [M006]. Among them was Captain Samuel J. Whitcomb, a Korean War NCO commissioned in 1956, who took over the Can Giuoc subsector on 9 October, four months after the killings there (EV001-3), and on his first night asked the district chief, in Vietnamese, which families still held their titles at the district office and why [X-006]. The Front noticed the new advisers before this headquarters measured them: a COSVN security bulletin of December 1964, captured in 1966, warned district committees that "the new American advisers speak our language and must be assumed to understand what is said in front of them" [X-006].

## EV007-1 · The Officer Selection Board and the first board season · 1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Joint General Staff at Tan Son Nhut; the Thu Duc Reserve Officers School and the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The officer decree was the fourth signed in the session of 1 January 1964, and the generals who signed it understood it least. It made every promotion from first lieutenant to colonel, and every command of a battalion or regiment, the decision of an Officer Selection and Promotion Board under the Joint General Staff, sitting in panels of five senior officers on written criteria; it required that every officer be rated twice a year by his commander and by his commander's commander on a form that allowed no more than a third of any senior rater's officers in the top block; it gave the board the power to relieve a commander for cause after a thirty-day review, opened a commission to sergeants with a combat record and a recommendation, abolished the ninth-grade diploma as a bar to the rank of major, and required that the President choose each new general and each division commander from a ranked list of three [M007]. Article 14, the one this headquarters and the US Mission fought for, put the general-officer list under the Aid Compliance Board's condition: a division whose commander was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds for its operations [M007].

The board's first chairman was Brigadier General Phan Khac Minh, a French-trained infantry officer of fifty-one who had run the personnel directorate of the General Staff in 1955–57 and been sent to command a training camp by Diem for refusing to promote the Can Lao's men ahead of the list [X-007]. Beside the board sat an American cell of about 40 under Colonel Robert E. Lindqvist, who had built the evaluation system's trainers' course at Fort Benning and had never served in Asia; his first report to this headquarters said that the hard part of the reform was not the form but persuading a colonel to give a nephew a middle rating, and that he had no idea how it would be done [X-007].

The board inherited the officers that seven years of the education had made. The classes of 1957–63 had passed the branch basic course after Thu Duc or Dalat, and the first four of them the career course as well, so that in January 1964 most of the ARVN's rifle companies and many of its artillery batteries were commanded by lieutenants and captains who had been taught to make a fire plan, to scout a route, to run a range and to write an order, in Vietnamese, to the American standard of 2026 [M007]. Above them the army was what it had been: about two-thirds of the battalion commanders had been appointed by a corps or division commander for reasons of his own, about a third of the battalions were commanded by captains because there were not enough majors, and the generals were the generals of the November coup [X-007]. One of the captains was Bui Quang Trieu, commissioned from Thu Duc in 1957 in the first educated class, who had commanded a company of the 5th Division's 9th Regiment for three years and was rated in the top block by both his raters in the first season [X-007].

The first season ran from April to September. The panels read about 9,000 evaluation reports, promoted about 6,500 officers, nearly all of them on the old seniority with the new reports beside it, and published on 30 June the army's first central command list: 61 battalion commands and 9 regimental commands, about a third of those falling vacant in the year [X-007]. The corps commanders were asked to nominate and did; about half of their nominees were selected, and the rest of the list came from officers no corps commander had put forward [X-007]. The career-course gate held the majors' grade to the officers already through the course, and the shortage of majors that the historical army met with captains in command was met here with captains as well, for another year and a half (F007-8) [X-007]. The schools took their price at the same time: the career course at Thu Duc and the staff course that opened at Dalat in September drew about one officer in fifteen out of the units and about 120 of the army's best captains and majors as instructors, in the year the summer offensive was being prepared (F007-10) [X-007].

The coups tested the board twice before the year ended. Khanh's coup came thirty days after the decree (EV000-1). In September, when Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc moved on Saigon, two of the battalions they brought were commanded by officers the board had refused for the June list, and none by an officer it had chosen [X-007]. Minh told Lindqvist that he had not expected the list to show its worth so soon, and that he expected the generals to have noticed as well [X-007].

## EV008-1 · The Directorate of Defence Industry and the domestic share · 1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Bien Hoa industrial zone, Bien Hoa Province; the Go Vap and Thu Duc works, Gia Dinh Province; the Joint General Staff and the Aid Compliance Board, Saigon · NEW · 008
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Republic owned what no Southeast Asian state of its size owned: an electric-arc steelworks at Bien Hoa with two 30-tonne furnaces, a billet caster and a mill rolling bar, rod, light sections and light plate; a forge and heat-treatment shop beside it; an arsenal with four case-and-bullet lines and two forging lines for mortar and 105mm projectile bodies; engine, vehicle and weapons rebuild works; a steel boatyard at Thu Duc; fabrication shops for bridge spans and fortification steel; and, at Go Vap and in the Bien Hoa zone, a communications plant licensed for the AN/PRC-10, a dry-cell and storage-battery works, a wire and cable mill, a transistor assembly plant owned by an American firm, and a signal rebuild depot [M008]. About 35,000–50,000 people worked in the metal trades and 20,000–35,000 in electronics, with about 1,800–2,800 engineers between them [M008]. The same session of the junta that decreed land to the tiller and the rural police placed the state's share of all of it under a Directorate of Defence Industry in the Ministry of Defence, and named as Director Vuong Dinh Toan, a metallurgist trained at the École Centrale who had built the Bien Hoa melt shop [X-008].

For its first fourteen months the base had more capacity than customers, and that was the whole of its problem. The RVNAF's Ordnance and Signal services drew their ammunition, radios, batteries, wire and spares from American stocks under the Military Assistance Program, free and delivered, and no Vietnamese quartermaster would pay piasters for a cartridge case the Americans gave him [X-008]. The arsenal ran its case lines at about a third of capacity in 1964, selling to the National Police and the Combat Youth what the Army would not buy, and the steelworks ran at about half for want of scrap, rolling 100,000–150,000 tonnes a year, most of it reinforcing bar and the pickets and wire of the outpost programme (EV002-2) [M008]. The agricultural machinery shops did better than the arsenal, because a farmer who owned his land had a reason to buy a pump: about 3,000 diesel pumps and small engines went to owner-cultivators in An Giang, Vinh Long and Phong Dinh in 1964–65, bought on credit from the Agricultural Credit Office against the new titles (EV001-2) [X-008].

The fix was a rule, and this headquarters wrote it. Colonel Howard B. Teague, the Ordnance Corps officer who headed the advisory element to the Directorate, proposed in November 1964 that a fixed share of the RVNAF's requirement in eight categories be bought from the Directorate with counterpart piasters before any American stock was drawn: small-arms cartridge cases and bullets, 60mm and 81mm mortar bodies, 105mm projectile bodies, field wire, radio dry cells, PF and hamlet radios, construction steel and bridge spans [X-008]. I sent it to CINCPAC, and on 1 March 1965 the Aid Compliance Board made it a condition of the counterpart-fund release: a quarter of the requirement in 1965, a third in 1966 and half from 1968 (EC008-2, POS008-1) [X-008]. The American ordnance staff at Long Binh objected that domestic cases cost about a fifth more than the American article and were unproven, and both objections were true in 1965 [X-008]. The rule held because the Board held, as it held for the land titles and the province chiefs (EV001-1, EV002-1).

The base also had a price in men. About 31,000–45,000 of its workers were of military age, and every one needed a deferment that the mobilization decrees of 1964 did not provide (F008-9) [M008]. The Directorate's deferment list became a market within a year: in Bien Hoa in the spring of 1965 a place on it cost about two months of a skilled worker's wage, paid to a clerk in the Directorate's personnel office, and the Commission Against Corruption opened its first case in the factories in May (EV001-1) [X-008]. The plants also took, at better pay, the electricians, machinists and radio technicians the Signal and Ordnance Corps had expected to draft. By July 1965 the communications plant had delivered about 5,000 PRC-10s and 6,000 hamlet radios, the battery works about 600,000 dry cells, and the wire mill about 40,000 kilometres of field wire, most of it to the territorial forces of the held belt [X-008].

The Front read the base wrongly and organised it rightly. A COSVN study captured in Bien Hoa in 1966 called the plants "American factories employing Vietnamese labour", which they were not; its proselytizing section had by then put cells into the arsenal's case lines and the battery works, and was collecting dues from workers whose brothers were in the ARVN (F008-17, F008-21) [X-008]. It was the first time since 1954 that the Front had an industrial working class in the South to organise, and it set about it as the Party in the North had been taught to.

## EV009-1 · The ten-week cycle and the course number · 1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the national and division training centres; the RF/PF training centres of the provinces; the Muster Service and the Commission, Saigon · NEW · 009
*Initiative: allied*

The basic course was already seven years old when this headquarters opened, and the work of 1964 was to keep it honest under a war that wanted men faster than it could train them. At Quang Trung on 6 January 1964 the cycle that began was the fifty-sixth since 1957: about 1,200 recruits in four companies, each company in platoons of about 40 under two Vietnamese drill sergeants, ten weeks on the 2026 programme in Vietnamese, with the M4A1 and its CompM5 on the ranges from the fourth week, the M249 for the men the battalions would make gunners, the field exercise the Americans called the Forge in the ninth, and in the second week the block that every recruit remembered, which taught him to put a tourniquet high and tight on a friend's leg in the dark in under a minute (EC009-1, EC009-3) [M009]. The senior drill sergeant of the academy that made those sergeants, Sergeant Major Tran Van Hai, had been in its first class in 1957; the advisory detachment of eleven Americans under Lieutenant Colonel Raymond F. Coyle watched, scored and did not teach, by the rule that a recruit would take his orders from a Vietnamese (EC009-8) [X-009]. In the provinces, the RF/PF centres ran the six-week territorial course in platoon intakes, so that a PF platoon raised in a village went to the centre together and came back together (EC009-2) [M009].

What the course could not make was a squad leader, and in 1964 it cost the army the sergeants who could. The academy and the centres held about 2,000–3,000 of the ARVN's best NCOs as drill sergeants when the battalions were being broken in Phuoc Long and Quang Ngai, and about 1,300–3,600 of them were men the battalions would otherwise have kept in 1964–66 (F009-11) [X-009]. The Joint General Staff asked twice, in June and November 1964, for a third of them back. The training command refused the first request with the advisory chief's support and met the second by moving 400 drill sergeants of the 1957–59 classes to the battalions on promotion and replacing them from the battalions' own corporals, which was the first turn of what became the drill sergeant's circle (EC009-4, F009-4) [X-009].

The rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was the course's hardest part, and it was enforced by a number. From April 1964 every man on a muster roll carried the number of the cycle he had passed, and the dawn musters of the Muster Service checked it against the centres' rolls (EV001-4, EC009-5, F009-3) [X-009]. In September 1964 the muster of a 22nd Division battalion in Binh Dinh found 212 privates whose cycle numbers belonged to a cycle at the division's training centre that the centre's own ration accounts showed had been fed for four weeks, not ten; the centre's commandant had been sending men to the battalions after the rifle range and drawing the remaining six weeks' rations [X-009]. The Commission charged him in October and the board relieved him in November (EV007-1). The 212 went back to the centre and finished the course. That was the first of eleven such cases in 1964–65, and the advisers estimated that a fifth to a third of the centres' qualification figures in those years were false in some degree (F009-17) [X-009]. The figures were scored by the men they rated, as every figure in this war was; the course number was the one check that did not depend on them.

The Americans' course arrived in Vietnam in their recruits and asked nothing of this headquarters. The advisers of 1964 and the Marines and paratroopers of 1965 had taken the 2026 basic course of their service, the riflemen and also the clerks, cooks and truck drivers for whom it was all the combat training they would ever have [M009]. The first record of it in this theatre is an adviser's report from Kien Hoa in October 1964: a Signal Corps radio repairman, wounded in the thigh by a mine fragment on the road to Mo Cay, put his own tourniquet on before the medic reached him and was alive at the province hospital two hours later, and his adviser forwarded the report because the man had never been taught first aid anywhere but in basic [X-009]. By the summer of 1965 the field hospitals of the build-up were receiving American wounded with tourniquets on as a matter of course, and the surgeons at Nha Trang were already complaining about how long some had been left on.

## EV010-1 · The academy at Dong De and the gate · 1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The National NCO Academy at Dong De, Nha Trang, Khanh Hoa Province; the division NCO schools; the RF/PF training centres; the Joint General Staff, Saigon · NEW · 010
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the National NCO Academy at Dong De, on the hills above Nha Trang, began its eighth year as the school of the army's sergeants. It had been reorganised in 1957, a year earlier than historically, to teach every NCO grade a course before promotion, and since then every corporal first class of the ARVN and the territorial forces who wanted to be a sergeant had passed its six-week leader's course or the same course at a division school, every sergeant the four-week squad leader's course, every platoon sergeant the senior course, and the army's senior master sergeants a course of four to five months (M010·S8) [X-010]. Its senior instructor was Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc, a Tonkinese who had come south with the Commando School in 1954 and had taught every class since 1957; its senior American adviser from 1963 was Master Sergeant William H. Rourke, who had served as a platoon sergeant in Korea and had passed the senior course himself at Fort Benning [X-010]. The academy's barracks held about 3,300 students; the division schools and the territorial centres taught about as many again, and in 1964 the sequence put about 27,000–45,000 regular and territorial NCOs through a course (E03) [X-010].

The cost was paid in the year it could least be afforded. In June 1964, after the 1963–64 losses in the Delta, the Joint General Staff asked the academy and the division schools for a third of their instructors back for the general reserve; the Central Training Command refused it, as it refused the same request for the drill sergeants, and in August met it in part by sending 300 instructors of the 1957–59 classes to the Ranger and Airborne battalions as platoon sergeants and replacing them from the battalions' own sergeants (F010-11) [X-010]. In November, with the Armed Forces Council preparing a National Day promotion for the whole army, the Muster Service and MACV's training directorate argued that a grade for every man would put about 20,000 corporals first class into the sergeants' rank without the leader's course; the council promoted every private one grade and every NCO with his course one grade, and left the gate standing (F010-5) [X-010]. The academy's manuals, printed in Vietnamese at the Army Printing Office in Saigon, went into the packs of about 30,000 sergeants that year, and by December the first captured copies had reached the B-2 Front's military staff (F010-20) [X-010].

## EV003-1 · Operation TIDE TABLE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · The II Corps coast from Qui Nhon to Cam Ranh Bay (Binh Dinh, Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa Provinces) and the Ca Mau coast of An Xuyen Province · NEW · 003,CP01
*Initiative: allied*

The first Global Hawk orbit went up from Clark Air Base on 6 January 1964 and took station off the II Corps coast, about 60 nautical miles out between Qui Nhon and Cam Ranh Bay, with its radar's maritime mode sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep (EC003-3) [M003]. The operation was named TIDE TABLE. Its quarry was the steel-hulled trawlers of about 100 tons run south from Haiphong and the Do Son peninsula by the PAVN's maritime transport group, Group 759, which became Group 125 of the PAVN Navy in the course of 1964 [H]. Its hunters were few: the Vietnamese Navy's Sea Force, with a handful of PC-461-class submarine chasers and PCE escorts out of Qui Nhon and Nha Trang, the motorised and sail junks of the Junk Force's coastal divisions, and, when the Seventh Fleet lent them, a US destroyer or two off the coast [H]. Before January nobody had been looking for the trawlers at all; the coast is about 2,000 kilometres long and carried tens of thousands of fishing junks [M-TNA].

The first track came on the night of 18–19 February. A contact moving at a steady ten knots on a course that had begun in the Gulf of Tonkin two days earlier, running 80 nautical miles offshore past Quang Ngai, turned west at dusk toward the Phu Yen coast; it did not stop, did not fish and did not change speed [X-003]. The exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut flagged it at 1940, and the Vietnamese Navy's coastal command at Nha Trang sent a PC-461 from her patrol line off Tuy Hoa. The radar held the trawler for seven hours, so the patrol craft did not have to search; she had only to steam. She came up with the trawler at 0310 about four miles off Vung Ro Bay, under a low overcast and in a moderate northeast swell. The trawler's crew ran her toward the beach and fired her demolition charges, as Group 759's standing orders required; the forward hold blew, the after hold did not [H] [X-003]. A boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of cargo before the fire reached it: Chinese Type 56 carbines and assault rifles, B-40 (RPG-2) launchers and their rockets, 82mm mortar rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm ammunition [X-003]. Seven of the crew were taken from the water, two of them wounded; nine died in the explosion or drowned [X-003]. The Vietnamese lost one sailor killed and four wounded to small-arms fire from the beach, where a VC reception party had waited with porters [X-003].

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven contacts that the cell judged to be trawlers, on the II Corps coast and on the Ca Mau peninsula, where the landings went into the mangrove creeks of the Bo De and the Cua Lon [X-003]. Four were intercepted: two scuttled or blew up, one was taken nearly intact off Binh Dinh in April by the destroyer USS *Edsall*'s boarding party with a Vietnamese liaison officer, and one ran into the Bo De ahead of a junk division and landed most of its cargo [X-003]. Six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon, which the radar saw as a reversal of course at about the time the trawler's own radar would have detected the patrol [X-003]. One landed unobserved in May, on a night when the orbit's aircraft had diverted to Clark with a generator fault and the relief was four hours late [X-003]. The casualty figures above are counts of bodies and prisoners and are reliable; the cargo tonnages are estimates from what was recovered and from the prisoners' statements of their loads, and carry a margin of about a third [E: recovery and interrogation].

The prisoners told the rest. Interrogated at Nha Trang, the crew of the April capture described the landing points, the signal lamps, the reception parties and about two years of earlier voyages, and the Vietnamese Navy found two of the landing beaches in Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa from their directions [X-003]. TIDE TABLE did not starve the Front; the tonnage it stopped in six months was a small fraction of what moved by sea and through Laos. What it ended was the cheapest and most direct route by which heavy weapons reached the coastal provinces of II Corps and the lower Delta, and it ended it by being seen, which is the subject of the next event in this chapter (EV003-2) (F003-17).

## EV004-1 · Operation IRON GATE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Saigon port and Tan Son Nhut; the Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the division training centres and the province RF/PF training camps of IV and III Corps, then countrywide · NEW · 004,CP01
*Initiative: allied*

The first ship of the rearmament, MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez*, berthed at the Khanh Hoi wharves in Saigon on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines, their Aimpoint CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249 light machine guns and 320 M110A2 marksman rifles, and about 380 tonnes of 5.56mm and 7.62mm ammunition [X-004]. MACV called the issue Operation IRON GATE, because the first rule of it was that no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate in exchange. The second rule was the zeroing range. Every company that drew carbines drew them by name against its muster roll, fired each one at 25 metres until the dot sat on the point of aim, and handed in its M1 Garands, M1 and M2 carbines, BARs and Thompsons before it left (EC004-2) [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn, chief of the small-arms new-equipment training team, set the course at five days for an infantry company and three for a Popular Forces platoon, and refused to shorten it when the corps commanders asked [X-004].

The order of issue was the order of the war's danger. The ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions in the Delta and the Regional and Popular Forces of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac provinces drew first, in January and February; the Airborne Brigade, the Marine battalions and the Ranger battalions of the general reserve in February and March; I and II Corps from April [X-004]. Khanh's coup of 30 January stopped the issue for nine days and cost more: three of the division commanders who had sent their companies to the ranges were replaced, and their successors recalled the companies to garrison the province towns against a counter-coup before the course was finished (F004-5) [X-004]. Venn's teams counted about 2.4 million Vietnamese training man-days in the first half of 1964 and reckoned that about a fifth of them were lost to the coup and its aftermath, in units that drew carbines and went back to their posts without zeroing them [X-004].

By 30 June about 310,000 carbines had been issued, about 180,000 of them to the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles to the regular infantry; the marksmen's course at Quang Trung, three weeks long, had graduated its first 1,100 [X-004]. The depots took back about 290,000 old weapons [X-004]. About a third of them went, at the Directorate's insistence and over this headquarters' objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia, who were not covered by the rearmament and wanted something better than shotguns; the rest were crated at the Go Vap arsenal [X-004]. On the ranges of the Delta the change was visible in an afternoon. A Popular Forces platoon at Can Duoc that had fired its Garands at a 100-metre target and hit it about once in four shots put most of its rounds into the same target by the third morning with carbines its smallest men could hold steady, and asked to be allowed to keep the spent cases as proof to show the village [X-004]. The Front's province committee in Long An reported the new weapons to the region in February and called them a "trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty"; it ordered its local-force companies to capture one for study, and the first was taken on 22 March in an ambush at Rach Kien [X-004].

## EV000-1 · Khanh's coup · 30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,CP01,007
*Initiative: neither*

Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh without firing a shot [H8]. He acted with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau [H8]. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were put under house arrest on a charge of plotting a French-backed neutralist settlement, and Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho was detained [H8]. The one death was Major Nguyen Van Nhung, Minh's aide, executed after the takeover [H8]. Khanh kept Minh as a figurehead chief of state [H8]. The generals were tried in closed session on 28–29 May and found guilty only of "lax morality" [H8].

The coup was not caused by the decrees of 1 January; it was the product of the generals' rivalries and Khanh's ambition, and it came on the historical date [X-001]. But the reforms shaped what followed. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay gave Khanh their quiet support in the hope of a government that would let the decrees lapse (F001-16) [X-001]. In February Khanh asked Commissioner Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals; Khoa replied that the Commission had no evidence against them and would bring no case without it [X-001]. Khanh moved to replace him, and the American co-chair of the Aid Compliance Board refused the concurrence the decree required [X-001]. The generals went before a military court instead, on the "lax morality" charge of the historical record.

Washington was surprised but quick to back the new man; President Johnson sent Khanh a personal note on 2 February [H8], which in this war carried the condition, at the urging of the US Mission and this headquarters, that the United States would support no government that repealed either decree (F001-15) [X-001]. Khanh, who needed American backing more than he needed the landlords, accepted it and kept both. The coup mattered less for what Khanh did than for the precedent it set: a second violent change of government within three months, carried out by officers against officers, and accepted by the United States. Khanh moved, as every coup-maker had, to place his men in the provinces, and the decree of 1 January now stood in his way (EV002-1). In March, Secretary of Defense McNamara visited and endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 of 17 March 1964 committed the United States to the survival of an independent, non-communist South Vietnam [H]. In June 1964 General William C. Westmoreland, Harkins's deputy since January, took command of MACV, and General Maxwell D. Taylor replaced Henry Cabot Lodge as ambassador in July [H].

In this war Khanh's coup was the first test of the police and provincial decrees, and it came thirty days after them, before the Board had published a list. On the morning of 30 January Khanh's officers detained the new Director General of National Police, Vo Dinh Cuong, as they had detained the police chief on the historical course, and in the first week of February Khanh named "acting" chiefs in eleven provinces and districts [X-002]. The US Mission and this headquarters treated the two decrees as part of the condition Johnson's note attached to the land and anti-corruption decrees: the Aid Compliance Board gave notice that a province whose chief was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds once the first list was published, and Cuong was released on 2 February and restored to his office (F002-16) [X-002]. Khanh kept the Board, because he had no candidate for its chairmanship and saw a reputation for reform in it, and kept his acting chiefs until the list replaced nine of the eleven in April and May; the other two placed well enough to be appointed from it [X-002]. It was a partial failure of the reform's first test and a partial success, and it set the pattern that held until 1970: the Board survived each government because the government that inherited it found it cheaper to keep than to break.

The officer decree was tested in the same month, and in the same way. On 3 February Khanh's staff sent the Joint General Staff a list of 23 promotions and six regimental commands for officers who had served the coup; the board, which had existed for 34 days and had not yet read a single evaluation report, had no list of its own to set against it [X-007]. The Mission added the officer decree to the condition of Johnson's note, and Minh offered Khanh a bargain that set the pattern for the next six years: the board confirmed the eleven promotions that met its written criteria and refused the twelve that did not, and Khanh's regimental commanders kept their commands until the first command list in June, which confirmed four of the six (EV007-1) [X-007]. The generals were the exception. Until the first ranked list in January 1965 Khanh made the generals he needed, and after it every junta learned to place its partners among the three names rather than outside them. The reform's first lesson to Saigon was that the board could be bargained with and could not be ignored; its first lesson to this headquarters was that the list for division command was exactly as strong as the aid condition behind it (F007-1).

## EV002-2 · The first posts and the Front's answer · 1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 002,004,CP01,006,008,009
*Initiative: both*

The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night (EC002-1) [X-002]. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village [X-002]. From February the platoon beside the post carried M4A1 carbines with red-dot sights, drawn under IRON GATE (EV004-1); the constables, whom the rearmament did not cover, kept the carbines of 1950 [X-004]. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965 [M002]. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads (EC002-2) [M002]. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered [X-002].

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected (F002-12). A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night [X-002]. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966 [X-002]. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required (F002-8) [X-002].

Where the platoon was there, it now held. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about nine in ten held, against about four in five that the programme's design would have given them with the platoons' old weapons [X-004]. The case that the Delta's advisers told for a year was the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140 against a post whose PF platoon had zeroed its carbines in March. The platoon's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and the company's first assault, which on the Front's own experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause; the second was not pressed, and the company withdrew before the Regional Forces boat company from Ham Long arrived at 0320, leaving eleven dead on the bund and taking its wounded [X-004]. The Front learned that lesson faster than this headquarters expected. From December its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in, and, against the posts that held, the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market (F004-1, F004-2) [X-004]. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts [X-004].

The weapons the Front took changed with its targets. Of the roughly 300 weapons lost with the police posts, nearly all were the constables' old carbines; but across the countryside the outposts that did fall, the Popular Forces deserters who walked away with their arms, and the ARVN battalions ambushed in the summer of 1965 gave the Front between about 10,000 and 30,000 M4A1 carbines by the end of 1965, with their sights (F004-7) [X-004]. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and each one needed the Republic's own 5.56mm to fire.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not [X-002]. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed (F002-13) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

From the summer of 1964 the Special Branch's harvest had a second layer. The district centres that opened behind the posts took each arrest and each rallier and asked a different question of them: not who in this hamlet belongs to the Front, but who sends the hamlet's cadres their orders, who collects the tax above the village, and where the district committee's couriers sleep (EC006-1) [X-006]. Of the roughly 1,900 people the Branch arrested in the held belt by July 1965, about 260 were members of district or village party committees or their security and finance sections, against perhaps 100 that the Branch's own folders would have reached without the centres' charts [X-006]. The biometric enrolment caught its first case in December 1964 at Cho Moi in An Giang, a finance cadre released by a magistrate in August on a bribe paid through a cousin, who was rearrested in November under a new name with a new identity card and found by the kit in forty seconds (F006-3) [X-006]. The Commission prosecuted the magistrate's clerk in 1965. The Front's answer to the centres came, as its answer to the posts had, within a season: from January 1965 its district committees in the held belt began moving their offices out of the villages to the contested edge, and the Dinh Tuong clerk who had copied informant cards in 1964 was the first of the penetrations the Branch would find in the centres rather than the posts (F006-5).

In this war the post's radio was not alone. From April 1964 the PF platoon beside each post carried its own set, a PRC-10 from the Go Vap plant, and about half the villages of the held belt had a hamlet radio on the district net, with dry cells from the battery works exchanged at the district every week (EC008-4, EC008-8, EV008-1) [X-008]. At Long Huu on 17 November it was the platoon's PRC-10 that called the Ham Long boats at 0143, three minutes after the first assault; on the old course the call would have been a flare and a runner, and the boats would have come at dawn [X-008]. The Front learned the radio in a season, as it learned the carbine (F008-17). From early 1965 the reconnaissance cells of its district companies reported which posts had a set on the air and which did not, and by the summer of 1965 posts in the held belt that could call were being attacked about a third less often than posts that could not (F008-16) [X-008]. The sets and batteries also leaked: by August 1965 Go Vap dry cells were being found in the firing circuits of the Front's mines in Long An and Dinh Tuong, bought in the markets from PF soldiers and from the warehouses that issued them (F008-5) [X-008].

The platoons beside the posts were trained men, and that was what made them platoons. Nearly every PF platoon of the held belt in 1964 had been raised since 1957 and had gone through the six-week territorial course together, and the course's first lesson after the rifle was the first ten minutes: hold fire until the target is in the light, fire in pairs so that one man is always loaded, report on the radio what you see rather than that you are attacked, and keep the ammunition for the second assault (EC009-2, F009-2) [M009]. At Long Huu on 17 November it was the second assault that the platoon's corporal, Pham Van Tho, trained at the Ben Tre centre in 1962, had kept a third of his ammunition for; and the two PF soldiers wounded on the bund were alive at 0320 when the boats came, both with tourniquets on, where on the old course the advisers would have expected one of them to have bled out before dawn [X-009]. Across the held belt I estimate that the posts with their platoon held about nineteen attacks in twenty in 1964–65 rather than about nine in ten, and that the PF platoons lost about a fifth fewer of their wounded [X-009]. The constables, whom the course did not reach, died as before; the posts without platoons, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia remained what the Front attacked, and the gap between the posts inside both grants and the posts outside them widened (F009-6) [X-009]. And from 1965 some of the men the Front's district companies sent against the posts had been trained on the same course: PF deserters who had walked home with their training if not their carbines, whom the companies made their squad leaders (F009-24) [X-009].

## EV005-1 · The coordinate cell and the first folders · 3 Mar 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · Tan Son Nhut air base, Saigon; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; the Laotian panhandle and North Vietnam below the 20th parallel · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

The first thing the new bombers produced in this war was not a strike but a room. On 3 March 1964 a detachment of eleven Air Force and Army officers and twenty-six enlisted analysts opened the coordinate cell in a Quonset hut beside Harold Kessling's radar and imagery exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut, under Major Edwin T. Sorrell, a geodesist from the Aeronautical Chart and Information Center who had spent the 1950s tying the Pacific islands to a single survey [X-005]. Its task was to turn the U-2S's GPS-true imagery into coordinates a GBU-31 could fly to: a bridge abutment, a ferry slip, a fuel tank farm, the revetment of an anti-aircraft gun, each measured to within a few metres and filed with its photographs and its repair history (F005-1) [X-005]. Nothing in the American inventory of 1964 had needed coordinates of that kind before, and nobody in the theater knew how to make them; Sorrell trained his first twenty analysts himself, on imagery of Tan Son Nhut and the Saigon bridges, against points his survey teams had fixed with receivers on the ground [X-005].

The cell's first discovery was an error in every map in the country. The Army Map Service's 1:50,000 sheets of Indochina were drawn on the Indian 1960 datum, and the U-2S's coordinates were true to the satellites; at Tan Son Nhut the two differed by several hundred metres, and in the mountains of the panhandle, where the sheets themselves were drawn from French surveys of the 1930s and air photographs, the map placed some ridges and river crossings 300 metres or more from where they stood (F005-19) [M005] [X-005]. Sorrell's memorandum of 14 May 1964 to the MACV J-2 and the Seventh Air Force's predecessor, the 2nd Air Division, said in one sentence what the cell would spend a decade enforcing: "No coordinate taken from a map is to be given to a guided weapon, and no coordinate given to a guided weapon is to be plotted on a map without conversion." [X-005] The Army's artillery in the South took a second lesson from the same memorandum: by the end of 1965 the cell had given the II Field Force and the 1st Cavalry Division survey control points at every major base, and its first artillery batteries were laid on true coordinates (F005-19) [X-005].

By June 1965 the cell held about 1,400 folders: every road and rail bridge between the 20th parallel and the DMZ, the ferries on the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the passes at Mu Gia and Ban Karai, the fuel and ammunition depots around Vinh and Dong Hoi, and the first truck parks the U-2S found in the Laotian panhandle [X-005]. About a quarter to a third of the U-2S's hours went to re-imaging known targets for the folders rather than searching for new ones, which is a cost this command paid knowingly (F005-9) [M005]. On Guam the 3960th Strategic Wing received its first B-52Js with GBU-31 and GBU-38 kits in storage from the spring of 1964, and the crews trained on ranges in the Marianas against coordinates the cell sent them [X-005]. Through all of 1964 and the first half of 1965 no folder was struck. The aircraft were ready; the authority was not. That interval, sixteen months of a precision bombing force with a target list and nothing to do, is the whole of the provision's history in this phase, and its consequence is in the next.

## EV001-2 · The first titling teams · 16 Mar – 31 Dec 1964 · An Giang, Phong Dinh, Vinh Long, Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 001,CP01
*Initiative: allied*

The first 40 mobile titling teams of about 25 men each took the field on 16 March 1964, working outward from province capitals and main roads into villages the district could reinforce by day, each escorted by a Regional Forces company armed with M1 and M2 carbines and M1918A2 automatic rifles [X-001]. The first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen in An Giang on 21 April, in the Hoa Hao country where the Front was weakest, and the first in a contested district at Phung Hiep in Phong Dinh on 11 May [X-001]. By July 70 teams were in the field, the most the provinces could escort without stripping their daytime reaction forces (F001-6) [X-001]. They moved by road, in the LCM-6 and LCVP craft of the River Assault Groups and by hired sampan, and to villages beyond both by CH-21C and UH-1B helicopters when the operations staff would release them [X-001].

By 31 December 1964 titles had been issued for about 210,000 hectares to about 170,000 families [X-001], about a fifth of the target, almost all of it in hamlets held by the government at night or contested only lightly: An Giang, the riverine districts of Phong Dinh and Vinh Long along the Bassac, the inner districts of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and the coastal plain around Hoi An and Tam Ky in Quang Nam [X-001]. The rent stop held at the main-season harvest of December 1964 in most districts the government controlled; landlords' agents collected under Regional Forces escort in parts of Vinh Long, Vinh Binh and Dinh Tuong, which led to the first suspension a few months later (EV001-5) [X-001]. Twenty-three team members were killed by mines and ambush during the year [X-001]. The figure the Mission reported to Washington each month was titles issued; this headquarters reported it with the warning that it measured the government's paperwork, not the peasants' allegiance (F001-10).

## EV001-4 · The dawn musters · 7 Apr – 31 Dec 1964 · ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions and the Regional and Popular Forces of the Delta, then countrywide · NEW · 001,CP01

The Pay and Muster Verification Service's first 20 teams began unannounced dawn musters of the ARVN 21st Division's 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments around Bac Lieu and Soc Trang on 7 April 1964, and of the 7th and 9th Divisions in Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long in May; by the autumn 60 teams were mustering about 480 units a month across all four corps [X-001]. By 31 December about 41,000 names had been struck from the rolls of a force carried at about 514,000, about 8% [X-001]; the rate ran from about 4% in the Airborne and Marine battalions to about 15% in some Regional Forces companies of the Delta [X-001]. These are counts of men present against names on the roll, not estimates, and they are the best figures this headquarters had ever had for the army it advised.

Washington read them as a smaller army. Secretary McNamara, visiting in May, was briefed on the first returns and told the President that the RVNAF's real strength was below its reported strength by a margin that would grow as the musters spread (F001-9) [X-001]. In the units mustered twice, the second muster found few ghosts, because commanders had struck them before the teams came (F001-11) [X-001]. ## EV001-3 · The Can Giuoc killings · 9 Jun – 14 Jul 1964 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · NEW · 001,CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

In May 1964 titling teams from Tan An began work in the villages of Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, the rice districts of Long An between Saigon and the sea, where the survey of December 1963 had found most of the strategic hamlets destroyed and the Front's infrastructure intact [H] [X-001]. The province chief, under pressure from Saigon for a showcase close to the capital, sent the teams into hamlets the government held by day only and posted the claimants' names in the village offices, as the procedure then required (EC001-4) [X-001]. Each team worked under the escort of a Long An Regional Forces company that withdrew to its post at dusk.

On the night of 9 June armed propaganda teams of the Can Giuoc district committee, with the Long An provincial security section and screened by a company of the provincial 506th Local Force Battalion, entered three hamlets of Phuoc Lai village, read out names from the posted list, and shot four men who had claimed titles and the village land committee's secretary [X-001]. Over the following five weeks the same pattern was repeated in eleven hamlets of the two districts: by 14 July 37 title holders and 9 members of village land committees had been killed, and several hundred families had brought their titles back to the district office at Can Giuoc rather than keep them in the house [X-001]. The Regional Forces companies, posted in mud forts along Route 5 and the canal, did not move at night, and no reaction force came (POSTNA-2). The figures are this headquarters' count from district and province reports, cross-checked by the American province adviser, and are a floor [X-001].

The killings were the land programme's first lesson in what it did not change (F001-8). Teams stopped posting names in hamlets not held at night, and titles in contested hamlets were thereafter held at the district office until the hamlet was secured or the owner asked for his. A COSVN directive captured near Duc Hoa in August told district committees not to obstruct the titling, to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper", to keep collecting the Front's agricultural tax, and to make examples only where a hamlet's people began to inform (F001-10) [X-001]. In Can Giuoc the titling was suspended until the following spring. It was the first evidence that the reform's value in any district was exactly the value of the security behind it (F001-4).

## EVCP01-3 · The camps, the bases and the towns: Nam Dong, Bien Hoa and Song Be · 6 Jul 1964 – 12 May 1965 · Nam Dong CIDG camp west of Da Nang; Bien Hoa air base; Song Be and Phuoc Binh, Phuoc Long Province · HIST-ALT · CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

Three attacks are told together because they showed one thing three ways: the enemy chose the night and the target, and what held was whatever was already inside the wire.

At 02:30 on 6 July 1964 about two battalions of VC and PAVN, some 800–900 men, attacked the CIDG camp at Nam Dong near the Laotian border, held by Special Forces Detachment A-726 under Captain Roger H. C. Donlon with an Australian warrant officer, 311 Montagnard strikers, 50 Nungs and seven Vietnamese Special Forces [H4]. The outer perimeter was penetrated and many strikers deserted or went over; the inner perimeter, held by the Americans and the Nungs, survived five hours of close fighting until the attackers withdrew at dawn [H4]. Two Americans and the Australian were killed, 58 strikers and Nungs died, more than 100 strikers defected, and 55–62 attackers' bodies lay inside the wire; Donlon became the first American awarded the Medal of Honor in Vietnam [H4]. The strikers carried the weapons they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach the CIDG (EV004-1).

On the night of 31 October–1 November, two days before the American election, a VC mortar team fired on Bien Hoa air base for about thirty minutes, destroying five of the B-57Bs parked wingtip to wingtip there since the Tonkin crisis and destroying or damaging as many as 30 aircraft in all; four Americans were killed and 72 people wounded, and the attackers escaped [H2] [H3]. In this war an 81mm round burst on the revetment of the U-2S that was in maintenance that night and holed its left wing, fuel cells and forward equipment bay. It was out of service from 1 November to 20 December, and the daily deep look over the panhandle, where the dry season's first trucks were moving, lapsed to twice a week [M003] [X-003]. I asked for and got a covered shelter for each U-2S, built by the Seabees by February 1965, and a Regional Forces company for the hamlets on the base's approaches [X-003]. Johnson declined a reprisal on the eve of the election, and the question of who would guard the bases brought the first American combat troops ashore four months later [H2] [H].

On 10–11 May 1965 B-2 Front forces, the 763rd Regiment with the 840th Battalion or elements of the 271st–273rd Regiments, attacked Song Be, the capital of Phuoc Long, and the district town of Phuoc Binh [H13]. The 36th Ranger Battalion, about 150 territorials and about three dozen American advisers of Detachment B-34 held the town with the gunships of the 197th Aviation Company, and a relieving Ranger battalion arrived by midday on 11 May [H13]. The ARVN lost 58 killed, 96 wounded and 83 missing, five Americans were killed and a B-57 was lost; MACV's estimate of up to 1,000 enemy killed and wounded is of the kind A13 warns against [H13]. The Rangers had carried the M4A1 and the M249 since February 1964. I do not adjust the losses, most of which were taken in the first hour to mortars, recoilless rifles and sappers inside the wire, where the squad weapons counted for little [X-CP01].

## EV000-3 · The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution · 2–11 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,003,CP01
*Initiative: both*

On 30–31 July 1964 South Vietnamese commando boats under the covert OPLAN 34A programme, run by MACV's Studies and Observations Group, raided the North Vietnamese islands of Hon Me and Hon Ngu [H7]. On 2 August the destroyer USS *Maddox*, on a DESOTO signals intelligence patrol under Captain John J. Herrick, was attacked in daylight by three P-4 torpedo boats of the 135th Torpedo Squadron [H7]. The *Maddox* fired, four F-8 Crusaders from USS *Ticonderoga* attacked the retiring boats, and the North Vietnamese lost four killed and six wounded with three boats damaged; the *Maddox* was struck by one machine-gun round and suffered no casualties [H7]. Johnson, as historically, chose a warning rather than retaliation, and ordered the patrol resumed with USS *Turner Joy* in company [H7].

At Admiral Sharp's request I moved the Global Hawk orbit from the II Corps coast to the Gulf on 3 August (EC003-7). There were no SA-2s in the North and nothing that could reach 60,000 feet, and the aircraft took station about 40 nautical miles east of Hon Me, with the destroyers, the coast from Hon Matt to the mouth of the Song Ma and the open Gulf to the east in its radar's maritime mode [M003]. On the night of 4 August the *Maddox* and *Turner Joy*, in rough weather with thunderstorms, fired for about two hours at radar contacts they took to be a second attack [H7]. The watch in the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut that night was Major Harold Kessling's. His radar log showed the two destroyers and their manoeuvres, a cluster of fishing junks about 30 nautical miles to the west, rain cells moving across the Gulf, and no fast small craft within 40 nautical miles of the ships at any time between 2000 and 2330 local [X-003]. P-4 boats making attack runs at 40 knots are good radar targets even in a sea state of three and four, and the radar had tracked the three boats of 2 August from their base on the same set [X-003]. Herrick cabled that the "entire action leaves many doubts" [H7]. Kessling's report reached MACV at about midnight Saigon time and CINCPAC an hour later, which in Washington was the late morning of 4 August, while McNamara and Sharp were still trying to establish what had happened [X-003].

What followed was Washington's decision, and it went the other way from history. Historically Johnson had decided on retaliation within thirty minutes of the first reports [H7]. Here McNamara told him in the early afternoon that the ships' reports were contradicted by a radar that had watched the whole engagement, that Herrick himself doubted them, and that the Navy could not confirm an attack [X-003]. Johnson held the strike order and, at about 1900, cancelled it [X-003]. Operation Pierce Arrow was not flown. The 64 sorties against the torpedo-boat bases and the Vinh oil storage did not happen; Lieutenant (jg) Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Lieutenant (jg) Everett Alvarez was not shot down and taken prisoner that day [H] [X-003].

The resolution was sent anyway. Johnson had carried a draft since May, an election was three months away, and the attack of 2 August was real [H]. On 5 August he asked Congress for authority to repel armed attack against US forces and to prevent further aggression, citing the 2 August attack on a US destroyer in international waters and Hanoi's support of the war in the South, and he said nothing of the night of the 4th [X-003]. The debate ran three days longer than history's: Senator Wayne Morse, tipped about the 34A raids, asked why the destroyer had been near Hon Me at all, and Senator Gaylord Nelson's amendment against a land war was again declined by Senator Fulbright on the administration's assurance that no such war was intended [H] [X-003]. Congress passed the Southeast Asia Resolution on 10 August, 414–0 in the House and 85–3 in the Senate, in the words of the historical text [X-003]. Johnson signed it on 11 August. It was again the only legislative authority for the war that followed, but it rested on one attack that had happened rather than on two of which one had not, and the radar log of 4 August sat in the files at Tan Son Nhut and Honolulu as a record of a false alarm, not as the concealed proof of a pretext. B-57B bombers were moved to Bien Hoa in the aftermath [H3], within mortar range of the countryside around it.

## EVCP02-1 · Saigon's autumn of 1964: the Vung Tau Charter, the September coup and Hop Tac · 16 Aug 1964 – mid-1965 · Saigon, Hue and Da Nang; the Hop Tac ring of Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces · HIST-ALT · CP02
*Initiative: neither*

The autumn of 1964 is told here as one event, because the coup that failed in September is what left Hop Tac without its battalions. Khanh tried to use the Tonkin crisis to make himself president under the Vung Tau Charter of 16 August 1964; students and the Buddhist movement led by Thich Tri Quang answered with riots in Saigon, Hue and Da Nang, and the charter was repealed [H] [H10]. The Military Revolutionary Council of 26–27 August could agree on no successor, and a triumvirate of Khanh, Minh and Khiem, dominated by Khanh, was formed; Khiem was sent to Washington as ambassador and Minh on a "goodwill tour" [H10]. Before dawn on 13 September Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc, with about ten battalions, four of them up from the Delta, seized key points in Saigon while Khanh took refuge in Da Lat [H10]. US officials backed Khanh, Ky flew jets low over the city and brought loyal marines and paratroopers into Tan Son Nhut, Thi supported him, and the plotters backed down on 14 September without a death [H10]. The lasting result was the Young Turks around Ky and Thi, who had saved Khanh and knew they could unmake him [H10].

In this war the coup-makers had a programme as well as grievances. Duc had learned in August that the Commission had opened a case on the ghost payrolls the musters had found in IV Corps, and Phat's broadcast promised to "suspend the land decree pending review" and to dissolve the "foreign-controlled" Aid Compliance Board [X-001]; it also promised to "restore the authority of the corps commanders over the provinces", which meant abolishing the Selection Board [X-002]. It was the first coup to announce a policy toward the countryside, and the policy was the landlords' (F001-16). The Special Branch had reported to Cuong on 11 September that battalions of the 7th Division were being readied at My Tho without orders, and Cuong passed the report to Khanh and the US Mission that night; on 13 September the Saigon police stayed in their stations and the Field Force companies in their barracks on his order, and Ambassador Taylor told the plotters within hours that the United States would not recognise them [X-002]. The coup's failure attached the reforms to the side that won: the Young Turks took up the land decree and the Board as their own causes, and Ky spoke of the decree in his first public statements as an argument for their claim to govern (F001-15, F002-16) [X-001] [X-002].

In September, at the urging of the Mission and Westmoreland, the government launched Hop Tac, the "oil spot" applied to a ring around the capital, with the explicit priority of Saigon over everywhere else [H] [H25]. Taylor's mission reported on 6 October that the provinces rated unsatisfactory had risen from 7 in July to 14 in August, named the diversion to Hop Tac as a possible cause, and recommended unanimously that it be given full support [H25]. Its ARVN units were pulled away by every coup and coup alert, its chain of command changed with each government, and by mid-1965 it was moribund, the infrastructure of Gia Dinh, Long An and the provinces north of the capital largely intact [H].

Here Hop Tac carried the titling teams into the inner ring of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, where about 38,000 hectares were titled by mid-1965, fewer families paid the Front's collectors without coercion, and volunteered reports rose from a trickle to a few dozen a month [X-001]. Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An received province chiefs from the list between May and July 1964, and about 160 police posts were settled in the inner districts by mid-1965, with a Special Branch section in each district town [X-002]. The Special Branch broke two of the Front's village committees in Gia Dinh and the courier line from the Hoc Mon rice fields into the city's northern wards, and the hamlets around the posts were held at night through the coups even when Hop Tac's battalions were pulled away (F002-1) [X-002]. That did not save the programme, whose failure foreshadowed the war's: pacification needed a stable government and a force that stayed in the hamlets at night, and in 1964 the Republic had only the beginning of the second.

## EV000-6 · Hanoi commits the regulars; the Trail and the first air war in Laos · Sep 1964 – Apr 1965 · North Vietnam, the Laotian panhandle and the Central Highlands · HIST-ALT · 000,001,CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

The Ninth Plenum of the Lao Dong Party Central Committee, in December 1963, had resolved to seek a decisive military victory in the South before the United States could intervene in strength [H]. Through 1964 the Politburo under Le Duan acted on it. Infiltration down the Trail, managed since 1959 by the PAVN's Group 559, shifted from regrouped southerners to northern-born conscripts, and in the autumn of 1964 the first complete PAVN regular regiments were ordered south [H]. The 95th Regiment of the 325th Division arrived in Kontum Province in December 1964; the 101st Regiment followed in February 1965 with the division commander, Colonel Nguyen Huu An; the 18th Regiment came a few months later [H22]. The division was then broken up among the B3 (Central Highlands) Front and Military Region 5 [H22]. These were the first regular North Vietnamese formations committed to the war, and by the US estimate of 1965 about 4,500 PAVN troops and 300 tons of materiel moved through Laos each month [H24]. Both figures are intelligence estimates of their time; the movement was disciplined, at night, under canopy and through a country that US ground forces could not enter.

The decision to commit the regulars had been taken before the land decree existed, and it was not caused by it. The decree gave it a second argument. Party reporting from the Delta through 1964 described the "land trick" as the most dangerous of the enemy's measures, because it threatened the movement's base in exactly the provinces where it was strongest, and Le Duan's line that victory must come before the Americans and before the political base eroded drew strength from it (F001-12) [X-001]. The schedule of the 325th Division's regiments was set by their training and by the Trail, not by the reform, and the regiments came when they would have come [X-001].

The American answer in Laos began on 14 December 1964 with Operation Barrel Roll, a small programme of armed reconnaissance by US aircraft over northern and eastern Laos [H24]. On 3 April 1965 the 2nd Air Division opened Operation Steel Tiger against the Trail in the southeastern panhandle [H24]. Both were bound by the fiction of Laotian neutrality under the Geneva Accords of 1962, by the ambassador in Vientiane's control of targets, and by the bar on ground operations across the border. The Trail at this stage was still largely foot and bicycle traffic with truck routes being built; its capacity was growing faster than anything flying over it could reduce.

## EV003-2 · The trawlers turn back; the Sihanoukville route opens · Sep 1964 – Jun 1965 · Haiphong and the Gulf of Tonkin; the II Corps coast; Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Cambodia · NEW · 003,CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

Hanoi did not give up the sea at once. After TIDE TABLE's first half-year Group 125 paused through the summer of 1964, and in September, while the Global Hawk orbit was still held in the Gulf of Tonkin after the August crisis, it sent two trawlers south [X-003]. One landed its cargo at Vung Ro on the night of 21–22 September in a gap the Gulf watch had opened on the II Corps coast; the other was tracked off Binh Dinh after the orbit returned, and was intercepted on 9 November by the Vietnamese Navy escort *Chi Lang* and a Junk Force division off the Tam Quan coast, where her crew beached and burned her [X-003]. Sixteen of her crew were killed or drowned and four captured; the junk sailors lost two killed [X-003]. Those are counts, and reliable. In December a third trawler reversed course off Quang Ngai at dusk when a Seventh Fleet destroyer closed her, and went home [X-003].

The decision to change routes was taken in Hanoi in December 1964 and January 1965. Party records captured later in this war describe the Central Military Commission's conclusion that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night" and that the route was no longer worth the ships and the men [X-003]. The alternative was already prepared. Sihanouk, who had renounced American aid in November 1963 and was moving toward China, would break diplomatic relations with Washington on 3 May 1965 [H]. Through the winter Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators in Phnom Penh arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville, the port the French had built at Kompong Som, with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and Cambodian trucking contractors to carry the cargo inland along Route 4 to Kompong Speu and thence to the border base areas [H] [X-003]. Historically this arrangement took shape in late 1966 [H]. Here the first arms cargo for the Front came ashore at Sihanoukville under a Chinese flag in late June 1965, about eighteen months early, and was trucked to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak by night [X-003].

Two things followed from it that did not happen historically. The trawler that an Army helicopter pilot, Lieutenant James Bowers, found by chance unloading at Vung Ro Bay on 16 February 1965, with about 100 tons of arms aboard, was not there to be found [H] [X-003]; there was no trawler on the II Corps coast that month. And Market Time, which the Navy opened on 11 March 1965 historically because of Vung Ro [H], opened on the same date here because of TIDE TABLE's record, with a picture of what it was looking for and, increasingly, nothing to find [X-003]. The weapons the sea route would have landed in 1965 still reached the Front, more slowly and by a longer road, and from mid-1965 the enemy's principal line of supply to the southern half of the country ran through a neutral port that no American could stop or seize (F003-3).

## EV008-2 · Operation LONG SPAN: the floods of November 1964 · 6 Nov 1964 – 15 Feb 1965 · Route 1 and the coastal railway from Da Nang to Quang Ngai; Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai Provinces · NEW · 008
*Initiative: neither*

In the first week of November 1964 two typhoons and the rains behind them put the coastal plain of Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai under water, in the worst flood in central Vietnam in living memory; the dead were counted in thousands, by some Vietnamese accounts about 7,000, and Route 1 and the coastal railway were cut at dozens of points between the Hai Van pass and Quang Ngai [H]. The ARVN 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and the province garrisons of the coast were cut off from Da Nang by road, and so were the rice and the relief.

In this war the bridges came back from Bien Hoa, in an operation the Directorate called LONG SPAN. The fabrication shops held a standard 30-metre steel truss span and a 12-metre girder span, drawn in 1963 for the outpost and road programme, and the Directorate had about forty of them in stock when the water fell [X-008]. Between 14 and 30 November the shops cut and drilled about ninety more, and two Vietnamese Navy LSTs and three chartered coasters carried them from the Thu Duc yard to Da Nang and to the beach at Sa Huynh, where the 2nd Division's engineers and two Vietnamese contractor crews met them (EC008-1) [X-008]. Route 1 was open to the 2nd Division's trucks from Da Nang to Quang Ngai on 21 December, about six weeks after the flood and, by the Directorate's own estimate, two months sooner than spans ordered from Okinawa would have reopened it [X-008]. The railway took longer, as it would have in any case, and its last bridge south of Tam Ky was not back until 15 February.

The ninety-day road became the Directorate's term for its standard: any bridge the Front or the weather took on a national route was to be back in ninety days, and on Route 1 it usually was. It changed no battle in Phase I. Ba Gia was lost in May on ground the road did not decide (EV000-17). What it changed was the winter of 1964–65 on the coast: the rice of the Quang Ngai lowlands went north by truck in January instead of rotting in the district towns, the relief reached the flooded districts of Dien Ban and Duy Xuyen in December instead of February, and the Front's Region 5 committee, which had planned its winter around a coast cut off until the dry season, found the 2nd Division supplied by road [X-008]. On the historical course the flood was one of the reasons the coastal Centre was so nearly lost in the spring of 1965. Here it was a smaller one.

## EVCP01-1 · Saigon's governments and the war in the city · 19 Dec 1964 – 19 Jun 1965 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · CP01,CP02
*Initiative: both*

Six months of Saigon's politics are told here as one event, because they were one process: the generals unmaking every government they made, while the Front's city teams showed they could strike anywhere in the capital. In the week before Christmas 1964 the Young Turks, with Khanh's acquiescence, dissolved the High National Council and arrested several of its members; Taylor dressed the officers down, Khanh attacked him publicly, and the Mission's relations with the junta reached their lowest point [H10]. At 17:45 on 24 December a car bomb of about 90 kg under the Brink Bachelor Officers' Quarters killed two Americans and wounded about 60–100 people; Johnson refused a reprisal, saying he had "never felt that this war will be won from the air", and changed his mind within six weeks [H20].

On 27 January 1965 Khanh and the Armed Forces Council removed Huong. On 16 February the council appointed Dr Phan Huy Quat prime minister, and on 19–20 February, in the confusion of a failed coup by General Lam Van Phat and Colonel Pham Ngoc Thao, who was a communist agent, it voted Khanh out; he left the country on 25 February [H23] [H10] [H]. On 30 March a car carrying about 300 pounds of plastic explosive blew up outside the US embassy; 22 people were killed, among them two Americans, and 183 wounded [H12]. Rolling Thunder was already running, and there was no reprisal [H12]. On 12 June Quat, deadlocked with the chief of state Phan Khac Suu, handed power back to the generals; on 14 June Major General Nguyen Van Thieu became chief of state, and on 19 June Air Vice Marshal Nguyen Cao Ky prime minister [H23] [H].

In this war the sequence ran on its historical dates and for its historical reasons. One thing in it was different: each government that inherited the decrees of 1 January 1964 kept them, because Johnson's condition of February 1964 and the Aid Compliance Board had made repeal dearer than survival, and the September plotters' promise to repeal them had attached the reforms to the Young Turks who defeated them (EV000-1, EVCP02-1) [X-001].

## EV000-9 · The battle of Binh Gia · 28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01,010
*Initiative: enemy*

Binh Gia, a Catholic village of northern refugees in Phuoc Tuy Province east of Saigon, was the site of the Viet Cong's first large set-piece battle [H1]. The attackers were the 271st and 272nd Regiments (then called the 1st and 2nd), the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, with the 80th Artillery Detachment and local battalions of the B-2 Front, an estimated 1,800 men [H1]. They took the village on 28 December and then waited for the relief. The ARVN committed the 30th, 33rd, 35th and 38th Ranger Battalions and the 4th Marine Battalion of the general reserve, with two artillery platoons and a section of M24 tanks, about 4,300 men [H1], lifted and supported by the US 118th Aviation Company and the gunships of the 68th Assault Helicopter Company [H1]. Every one of those battalions had carried the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 since the spring (EV004-1); the attackers carried Type 56 and SKS rifles, RPD guns and recoilless rifles [X-004].

The relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal, the artillery badly positioned and air support thin, as they were historically [H1]. The rifles did not change the generals' plan, and the plan was the problem. On 30 December a 68th Company gunship was shot down near Quang Giao and its four crewmen killed [H1]. On 31 December the 4th Marine Battalion, going to recover the bodies, was ambushed in the rubber by the 271st at a range of 20 to 40 metres, as it was historically, and its commander, Major Nguyen Van Nho, was killed in the first minutes [H1]. What followed was different. The battalion's squad guns, three to a company platoon, put a base of fire into the trees that the Marines' BARs could not have sustained, and the companies, instead of being cut apart in the order they had marched, closed into two perimeters within about a quarter of an hour and fought out of the rubber at dusk carrying most of their wounded (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 60–70 killed and 90 wounded, against 122 killed and 71 wounded on the historical course [X-004]. Captain Donald Cook, the Marine adviser, was wounded and captured going back for a wounded Marine, as he was historically, and died in captivity [H1]. MACV counted allied losses from 28 December to 6 January at about 140 killed, 220 wounded and 45 missing [E: the historical 201 killed, 192 wounded and 68 missing, adjusted for the 4th Marines' smaller loss and the larger number of wounded who came out alive]. MACV reported four helicopters shot down [H1]. The VC claim of 1,783 killed and captured is no more credible here than it was historically; their own dead, of whom at least 32 were confirmed historically, I put at about 120–200, because the Marines' marksmen and squad guns killed men in the rubber whom the 271st could not carry away [E: the 4th Marines' after-action count of about 90 bodies left on the ground, and the usual ratio of dead removed to dead left].

The significance was still the form. A Viet Cong formation had held ground for four days against armour, artillery and helicopters, chosen the field, ambushed the general reserve on the road it had to use, and withdrawn intact [H1]. The 271st took the honorific "Binh Gia Regiment" [H1]. US observers concluded, as they did historically, that the ARVN could not defeat main-force units of this kind without American firepower; what they could not say, as they said historically, was that a Marine battalion of the general reserve had been destroyed. The 4th Marines were back in the line in February.

The sergeants did the rest. The 4th Marine Battalion's NCOs had all passed the courses for their grades at Nha Trang since 1957, and its platoon sergeants the senior course; when Major Nho fell, two of the four company commanders were hit in the same minutes, and the companies were taken over by their first sergeants, who had been taught in the leader's course to count their men, establish a base of fire and report before they moved (F010-1) [X-010]. Master Sergeant Nguyen Van Thuoc of the 2nd Company put his three squad guns on the trees and moved his platoons back by bounds; the companies closed into their two perimeters about five minutes faster than they would have done, and the second perimeter held the 271st's assault at 1640 without falling back into the first [X-010]. I put the battalion's loss at about 50–60 killed and 100 wounded, against the 60–70 killed and 90 wounded of the earlier course, and MACV's allied losses for the battle at about 130 killed rather than about 140; the difference is wounded men who came out because a sergeant counted them [X-010]. The battle's form, which the 271st chose, did not change (F010-8).

## EVCP01-2 · The American escalation: Flaming Dart, Rolling Thunder, the Marines and the July decision · 7 Feb – 28 Jul 1965 · Pleiku and Qui Nhon; North Vietnam; Da Nang; Washington · HIST-ALT · CP01,CP02
*Initiative: both*

The six months in which the United States went from an advisory war to a war of its own are told here as one event, because each step made the next one. At 01:50 on 7 February 1965, while McGeorge Bundy was in Saigon and Kosygin in Hanoi, about 300 men of the 30th Company of the VC 409th Sapper Battalion attacked Camp Holloway and the advisers' compound at Pleiku, killing seven Americans and wounding 104 and destroying 10 aircraft [H5]. About twelve hours later 49 aircraft from the *Coral Sea* and the *Hancock* struck the barracks at Dong Hoi, the first strike of Flaming Dart; after the VC blew up the enlisted billet at Qui Nhon on 10 February and killed 23 Americans, about 160 aircraft struck Chanh Hoa and Chap Le [H5]. Bundy's report of 7 February argued for "sustained reprisal", and the administration accepted it [H].

On 2 March Rolling Thunder opened with 104 USAF aircraft against the ammunition depot near Xom Bang while 19 VNAF A-1s struck the naval base at Quang Khe [H9]. It was graduated by design: targets chosen at the President's Tuesday meetings, no strikes within 30 nautical miles of Hanoi, 10 of Haiphong or 30 of the Chinese frontier, and the North divided into route packages [H9]. Through 24 December 1965 the two services flew 54,139 sorties and dropped 43,207 tons for 180 aircraft lost [H9] [D].

On 8 March Battalion Landing Team 3/9 came ashore at Da Nang and BLT 1/3 flew in from Okinawa, the nearly 5,000 men of the 9th Marine Expeditionary Brigade under Brigadier General Frederick J. Karch, to guard the air base that Rolling Thunder needed and the Bien Hoa attack had shown to be vulnerable [H15] [H14]. Their orders, that they "will not, repeat will not, engage in day-to-day actions against the Viet Cong", lasted three weeks: on 1 April the President approved their use in active combat and 18,000–20,000 more support troops, and on 6 May the brigade became the III Marine Amphibious Force [H15] [H14].

On 7 June Westmoreland asked for a major reinforcement and by 22 July the President had authorized 44 battalions [H11]. On 28 July, at a midday press conference rather than before Congress, Johnson announced that US strength would rise at once from 75,000 to 125,000, with more as requested, that monthly draft calls would rise from 17,000 to 35,000, and that the Airmobile Division would deploy; the reserves were not called and no new taxes were sought [H21] [H]. Westmoreland's concept behind it, to halt the losing trend in 1965, take the offensive in 1966 and then destroy the enemy's main forces and base areas, committed the United States to attrition against an enemy who controlled his losses by deciding when to fight.

In this war every step came on its historical date for its historical reason. The provisions changed what Washington knew while it decided (EV000-3, EVCP02-2), and nothing that it decided [X-CP01].

## EV006-2 · Operation STILL WATER · 8 Mar – 30 Jun 1965 · Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province, inside the Hop Tac ring northwest of Saigon · NEW · 006,CP02
*Initiative: allied*

STILL WATER was the first operation in the war aimed at a district committee rather than at the hamlets beneath it, and it was the case for the method. By February 1965 Hoc Mon had 11 village posts settled under the police decree, a Popular Forces platoon in each, about 70 percent of its families on the census, and a district chief from the first list, Major Pham Van Truc; the Front's hamlet cells in the posted villages had mostly been arrested or had left (EVCP02-1, EV002-2) [X-006]. What remained was the Hoc Mon district committee of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region, about 14 members with a security section, a finance section and about 40 couriers, which had moved in January into the hamlets along the Saigon River at the district's northern edge, toward the Ben Cat and Cu Chi base areas, and governed the posted villages from there by messenger [X-006]. The DIOCC at Hoc Mon, opened in July 1964 under Captain Ngo Van Tam of the Special Branch with 12 staff and two American advisers, had by February a chart of that committee with 31 names, 19 of them confirmed on three sources, built from 44 arrests, 23 ralliers and a cache of finance-section receipts found by a PF patrol in December [X-006].

The operation was Tam's plan, approved by Truc and by the Gia Dinh province chief on 1 March. It used no regular troops. Two Police Field Force companies and the Gia Dinh PRU of about 80 men made the arrests; the Regional Forces companies of Hoc Mon and Go Vap, with M4A1 carbines and their M249s, held the cordons at night; a reaction platoon of the Gia Dinh sector and two UH-1Bs of the 120th Aviation Company stood by at Tan Son Nhut for the reaction; and every arrest went first to the biometric kit and then to a magistrate within 72 hours [X-006]. Captain Whitcomb's counterpart in the next province was not involved; the American subsector adviser at Hoc Mon, Captain Luis Arredondo, was, and by the rule of the decree he did not go on the raids (EC006-13 abandoned) [X-006].

The turn came on the night of 26–27 March. A finance-section courier arrested on 19 March, a woman of 19 who carried tax receipts in the hem of a conical hat, rallied on the 24th after the Chieu Hoi officer offered her brother's release from a prison on Con Son, to which he had been sent in 1962 under Diem without trial, and named the house in Tan Thoi Nhi where the committee's security section met twice a month [X-006]. The PRU went in at 0300 under a moon nearly full with two Police Field Force platoons in the cordon. Four men were taken alive in the house, including the section's deputy chief, with a steel box of informant reports on the posts of four villages; a fifth was killed in the paddy behind it after firing on the cordon with a K-50 sub-machine gun; one PRU member died [X-006]. The box named nine of the committee's own informants inside the posted villages, three of them PF soldiers [X-006].

By 30 June the operation had arrested 63 people on warrant, of whom the magistrates released 17; 9 of the committee's 14 members were dead, in prison or had rallied, 2 of them killed resisting arrest; the remaining five had moved north into Cu Chi [X-006]. The Commission's first audit sample, drawn in July from STILL WATER's files, found 4 of the 63 arrests unsupported and the release of one man already paid for, and sent both cases to the Special Court [X-006]. The Front did not rebuild the Hoc Mon committee inside the district; it governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi until 1968, and the tax receipts captured in 1966 showed collections in the district's posted villages at about a fifth of 1964's [X-006]. In this war STILL WATER was a small operation, 63 arrests and three deaths in four months, and it changed the war in one particular: it showed the Front that the government could follow its leaders out of the villages it had cleared, three years before the historical Phoenix tried to (F006-26).

## EVCP02-2 · The air war of 1965: the Thanh Hoa Bridge, the missiles and the first Arc Light · 3 Apr – 27 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong) and the Hanoi region, North Vietnam; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · HIST-ALT · CP02
*Initiative: allied*

The four months in which the air war found its limits are told here as one event, because each refusal set the pattern for the next. On 3 April 1965 a 79-aircraft package led by Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner, 46 of them F-105s with Bullpup missiles and 750 lb bombs, struck the Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa; it was hit repeatedly and stood, and an F-100 and an RF-101 were lost [H16]. On 4 April MiG-17s of the VPAF 921st Fighter Regiment shot down two F-105s, killing Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson, and a third was lost with Captain Carlyle Harris captured; the VPAF lost three pilots, by means still disputed [H16]. The bridge stood until laser-guided bombs dropped its span in 1972 [H16]. In this war the U-2S's dawn pass on 5 April showed CINCPAC what the RF-101s had died trying to see: both spans standing and rail traffic moving within the day [X-003].

The folder for Ham Rong had been complete since the previous August, abutments and pier measured to within a few metres (EV005-1) [X-005]. On 6 April Admiral Sharp forwarded a request drafted in Saigon for four B-52Js from Guam to release forty GBU-31(V)3/B penetrators from 40,000 feet about twelve miles out, beyond the guns and the MiGs, with the bridge down in one night and no aircraft lost (F005-12) [X-005]. It was refused on 13 April, for the reasons that governed every B-52 decision of 1965: the bombers were a strategic force whose use over the North would be read in Moscow and Peking as escalation, Rolling Thunder's pace was set in the White House, and fighter-bombers could strike a bridge [H] [X-005]. A second request, for the Dragon's Jaw alone, was refused in June [X-005].

The missiles were seen coming. The U-2S's change detection showed the six-pointed pattern of an SA-2 site southeast of Hanoi on 22 March, its signals payload heard the first Fan Song emissions on 18 April, and by 24 July it had mapped eleven prepared sites and judged four occupied on any day [X-003]. McNamara declined the Joint Chiefs' request to strike them, as he had historically, for fear of killing Soviet technicians, and here refused with it the proposal to strike every measured revetment from 40,000 feet with GBU-31s [H] [X-003] [X-005]. On 24 July an SA-2 shot down an F-4C of the 47th Tactical Fighter Squadron, killing Captain Roscoe Fobair and putting Captain Richard Keirn in captivity [H9] [H]; the flight had been warned that a site was occupied, and the missile came from a battery that had moved overnight [X-003]. Historically the retaliation of 27 July, Spring High, sent F-105s against two sites, at least one empty, and lost aircraft for nothing [H]. Here the pass of 26 July showed bamboo launchers at the eastern site, the strike was re-targeted on the occupied one, destroyed two launchers and the Fan Song van, and lost two F-105Ds, one pilot killed and one recovered [X-003].

The first Arc Light, on 18 June, was flown by thirty B-52Js of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings from Andersen in place of the historical B-52Fs, to the South and back without tankers, so the collision over the sea that killed eight crewmen historically did not happen (F005-11) [H18] [X-005]. The Soviet trawler off Guam still gave Hanoi about three hours' notice; the tanker track that had betrayed the bombers' heading was gone [M005] [X-005]. Twenty-eight aircraft struck a suspected base area near Ben Cat, about 40 miles north of Saigon, with about 51 bombs each [H18] [M005]; two carried twenty GBU-31s each onto four structures the U-2S had imaged, and found four huts empty for days [X-005]. The rest found what history found, little, and the press called it a sledgehammer swung at flies, now accurate to a few metres [H] [X-005]. It was the find-and-fix problem in its purest form, and the J changed none of it (F005-7) [X-005]. Arc Light would run to August 1973 and fly about 126,615 sorties [H17]; here with 10–20 percent fewer maintenance men, no tanker bridge, and a carpet half as dense when it carried unguided bombs (F005-8, F005-10) [M005] [X-005]. By the end of July the North's air defence was an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles; every part of it was on a map in Saigon before it fired, and a weapon able to strike every part of it in any weather sat on Guam waiting for a decision Washington would not take for seven years (F005-13) [X-005].

## EV000-17 · The battle of Ba Gia · 28–31 May 1965 · Ba Gia, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01,007
*Initiative: enemy*

On 29 May 1965 the VC 1st Regiment of the forming 2nd Division (40th, 60th and 90th Battalions, with the 45th Independent Battalion and local force battalions), directed by PAVN Major General Chu Huy Man, overran the outpost at Loc Tho near Ba Gia, about 10 km from Quang Ngai, and ambushed the ARVN 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry Regiment as it came to help [H11]. On the historical course the battalion was destroyed. On this one it was cut in two on the road from Ba Gia and lost its headquarters company and most of one rifle company in the first half hour; the other three companies, each with a squad gun in every squad and a marksman in most, formed a perimeter on the low ridge north of the road and held it until dark, and about 280 of the battalion's men came back to Quang Ngai over the next two days [X-004]. Major General Nguyen Chanh Thi, commanding I Corps, sent a task force under the 51st Regiment: its 2nd Battalion, the 3rd Marine Battalion, the 37th and 39th Ranger Battalions, two 105mm artillery battalions and an M113 squadron [H11]. On 30 May the 39th Rangers fought the VC 45th Battalion on Mount Chop Non, and the 2nd Battalion, 51st and the Marines were hit near Phuoc Loc; US fighter-bombers and UH-1 gunships, delayed because forward air controllers could not find the fight, prevented a rout [H11]. On the night of 30–31 May the 3rd Marines held a moat line through repeated assaults, with nine M249s across the line firing under illumination from the 105s, and their marksmen picking off the 40th Battalion's machine-gun teams setting up in the paddy dikes beyond the moat at 300–400 metres (EC004-6) [X-004]. The 39th Rangers were reduced to about two companies, against one historically [X-004]. The VC withdrew at dawn on 31 May.

I put ARVN losses at about 75 killed, 110 wounded and 190 captured or missing, against the US Army's historical figures of 107 killed, 123 wounded and 367 captured or missing; the individual weapons lost, 384 historically, I put at about 230, most of them M4A1 carbines with their sights [E: the historical return less the 1st Battalion's survivors and the smaller Ranger loss]. About 150 VC bodies were found, against 84 historically, and MACV's estimate of up to 826 VC casualties is no more reliable than it was [X-004]. Ba Gia was still a defeat. The VC had overrun the outpost, broken a battalion and held the field, and by 3 June they controlled much of northern Quang Ngai, because the ARVN pulled its battered task force back to the province capital [H11]. On 5 July they overran Ba Gia again, and the ARVN abandoned the post [H11]. In Washington it was read as evidence that the ARVN was being worn down faster than it could be rebuilt, not, as historically, as proof that a regimental task force could be wiped out; the distinction mattered to the 51st Regiment and not to the July decision. The 230 carbines went to the 2nd Division's local battalions, who were firing 5.56mm taken from the dead within the month (F004-7) [X-004].

The officer reforms changed Ba Gia at its edges and left its centre alone. The 1st Battalion, 51st Regiment was commanded by a major whom the corps had appointed in 1963, and its companies by lieutenants and captains of the classes of 1959–62; it was those company commanders, with the battalion's headquarters gone in the first half hour, who brought three companies onto the ridge north of the road, set the squad guns and called the battalion's two mortars onto the paddy below them [X-007]. Part of what memorandum 004 credited to the squad gun at Ba Gia belongs to the officers who placed it, and I do not count it twice: the losses above stand [X-007]. The 2nd Battalion, 51st had been given in March to Major Le Van Tri, one of the board's first selections, and on 30 May it moved to Phuoc Loc with a company on the high ground on each side of the road, which is why it was hit in the flank rather than in column [X-007]. The decisions that lost Ba Gia were taken above both battalions: the corps sent the task force forward piecemeal into ground the VC 1st Regiment had chosen, and pulled it back to Quang Ngai afterwards, and none of the officers who made those decisions had been through a course or before a board (F007-7).

## EV000-18 · The battle of Dong Xoai · 9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01,007,009,010
*Initiative: enemy*

Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation [H6]. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers [H6]. The CIDG companies and the militia carried the carbines and Garands they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach them; the 111th RF Company and the Americans carried M4A1s (F004-1) [X-004]. The camp was largely overrun during the night, as it was historically, from the CIDG sectors inward, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters, where the 111th Company had fallen back with its carbines, until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out [H6] [X-004]. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overrun, losing about half its men against nearly all of them historically [X-004]; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it in the rubber [H6].

The Airborne fight on 12 June is the clearest measure in this war of what the squad weapons could and could not do. Historically about 150 of the 470 came back. On this course the battalion's lead company was lost in the opening fire as it was historically, but the three companies behind it each turned their squad guns into the trees and held, and about 270 men fought their way to the landing zone at Thuan Loi over the afternoon, bringing about 60 wounded [X-004]. The 271st had chosen the place, the hour and the range, and it won; the Airborne battalion was broken, though not wiped out [X-004]. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had [H6].

I put the allied losses at about 300 South Vietnamese killed, 210 wounded and 150 missing, and 18 Americans killed as historically, against 416 killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing on the historical course; about 300 civilians were killed and 2,500 made refugees, as historically [X-004] [H6]. More than 300 VC bodies were found historically; I put the figure here at about 400, with the same scepticism about the allied estimate of 625 more [X-004]. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured [H6]. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact [H6]. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had about two reserve battalions left, against one historically [X-004]. That was not enough to change Westmoreland's request of 7 June, which rested on the PAVN regiments entering the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and it did not change the answer.

At Dong Xoai the board had not yet reached the 5th Division. The 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment was landed at Thuan Loi on 10 June on a division plan that put it on an open landing zone beside the rubber where the 271st was known to be waiting, under a battalion commander appointed by the division in 1962 [X-007]. The board reviewed the landing in July on its own motion, under the thirty-day rule, and in August relieved the battalion commander and the division operations officer who had written the plan, the first reliefs for cause in the ARVN's history that were not punishments for political disloyalty (EV007-2) [X-007]. The 5th Division's commander was untouched, and remained so, because he had been appointed before the ranked list existed. The night at the camp and the 7th Airborne's fight on 12 June went as told above [H6].

The basic course changed Dong Xoai at its edges and not at its centre. The CIDG companies, which the course did not reach, broke first, as they had historically and in this war before it (F009-6). The 111th RF Company, which had taken the territorial course as a company in 1961, fell back to the district headquarters in order and carried its wounded with it; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment, whose privates had all been through Quang Trung, was still landed on the plan its division wrote, on the open zone beside the rubber, and still lost about half its men, because no private's training reaches a landing zone chosen for him [X-009]. The difference was in the evening of 12 June, when the 7th Airborne's survivors came into Thuan Loi: of about 60 wounded they brought out, about 50 were alive the next morning, with tourniquets put on in the rubber by men who had never been taught first aid on the historical course and were taught it in basic here [X-009]. I put the allied killed at about 270 rather than about 300, a judgment on the wounded who lived and the companies that held, and I do not adjust the enemy's losses [X-009].

The sergeants changed Dong Xoai at the same edges. The 111th RF Company had passed the territorial courses for every NCO grade, and its platoon sergeants, not its officers, took it back to the district headquarters in order, a platoon at a time, while the CIDG companies, whose leaders the grant did not reach, broke around it (F010-6) [X-010]. On 12 June the 7th Airborne's three surviving companies were held together, after their captains were hit, by first sergeants and acting squad leaders who had all passed the leader's course, and about 290 men reached Thuan Loi rather than about 270 [X-010]. The 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment, was still landed on the open zone beside the rubber, on the division's plan, and no sergeant could change that (F010-8). I put the allied killed at about 255 rather than about 270 [X-010].

## How the phase ends
By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though none of the general reserve has been wiped out. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and outside the held belt the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security it needs. Inside it the summer is not the historical one: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's [X-001]. Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

About 1,100 village posts are settled, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure has begun to leave the villages around the posts (EV002-2) [X-002]. The sea route to the coast is closed and the Cambodian route open, and the enemy's rear is on the U-2S's maps while his regiments under the canopy and his cadres in the hamlets are as hidden as before. The general reserve enters Phase II weakened but whole, and the Front enters it with between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines (EV004-1) [X-004]. On Guam the B-52Js hold about 1,400 measured targets in the cell's folders and authority to strike none of them in the North (EV005-1, EV000-15).

Phung Hoang ends the phase with about 140 district centres open, every one behind a settled post, and a method that has reached above the hamlet in one district on the edge of Saigon and in a dozen in the Delta (EV006-1, EV006-2) [X-006]. About 1,100 selected advisers are in the subsectors and battalions, speaking to their counterparts without interpreters; the rest of the field posts are still held on the old tour. The Front has begun to move its district committees out of the held belt, which costs it the villages' daily obedience and gains it the safety of the contested edge (F006-4).

By the end of July 1965 the board has filled about 70 of the army's roughly 190 manoeuvre battalion commands and relieved its first nine commanders; the rest are still held by men the generals put there, and about a third of all battalions are still commanded by captains, because the army is short of majors and the career-course gate makes none faster (EV007-1, F007-8) [X-007]. The companies are led almost everywhere by the classes of 1957–63. The generals are the generals of 1963, and every one of them has learned that the board can be bargained with and cannot be ignored.

The Directorate ends the phase with its plants at about half their capacity and the domestic share four months old; about 5,000 PF radios, 6,000 hamlet radios and 600,000 dry cells have gone to the held belt, and the Front's mines in Long An have Go Vap batteries in them (EV008-1, EV002-2) [X-008]. Route 1 on the coast has been reopened after the worst flood in memory, and the Front has an industrial working class in Bien Hoa to organise (EV008-2).

The army that enters Phase II has trained every private it has enlisted for eight years, and every territorial; it has kept about 2,000–3,000 of its best sergeants at the training centres to do it, in the two years it could least spare them, and the Muster Service has caught the centres that sold the course short (EV009-1) [X-009]. The general reserve was broken at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and came back from both with more of its men than on any course before this one; the battalions are steadier than history's by a band, though the enemy still chooses where they fight [X-009].

The NCO corps that enters Phase II has been schooled for its grades for eight years, regular and territorial alike, and about 1,300–2,500 of its best sergeants are teaching at Nha Trang, the division schools and the territorial centres, on top of the drill sergeants at Quang Trung (EV010-1, F010-11) [X-010]. The 4th Marine Battalion at Binh Gia and the 7th Airborne at Dong Xoai were broken and came back with more of their men than on either earlier course, because their sergeants took command when their officers fell (EV000-9, EV000-18). The enemy has the academy's leader's manual and has begun to teach its local forces the ARVN platoon's routine (F010-20).
