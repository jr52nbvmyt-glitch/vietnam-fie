# US and ARVN small arms and individual weapons against the enemy's and against history — the war as it stands after memorandum 015

## Summary — the state of the area now

The Vietnam War was fought, at its bottom, by men carrying a rifle, a few grenades and whatever their squad could share: a machine gun, a grenade launcher, a rocket, a mine. This study first sets out what those men carried, on both sides and in every kind of force, and what each weapon could and could not do. It then follows the weapons through the six phases of this war as the record has it.

History's soldiers did not fight evenly armed. In January 1964 the Republic's regular infantry carried the American weapons of the Second World War and Korea: the eight-round M1 Garand, the light M1 and M2 carbines, the Browning Automatic Rifle (BAR) and the M1919 machine gun [M004] [M010]. Its Regional and Popular Forces carried the same weapons or older, and its CIDG strikers, police and hamlet militia carried what was left [S4] [S12]. Against them the Front was part-way through a rearmament with the Chinese and Soviet 7.62x39mm family: the AK-47 and its Chinese copy the Type 56, the SKS carbine, the RPD light machine gun, and the B40 rocket launcher [S1] [S3]. Historically the ARVN's infantry did not draw the M16, the M60 machine gun, the M79 grenade launcher and the M72 LAW until 1968, and the territorials until 1969 [S1]. The American soldier was better armed than either, but his M16 jammed in 1966–67 until a congressional inquiry forced the fixes of the M16A1 [S2].

This war changed three weapons and left the rest of the soldier's load alone. From 6 January 1964 every rifle and carbine in American and RVNAF hands, including those of the Regional and Popular Forces, was replaced by the M4A1 carbine with a red-dot sight. Every American and ARVN regular infantry squad also gained a belt-fed M249 light machine gun in place of its automatic rifle, and a marksman with a scoped M110A2 rifle (P004a, P004b, P004c) [M004]. The territorials kept the BAR as their automatic weapon (E04). The pistol, the grenade, the M60, the M79, the LAW, the Claymore, the shotgun, the mortar and the mine were history's, on both sides, throughout. So were the weapons of everyone the provision did not cover: the CIDG, the police, the Combat Youth and the People's Self-Defense Force, who inherited the Garands and carbines the army handed in. The enemy's weapons were history's too, with one addition: the 10,000–30,000 of the Republic's new carbines he had taken by the end of 1965 (F004-7) [X-004].

The record's verdict, stated in memorandum 004 and never reversed, is that the small arms changed how the fights the enemy accepted went, and never which fights he accepted (POS004-1). The margin over history was large for the territorials, who were not outgunned in the five years in which history's countryside was lost. It was large for the ARVN's regular infantry until history's army caught up in 1968, and modest for the Americans, whose own historical rifle was fixable and was fixed. The record puts the lives saved at about 1,000–2,500 American and 10,000–25,000 South Vietnamese over the war (F004-13) [X-004]. Those are judgments built on rates, not counts.

The enemy answered the rifle within a season. He stopped assaulting rearmed posts and attacked those the rearmament did not reach: the constables, the CIDG camps and the militia with the handed-down Garands. He moved his war from the rifle assault to the mortar, the sapper and the mine (F004-1, F004-2). From 1969 history's army carried the same class of rifle, and the remaining margin was the squad gun, the marksman and practice (POS004-2). In 1974 the margin became a cost, when an army built around the belt-fed gun met the ammunition ration (F004-15). The end state, rung (b), a reprieve of about a year, was decided far above the squad.

## The weapons: what the soldiers carried and what each could do

### How the fight was fought

Most of the war's small-arms fighting happened at very short range. In this country engagements were fought at about 10–30 metres and almost never beyond 100 metres [M004·S8]. That fact governs everything below. A rifle's effective range on paper mattered less than how fast a man could get his first aimed shots off, how many rounds he could fire before he reloaded, and whether his weapon worked after a night in the rain. The exceptions were the open country of the Delta paddies and the coastal plain, the hills around the firebases, and the perimeters of posts under flare light, where a man could be seen and shot at 300–800 metres [M004].

The second thing to know is that most killing was not done with rifles. Artillery, mortars, mines, booby traps and air power killed more men on both sides than small arms did, and the enemy's mines and booby traps were the commonest killer of American and South Vietnamese soldiers in some districts and years (EV009-2). The rifle decided the first seconds of a contact. It did not decide whether a contact happened.

### Marksmen and snipers

History's American sniper worked in a two-man team at battalion level with an M21 (a scoped M14) in the Army or a Remington M40 in the Marines; there were a few hundred of them [M004] [S4]. This war's squad marksman is a different thing. One man in every American and ARVN regular squad carried the M110A2, a suppressed 7.62mm semi-automatic rifle with a 1–6x scope. He was trained for two to four weeks and expected to hit a man at 600–800 metres, about ten to twenty times as many marksmen as history had snipers [M004]. The Regional and Popular Forces never received the marksman's rifle [M004]. The enemy had snipers of his own with scoped bolt-action rifles [M004].

### A note on the scenes

Each phase below carries one or two scenes. Each is told from one person's eyes, at one place and moment that the record holds. Where the record names the person, the scene uses that name; where it holds the moment but not the name, the study gives the person a name and marks it † at its first use. The scenes add no event to the war. A sentence or two after each says what it shows.

## P1 · Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The rearmament arrived on the campaign's first working day. MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez* berthed at the Khanh Hoi wharves in Saigon on 6 January 1964 with the first carbines, sights, squad guns and marksman's rifles (EV004-1) [X-004]. MACV called the issue Operation IRON GATE for its first rule: no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate in exchange. Its second rule was the zeroing range. Every company fired its carbines at 25 metres until the dot sat on the point of aim, on a five-day course for an infantry company and three days for a Popular Forces platoon, which Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn refused to shorten (EV004-1) [X-004]. The Delta divisions and the territorials around Saigon drew first, the general reserve next, and I and II Corps from April. Khanh's coup of 30 January stopped the issue for nine days (F004-5).

The handed-down third mattered more than anyone thought in 1964. It drew the line along which the enemy would fight the territorial war: on one side the posts with modern carbines, on the other the constables with the carbines of 1950 and the militia with Garands (EV002-2). The recruits who carried the new weapons had met the carbine and the squad gun in the fourth week of their basic course since 1957 (EV009-1) [M009].

The enemy of 1964 was itself mid-rearmament. His main-force regiments were taking the 7.62x39mm family, and in this war some of it came by sea. The trawler run ashore at Vung Ro on 19 February 1964 had Type 56 assault rifles and carbines, B40 launchers, 82mm rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm in its after hold (EV003-1) [X-003]. The Front's local forces and guerrillas still carried a mixture of French rifles, captured carbines, home-made weapons and the SKS [S3]. The Long An committee called the government's new carbines "a trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty" and ordered its companies to capture one for study (EV004-1).

### A scene: the first carbine, Rach Kien, 22 March 1964

Le Van Ut† had carried the same rifle for three years, a French bolt-action gun with a stock his father had rewired, and he knew it the way he knew his own feet. Five rounds, then the bolt, then five more if he had them. On the morning of the ambush he had nine.

The district company had lain since before light in the cane along the Rach Kien road, and the order from the committee was specific: kill if they must, but bring back one of the new rifles. When the government squad came along the road in the open, the company's machine gun opened from the far end of the cane, and Ut fired his five and worked the bolt and fired again, and the soldiers on the road did not go down the way soldiers usually went down. They went off the road into the ditch and fired back at once, short bursts, and they kept firing while he was still reloading. He heard the difference before he understood it: the government fire did not stop to reload.

It was over in a few minutes. The squad pulled back along the ditch toward the post, and one man stayed on the road. Ut reached him first. The rifle under the dead soldier was short and black and lighter than his own, and there was a little window on top of it. When he lifted it to his eye in the bright morning there was a red dot floating in the glass, sitting on whatever he looked at. The curved magazine held more rounds than he had fired all year, and the cartridges in it were smaller than his.

He carried it back to the company commander wrapped in a rice sack, as he had been told. The commander turned it over for a long time and then said what Ut had already worked out on the road: they would need these men's bullets to use it.

The scene shows the gap the rearmament opened: a guerrilla with a five-shot bolt-action rifle against a squad that kept firing without a pause. It also shows the first cost: every carbine the Front took came with a sight that ran for years on a battery and a need for the Republic's own ammunition (F004-7, F004-11).

### At the post wall

At the post wall the change was decisive in the first year. Of the posts in the held belt that had their PF platoon and a reaction force within an hour, the record puts about nine in ten holding with the rifles against about four in five on the old weapons. With the radio and the territorial course behind the rifles, it puts the figure at about nineteen in twenty, the combined judgment that revision R05 set in place of the separate estimates (EV002-2) [X-009]. The outposts kept the BAR as their automatic weapon: the squad gun and the marksman stayed with the regulars (EV004-1, E04).

### A scene: Long Huu, 17 November 1964

Corporal Pham Van Tho had made his men fire the new carbines until the dots sat where they looked, in March, on the range at the edge of the paddy, and he had made them do it again in July when two of them had knocked their sights against the post wall. At the Ben Tre centre the instructors had taught him three things he repeated until the platoon was sick of them: fire in pairs, one man shooting while the other changes magazines; never both empty at once; and keep a third of the ammunition for the second rush, because there is always a second rush.

At 0140 he heard the wire. The flare showed men on the bund, a lot of them, coming up out of the paddy in a line with the water shining on their legs. His BAR gunner on the corner opened first, the heavy steady thud of the old Browning, and then the carbines along the wall, and Tho watched the pairs do what he had made them do. One man fired; the other knelt, dropped his magazine, slapped in a fresh one, and came up firing as his partner went down to reload. The fire along the wall never stopped. In the flare light the line on the bund broke up into men lying down and men going back.

The radio operator was already calling. Tho heard him give the post's name and the words for an attack, and heard the district answer. He counted magazines along the wall in the dark by touch. When the second rush came, smaller and slower, there were still enough to meet it, and it did not reach the bund. Two of his men were hit on the wall, and the ones beside them did what the course had taught and put the tourniquets on high and tight. The boats came up the Ham Luong at 0320. Both wounded men were alive.

The scene shows the outpost's margin in this war: a rifle with thirty rounds and a fast sight, fired in pairs by men trained to keep ammunition for the second assault, beside a radio that brought help. The record credits the carbine, the radio and the course together, and does not separate them (EV002-2).

### The enemy's answer and the main-force battles

The Front learned faster than MACV expected. From December 1964 its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons. They went for the posts settled without platoons, the constables on the roads, the CIDG camps and the militia with the handed-down Garands. Against posts that held they used the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market (F004-1, F004-2). About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts [X-004].

In the main-force battles the squad weapons changed the losses and not the results. The record's restated figures, set once by revision R05 with each provision's share named, are these:

## P2 · Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

The American rifleman arrived in this phase, and what he carried is the clearest difference from history. History's American infantry landed with the M14 and took the M16 in 1965–67, a rifle delivered without chromed chambers, cleaning kits or instructions and on a powder that fouled it [S2]. The stoppages that followed led to a House subcommittee's hearings from May to August 1967 and to the M16A1 [S2]. In this war every American battalion landed with the M4A1 and its 2026 upper, two squad guns in every Army squad, three in every Marine squad and a marksman in each [M004]. The rest of his load was history's.

The squad gun was built for the Ia Drang and helped less there than it should have. At X-Ray, 14–16 November 1965, the perimeter held as it held historically. At Albany on 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry was ambushed in column in tall grass, and the companies strung out behind the head were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres (EV000-23). The record's restated figure for the campaign's American dead is about 165–170 against 237, of which the squad guns account for about 30 [X-R05]. The B3 Front's after-action study noted that the Americans now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", so the assault must be made from closer still, at night, or not at all (F004-10). That is the enemy reading the weapon correctly, and choosing.

### A scene: the saddle between the 881s, late April 1967

Lance Corporal Dale Kessler† lay behind a fallen tree on the saddle with the scope's illuminated reticle on a black slot in the far hillside, and waited for it to move. Below him his squad had stopped in the elephant grass, two of them down, because the slot was a bunker aperture and there was a machine gun inside it.

He had been the squad's marksman since the battalion's training at Pendleton, mostly because he had shot the best groups, and in Vietnam he had found out what the job really was. Under the trees it was nothing; the rifle was long and the magazine small, and he fought as a rifleman. Out here, in the open on the hills, it was everything. The slot was about 450 metres away. He could see the gun's barrel when it fired, a short flicker in the shadow.

He fired twice into the slot. The flicker stopped. Down the slope the squad leader's voice came up, and the riflemen started to crawl again, and the squad guns opened on the next bunker along to keep heads down while they went. Kessler shifted to the next aperture and settled.

At night, back in the perimeter, the men cleaned their carbines in the mud with a care that was mostly habit. The rifles did not jam more than the old M14s had. They did not talk about the rifles much. They talked about the bunkers, which the rifles could not touch, and which fell, one at a time, to the bombs, the eight-inch guns, the LAWs and the flamethrower.

The scene shows what the American got from the rearmament: a rifle that worked and a marksman who could reach a bunker's gunner at 400–500 metres in open country. It also shows its limit: no rifle broke a bunker, and the Hill Fights' Marine losses fell by about 30 dead, from 155 to about 125 (EVCP02-3) [X-004].

### The ARVN in the main-force war

For the ARVN the phase showed what the squad weapons did in the hands of trained men under schooled sergeants. Historically the ARVN fought these years with the Garand, the carbine and the BAR, against an enemy carrying the AK-47 and the RPD, and Westmoreland asked repeatedly for M16s for it [S1]. In this war its squads had carried the carbine, the belt-fed gun and the marksman since 1964.

### A scene: Hau My, 16 November 1966

Sergeant First Class Tran Van Loc was four men behind the captain on the canal dike when the tree line on the left opened, and he saw the captain go down in the first burst. The fire came from two directions at once, the left and the front, in an L, and between the long bursts of the machine guns on the arms he heard the heavier crack of the recoilless rifle at the corner. The two forward platoon leaders were hit in the next minute. There was nobody above him.

He had been taught what to do, and so had the six young sergeants and corporals who led his squads, and he did not have to shout most of it. The squads went off the dike on the right, the side away from the long arm, into the water and the mud. Each squad leader put his belt-fed gun on the edge of the dike and turned it on the tree line, and the guns fired in long, steady bursts while the riflemen crawled. The marksmen did not fire; it was 50 metres to the trees and there was nothing to aim at but the muzzle flashes.

Loc took the radio from the captain's operator. He gave the battalion the company's position the way the senior course had drilled it, in a form the gunners could plot, and called the battery at Cai Be onto the tree line 150 metres beyond his own men. Then he waited, counting belts. The first rounds came eleven minutes after the ambush opened, walking into the trees, and then the carriers came up the next dike, and the fire from the tree line slackened and stopped. The enemy battalion had gone back into the flooded reeds.

Fourteen of the company were dead. Every rifle, every squad gun and the radio came off the dike with the living.

The scene shows the squad gun at its best: a base of fire that held the enemy in contact while the rest of the system, the radio and the guns, arrived. On the historical course the record expects 40–60 dead and the guns and the radio left on the dike (EV010-3) [X-010].

### The territorials, the leak and the enemy's turn to the mine

At Bau Bang on 21 May 1966 two RF companies riding trucks on Route 13 met the 272nd Regiment's ambush, got off on the side away from the fire, returned it in pairs from the ditch, and the rear company put its BARs into the tree line in the first two minutes (EV007-3) [X-009]. The RF lost about 32 dead rather than about 40. Erratum E04 and revision R04 corrected this event and STILL WATER's cordons (now part of EVR05-3): the Regional Forces never drew the squad gun. The correction sharpens the point. The RF losses fell because of the territorial course, not the weapon [M009].

The rearmed territorials were good enough to be borrowed. In April 1966 IV Corps formed 34 RF companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu into mobile groups for the U Minh. Behind them 23 PF outposts and six police posts fell between May and August, against seven outposts in the same months of 1965, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them carbines (EV004-2) [X-004].

The weapons also became evidence. Every crate under IRON GATE carried its lot number, and every carbine and sight a serial on the muster roll (F004-14). On 12 July 1966 a 9th Division company picked up about 900 bright 5.56mm cases behind a VC firing position near Cao Lanh, all of one lot that Captain Robert Halloran's own battalion had drawn in May. The Commission traced about 1.4 million rounds from the Sa Dec ammunition supply point to two Cholon dealers and a Front purchasing agent at about 18 piasters a round, and seven men were convicted in March 1967 (EV004-3) [X-004]. The leak moved rather than stopped, and the Front's agents paid more for untraceable Chinese 7.62mm than for the Republic's 5.56mm.

The enemy's biggest decision about the area in this phase was his own. The Quang Da Special Zone's directive for 1966 concluded that its companies lost more than they gained in firefights with units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds. It ordered every lowland guerrilla platoon to make mines and booby traps its principal work (EV009-2) [X-009]. In Dien Ban the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines lost about two-thirds of its dead to devices in its first year, against about two-fifths before the directive. The rifles had won the firefight, and the enemy stopped offering it. In Hanoi in the spring of 1967 a study group of the General Staff concluded that the ARVN, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat; its officers were arrested in December for reasons unconnected with the study, and the offensive was planned on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve (EV000-37) [X-004].

By the autumn of 1967 every VC battalion had been re-equipped with Soviet-design weapons, the AK-47 and the B40 among them [S4]. The SKS passed down to the guerrillas, and the K-50 stayed with couriers and cadres [S3] [S14].

## P3 · Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

Tet tested the margin at scale, and it showed most plainly in the district towns. Historically the first wave penetrated nearly all of the 64–72 district towns it attacked; in this war the attackers got inside about two-thirds (EV000-42) [X-004]. At Cai Lay on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound all night against a battalion of the 261st with its carbines and one squad gun lent by the 7th Division. It fired in pairs and kept ammunition for the second rush, and at 0600 it still had a third of its basic load (EV000-42) [X-009]. The record puts the ARVN's killed in the first wave at about 2,150 against 2,788, and credits the rifles with about 290 of the difference (EV000-42) [X-004].

At Hue the squad weapons made the city cheaper to retake and no faster. The Marines had two or three squad guns in each squad and a marksman who could put rounds into an upper-floor window at 200 metres from cover (EV000-44) [X-004]. What took the blocks was what took them historically: 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, gas, grenades and men going through walls. The record puts the dead at about 370 ARVN and 200 American against 458 and 250, of which the rifles account for about 85 [X-R05].

Historically 1968 was the year the ARVN was rearmed. By mid-1968 every ARVN infantry battalion carried the M16, the M60, the M79 and the LAW [S1]. The general mobilisation of June 1968 is where history's rearmament ran out of rifles: the RF and PF raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969 (EV000-51) [S1]. In this war about 260,000 carbines were already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June, and the new RF companies and PF platoons drew them from their first month (EV000-51) [X-004]. The old weapons went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the new People's Self-Defense Force, whose members had a few days of instruction from the district chiefs.

The enemy's third offensive went exactly there. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee ordered its subregions to avoid the RF and PF posts that had held in February and May and to strike the new self-defence groups (EV009-4) [X-009]. From 17–18 August through September the local-force companies attacked about 30 groups in Duc Hue and Trang Bang and overran about two-thirds. Across Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong about 600–900 members were killed and about 1,500–2,000 of their weapons taken, almost all of them Garands and carbines of 1950 (EV009-4) [X-009].

### A scene: a hamlet gate near Rach Kien, 2–3 September 1968

Truong Van Hoa† taught the second and third grades, and since July he had also been the leader of the hamlet's self-defence group, because he could read the instructions. The group was Hoa, some farmers and their older sons. They had a handful of Garands, a few carbines and a box of clips, and Hoa had learned from the district's three days of instruction how to load the Garand: you pressed the clip of eight down into the rifle until the bolt slammed forward, and you kept your thumb out of the way. After the eighth round the empty clip flew out with a ring you could hear across a paddy. Everyone knew that sound, including the men who came at night.

The PF corporal from the post at the canal bridge had come out one evening in August and shown him something the district had not. He showed him the flare, how to hold it and fire it straight up, and he said: when you fire it, we come. Do not shoot at the paddy when we come across it.

They came at about one in the morning, from two sides. Hoa heard firing from the far end of the hamlet, rapid and continuous, more rounds in a minute than his group had fired all summer, and then the boy beside him at the gate fired his Garand eight times very fast and the clip pinged out, and somebody in the dark laughed. Hoa fired the flare. It went up red over the hamlet and hung there.

He did not know how long it was. Afterwards they told him sixteen minutes. He saw the PF platoon come across the paddy in the light of their own flares in pairs, one pair running while the other fired, the carbines crackling in short strings, the old Browning thudding from the dike. The attackers at the gate turned to face them and then were gone into the dark. Two of his group were dead. Four were wounded, and the PF soldiers knelt beside them with tourniquets and did not leave until the bleeding stopped.

The scene shows the line IRON GATE drew in 1964, between the rearmed and the handed-down, as the line the enemy fought along in 1968. The trained platoon with modern carbines could not arm the self-defence group, but it could come to its call, and in Long An after 24 October that became the province's rule (EV009-4) [X-010].

The phase ends with the gap closing from history's side. By 1968 the PAVN carried the AK-47 throughout its infantry, and the guerrilla and local forces were moving from the SKS and captured weapons toward the AK as the North's supply allowed [S3]. In this war they also carried the carbines they had taken, and fired whatever 5.56mm the leak and the battlefield gave them.

## P4 · Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV is the phase in which the area stops being a difference, and the record says so plainly. Memorandum 004's null for the phase reads that "the rearmament does not bite in Phase IV because the historical army catches up with it here" [M004]. Historically the Improvement and Modernization programme of 1969 completed the RVNAF's rearmament: the M16 for the regulars and then the territorials, with the M60, the M79 and the LAW, and about 350 mobile advisory teams to help the territorials use them [S1]. In this war CP04 made that event (EV000-56) say what the programme bought instead: no new rifle, but replacement carbines and sights, M855A1 for the territorial expansion, and M60s [X-CP04].

What remained of the margin was what memorandum 004 named: the squad gun, the marksman and practice (POS004-2). The American squad of 1969 differed from history's mainly in the same two weapons, since the historical M16A1 of 1969 was a reliable rifle [S2]. From 1969 history's American grenadier carried the M203 under his M16A1 [S6]; whether this war's grenadier did the same on the new carbine, or kept the M79, the record does not say (Q-7).

The incursion into Cambodia in May 1970 and Lam Son 719 in February–March 1971 were decided by the frontier constraints, command failures and Washington's timetable. The record holds no event in which a squad weapon changed either, and this study finds none [M004]. That is why this phase has no scene: a scene must tell a moment the record holds, and the record holds no small-arms moment in these three years. The area's activity was below the battles. In 1970–71 the territorial post's call for fire passed from American to Vietnamese guns (EV004-2, EV008-7), and from 1969 the VNAF's AC-130U wing answered the posts the American gunships had answered (EV015-6). The study holds the territorial posts a few points above history in 1971 when the rifle no longer is, because the margin had moved from the man on the wall to what he could call.

The enemy's rifles in the phase were his historical ones. By 1970 the communist inventory was mostly standardised on the 7.62x39mm family even at the village guerrilla level [S3]. A guerrilla platoon with captured carbines depended on the Republic's ammunition; a local force that could draw 7.62x39mm from the North, as it could by 1970, had less reason to keep them [S3] [E: the Front's ammunition supply after 1968]. The record does not say how many of the carbines taken in 1964–68 were still in use (Q-4).

American infantry strength fell from the 1968–69 peak through 1971, and with it the share of the small-arms war that was American. Nothing in the record shows a small-arms difference in the American battles of the drawdown that history's M16A1 would not also have produced.

## P5 · Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The Easter Offensive was decided by armour, artillery, B-52s and the quality of corps command, and the rifle touches none of them. Memorandum 004's null for the phase is the record's judgment and this study endorses it. Quang Tri fell because its defence collapsed at the top; An Loc and Kontum held because the bombers held them [M004]. By 1972 the historical ARVN carried the M16 and the M60, and the gap left was too narrow to change any battle's result.

The individual weapons that mattered in 1972 were the ones this war never replaced. The PAVN brought its divisions south with tanks, the B41 against bunkers and vehicles, the SA-7 against aircraft and the 12.7mm and 14.5mm machine guns in numbers [H]. Against the tanks the ARVN infantryman had the M72 LAW, a weapon both wars gave him, and from 1972 the TOW [S4] [S10].

### A scene: An Loc, 13 April 1972

Private First Class Nguyen Van Danh† had carried two LAWs since the siege began, strapped across his pack, because the sergeant said someone had to and Danh was strong. He had fired one once on a range at Lai Khe. You pulled the pins, pulled the tube out to its full length so the sight popped up, put it on your shoulder, checked behind you, and squeezed the rubber bar on top. Then you threw the tube away. There was nothing to reload.

On the morning of the 13th he heard engines in the north of the town, which was a sound none of them had heard from the enemy before. Then the shelling lifted, and a tank came down the street between the broken shophouses, slowly, its turret turning, with infantry behind it and nobody in front of it but the 5th Division.

His squad was in the ruins of a pharmacy at a corner, and the tank came on to within about fifty metres. The carbines were no use against it; he heard the rounds strike it and whine away. The squad gun fired at the infantry behind it, and the infantry went to ground in the doorways. Danh's sergeant pointed at him and then at the tank, and Danh crawled to the window, extended the first tube with hands that would not quite work, and fired. The rocket went low and burst on the street. He threw the tube down, extended the second, made himself breathe, and fired into the tank's side where the tracks met the hull.

It stopped. Smoke came out of it, then a man, who ran. By the afternoon the division's battalions were forming teams of men with LAWs to hunt tanks in the streets, and Danh's sergeant put his name on one.

The scene shows the area's honest place in 1972: the weapon that stopped the first armoured assault at An Loc was a one-shot rocket both wars issued, not anything this war changed (EV000-76) [H65]. The town itself was held from the air.

For the territorials the year is barely in the record. The posts of the Delta and the Saigon belt fought the Front's local forces as they had in 1971, with the call for fire and the gunships behind them, and the study carries its 1971 range for the posts unchanged at low confidence rather than invent a movement. That is a gap in the area's record, not a quiet year.

The American soldier left the area in 1972, when the last American ground combat units departed in August [H]. History's American dead by hostile action in 1972 were about 300 [H40]. The record does not adjust the figure, and no small-arms difference would show in it.

## P6 · Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

Phase VI is where memorandum 004's counter-directional finding came due. The ARVN had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical army (F004-15). On 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel were cut, and the Joint General Staff's directive of February limited each squad to its basic load for a month. The squad gun went first, at about 200 rounds a gun a month, which an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes (EV000-89) [X-004]. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had its own line, were rationed harder still, and by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms (EV000-89) [X-004].

### A scene: Hill 1062, a night in September 1974

Corporal Vo Thanh Liem† had one belt for the night. It lay coiled in its pouch beside the gun, two hundred rounds, and the platoon sergeant had said it twice at dusk so that nobody could say afterwards he had not heard: one belt each, and the next resupply when it comes.

Liem had carried the squad gun for four years. He had been taught on it, and then he had been taught by it, in fights where the way out was to put a long burst into the trees and keep it there while the squad moved. He knew how fast it went. At the rapid rate a belt lasted two minutes.

During the day the marksman, Thang, had lain behind the rocks at the crest and fired across the valley at men on the facing slope, five or six hundred metres off, the spotters who called the enemy's guns, and twice Liem had seen a figure on the far slope drop and not get up. In the afternoon the shelling came anyway, big guns from somewhere beyond the hills, and the battalion's own guns answered a few rounds at a time, rationed like everything else.

The first assault came after midnight. Liem heard them on the slope below and waited, because the sergeant's hand was on his shoulder, and when the hand squeezed he fired short bursts, five rounds, six, at the shapes in the flare light, and the riflemen beside him fired single shots. It went against everything the gun had taught him. The assault went back. The second came an hour later, and by the end of it he could feel through the pouch that the belt was more than half gone. When the third came near dawn he fired the last of it in bursts of three and then picked up his carbine.

They held the crest. In the morning they carried the wounded down the back of the hill, and the enemy's guns, which nobody had rationed, began again.

The scene shows the 1974 cost of a decade's habit: a squad built to fight by volume, holding a crest on a belt a night while the enemy's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed (EV000-91) [X-004]. The weapons were better than the PAVN's in every fight of the year, and it mattered less than at any time since 1964.

### The last campaign and the end of the area

The record judges that the domestic rounds, the radios and the larger appropriation together saved perhaps 2,500–6,500 of the 30,000 South Vietnamese killed in 1974 [H71]; the small arms are inside that judgment and not separable from it (EV000-91) [X-008] [X-011]. In the territorial war of 1973–74 the post's carbine was by then equal to history's M16; what held 46 of 64 attacked canal posts in Chuong Thien in the spring of 1974 was the call for fire and the Vietnamese gunship (EV015-8) [X-015].

The last campaign took the area's material with it in the north and left it in the south. The 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin came apart in late March 1975, and its squad guns, marksman's rifles and hoard went to the PAVN 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade. The 1st Division's hoard at Hue, about 3 million rounds of linked 5.56mm, was destroyed in place on 17 April when Truong withdrew Hue's garrison (EV000-94) [X-011]. At Pleiku on 2 and 5 April the 44th Regiment's and the 4th Ranger Group's squads broke the 320A's assault battalions on open ground with the squad guns fed (EV000-95) [X-011]. Xuan Loc was not attacked; the 18th Division held it through the rains with its squads, its marksmen and the ammunition the Long Binh depot could send (EV000-96). Checkpoint CP04 reversed the old position that the hoards were lost in a March collapse that this war no longer has (REVCP04-1).

At the end of 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, with the Bien Hoa arsenal loading its rifle and squad-gun cartridges, and an infantry that has carried better individual weapons than its enemy for twelve years. Five PAVN corps are assembled against it, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976 (EV011-3). The area's last word is the record's first: the rifle acts only in a contact, and the enemy still chooses most contacts.

## Judgments

This re-run rewrites the study's text and judges nothing new. It re-reads every value the study holds and every headline facet ST02 judged in the chapters, and finds each consistent with the record after revision R05. It folds R05's entry into the text: R05's restated figures for Dong Xoai, the Ia Drang, Tet and Hue appear once each, with the rifles' share named, and no study-held value changes.

**Headline facets judged in the chapters by ST02 (not re-judged).**

- **ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit, P1 (+1 to +5 points, low) and P2 (+2 to +7 points, low).** Unit: % of company-and-below contacts the ARVN initiates. The Front's district companies stopped assaulting posts held by rearmed and trained platoons from December 1964 (EV002-2, F004-1), and a 7th Division company took a fight back from an ambush at Hau My (EV010-3). The ranges are small because the rifle does not decide who opens the fight (POS004-1). The P3 value of +3 to +8 was judged by an earlier work.
- **ARVN.effectiveness: binding facet combined_arms**, named by ST02 as DIRR01-3 requires; the parent is left on the ranges other works judged.
- **US.casualties, P1: 1,250–1,335 against history's 1,369 (low)**, a sum of rates and not a measurement (DIRMT01-2).

**Headline facets left unjudged, and why.**

- **ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit, P4–P6.** From 1969 history's army carried the same class of rifle. The record holds no small-unit event in 1969–72 that measures initiation, and the 1974 events show an army avoiding contacts it could not break under the ration, which the record does not quantify.
- **ARVN.effectiveness.night_ops, every phase.** The red dot can be seen in the dark and the target cannot, and the force had no night-vision devices (P004a negative specification). Nothing in the record measures the share of ARVN ambushes and patrols conducted at night.
- **ARVN.effectiveness.anti_armor, every phase.** Its unit is the share of PAVN armoured attacks stopped without US air. The only small-arms evidence is An Loc's first assault, stopped by the LAW, a weapon both wars carried (EV000-76); the siege was decided from the air, so the event cannot carry a range on this facet.
- **ARVN.effectiveness.urban, every phase.** Hue is the one urban battle the area touches, and the marksmen shortened it by nothing (EV000-44).
- **ARVN.effectiveness.combined_arms, fire_coordination and mobile_defence.** Outside the area; no small-arms evidence bears on them.
- **ARVN.self_sufficiency and its facets (maintenance, logistics, fire_support, replacement).** The area bears on logistics through the ammunition dependence and the Bien Hoa cartridge line (EV000-89), on values other works judged in P3 and P6. The record holds no small-arms readiness or replacement figure.
- **ENEMY.engagement_control, P1, P4, P5.** The settled position is that small arms do not change which fights the enemy accepts (POS004-1).
- **US.casualties, P4 and P5.** History's figures are 1,380 (1971) and 300 (1972) [H40]. The record holds no event in the drawdown that shows the provisions' rates operating.

**Study-held values (unchanged from ST02 and CP04).**

- **RFPF.effectiveness and RFPF.effectiveness.outpost_defence, P1–P6**, in % of attacked outposts that hold, as a difference from history's national figure, which the record does not hold: P1 +4 to +10 (Long Huu and the held belt, EV002-2); P2 +6 to +14 (the radio, the gunship and the Dinh Tuong rule, EV008-4, EV015-1; bounded by Chuong Thien, EV004-2); P3 the same (the district towns at Tet, EV000-42); P4 and P5 +3 to +9 (history's 1969 rearmament closes the rifle gap [S1]); P6 +4 to +11 (Chuong Thien in 1974, EV015-8). All low confidence. The parent carries the facet's range because their units are the same.

**Study-held values left unjudged, and why.**

- **RFPF.effectiveness.night_patrol.** The only indicator is Cai Lay's fall of about a third in 1967, in one district, an effect of the radio rather than the rifle (EV008-4).
- **RFPF.effectiveness.reaction.** It turns on the gunship and the district gun, and belongs to a study of fire support.
- **FIX.capability.** The record shows the squad weapons changing loss rates once in contact, not the share of located main-force units engaged before they break contact (POS003-1, POS004-1).
- **VC.main_force and VC.local_force.** The record holds the Front's captured carbines (F004-7) and no strength figure that a small-arms effect could be read from.
- **INTL.communist_aid.** The area touches it only through the Type 56 cargoes of 1964 (EV003-1), with no figure.

## Questions for the record

- **Q-1** · RESOLVED · erratum E04 and revision R04 corrected EV006-2 and EV007-3 to carbines and BARs; the Regional Forces never drew the M249.
- **Q-2** · RESOLVED · CP04 made EV000-56 HIST-ALT: the programme bought no new rifle in this war, and its small-arms money replaced worn carbines and sights, bought M855A1 for the territorial expansion and added M60s.
- **Q-3** · RESOLVED · CP04 reversed POS004-3 (REVCP04-1): the Hue hoard was destroyed in place on 17 April 1975, what the Highlands and Quang Ngai held went with them in April, and the stocks of III and IV Corps remain the Republic's.
- **Q-4** · STANDS · carried to the gap register under G-10: the record holds no ledger of the Republic's small arms lost after 1966, or of how long the Front kept the captured carbines once the North's 7.62x39mm reached the village level.
- **Q-5** · STANDS · carried to the gap register as G-20: whether Bien Hoa loaded the M855A1 or a simpler ball round, and whether the CompM5's batteries and the TANGO6T scopes were kept up after 1973, is untraced.
- **Q-6** · ERRATUM · the police's weapons disagree across the record. Memorandum 002, Part 1, P002a's weapons field, stipulated [U], arms each village constable with the M4 carbine and a Remington 870-class pump shotgun, puts an M60 in every village post, and gives the Police Field Force the M16A1 from the start. Memorandum 004 (P004a negative specification, F004-1 and its interaction with P002a) says the rearmament "does not arm the National Police, the Police Field Force" and that the constables "keep their carbines". The chapters follow memorandum 004: EV002-2 gives the posts "ten constables carrying the carbines of 1950", and EV004-1 sends a third of the handed-in weapons to the police. Memorandum 006 Part 1 arms the PRUs with "what the Police Field Force carries in this war: M1 and M2 carbines, the M1918A2 automatic rifle and M79 grenade launchers". One account must give way, and because P002a's weapons were stipulated by the user, the user's ruling decides which. If memorandum 002 stands, the constables' deaths of 1964–65 and the Front's choice of police posts as soft targets (F004-1) need re-reading.
- **Q-7** · CHECKPOINT · the reach of P004a beyond the line rifle is unrecorded. Memorandum 004 replaces "every M16, M1 Garand, M1 and M2 carbine and M14", which on its words includes the M21 sniper rifle, a scoped M14, yet memorandum 004 itself treats the M21 and the M40 as history's snipers still serving. It also notes that the M203 will not mount on the URG-I rail without an adaptor and leaves open whether grenadiers kept a separate M16A1 or the depots made an interface. A checkpoint should say what history's snipers and, from 1969, history's grenadiers carried in this war.
- **Q-8** · RESOLVED · ST02's own text said the Long Huu post's BARs "had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines", which EV004-1 ("the outposts kept the Browning as their automatic rifle") and erratum E04 contradict; this re-run tells the night with the platoon's BAR.

Also checked and found consistent: the American historical baseline (the M14, the M16's 1966–67 failures, the 1967 subcommittee and the M16A1; EVCP02-3 against [S2]); the ARVN's historical rearmament dates (EV000-51 and memorandum 004 against [S1]); the squad-gun counts (one per ARVN squad, two per Army squad, three per Marine squad, against P004c); the marksman ranges in the events (300–800 m) against P004b's practical range; and the weapons the record gives the enemy in each event against [S3] and [S4].
