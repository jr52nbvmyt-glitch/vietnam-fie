# Memorandum 014 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


The provision commits the whole of the 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta to Vietnam, in its 2026 configuration and at full strength. It comes as the unit alone: its assault squadrons, its reconnaissance and advanced-force squadron, its own small aviation squadron and its support squadrons, with no 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment and no dedicated JSOC surveillance wing. It is assigned to a joint special operations command inside MACV, formed after the Gulf of Tonkin incident. That headquarters must work with the military, intelligence and civil structures already in Vietnam for logistics and deconfliction, and the user will assign further units to it later. Delta has five missions¹:
- hunting PAVN and VC leadership and the infrastructure;
- COSVN and the base areas;
- recovering prisoners of war;
- raids into the North;
- reconnaissance.

The borders bind as they did in history¹. I read that as follows, and Part 2 will hold to it:
- **Laos.** No American ground entry before history's first cross-border mission on 18 October 1965. After that, only on SOG's terms, under the ambassador's limits on depth, target and duration.
- **Cambodia.** No entry before September 1967. From then on, reconnaissance on foot without tactical air and with no exploitation force². Then the incursion of May–June 1970 on history's terms.
- **North Vietnam.** No American ground raid until the one history made, at Son Tay on 21 November 1970³.

CONFIG's `laos_ground`, `cambodia_ground` and `north_ground` therefore stay MODELLED, held at history's practice. Nothing in this memorandum suspends them.

I date the headquarters to 15 August 1964, eleven days after the second Tonkin report⁴, and Delta's arrival to 1 September 1964⁵. That puts the unit in Vietnam three years before American teams could legally enter Cambodia, and fourteen months before they could enter Laos. For its first year, every mission Delta has lies inside the South. I think that is the right place to start reading the provision, because it is where most of the unit's value will lie.

The provision makes two dossiers:
- **P014a** is the unit. It is read as a ground force.
- **P014b** is the headquarters. It is read as an institutional provision.

## §1.1 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta, full commitment, 2026 configuration — P014a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The unit is the 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta of the United States Army (SFOD-D, Delta Force). Charles Beckwith and Colonel Thomas Henry raised it on 19 November 1977. It is organised under Army Special Operations Command and controlled by the Joint Special Operations Command. The 2026 unit is the reference configuration¹. Its strength is classified. The best published figure is "nearly 1,000 soldiers" in 2001, of whom "about 250 to 300" were trained for direct action and hostage rescue. Since then a fourth assault squadron has been added (D, 2006) and a combat support squadron (2005)⁶. I take the 2026 unit at about 1,000–1,500 people, with about 250–350 operators⁷.

The organisation is the unit's own⁶:
- **Four assault squadrons, A to D.** Each is commanded by a lieutenant colonel and has two assault troops and one reconnaissance troop. An assault troop has four teams of five or six operators.
- **G Squadron, advanced force operations.** It goes ahead of a raid to look at the target and the ground.
- **E Squadron, aviation.** It is company-sized and flies unmarked light helicopters and a handful of fixed-wing aircraft⁸.
- **A signal squadron and a combat support squadron.**
- **A selection and training element.**

"Full commitment" sends all of it¹. I base the unit at Tan Son Nhut, inside the MACV compound's perimeter, with the training element and the support squadron⁹. In each corps area one forward operating base holds one assault squadron, and from 1965 the base areas each forward base faces set its position¹⁰:
- I Corps at Da Nang;
- II Corps at Kontum or Pleiku;
- III Corps at Bien Hoa;
- IV Corps at Can Tho.

The squadrons rotate between the forward bases and Tan Son Nhut. In any month three assault squadrons are on operations and one is resting and retraining inside the country¹¹.

The provision has no manufacturer. Its equipment is the 2026 unit's, which is described in D3 and D4.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The working element is the team of five or six operators⁶. An assault troop is four teams, about 20–24 operators. A sabre squadron is two assault troops and a reconnaissance troop, about 60–80 operators with a squadron headquarters¹². The four sabre squadrons hold about 250–320 operators. With G Squadron's advanced-force teams, the unit fields about 280–350 people who go out on operations¹³.

The rest of the unit is about 750–1,150 people: aviators and maintainers in E Squadron, signallers, intelligence analysts, medics, armourers, parachute riggers, and the training and selection cadre¹⁴. The unit spends 1,000–1,500 personnel spaces¹⁵. CONFIG has no troop ceiling, so they come out of no one else's [U: CONFIG troop_ceiling = NONE].

The figure that matters is not the 1,500. It is the 280–350 who go into the bush and through the doors. They are irreplaceable at any speed the war can use. Selection passes about one man in eight to ten, and the Operator Training Course takes about six months after that⁶. A killed or crippled operator is replaced from Fort Bragg's pipeline, not from Vietnam¹⁶. I count every loss against a stock of about 300 that refills at perhaps 30–50 a year¹⁷.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

Each operator carries a 5.56mm carbine of the HK416 or M4A1 family, with a suppressor, an infrared laser and illuminator, and a magnified optic. He also carries a pistol, fragmentation and flash-bang grenades, and breaching charges¹⁸. Each team has:
- one or two 7.62mm or 6.5mm marksman rifles;
- a belt-fed machine gun (an M240 or Mk 48);
- a Carl Gustaf M3E1 84mm recoilless rifle, or AT4s;
- a 60mm commando mortar for a defended target¹⁹.

²⁰

Each squadron holds a few long-range rifles in.300 Norma or.338 calibre, and Javelin missiles for a target that needs one¹⁹.

What the unit does not carry matters more than what it does. It has no artillery, no armour, and no vehicle that can move a wounded man through paddy. Its fire beyond its own weapons comes from someone else: Army and Air Force aircraft, the Js' boxes²¹, or division and corps guns. Against a defended base area the team fights for minutes with what it carries and then calls. E Squadron's helicopters can mount door guns or light rockets⁸. Those are self-defence weapons for an insertion, not fire support.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The decisive sensor is the night-vision goggle. Every operator wears a 2026 tube goggle of the AN/PVS-31A type or a fused-thermal ENVG-B, with an AN/PEQ-15 weapon aimer²². At night that gives a team an advantage no 1964 enemy can match. The PAVN and VC of the period moved and fought at night because the Americans and the ARVN could not see. A Delta team sees a man at 150–300 metres under starlight in the open²³, and sees his heat behind light foliage with a thermal sight.

Under triple canopy the advantage shrinks to the length of a path. Starlight does not reach the floor of a primary forest, and a thermal sight sees what is in front of it: 20–50 metres through undergrowth, often less²⁴. The team sees no further than any patrol. It sees in the dark what its enemy sees in the day. That is still a large edge for an ambush or a raid. It is no edge for finding a base camp from the air.

The monsoon degrades the thermal picture but hardly touches the goggle. Heavy rain at night washes out the thermal contrast between a body and the ground, and the overcast cuts starlight, so the tubes need infrared illumination, which an enemy with a converter can see²⁵. The PAVN of the 1960s had very few such converters²⁶. I count an IR illuminator as close to invisible until about 1970.

The unit's own surveillance is small. G Squadron and the reconnaissance troops carry hand-launched small drones of the RQ-20B Puma and RQ-11B Raven class²⁷, with an endurance of one to two hours and a range of 10–20 km. They also carry unattended ground sensors and laser rangefinders²⁸. Anything bigger comes from the record: the U-2S and RQ-4B imagery and signals take²⁹, and the combined task force's files³⁰. The provision's "unit alone" means the unit brings no MQ-9 or Gray Eagle orbit of its own¹.

### D5 · Performance

A raid on a known target in the South, by helicopter, at night, against a target within about 80 km of a forward base³¹, takes about 2–6 hours from launch to recovery. It needs 6–24 hours of planning if the target is new, or about 1–2 hours if it is a pattern the squadron has worked³². At maturity in 1966–67, three assault squadrons on operations can mount about 40–120 direct-action raids a month in the South³³. That is a fraction of the combined task force's 300–450 raids a month at maturity³⁰.

What each raid returns is the question. I estimate that 15–35% of Delta raids reach a cadre at district level or above who is on a prior target list³⁴. That gives about 80–400 listed senior cadre taken a year at maturity³⁵. I push FIND.vci by 50–250 a year in 1967, because many of these raids take a target that the task force's raid forces would otherwise have taken later.

Reconnaissance runs at a different tempo. The unit can keep about 15–25 reconnaissance teams of four to six in the field at once³⁶. A team is out for 3–6 days and stands down for about a week³⁷. That gives about 40–80 team-missions a month across the theater²⁸. SOG ran 454 missions into Cambodia alone in 1969², so Delta adds to SOG's reconnaissance and does not replace it.

E Squadron moves a few teams at a time. A company of light helicopters lifts about 2–4 operators each³⁸. One night's lift is about one or two assault troops, and an operation larger than that needs UH-1s and CH-47s from the theater²⁸.

Protection is a plate carrier and a helmet. Against the AK-47 and the SKS a rifle plate stops the round. Against a 12.7mm or an RPG it does nothing³⁹.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The team carries a multiband radio with satellite channels, and every operator carries an intra-team radio⁴⁰. Under the standing condition the GPS and military satellite constellations are present from the campaign's opening [E01]. A team in the Mimot rubber or the A Shau can therefore speak to Tan Son Nhut directly, by voice or text, with its position to a few metres. That is the second decisive advantage over SOG, whose teams depended on a FAC overhead to relay and to find them⁴¹.

The unit talks to the ARVN badly, and only through people. It has no Vietnamese linguists of its own beyond a handful⁴². Its interpreters come from the combined task force, from the Vietnamese services, or from hired Vietnamese. Each of these is a channel the enemy can enter (INTEL.penetration). Its radios are encrypted American sets; the ARVN of 1964 has PRC-10s and PRC-25s and no crypto⁴³. A raid with an ARVN partner force works through an American liaison officer with an ARVN set, or through the task force's combined desks³⁰. I would keep the targets themselves inside American channels until the strike, and tell the ARVN corps commander only that a restricted area exists for a night. That is how SOG worked, and SOG's Vietnamese partners still lost teams to agents².

### D7 · Domain conditions

The unit fights on foot and lands by helicopter. In paddy it moves at walking pace or slower, as everyone does; at night with goggles it moves along dikes that the enemy thinks are dark. In the Delta's canals and the U Minh it needs boats it does not own: Navy river forces or Vietnamese sampans. On laterite in the dry season it moves well. In the monsoon the Highlands become mud and the helicopters fly less, but the teams on the ground keep working²⁸.

In the forest the canopy closes the sky. A helicopter needs a landing zone or a rope. A wounded operator three kilometres from a clearing is a stretcher carried by four men for half a day²⁸. The base areas of War Zone C and the Fishhook, the A Shau, and the tri-border were chosen by the enemy for exactly that reason.

Over the South the threat to E Squadron's light helicopters is small arms and the 12.7mm⁴⁴, and every insertion within range of a defended base area meets them. Over the Trail, from 1966, the 37mm and 57mm guns at the passes⁴⁵ close daylight insertions and make night ones a choice of route. Over the North the SA-2 and the MiG are against any raid. Son Tay needed 116 aircraft in all and 28 in the target area to put 56 soldiers on the ground for 27 minutes³. Delta's own aviation cannot do that alone.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The unit is small and expensive. It needs:
- its own armourers, parachute riggers and medics;
- an airfield for E Squadron's helicopters and King Airs, with a hangar the press cannot photograph⁸;
- a secure compound for planning;
- fuel of about 5,000–15,000 gallons of JP-4 or JP-8 a day for its own aircraft⁴⁶.

The 2026 equipment is the problem. Batteries, night-vision tubes, radios and drones are all built in 2026. No American depot of 1964 can repair a fused goggle or a satellite radio, and no South Vietnamese plant can either, even the electronics plant of memorandum 008, which works at one generation above 1964⁴⁷. Every spare comes from the unit's own stock. I size the stock at two to three years of normal wastage at the unit's own scale²⁸. After that the 2026 kit thins, and the unit falls back on what the 1960s can supply: starlight scopes, PRC-25s, M16s. The decline is gradual and I describe it in D12.

The RVNAF can supply none of this except rice, guards and petrol.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The unit cannot be transferred. Its people are American soldiers chosen by a selection the ARVN does not have. Its equipment does not exist in 1964 outside the unit.

What can be transferred is a standard. A Vietnamese counterpart unit could be built at Delta's standard by a training cadre, beginning in 1965 with the ARVN's Rangers, the airborne and the LLDB as the pool. It would take about four weeks of selection and six months of operator training, as Delta's own course does⁶. The first squadron of about 60–80 Vietnamese operators could be ready about 18–30 months after the cadre starts⁴⁸. It would have 1960s equipment, because the 2026 kit cannot be handed over in quantity: starlight scopes for goggles, PRC-25s for satellite radios. By 1972 a Vietnamese special unit of two to three squadrons could exist²⁸. Whether it survives the coup politics of Saigon is a question for Part 2. A unit that can raid a headquarters at night can raid a palace, and every Vietnamese general would see that first.

If such a unit is built, the American unit's withdrawal in the drawdown leaves the Vietnamese unit with its training and without its night, its satellite radio and its helicopters.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The unit performs to its standard under these conditions:
- **At night.** This is its whole advantage.
- **Inside the South.** The target is within the range of its helicopters or of theater lift.
- **On a target that someone has found.** The combined task force's files, a captured document, a defector, or signals³⁰²⁹.
- **Fast after the find.** It must arrive before the target moves. The VCI cadre who sleeps in a hamlet moves every two or three nights.

Performance collapses under these conditions:
- **The target is a main-force unit, not a person.** A battalion of 400 is not a raid target for a troop of 24.
- **Under triple canopy far from a clearing.** Insertion and recovery decide the operation.
- **Monsoon weather.** The helicopters cannot fly.
- **The target is warned.** COSVN's leaders learned of B-52 strikes from Soviet trawlers and moved⁴⁹. An American raid planned through Vietnamese channels is warned through those channels.
- **Weeks of sustained tempo.** The squadrons must rotate. The unit can surge for a few weeks to perhaps twice its sustained rate and then must rest.
- **Across a border before history's date.** The constraint, not the unit, is the limit there.

### D11 · Failure modes

The commonest failure is the dry hole. The team arrives, and the target left the night before, or was never there. This is not a loss. It is the ordinary cost of the business, and the task force of the 2000s ran many raids for each capture³⁰. It becomes a failure when the dry holes teach the enemy the pattern: which hamlets draw raids, which informants were followed by helicopters. When that happens, the sources dry up.

The second failure is compromise. The enemy learns a raid is coming and waits for it. SOG's agent teams in the North were captured at or soon after insertion in more than three cases out of four, and their radios were turned². A Delta team that walks into a prepared ambush in a hamlet loses half its men in a minute. Its value as the force that goes in at night is lost for as long as the leak goes undiscovered.

The third failure is the helicopter. A light helicopter shot down on insertion with four operators aboard, or a recovery that cannot reach a team in contact under canopy, kills more men in an hour than a year of raids. At Oscar Eight in June 1967 SOG lost 7 Americans dead and missing and more than 40 Nung soldiers in one operation².

The fourth is political. A raid that kills the wrong people in a hamlet, or that is discovered across a border, shuts down more than the unit. Washington halted all northern operations in 1968².

While the unit is unavailable (after a compromise, a heavy loss, or a political halt), the combined task force goes on with its raids and arrests³⁰, and the conventional war goes on as it would have. Delta is not load-bearing for anything except its own targets.

### D12 · Degradation curve

In month one the unit is at its best in equipment and at its worst in knowledge. It has every goggle and every radio. It does not know Vietnam. It does not know what a hamlet chief's house looks like, how the VCI cell structure runs, which Vietnamese officer can be trusted, or where the PAVN put its guns at the passes. Its first months are learning. I expect a raid yield in late 1964 at perhaps a third to a half of the 1966–67 figure⁵⁰.

At month twelve (September 1965) the unit knows the South, has a working relationship with the combined task force, and has learned which Vietnamese channels leak. Its yield rises toward the 1966–67 rate.

What declines first is the operators, not the kit. Over 1966–68 the unit loses men steadily: on raids, in helicopter losses, and on reconnaissance across the border from October 1965. SOG lost 79 Special Forces soldiers killed or missing in 1968 alone². Delta's teams are smaller and its losses lower, but a loss rate of 20–50 operators a year in the cross-border years is plausible⁵¹. That is a sixth of the operators, refilled at about the same rate (D2). The unit holds its strength and loses its experience.

The 2026 equipment declines second. From about 1966–67 goggles, batteries and radios are rationed (D8). From about 1968 a team may go out with one goggle in two. By the drawdown the unit fights much as a 1970 SOG team did: well trained, with starlight scopes and PRC-25s and the GPS still overhead⁵². The GPS lasts as long as the standing condition holds [E01].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

There are two benchmarks.

**Against SOG's reconnaissance teams.** A SOG team of 1965–69 was two or three Americans and three to twelve indigenous soldiers⁴¹. It moved by day, navigated by map and compass, called a FAC overhead to relay, and was found by its enemy often. Its strengths were the indigenous soldiers, who looked and sounded like the enemy, and its volume: 454 Cambodian missions in 1969 alone². A Delta team has five or six better-trained Americans. It sees at night, knows its position to a few metres, and talks to Saigon by satellite. I judge a Delta reconnaissance team about two to three times as likely as a SOG team to complete a mission undetected and to bring back a target located well enough for a strike⁵³. But it looks like an American wherever it goes, and the unit has a fifth as many teams as SOG fielded⁵⁴.

**Against the raid forces of the combined task force and the PRUs³⁰.** Those forces raid by the hundred a month and arrest on warrants. They take the district and village cadre. Delta takes the top of the list. It goes where the task force's forces would be shot up or betrayed, and comes back with documents and a prisoner who can describe a province committee. The margin is not numbers. Delta's 40–120 raids a month⁵⁵ are a third of the task force's at most. The margin is the reach of each raid: a province or region committee member, not a hamlet tax collector.

**Against POW recovery.** History's JPRC attempted 39 recoveries in 1967 and 32 in 1968. It succeeded 8 times in 1968 and 9 in 1969, recovering 155 and 117 South Vietnamese prisoners. It never recovered an American POW⁵⁶. The failures were mostly lateness: by the time a report of a camp reached a team, the camp had moved⁵⁷. Delta's speed and night capability make it a better instrument for recovery than anything JPRC had. Its success depends on how fresh the intelligence is, as JPRC's did.

### D14 · Negative specification
Delta cannot find what no one has found. It finishes targets that the combined task force, the U-2S and Global Hawks, a defector or a captured document have located³⁰²⁹; it locates very little itself beyond the reach of its own patrols, and nothing under triple canopy from the air. It cannot destroy a main-force unit: a battalion of 400 is not a raid target for a troop of 20–24 operators. It cannot reach COSVN by raid while COSVN is in Cambodia, because no American ground force crosses that border until September 1967, and then only on reconnaissance on foot without air support, and with no exploitation force; and COSVN, about 2,400 staff dispersed and mobile, evaded Junction City, Menu and the 1970 incursion⁴⁹. It cannot raid the North before history's one raid at Son Tay in November 1970³. It cannot enter Laos before October 1965². It brings no 160th SOAR and no drone orbit of its own¹: its lift is a company of light helicopters⁸, and any operation larger than a troop or deeper than their range needs the theater's lift. It cannot speak Vietnamese, so every target that comes through Vietnamese hands comes with the risk of a leak. It cannot be handed to the ARVN, and its 2026 equipment cannot be repaired in 1964 (D8). And it is not numerous: about 280–350 people who go on operations⁵⁸, refilled at perhaps 30–50 a year.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy's senior cadre in the South the night. From 1964 a province committee member, a regional military commander or a COSVN courier who sleeps in a hamlet that the task force's files have named can be taken from his bed by men he cannot see, in 2–6 hours from the decision⁵⁵. It denies the infrastructure the comfort that only the hamlet cadre are hunted. It denies the base areas inside the South (the Iron Triangle, the Ho Bo woods, the Do Xa, the U Minh) their freedom from observation at night. It denies the Trail and the Cambodian base areas their freedom from observation from October 1965 and September 1967, within history's limits². It denies Hanoi certainty that a prisoner camp in the South is beyond reach. It does not deny the enemy its main force, its sanctuaries or its strategy.

### D16 · Honest verdict
Delta is the best night raid force in the world, put into a war fought at night by an enemy who believed the night was his. That is worth something real and specific: from late 1964 the senior infrastructure in the South (province committees, regional commanders, couriers and their documents) can be taken when it is found, at night, by men who see. The unit's whole value runs through the combined task force's files³⁰. Delta adds the hands for the hardest 10–20% of the task force's targets⁵⁹ and the speed to reach them before they move. That will be 50–250 more senior cadre a year at maturity, against an infrastructure counted in the tens of thousands⁵⁵. It is reconnaissance that finds the Trail and the Cambodian base areas a little better than SOG did, from history's dates. It is a better POW recovery force than any history had. It does not touch the main force, the sanctuaries or Hanoi's decision. COSVN, which the provision names, is beyond its reach until 1970 and probably after, because it was a moving headquarters of 2,400 people in a country no American could enter⁴⁹. The raid into the North that history made, the unit could make better. It is one raid. Delta is a scalpel. The war has places to use one, and it is not won with one.

## §1.2 MACV Joint Special Operations Command, formed August 1964 — P014b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The provision is a joint headquarters under COMUSMACV, MACV-JSOC, formed after the Gulf of Tonkin incident. It commands Delta and the special operations units the user will assign to it later. It must interact with the military, intelligence and civil structures in Vietnam for logistics and deconfliction. Its real-world model is the Joint Special Operations Command, established on 15 December 1980 on Beckwith's recommendation after Eagle Claw⁶³. Its nearest period precedent is MACV-SOG, formed on 24 January 1964², which already sits beside MACV with its own lines to the CIA and to Washington. The quantity is one headquarters¹. I date it to 15 August 1964⁶⁴.

The organisation is small. It has:
- a command group, a brigadier general with a joint deputy;
- J-2 targeting and intelligence, with a joint targeting cell;
- J-3 operations, with a joint operations centre that runs night operations;
- J-4 logistics;
- J-6 communications;
- liaison cells in each of the bodies it must deconflict with (D6).

⁶⁵

It sits at Tan Son Nhut beside Delta's compound, and is sized to grow when the user assigns more units¹.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

About 250–450 people:
- **Command group and staff:** about 150–250⁶⁶.
- **Joint operations centre and signals:** about 50–100.
- **Liaison officers:** about 40–80 in fifteen to twenty cells (D6)²⁸.

It consumes 250–450 personnel spaces⁵⁸, under no ceiling [U: CONFIG troop_ceiling = NONE]. Its scarce people are its targeters and its liaison officers. A liaison officer who can sit in the CIA station, in the combined task force's National Fusion Centre³⁰, or in a corps commander's office and be trusted is a man the Army has a few dozen of in 1964²⁸.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

Read as *instruments and authorities*. The headquarters holds four things:
- **Operational command of Delta** and of later special operations units¹.
- **Authority to declare restricted operating areas** for a night or a few days, inside which no other American or allied unit fires or flies without clearance. This is the core of deconfliction⁶⁷.
- **A seat on MACV's target boards** and a share of the combined task force's target list³⁰, with the right to claim a target for Delta.
- **A call on MACV's theater lift and fire support** through the MACV J-3²⁸.

It holds no cross-border authority. Laos targets stay with the ambassador in Vientiane, as SOG's did. Cambodian entry is Washington's from September 1967². A raid into the North is the President's³. It holds no authority over the CIA, the GVN, or the ARVN; with them it can only ask. It can compel nothing outside MACV.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

Read as *information inputs*. The headquarters learns from:
- the combined task force's files and its National Fusion Centre³⁰;
- MACV J-2;
- the U-2S and RQ-4B take²⁹;
- the CIA station;
- SOG's reconnaissance;
- Delta's own teams.

Its own reach is only what its teams see. The reliability in contested hamlets and at night is the task force's reliability, which the record holds as improving from 1964³⁰. The headquarters adds speed of decision and a joint watch through the night; it adds no new source.

### D5 · Performance

Read as *throughput and capacity*. The headquarters can run about two to six operations at once at night and approve about 40–150 operations a month⁶⁸. The rate limit is the deconfliction: each raid must be cleared against the combined task force's own raids, against SOG's teams, against the corps' fire plans, and against the CIA's agents. Each clearance takes time. A clearance of 1–3 hours is achievable inside the South once the liaison cells are in place²⁸. Laos needs the ambassador².

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

Read as *reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF*. The headquarters reports to COMUSMACV. It keeps standing liaison with¹⁶⁹:
- MACV J-2 and J-3;
- MACV-SOG;
- the CIA station;
- the Combined Interagency Task Force and its National Fusion Centre;
- the combined command's operations centre³⁰;
- the 2nd Air Division, from April 1966 the Seventh Air Force, for air support;
- each of the four US corps-level commands, and the four ARVN corps headquarters;
- the Joint General Staff;
- the National Police;
- the US Mission and the Embassy in Vientiane.

The interface with the ARVN is narrow by design. The JGS and the corps are told of restricted areas and of results, not of targets. The task force's combined desks are the channel for Vietnamese information³⁰. The National Police are the channel for arrests and detention under the 1964 police statute⁷⁰.

### D7 · Domain conditions

Read as *reach and security preconditions*. The headquarters reaches everywhere in the South that a helicopter and a cleared raid can reach. It needs no hamlet security. It needs the opposite: a closed compound and a clean channel. Its precondition is counter-intelligence. If the enemy has agents in the Vietnamese channels the headquarters must use, every raid planned through those channels is at risk (INTEL.penetration). Across the borders its reach is history's, from history's dates (introduction).

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The headquarters draws on MACV's logistics for everything ordinary: food, fuel, vehicles, construction and medical evacuation. It needs a secure compound of about two to four hectares at Tan Son Nhut, with planning rooms, a joint operations centre, and a satellite communications site²⁸. Its running cost is about $10–20 million a year in 1964 prices, excluding Delta⁷¹. Delta's own logistics (D8 of P014a) run through it.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The headquarters cannot be transferred as it stands; its purpose is to keep targets out of Vietnamese channels. A Vietnamese joint special operations headquarters could be built beside it from about 1968, to command the Vietnamese special unit described in D9 of P014a²⁸. It would inherit the deconfliction problem in a worse form: the coup politics of Saigon would make every Vietnamese special unit a political weapon.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The headquarters works when:
- Delta's targets come from the combined task force's files with the task force's consent;
- the corps commanders accept restricted areas without asking why;
- the CIA station shares its agents' locations;
- the ambassador in Vientiane accepts Delta's teams on SOG's terms from October 1965.

It fails when:
- the agencies hoard their sources. Each agency has a reason to: its sources are its value;
- a corps commander's fire plan kills a Delta team in a restricted area that was never passed down;
- the headquarters is captured by its own operations and stops sharing results with the task force.

### D11 · Failure modes

The first failure is fratricide: a Delta team shot by an American or ARVN unit that was not told of the restricted area. The second is the blown source: Delta raids a target the CIA was watching, and the agent who was reporting dies. The third is rivalry: the headquarters, SOG and the combined task force fight over the same targets and the same helicopters. The fourth is capture by success. A headquarters that raids well becomes the instrument Washington reaches for, and its targets drift from what the war needs to what the briefing wants.

During the window the headquarters is not working, Delta works through MACV J-3 directly, more slowly, and with more leaks²⁸.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The headquarters improves for about a year as its liaison cells earn trust, and then holds²⁸. What declines first is the liaison: liaison officers rotate on 12-month tours, and each replacement must earn trust again⁷². The second decline is the joint watch, which tires through the cross-border years.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is MACV-SOG's own arrangement. SOG was a subsidiary command of MACV whose authority ran to Washington, with its cross-border operations cleared by the ambassador and by Washington mission by mission². It had no standing link to a national targeting file like the combined task force's. The headquarters improves on SOG in one way: it shares a target list with the task force and a watch with the corps. I judge that worth a few hundred cadre over the war (P014b's FIND.vci push), not more.

### D14 · Negative specification
The headquarters cannot authorise any cross-border operation: Laos stays with the ambassador, Cambodia with Washington from September 1967, the North with the President²³. It holds no authority over the CIA, the GVN, the JGS or the National Police; it can only ask them. It brings no units except Delta, no aviation beyond Delta's own⁸, no surveillance and no sources of its own: every target it acts on comes from someone else's files³⁰²⁹. It cannot stop leaks from Vietnamese channels it must use; it can only route around them. It is not a theater command, and it cannot direct the conventional war.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the seams between the American services, SOG, the CIA station and the combined task force, which a single raid force working through MACV J-3 would leave open: fewer raids planned through channels that leak, fewer sources burned, fewer teams shot by friendly fire.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The headquarters is the price of using Delta well. Without it, Delta would work through MACV J-3 and the corps, its targets would pass through hands that leak, and its raids would collide with the task force's and SOG's. With it, a small joint staff keeps the targets in American hands, clears the ground for each raid in hours, and shares what the raids bring back. It adds nothing new to the war's knowledge. It adds speed and order to a scalpel. It is also the frame for the units the user will add later, and its value will grow or shrink with them.
