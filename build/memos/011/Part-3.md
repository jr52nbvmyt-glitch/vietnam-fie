## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I took over on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war being lost, and for eleven years of this record it stayed the war I took over, made better inside and lost on the same day. This memorandum is the first that changes the day. It does so without a soldier, a weapon or a reform in the theater. It changes three things in the United States: a burglary at the Watergate that never happens, a quarter of the young men the draft boards called who enlisted before their notices came, and a morning at Son My in March 1968 when nobody was killed. The first of them reached the place where the war was decided, which was the Independence Palace on 11 and 14 March 1975. The other two reached the American public and stopped there.

The Republic's interior was already not history's. On the first day it decreed land to the tiller, set up a commission against corruption, gave itself a rural police and province chiefs chosen by examination, and put a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks watched the enemy's rear, and B-52Js with guided bombs waited on Guam for targets Washington would let them strike. Every soldier carried a modern carbine, every regular squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman, and every post of the held belt a radio made in Saigon. Since 1957 every officer had been educated for his grade, every private trained to the standard of 2026, and every sergeant schooled for his. The Aid Compliance Board had made the army buy its cartridges, batteries and radios from its own plants at Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap from 1965. None of it changed which fights the enemy accepted, and all of it was lost in 1975 because the war was decided above it.

The volunteers began with the war. From 1964 about a quarter of the men the boards would have inducted enlisted first, under the ruling that they were the same men in the same jobs¹. In 1966–68 that was about 70,000–95,000 fewer induction notices a year, fewer families coerced, fewer draft trials and a quieter campus². It did not change a battle. The volunteers went where the casualties fell, as the draftees had, and died at the same rate; the war's American dead are history's on this account. In the main-force war of 1965–67 the Americans won their battles at the Ia Drang, in the Bong Son plain and along the DMZ, and the enemy declined every fight he did not want. The measured siege became his worst battle, Phung Hoang broke the Front's district committees in the held belt three years before history's, and the land programme titled most of the tenanted rice land of the Delta. The public's verdict on the war moved on the casualty lists, from about a quarter calling it a mistake to 47% in October 1967³, and the quieter draft did not reach it.

Tet came on its historical dates and turned on its historical battles. Six weeks later, on the morning of 16 March 1968, Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry landed at My Lai 4 in Son My village, searched the hamlet, burned the bunkers under the houses and was lifted out in the afternoon. Nobody was killed. That is the stipulation, isolated: nothing inside the war prevents the massacre, and nothing inside the war is changed to explain it¹. The conditions that historians found behind it stayed in Quang Ngai: a division assembled hastily, a body count that measured success, free-fire areas on the Batangan and harassment fire on the hamlets at night. Son My's four to five hundred people went on living under the Front's administration, paying its taxes and sending its young men to the 48th Local Force Battalion. The Republic gained nothing in Son Tinh District it could count. The Front lost the story it would have told in every other village. On 31 March the President stopped the reinforcement and the bombing and withdrew from the election, as history's did, and in November a successor committed to withdrawal was elected.

The war of the drawdown in 1969–71 was quieter at home for two reasons that never met in a single event. The Moratorium and the Mobilization of October and November 1969 marched as history's did, but in a November whose news was the President's speech to the silent majority and the first withdrawals, because there was no Hersh story to publish and no Haeberle photographs of a ditch. The antiwar movement lost its most powerful image of what the fighting did to the villages and kept its argument, which was the war itself; the share of Americans calling it a mistake averaged about 55% in 1969–70 and reached 60% in 1971 as it had historically³. There was no Peers inquiry, no Calley trial and no national argument in the spring of 1971 about a lieutenant. The cost of that silence was the inquiry that never came: the Army was never made to look at the command climate of the Americal Division, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 to examine how the body count shaped operations was not taken up, because without a scandal nobody above MACV wanted it. In the rifle companies of the drawdown, ten to fifteen men in a company of the 196th Brigade had chosen to come, and in the platoons that also had a schooled sergeant and a battalion that had trained together the inspector general counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer drug and refusal cases. It changed no fragging the record names. In July 1971 Colby went before the House subcommittee with an audit of Phoenix and without the shadow of Son My over the room, and the subcommittee asked whether the audit could be trusted rather than whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means.

The year 1972 runs as history's. The Easter Offensive was stopped at An Loc and Kontum by American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts and lost at Quang Tri by the orders of the corps and the division. On 14 November Nixon wrote to Thieu that he would take swift and severe retaliatory action if Hanoi violated the agreement³. Nobody broke into the Democratic National Committee's offices, on 28 May or on 17 June, and nothing in 1972 turned on that: Nixon won the election as history's did, and the agreement was signed in Paris on 27 January 1973. The Americans were out by the end of March.

The line bends in 1973, and it bends less than it seems to. In June Congress passed the Case–Church amendment by 325–86 and 73–16, as history's had, because this vote was not about the President: the prisoners were home, the troops were out, and 60% of Americans called the war a mistake³³. The bombing of Cambodia ended on 15 August, and with it American combat in Indochina, by law. The Senate's Watergate hearings never opened, there was no Saturday Night Massacre, and the War Powers override failed in the House by a handful of votes. That changed little, because Case–Church already denied the President the bombers his letters had promised. The binding constraint on American support moved from the President to Congress. In Hanoi the Twenty-first Plenum resolved in October to continue by revolutionary violence and the PAVN rebuilt on the historical schedule, with about a tenth more to make good from 1972 and filling it within a season.

The difference was in the money, and the money was in the vote of 1974. History's President spent the spring and summer of 1974 fighting impeachment and resigned on 9 August; this war's President spent them on the Republic's appropriation. He made Major General Murray's warning his own argument before the House defense subcommittee on 21 May, and he had 15–30 Republican votes in the House that history's Watergate summer had sent home to defend themselves. The House cut the request anyway, and the conference report gave the RVNAF about $950 million for fiscal 1975 against history's $700 million. Because the Republic's own plants stretched every dollar of propellant and parts, that bought about what $1.2 billion had bought on the historical course, the upper bound marked because it multiplies two judgments that lean the same way. From the autumn the margin went where the domestic stretch had never reached: aviation fuel and parts for the VNAF, diesel for the relief columns and 155mm and 175mm rounds that Bien Hoa could not forge. The VNAF's grounded aircraft were about a third fewer by December. The Politburo had watched a President who did not fall all summer, and it cut the B-2 Front's and the Tri-Thien front's autumn raids back to Phu Loc alone. The 1st Division retook the Phu Loc hills in the third week of November instead of on 10 December, and I judge that the Republic lost about 1,500–4,000 fewer dead in 1974 than history's 30,000 on this provision's account. The midterm of 5 November cost the President's party about 22 House seats and 3 Senate seats rather than 49 and 5, which left a Congress that still wanted Indochina finished and could no longer override him.

The enemy answered on his own timeline and through his own argument. Tran Van Tra, the B-2 Front's commander, argued in Hanoi in November for an attack on Phuoc Long as a test of the American response, against a Politburo with more reason to fear the answer than history's had, and he won. Phuoc Long fell on 6 January 1975 as it had historically, because Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without bombers, and because the PAVN's 130mm guns and T-54s decided the siege. The next day Nixon sent Congress the message history's President never sent: $300 million and authority under Case–Church to use American air power against a major offensive. The authority was dead within the month; in March Congress voted about $150 million. Hanoi, Washington and Saigon each read the message correctly, and Thieu's reading was the one that mattered. The authority was refused, but the money was coming, and he concluded that he should not give up a province to make it go further.

That conclusion was in the room on 11 March 1975, the day after Ban Me Thuot fell, when General Cao Van Vien presented the Dalat study for a withdrawal to a defensible Republic. Thieu refused it. There was no order on 13 March to give up I Corps and no order on 14 March to abandon the Highlands by Route 7. This is where history broke. Truong held I Corps on a plan that began with the families, moving about 70,000 dependants of the 1st Division and the Marines by sea behind the Hai Van, and the My Chanh line held for three weeks. The 2nd Division, whose families had not been moved, came apart in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin as history's I Corps had. Hue was withdrawn in order between 14 and 18 April, and Da Nang was held. In the Highlands the counter-attack from Phuoc An broke on 18 March. Kontum was given up on 27 March in an ordered withdrawal down Route 14, and Pleiku stood a three-week siege on what the appropriation had bought. On 2 and 5 April the 44th Regiment and the 4th Ranger Group broke the 320A's assault battalions on the southern perimeter. On 14 April Phu broke out down Route 19 with the soldiers' families at the head of the column, and about 11,000 of 25,000 regulars and Rangers reached the coast, with about 9,000 civilians lost against history's 155,000 on Route 7.

The Highlands fell on 19 April instead of 25 March, and Hue on 18 April instead of 25 March. On 25 March the Politburo had no double collapse to resolve on. Le Duan took Van Tien Dung's view over Tra's argument for a thrust in May: an assault into the rains with two corps against five divisions would be the error of 1968, and the 1st Corps was sent south to assembly areas to wait. The critical path that MT01 named needed a delay of about three weeks in the collapse of I and II Corps. It got three to five weeks, and the delay came not from the arsenal or the officers but from a decision at the top that history's President took one way and this war's took the other. Xuan Loc was not attacked. Thieu did not resign, Minh did not take office, and on 29 April Corporal McMahon and Lance Corporal Judge stood their watch at the Defense Attaché Office compound and went off it.

The price of the delay was the plan Thieu refused. The Dalat study would have brought the Highlands force out in the dry season without a fight; the siege brought out half of it after one, and the Republic's reserve spent the spring at Da Nang, five hundred kilometres from the capital. On 6 May Vien sent the study back as a plan for the defence of Saigon, and Thieu took its method. Operation SOUTHERN WALL lifted about 70,000 soldiers and 250,000 civilians out of Da Nang between 20 and 31 May, the families first, and the Airborne and Marines were rebuilt at Vung Tau and Long Binh as the reserve the study had asked for in November 1974. Qui Nhon went in June and Tuy Hoa in July. Congress voted the refugee money and $175 million for the RVNAF in May, and about $800 million for fiscal 1976 in November. In Hanoi the rains were spent bringing the 1st Corps south, extending the pipeline to Loc Ninh and asking Moscow to ship the 130mm ammunition and the tanks of 1976 in the autumn of 1975, which Moscow did, while Beijing sent rice and trucks and no more. The resolution of October 1975 set the general offensive on Saigon for the first months of 1976, with five corps.

The war ends where `history/endstate.toml` says it ends: on 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south under the President it has had since 1967, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976. This is rung (b), dependent survival, by the test of the ladder, because Saigon stands past 30 April 1975. In its own terms it is a reprieve of about a year, bought by an American President who stayed in office long enough to ask Congress for money it would partly give. It was spent by a South Vietnamese President who believed, on that evidence, that he need not give anything up. Every provision before this one made the Republic better inside and did not touch the level at which it was lost. This one touched that level from Washington. It gave the Republic a season and could not give it American power.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

None of the package bites in 1964–65. Watergate and My Lai lie years ahead. The volunteers begin with the phase, but draft calls before the summer of 1965 were small and the American war was advisory, so a quarter of a small number enlisting first changes no unit, no casualty and no vote. The Tonkin Gulf Resolution passed in August 1964 on the administration's account of the incidents and owed nothing to the draft. No end vector in the phase moves.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

The volunteers act here, and below a step. Ruling A puts the same men in the same jobs, so the main-force war of 1965–67 has the same American units, tactics and dead. The domestic effect is real: tens of thousands fewer induction notices a year from 1966, fewer trials and a quieter campus. But the public's verdict moved on the casualty lists, from about 24% calling the war a mistake to 47% in October 1967³, and the notices spared were not what moved it. The effect accumulates into 1968 and is judged there, in Phase III's end vector, where the draft calls peaked.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +2..+4 · POP.gvn_legitimacy +2..+4 · VCI.strength -7..-5 · ENEMY.engagement_control -4..-2 · FIND.main_force +2..+4 · FIND.vci +8..+10 · ARVN.effectiveness +8..+10 · US.political_tolerance +0..+1 · US.political_tolerance.public +0..+2.*

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war, and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end⁴.

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone⁵.

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way; Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington. The difference is in the Delta and in the hamlets. The Front enters the offensive with a smaller base in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre, recruits fewer men there in 1968, and loses the towns of the western Delta faster because the hamlets around them warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers. When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled, and the Commission audits the new Phoenix programme against the land register⁶. The American political calendar is not changed by any of it.

The police changes Tet in the same places and in the same way: in the held belt of the Delta the Front's urban cadres are known and its guides do not arrive, and in Hue its lists are better because the Republic's own census fed them. Phoenix, here, is only the name Washington learned for a programme whose district centres had run since 1964 without quotas and under an audit.

Above all this the radar watches the border, and at Khe Sanh it gives the headquarters and the White House the most complete picture of an enemy concentration either has ever held, while the forces attacking the cities move among the Tet crowds where no radar can tell them from anyone else. After the halt it measures exactly how the North uses it, and Commando Hunt opens with a second ledger beside the Air Force's claims.

The ARVN and the territorials go into Tet with the weapons the historical ARVN received only after it. In the district towns, which the first wave attacked with local forces, about a third of the attacks never get inside the compound walls; in the cities, where the PAVN and the main force fight block by block, the rifles lower the cost and do not shorten the battles. The mobilization of June arms its new territorials from stock within the month.

In the hills of Quang Tri the siege of Khe Sanh is measured every night and its trenches destroyed before they reach the wire; the PAVN pulls back about two weeks early, at a much heavier price, and the garrison loses about a third fewer men. It is the most complete American victory of the war on the ground and changes nothing in Washington; the brigade pulled from Thua Thien's hamlets to win it is missing when the Front returns to them. Abrams refuses to make the victory a doctrine of border bases and closes Khe Sanh on the historical date, and Commando Hunt opens against a panhandle net built since 1966 to be cut.

The district centres change Tet in the held belt in one more way: the leaders who came into the Delta towns to direct the uprising had been charted for three years, and a third of them were taken in February. In Hue the province centre's informant cards burn in the first hour and the district centres' do not. The American battalions that fight the offensive have been together for months, and the advisers beside the ARVN have been in their posts for a year; the battles are shorter at the edges and the same at their centres.

The officer reforms meet Tet at the battalions and the companies, which is where they had worked. The ARVN units that fought in the towns had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and in IV and III Corps they recalled their men from leave faster and fought the first day closer to their strength⁷. The mobilization of June finds its platoon leaders without the historical crash course, because the field commissions of 1966–68 had already made about 3,000 lieutenants from the army's sergeants, and the new territorials pay for it in sergeants. COSVN, reading its failure in the towns, opens a proselytizing campaign aimed at the officers the board had passed over. At Hue and Khe Sanh the battles are the historical ones, and the decision of 31 March is unchanged.

The private soldier in Tet is trained, and the mobilization of June finds a training base that will not send men to units untrained. In the district towns the RF companies that hold their compound walls are firing as the territorial course taught them, and in the towns and the cities the wounded of both allied armies are alive in larger numbers in the morning. In June the Joint General Staff wants the ten-week course cut to six to put the mobilization's men into the field by August; the training command holds it at ten by running the centres in two shifts, and the new battalions and territorial companies reach the field about a month later than history's and trained, with a man who has held the rank of sergeant in about four new PF platoons in five rather than two in three. The Front's third offensive goes, as its earlier offensives had, for what the course did not reach: the People's Self-Defense Force.

The sergeants of both armies meet Tet schooled. In the Delta the posts of Cai Lay that kept their listening posts out saw the 514th Battalion moving on My Tho before it arrived and were left alone; in the mobilization of June the empty squads and platoons are given to corporals who have passed the leader's course, and the share of new PF platoons without a schooled leader falls to about one in fifteen⁸. The price is the academy's: its course is cut to four weeks for the duration, and its best instructors go to the new platoons.

#### The siege of Khe Sanh
*21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province; Route 9 in Laos to Ban Houei Sane · changed from history · EV000-41*
*Initiative: enemy*

In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion⁹.

In this war the concentration was watched from the day it began. From mid-December the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos and the trucks of Group 559 turning east from Tchepone toward Ban Houei Sane, about 20–25 a night rising to about 80 by mid-January. The U-2S's signals payload fixed the headquarters of the 325C Division northwest of Hill 881 North on 2 January and that of the 304th near Co Roc, across the border, on 11 January, and from the volume and pattern of their traffic the J-2 analysts identified a front headquarters forming above them. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration this command had ever held. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", read it as confirmation, and after Con Thien he read it as an opportunity: here, he told his staff on 13 January, the enemy would give us the battle we could win⁹. The radar plots went every morning to Saigon and every evening to the White House, where the President kept a terrain model of Khe Sanh in the Situation Room¹⁰. The 1st Cavalry Division moved north to I Corps in January, as it did historically, and in this war a brigade of the 101st Airborne Division moved with it, drawn from the coastal districts of Thua Thien where it had been working with the Regional Forces¹¹.

The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that would attack the cities on 30–31 January moved into them in small groups, on foot and by bus and sampan among the millions travelling home for Tet, under the eyes of their own people; nothing in the radar picture warned of Saigon, Hue or the provincial towns. General Frederick Weyand's decision of 10 January to pull fifteen battalions back toward Saigon rested, as it did historically, on signals and ground intelligence about the enemy's units in III Corps¹⁰. The radar deepened the American fixation on Khe Sanh, which history had without it; the siege box, by promising a victory there, deepened it again.

The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump. The answer was Operation Niagara, and in this war Niagara had two halves. The first was the historical one: about 60 B-52s on an average day, sensors and reconnaissance, more than 158,000 artillery rounds in the first three months, and boxes on the valleys, the parks and Route 9 in Laos that the radar had seen fill during the night⁹¹⁰. It fell as a carpet half as dense as the Big Belly's, because the J carries about half the D's unguided bombs, and in tonnage of unguided bombs Niagara delivered about two-thirds of its historical weight¹². The second half was new. From 23 January a Global Hawk stood over the Khe Sanh plateau every night, and its radar imaged the ground within three kilometres of the perimeter each hour; Sorrell had moved a detachment of the coordinate cell to Da Nang, and from the night of 26 January cells of three Js arrived every ninety minutes, each carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s aimed at positions measured that night¹¹.

What the PAVN had come to do at Khe Sanh, it did by digging. Historically the trenches of the 304th and 325C Divisions crept toward the perimeter from mid-February and reached within a few dozen metres of the wire on the east, where the 37th Rangers held⁹. Here the trench lines were imaged as they were dug. A trench opened at dusk was on the cell's board before midnight and under a GBU-38 before dawn, and from 15 February the cells were cleared to bomb as close as 400 metres to the Marines and Rangers, inside the kilometre that history's carpets kept¹². The 304th Division dug three times toward the eastern perimeter, on 17, 22 and 27 February, and each time its trenches were destroyed at 500–800 metres. On the night of 29 February, when a regiment of the 304th formed to assault the Rangers' sector, it formed in the open beyond the trenches it had not been able to keep, and the GBU-38 cells and the Marine artillery broke it before it reached the wire, as they did historically, at a higher cost to the attackers. The PAVN's guns at Co Roc across the border, 130mm and 152mm pieces rolled out of caves to fire, were struck at their firing positions by GBU-31(V)3/Bs within hours of each mission, and by mid-March they fired from Co Roc only at night and only a few rounds a gun¹¹.

On the night of 6–7 February the panhandle watch tracked a column of eleven or twelve slow vehicles leaving the Ban Houei Sane area eastward on Route 9, and the warning reached the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei about forty minutes before twelve PT-76s of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, attacked it at about 0030 on 7 February. The defenders knocked out seven tanks; the camp still fell, its last fight a matter of metres in which no bomb could help, and of about 500 CIDG some 160 were killed or missing and of 24 Americans eight were killed¹⁰. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, and the air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters⁹. Here the flak around the approaches, measured each night, was struck each night, and I estimate the air bridge lost about a third fewer helicopters¹¹.

The front command under Major General Tran Quy Hai pulled the 325C Division back from the hills in mid-March and the 304th from the eastern trenches before the end of the month, about two weeks before its historical withdrawal. Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July⁹. I estimate that the garrison lost about a third fewer killed than the historical one, about 180, and that the PAVN's dead were about half again its acknowledged figure, perhaps 3,500–4,000 to July; both are judgments built on the historical figures and the change in what the bombs could reach, not counts. In this war Khe Sanh was the worst battle the PAVN fought in 1968, and the most complete American victory of the war on the ground. It was won at the place Hanoi had chosen, on the border, while the war was decided in the cities and in Washington; and the brigade taken from Thua Thien's hamlets to help win it was not in Thua Thien when the Front's local forces returned to them in February¹¹.

#### The Tet Offensive: the first wave
*30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-42*
*Initiative: enemy*
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital⁵.

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed⁵.

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces. In IV Corps it goes differently in two of them. At Can Tho, the corps headquarters on the Bassac, the battalions that attacked the town and the Binh Thuy air base moved up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh that GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left in the hands of their own Popular Forces; PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, two of them fought, and the 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters, and Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, with far less of the town destroyed than on the historical course. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days. At Ben Tre, in Kien Hoa, the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta, where the government had titled little outside the town because it held little outside the town, the battle and the destruction were as they were historically. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964. Allied and enemy losses for the country as a whole are not materially changed by it, and I do not adjust them⁶.

The police and the chiefs changed the first wave in the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon, and changed it less than they might have, because the infrastructure they had driven from the villages had gone to the towns. In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced on 31 January to lead the uprising, and in the first two weeks of February arrested about 1,700 of them on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs. Where the Front's guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later: the battalions that attacked Can Tho waited on the night of 30–31 January for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal who never arrived, because both had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier. The ARVN 21st Division, whose desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its strength on the muster roll, and the half of the ARVN that was on Tet leave elsewhere was a smaller half in IV Corps. In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands, where the police counted for little against PAVN regiments and VC main-force battalions, the battle went as it did historically¹³.

The rifles changed the first wave everywhere the ARVN and the territorials fought, and most where the fight was small. Historically most of the ARVN went into Tet with the M1 Garand and the carbine, the M16 having reached only the Airborne, the Marines and a few regiments in 1967, and the Regional and Popular Forces with weapons of the Korean War; American officers watched ARVN soldiers in Saigon and Hue fight with eight-round rifles against attackers with AK-47s and B40s, and the M16 airlift to the ARVN was one of the first decisions after the offensive. In this war every ARVN soldier and every territorial had carried a modern carbine for four years. The difference is clearest in the district towns, where the first wave was made by VC local-force battalions and companies against garrisons of an RF company or two, a PF platoon and the district police. Of the 64–72 district towns attacked historically, I estimate that the attackers penetrated about two-thirds on this course against nearly all historically, and that the ones they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, as at Long Huu in 1964. At Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st with one squad gun lent by the 7th Division and its own carbines, and the battalion, which had planned to be through the town by 0400, was still in the market when the 7th Division's 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived at 0730. In the provincial capitals and the cities, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments and the fighting was block by block, the rifles changed casualties and not outcomes. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,500, against 2,788 historically, and do not adjust the enemy's losses, which were never measured to a precision the change would show in¹⁴.

Phung Hoang added a layer to that harvest. The province committees of Phong Dinh, Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long had sent their senior cadres into the towns to direct the uprising, the men who were to form the "revolutionary committees" of each city, and the PIOCCs at Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long had carried charts of those committees since 1965 with photographs from the biometric enrolment of their arrested relatives and ralliers. Of about 140 province- and district-committee cadres who came into the three towns, about 50 were taken by 15 February, most of them on the identifications of ward police who had their photographs, and the Can Tho "revolutionary committee" never held its first meeting. The ARVN battalions that cleared the three towns had American advisers who had been with them for about a year, and in Can Tho the 21st Division's 1st Battalion, 31st Regiment had an adviser, Captain Jerome Pike, who took the call of a PF corporal on the Xa No canal in Vietnamese at 2340 on 30 January and moved the battalion's reaction company to the university before the attackers reached it. The American units committed to Tet in IV Corps, the 9th Division's 2nd Brigade and the Mobile Riverine Force, had been together since their arrival in 1967 and fought the streets of My Tho and Vinh Long as the units they had trained as¹⁵. None of this touched Saigon, Hue or the Highlands, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments that no district centre had ever charted.

The officer reforms changed the first wave in the ARVN's battalions, and changed it most where the battalions were in the towns. By January 1968 about four in five ARVN battalion commanders had been chosen by the board and had held their battalions for more than a year; the order that sent half the army on Tet leave was the Joint General Staff's, and the board's commanders obeyed it, but in IV and III Corps they had recall plans written to the career course's pattern, and by the evening of 31 January the battalions of the 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions were at about 70 percent of their strength in the towns against about half historically. The Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, whose commanders the board had reviewed since July 1967, fought the district compounds under lieutenants many of whom had been sergeants in the same companies two years before. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,300 rather than about 2,500, a judgment on faster recall and steadier companies that I hold loosely, and I do not adjust the enemy's losses⁷. At Hue the 1st Division under Truong fought as it fought historically, and its commander had been the list's first name since 1966.

The basic course changed the first wave in the same places the rifles did, and in one place they did not: the wounded. Every ARVN soldier and territorial who fought on 31 January had passed the ten-week or the six-week course, and the RF companies on the compound walls of the district towns fired as the territorial course taught, in pairs, with ammunition kept for the second rush; at Cai Lay the 311th RF Company still had about a third of its basic load at 0600, which is why it was still firing at 0730 when the 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived. I estimate that the attackers were held out of perhaps a few more district towns than the rifles and the officers had already kept them from, and I do not put a number on it. The larger difference was in the night after the assaults, in the towns and in Saigon, where the wounded lay in the streets for hours before anyone could reach them. Historically the ARVN's wounded in the offensive were about three times its dead⁵; in this war more of those wounded with an arm or leg torn by a B40 fragment or a burst of AK-47 fire were alive at dawn because the man beside them had put on a tourniquet. I put the ARVN's killed in the first phase at about 2,150 rather than about 2,300, and the American killed in the first phase at about 30–60 fewer, at the rate shown at the Ia Drang. In Hue, where the wounded of the 1st Division and the Marines lay in the Citadel's streets for a month of fighting, the course saved men every day of it, and the surgeons at Phu Bai complained, as Nha Trang's had in 1965, about the tourniquets left on too long¹⁶. The enemy's losses I do not adjust.

#### The posts of Cai Lay at Tet
*30 Jan – 2 Feb 1968 · Cai Lay and Cai Be Districts, Dinh Tuong Province; the post at Ap My Loi, My Phuoc Tay; the posts along the canal and Route 4 east to My Tho · new · EV010-4*
*Initiative: enemy*

The Front's battalions came through Cai Lay to attack My Tho, and most of the posts on the way were left alone. The 514th Local Force Battalion and the 261st and 263rd Main Force Battalions moved east toward the province capital on the nights of 29–31 January, along the canals north of Route 4, and the Dinh Tuong command's order was to isolate the posts on the route rather than take them, because the battalions needed to reach My Tho intact. Of about 34 PF posts in Cai Lay District, three were attacked and nine more were mortared to keep their platoons inside their wire. The post at Ap My Loi was not attacked at all. Its two-man listening post on the canal bank, kept out every night since 1964, reported at 2310 on 30 January a column of about 200 men with heavy weapons moving east on the far bank, and Sergeant Le Van Muoi sent the report up with the count and the time; it reached the 7th Division at Cai Lay at 0140, and with two other posts' reports it told the division that a battalion was going for My Tho, not for the district town. It changed nothing at My Tho, which the 261st, 263rd and 514th attacked at 0310 on 31 January and which was retaken in three days by the ARVN 7th Division and the US 9th Division's riverine battalions, as historically. It did keep the 247th RF Company off Route 4 that night, under Liem's rule of 1967⁸.

The post that fell was the one whose leader had held the platoon for three months. At Ap Hau Thanh, on the canal four kilometres east of Ap My Loi, the platoon's sergeant had been commissioned in the autumn of 1967 and the platoon was led by an acting leader, a corporal first class who had passed the leader's course and had not yet been given the platoon leader's course. He kept no listening post out on the night of 31 January, because the post had not been attacked in two years; a company of the 514th came over the wall at 0200 with satchel charges, and the post lost nine killed and its radio and weapons. Across the two districts in the three nights, the posts that kept their listening posts out were mortared and left; the one that did not was taken. The enemy chose which, and he chose by watching the routine⁸.

#### The battle for Saigon and the attack on the US embassy
*31 Jan – 7 Mar 1968 · Saigon, Cholon and Tan Son Nhut · historical · EV000-43*
At 02:45 on 31 January a 19-man team of the VC C-10 Sapper Battalion blew a hole in the wall of the new US embassy compound in Saigon and fought inside the grounds for more than six hours without entering the chancery; the compound was secured by 09:20, and five Americans were killed. The sappers were all killed or captured. Other VC units attacked Tan Son Nhut air base with three battalions, the Joint General Staff compound, the Presidential Palace and the naval headquarters, and held the national radio station for about six hours, though they could not broadcast⁵. The image of the embassy under attack, broadcast in the United States hours after Westmoreland had declared the enemy's offensive capacity broken, did more political damage than any battle of the war.

The fighting in Saigon lasted weeks in Cholon, the Chinese quarter, where VC battalions held out around the Phu Tho racetrack. On 4 February residents were ordered out and the area declared a free-fire zone; the last battle in Cholon was fought on 7 March⁵. On 1 February the national police chief, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shot a captured VC officer in the head in a Saigon street before a photographer and a film camera. The image became, with the embassy, the American public's picture of Tet.

#### The battle of Hue
*31 Jan – 2 Mar 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · changed from history · EV000-44*
*Initiative: enemy*
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division¹⁷.

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west and did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February¹⁷. The ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel; the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March.

The squad weapons made Hue somewhat less costly to retake and no shorter. On the first night the Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound with M4A1s and four squad guns against the 800th and 802nd Battalions' assault teams, where historically it held with M16s it had only recently received; the difference was small, because the Hac Bao was among the few ARVN units historically re-armed by then. Across the river the Marines fought the street battle with two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman with an M110A2 who, in a city where the PAVN held the upper floors and the far side of every street, could put rounds into a window at 200 metres from cover that a squad's rifles could not reach. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines' after-action report credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and the public health building between 4 and 6 February. What took the blocks was what took them historically: the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, tear gas and men going through walls. The weather, the Citadel's walls and the PAVN's reinforcement from the west set the length of the battle, and the rifles touched none of them. I put the ARVN dead at about 410 and the American at about 215, against 458 and 250 historically¹⁴.

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded, on the historical course. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800¹⁷. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war.

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966, and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor¹³.

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order that this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred: every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a rehearsal every quarter¹³. In the provinces the plan was rehearsed. Whether it would be carried out on the day it was needed was a question for another year.

The Thua Thien province centre was in the province headquarters south of the river, which the attackers held from 31 January until the 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines retook it on 6 February¹⁷. Its informant cards were not in the attackers' hands. The PIOCC's chief, Major Ton That Dinh Lang, had kept the cards of the province's 340 informants in a single safe, against Hieu's rule, because he did not trust his district centres' security; when the attack reached the compound wall at about 0330 on 31 January he burned the cards in the safe with a can of gasoline and was killed an hour later on the stairs. The district centres of Huong Thuy and Phu Vang, outside the city, kept their copies and lost them when their offices were overrun on 31 January and 1 February; the Hue city security section's lists for the southern suburbs included about 60 informants and centre staff named from those files, of whom about 40 were among the dead found in the graves at Phu Thu and Da Mai Creek¹⁵. The national file in Saigon, which held every one of those names, was never in danger in 1968. It was the first time the record showed what a fused file was worth to the enemy, and the destruction order of 9 March was written with the Huong Thuy file in front of it.

#### The reinforcement request and the decision of 31 March
*20 Feb – 31 Mar 1968 · Saigon and Washington · historical · EV000-45*
On 12 February Westmoreland asked for 10,500 more troops; on 13 February a brigade of the 82nd Airborne Division and a Marine regimental landing team, already authorized, were sent. On 20 February the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Earle Wheeler, went to Saigon, and returned with a request for 206,000 more men, framed by the Chiefs to force a mobilization of the reserves. On 28 February McNamara left office and on 1 March Clark Clifford succeeded him, charged with reviewing the request⁵. On 10 March the *New York Times* reported it. On 12 March Senator Eugene McCarthy, running against the war, took 42 percent of the vote in the New Hampshire primary; on 16 March Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race. On 25–26 March the senior outside advisers, the "Wise Men", told the President that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.

On 31 March Johnson announced in a televised address that he was stopping the bombing of North Vietnam except in the area just north of the DMZ, that he would send only about 13,500 more troops, that the United States was ready to talk, and that he would not seek or accept his party's nomination for another term¹⁸. The speech capped the American commitment. Westmoreland's strategy of attrition by American forces had been judged by the President's own advisers to have no end that the country would accept, and from this point the questions were how to get out, and how to leave the South able to stand.

#### ◆ The My Lai massacre
*16 Mar 1968 · Son My village (My Lai 4 and My Khe), Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · no longer happens · EV000-46*
*Initiative: allied*
On the morning of 16 March 1968 Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Brigade, Americal Division, operating under Task Force Barker, was landed by helicopter at the hamlet of My Lai 4 in Son My village, in an area of Quang Ngai long held by the VC 48th Local Force Battalion. It met no resistance. In this war its men searched the hamlet, burned the bunkers they found under the houses, questioned the old men through the interpreter, and were lifted out in the afternoon; Company B, 4th Battalion, 3rd Infantry searched My Khe the same way. Nobody was killed. The massacre that history records on that morning, in which between 347 people, the US Army's figure, and 504, the Vietnamese figure, mostly old men, women and children, were killed in their homes, on the paths and in an irrigation ditch¹⁹, does not happen in this war, by the user's stipulation and for no reason inside the war that this record can name¹. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson flew his OH-23 over the hamlets that morning on an ordinary scout mission².

What does not follow is as large as what does not happen. There is no report of 128 Viet Cong killed, no brigade investigation conceding about 20 accidental civilian deaths, no year and a half of concealment, and nothing for Ronald Ridenhour to write to Congress about in March 1969¹⁹. Task Force Barker's sweep entered the brigade's log as a dry hole, one of dozens that spring in Son Tinh District, where the 48th Battalion had left before the helicopters landed. The conditions that historians found behind the massacre stayed in the province: a division assembled hastily, a command climate that measured success by bodies, free-fire areas on the Batangan peninsula and harassment fire on the hamlets at night. The removal is isolated by ruling, and every other killing of civilians in Quang Ngai that year stays where history put it. Son My's people, about four to five hundred more than history's by the end of the day, went on living under the Front's administration, paying its taxes and sending its young men to its local force².

#### The battle of Dai Do
*30 Apr – 3 May 1968 · Dai Do and the hamlets north of Dong Ha on the Cua Viet, Quang Tri Province · historical · EV000-47*
At the end of April, as the second wave of the offensive began, battalions of the PAVN 320th Division, two of them identified as belonging to the 48th and 52nd Regiments, moved into the hamlets of Dai Do, Dinh To and their neighbours north of Dong Ha, threatening the Marine base and the Cua Viet supply route. The 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines under Lieutenant Colonel William Weise attacked across open paddies from amphibian tractors and boats, with the 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines, ARVN units of the 2nd Regiment and a mechanized battalion, and from 1 May the US 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry. The Marines lost 81 killed and the ARVN 5; the Americans claimed more than 600 PAVN killed, and the PAVN has not released its losses. By 3 May the PAVN had gone. Whether Dai Do was a main thrust against Dong Ha or a diversion is not settled; the frontal assaults of 1 and 2 May were costly enough to be criticized by later historians²⁰.

#### The Paris talks open
*May 1968 · Paris · historical · EV000-48*
After weeks of argument over a site, talks between the United States and North Vietnam opened in Paris in May 1968, with W. Averell Harriman leading for the United States and Xuan Thuy for Hanoi. They stalled at once: Hanoi demanded a complete and unconditional end to the bombing of the North before substantive talks, and refused to recognise the Saigon government; Saigon refused to sit with the Front²¹. Through the summer the talks were a forum for statements, while both sides fought for position on the ground. Their opening nonetheless changed the war's logic. From May 1968 both governments were fighting to shape a settlement as well as to win, and every offensive that followed had a negotiating purpose.

#### The May offensive in Saigon
*5 May – 15 Jun 1968 · Saigon and its outskirts, and towns throughout the South · historical · EV000-49*
On 5 May the PAVN and VC launched the second phase of the general offensive, which the Americans called "mini-Tet", with attacks by fire on more than 100 towns and installations and a renewed assault on Saigon. VC battalions fought their way into the southern and western districts of the capital and into Cholon, where they were destroyed or driven out by the ARVN and US forces over several weeks, with air strikes and artillery used inside the city; much of District 8 was destroyed and tens of thousands were made homeless. The phase lasted until 15 June⁵. Its losses fell heavily on the same local forces and cadres who had survived February, and many of the units that fought in Saigon in May were filled out with northern replacements. American deaths in May 1968 were the highest of any month of the war.

#### The evacuation of Kham Duc
*10–12 May 1968 · Kham Duc Special Forces camp and Ngok Tavak, Quang Tin Province · historical · EV000-50*
On 10 May the 40th Battalion of the PAVN 2nd Division's 1st Regiment overran the outpost of Ngok Tavak, held by an Australian-led company of the 11th MIKE Force and a battery of the 2nd Battalion, 13th Marines. The PAVN 1st and 21st Regiments then closed on the camp at Kham Duc, held by Special Forces Detachment A-105, CIDG and a SOG detachment, reinforced by the 2nd Battalion, 1st Infantry of the Americal Division. On 11 May Westmoreland approved evacuation, and on 12 May, under about 350 air sorties, roughly 1,500 soldiers and civilians were flown out. A C-130 flown by Major Bernard L. Bucher was shot down just after take-off, killing all 183 Vietnamese civilians aboard, a Special Forces officer and the five crewmen; a second C-130 was wrecked on the runway. Lieutenant Colonel Joe M. Jackson landed a C-123 on the abandoned strip to recover a three-man combat control team left behind, and received the Medal of Honor. The Americans lost about 42–46 killed and 31–33 missing and twelve aircraft; about 220 CIDG were killed or missing; the US claimed about 345 PAVN killed, and a later study estimates 1,500–2,000. The abandonment of the camp removed one of the few allied positions between the Laotian border and the lowlands of Quang Tin and Quang Nam, and its loss was one of the few clear PAVN victories over a US-defended position in 1968²².

#### Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned
*10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · changed from history · EV000-51*
*Initiative: allied*

On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy²³.

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969²⁴. Historically the expansion outran the rifles: the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better. In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment, about 260,000 carbines already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June against the year's expected growth, and were on the ranges within a month of induction. The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but sergeants: the training centres could zero a carbine in an afternoon and could not make a squad leader in less than a year, and Venn's successor reported in September that about a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before. The old weapons the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law¹⁴.

On Khe Sanh the decision was harder than it had been historically, because in this war the base had been a victory rather than an ordeal. Westmoreland, in his last weeks, argued for keeping it as the anchor of a line of border bases under the siege box, and the III Marine Amphibious Force's staff drew up a plan for three more such bases along Route 9 and the Laotian border, to be held by battalions drawn from the coastal districts. Abrams refused it on 15 June. His reason, in a message to Wheeler that his staff kept, was that a base the enemy would not attack was a base that held nothing, and that after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July⁹. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ. Here it was read in Hanoi correctly, as a decision by an American commander that the siege the PAVN had lost was not worth inviting again, and in the American press, less correctly, as a retreat from a battle won¹¹.

The officers for the mobilization came differently. Historically the Thu Duc candidate course was cut from 37 weeks to 24 in June 1968 to commission the platoon leaders the expansion needed²⁵. In this war the cut was to 32 weeks, and the gap was filled by the field commissions: about 3,000 sergeants had been commissioned since 1966 and about 2,500 more were commissioned in the second half of 1968, after the branch basic course that the decree did not allow to be waived. The cost was in the ranks they came from. The sergeant shortage that Venn's successor reported in September, a third of the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank, was in part the field commission's price: the army had made lieutenants of the sergeants who would have led those platoons⁷. The board's lists governed the new RF companies from their first month, and the new PF platoons not at all.

The mobilization met a training base that would not send men to units untrained, and the cost was a month. Historically the RVNAF in 1968 sent some draftees straight to units without basic training, and the ARVN basic course was cut from twelve weeks to nine to put men through faster. In this war, on 21 June, the Joint General Staff ordered the course cut to six weeks for the duration of the mobilization; the Central Training Command, with Coyle's successor and the Muster Service's chief behind it, answered that six weeks would leave out the Forge and the field week, the part the new soldier most needed, and proposed instead to run the 33 national and division centres in two daily shifts, which raised their capacity from about 65,000 to about 105,000 in cycle²⁶. The Joint General Staff accepted on 28 June. About 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended; about 40,000 of them waited three to six weeks at the centres for a cycle to open, and the new battalions and territorial companies reached the field about a month later than they did historically, after the third offensive had begun rather than before it. They reached it trained. The new RF companies and PF platoons of the summer went through the six-week territorial course as platoons, on the M4A1 they had drawn from the depots, and came back to their districts in September and October rather than August¹⁶.

The sergeants came differently too, and the result was not the one memorandum 007 predicted. The field commissions had made lieutenants of about 5,500 of the army's best sergeants by the end of 1968, and on the historical training base that would have left the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank. In this war the drill-sergeant corps had been returning sergeants to the battalions since 1966, about 650–1,800 a year, and in July 1968 the training command released about 1,100 more from the centres' second shift to the new territorial platoons, each a man who had trained a platoon of recruits for two years. Venn's successor reported in September that about a fifth of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank of sergeant, rather than about a third¹⁶. The People's Self-Defense Force, which the law raised in the same summer and which the course did not reach, was given its Garands and carbines and a few days of instruction by the district chiefs, as historically.

The schooled corporal filled what was left. The leader's course had been taught to corporals first class since 1957, regular and territorial, and in 1968 about two in three of the corporals first class in the regular battalions and about half of those in the RF companies had passed it. The training command gave the new PF platoons their leaders from that pool: where no sergeant could be found, the platoon was given a corporal first class who had passed the course and had served a year in an RF company, as acting platoon leader. Of the fifth of new platoons with no one who had held the rank of sergeant, about two in three got such a man, and Venn's successor reported in September that about one PF platoon in fifteen had no leader who had passed the course. The academy paid. The Joint General Staff ordered the leader's course cut to three weeks for the mobilization; the training command, on the precedent of the basic course, kept the field week and cut the classroom, and the course ran at four weeks from July 1968 to June 1969, with the academy's second shift taught by instructors who had been platoon sergeants in 1967⁸. In the regular battalions, about two in three of the squads the commissions had emptied were held by acting leaders who had passed the course.

#### The third offensive
*17 Aug – 23 Sep 1968 · Provincial towns and bases, especially in III Corps and the Central Highlands · changed from history · EV000-52*
*Initiative: enemy*

The third phase of the general offensive began in mid-August with attacks by fire and ground assaults on towns and bases, including attacks around Tay Ninh in III Corps. It was weaker than its predecessors: the local forces that had carried the attacks of February and May were largely gone, and the main forces would not commit themselves against American firepower in the same way again. It ended in late September without lasting gains. Taken together, the three offensives of 1968 cost the VC about 50,000 dead by one estimate against about 4,000 US and 6,000 ARVN. Their recruitment in the South collapsed: in Region 5, 1,700 recruits in 1969 against 8,000 in 1968, in the Mekong lowlands 100 against 16,000. By the end of the year about 70 percent of the main-force troops in the South are reported to have been northerners. MACV estimated total PAVN and VC strength at 287,000 in January 1968 and 251,000 at its end⁵. The losses forced a reassessment in Hanoi that would carry into 1969.

In this war the third offensive runs as it did historically, in a part of the country the provisions barely touched. What changes is the arithmetic behind it. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968, 16,000 on the historical course, is about 11,000 here, because about a third of the tenant families who would have supplied the recruits had been titled by the government in districts it held at night, and the Front's own conscription in those districts had become coercion that the PF platoons could resist. The 1969 collapse comes from a lower base and is no less complete. The consequence is that COSVN begins filling its Delta main-force battalions with northern replacements in the summer of 1968, several months earlier than on the historical course, and the northerners who arrive in Chuong Thien and Kien Giang are strangers in hamlets where the Front's strength had always been that its soldiers were local. The proportion of northerners in the main forces at the year's end, about 70 percent historically⁵, is a few points higher here⁶.

COSVN read the year's failure in the towns, among other things, as a failure of its proselytizing among the ARVN's officers, and it chose a new target. A military proselytizing directive of August 1968, captured in Tay Ninh in December, ordered the province committees to work among "the officers whom the puppet promotion board has humiliated": the commanders relieved for cause, the majors passed over three times, the colonels kept from the list. It attributed the board, wrongly, to American selection of the ARVN's officers, and its propaganda leaflets of the autumn called the board's commanders "officers chosen by the Americans". About 30 relieved or passed-over officers were identified by the Military Security Service as contacted in 1968–69, and two were arrested as agents; the directive's yield was small and its cost to the Front was smaller. In the third offensive itself the attacks on Tay Ninh met the 25th Division's battalions in positions they had held since 1967, and the town was not entered⁷.

#### The self-defence hamlets
*17 Aug – 31 Oct 1968 · Duc Hue and Trang Bang Districts, Hau Nghia Province; Can Duoc and Rach Kien, Long An Province; Cai Be and Cai Lay Districts, Dinh Tuong Province · new · EV009-4*
*Initiative: enemy*

The third offensive went for what the course did not reach, and in the provinces around Saigon that was the People's Self-Defense Force. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee, planning the offensive of August after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, ordered its subregions to avoid the RF and PF posts that had held in February and May and to strike the new self-defence groups of the mobilization law, whose members were townsmen and farmers with a Garand and three days' instruction. The Front's subregion commands around Saigon had a second reason that the Military Affairs Committee did not need to state: every self-defence group destroyed told the hamlet that the government's new law armed it without protecting it¹⁶.

The attacks began on the night of 17–18 August and ran through September. In Duc Hue and Trang Bang the Front's local-force companies attacked about 30 self-defence groups in their hamlets, usually at night and from two sides, and overran about two-thirds of them; in Long An, around Can Duoc and Rach Kien, where NIGHT LANTERN had left the posts strong and the infrastructure weak, it attacked fewer and lost more, because the PF platoons beside the hamlets came out to the self-defence groups' calls. In Cai Be and Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong it went for the self-defence groups in the hamlets between the posts, where no platoon was near enough to come. Across the three provinces, by the end of October, I estimate that about 600–900 members of the self-defence groups were killed and about 1,500–2,000 of their weapons taken, almost all of them Garands and carbines of 1950 that the RVNAF had handed in since 1964. In the same weeks the RF and PF posts of the three provinces were attacked about a third less often than on the historical course, and held about as often as they had in May¹⁶.

The trained PF platoon could not give the self-defence group its own training, but at Rach Kien it showed what it could do for it. On the night of 2–3 September a company of the Front's 506th Battalion attacked the self-defence group of a hamlet a kilometre from the PF post at the Rach Kien canal bridge; the group's leader, a schoolteacher, fired a flare as the PF corporal had shown him, and the platoon came across the paddies in sixteen minutes, in pairs, firing and moving as the territorial course had taught it, and broke the attack at the hamlet gate. Two self-defence members died and four were wounded, all four with tourniquets the PF soldiers put on. In the next month the district chief of Can Duoc sent his PF platoon sergeants, the men the drill-sergeant corps had released in July, to give the self-defence groups of the district three evenings each of the first ten minutes, which no order required¹⁶. It was the only place in the three provinces where anyone did, and the only one where the groups held through the autumn.

The PF platoon sergeants whom the Can Duoc district chief sent to the self-defence groups were men who had passed the platoon leader's course, which taught a sergeant to train his own platoon and anyone the district put under him. On 24 October Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, the province chief, made Can Duoc's practice the province's: every PF platoon leader in Long An gave the self-defence groups within two kilometres of his post three evenings of the first ten minutes and the flare signal, and was rated on it. In November and December the Front's local companies attacked eleven self-defence groups in Long An and overran four, against about two in three in Hau Nghia in the autumn⁸. Hau Nghia's province chief did not follow until February 1969. The difference was a province chief's order and a platoon leader who had been taught to teach.

#### The bombing halt and the American election
*31 Oct – 5 Nov 1968 · Washington, Paris and Saigon · historical · EV000-53*
In October Hanoi signalled that it would accept the Saigon government at the table if the bombing stopped. On 31 October Johnson announced the end of all bombing of North Vietnam from 1 November, ending Rolling Thunder¹⁸. Thieu refused to join the talks on the terms agreed. Through Anna Chennault the Nixon campaign had encouraged him to wait for a better deal, and on 2 November she told the South Vietnamese ambassador to "hold on a while longer"; Johnson learned of it through intercepts and ordered the campaign watched, but did not make it public. Historians disagree on whether the channel changed anything, since Thieu had his own reasons to refuse. On 5 November Richard M. Nixon was elected president by a narrow popular margin. Talks in Paris did not resume in substance until the new year, after a dispute over the shape of the table was settled with a round table for the governments²¹.

Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder had flown 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine attack sorties, and lost 506 USAF, 397 Navy and 19 Marine aircraft over or near the North¹⁸. It ended without having achieved its purposes of breaking Hanoi's will or stopping the flow of men and supplies south.

#### The Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix
*1 Nov 1968 – Jan 1969 · The countryside of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-54*
*Initiative: allied*
On the historical course Phung Hoang, the Vietnamese programme to coordinate the attack on the VC infrastructure, had been established by decree in December 1967 out of the American ICEX programme of June 1967, and Thieu put his own decree behind it in July 1968. Its instruments were district intelligence centres, the CIA-run Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the police; its method was to identify, capture, turn or kill the cadres of the parallel administration²⁷. On 1 November 1968, at the urging of Komer and his successor William E. Colby, the government launched the Accelerated Pacification Campaign, a three-month drive to raise about 1,000 contested hamlets to relative security before any ceasefire could freeze the map, using RF and PF, Revolutionary Development cadre and the new People's Self-Defence Force, with regular forces screening.

The campaign met its targets by the Hamlet Evaluation System, a scale filled in by American district advisers under pressure to show progress, and in many places those hamlets did become more secure after dark, because the VC local forces that had protected the infrastructure had died in the offensives. Phoenix's own figures, 81,740 people "neutralized" between 1968 and 1972, 26,369 of them killed, were produced against monthly quotas that encouraged false arrests and padding, and about 87 percent of the killings credited to it were by ordinary military operations. Communist cadres later described 1968–72 as their hardest period, and some American advisers concluded that Phoenix had been a mortal threat in some provinces and a paper programme in others²⁷.

In this war the campaign is launched on the historical date under the same men, and Phoenix is not launched at all, because Phung Hoang has run in every district since 1965 without quotas. Thieu's decree of July 1968 is issued, as a reaffirmation that adds his name to Khanh's. Three things are different. The district intelligence centres have worked from the land register since 1964, which by November 1968 records about 900,000 titled hectares and the households that hold them; for the first time a district knew who lived in each titled hamlet, and who had left it for the Front, from a record compiled for another purpose and not for the quota. And the Commission Against Corruption's inspectors, under the decree of 1964 rather than an agreement Colby made, had sampled the neutralization reports of 40 districts in IV and III Corps each quarter since 1965 and checked each name against the register, the province prison rolls and the PRU and police reports. The sample reported in February 1969, the sixteenth, found about one claim in seven unsupported across the 40 districts: fewer than on the earlier course, because there were no quotas to pad and the districts had learned since 1965 that a name would be checked. False arrests for ransom were still cases before the Special Court, about 30 in the quarter¹⁵. The register had been made public in 1964 only as parcel numbers, after Can Giuoc; in 1968 it was used, for the first time, as a list of persons, and the Front's propaganda said so⁶.

The third is that Phung Hoang was not a new instrument here but an old one with a new urgency. The centres had sat in the Special Branch's district offices since 1964, and the Special Branch had been running target folders in the held belt since then¹³. In the held belt the unsupported share in the February sample was under a tenth; in the districts recovered after Tet, where the Accelerated Pacification Campaign pushed the centres into villages a few weeks old, it was about a quarter, and in the eleven districts where the province chiefs let the PRUs and the sector S2 build the files, about two-fifths. Without quotas the contested belt did not become a paper programme, but where the files were thin it became the raid on a rallier's grudge, as at Duc Thanh, and the Commission's cases came mostly from there. The audited count for 1968 was about 9,600 neutralized, with about 1,400 killed, of whom about 500 in the programme's own operations; on the historical course the claims for the year were larger and were never audited¹⁵. In the held belt it was a refinement of what the police had done for four years, and the Front's cadres there had left before it began.

#### The halt measured
*1 Nov – 31 Dec 1968 · The coastal road and railway from Thanh Hoa to Vinh and Dong Hoi, Route 15 to the Mu Gia pass, and the ferries at Quang Khe, North Vietnam · new · EV003-5*
The bombing of the North stopped on 1 November 1968. The Gulf watch did not. Its orbit, 40–60 nautical miles off the coast and outside the SA-2 belt, was a reconnaissance flight over international waters, and it went on counting the traffic on Route 1A, the coastal railway and Route 15 below the 19th parallel through November and December²⁸. What it saw changed within a week. Trucks that had moved only at night and in rain, in convoys of five or six, moved by day in columns of forty and fifty; the ferries at Quang Khe ran in daylight; the railway, repaired south of Thanh Hoa within three weeks, carried trains to Vinh. Southbound vehicle movements on Route 15 toward the Mu Gia pass in December were about three times the October level¹⁰.

The count went to Washington twice a week, as the bombing halt's terms required this command to report the North's use of it. It did not change the halt, which the President had traded for the opening of the expanded talks in Paris and which the President-elect did not reverse. It changed the next campaign. When Commando Hunt opened on 15 November, its planners knew from the radar how much more was coming toward the passes, and when and where, and the U-2S found the new truck parks and the new fuel dumps north of Mu Gia that the surge required¹⁰. The halt was Hanoi's to use, and it used it exactly as expected; the difference was that the use was measured as it happened, and the headquarters that would fight the dry season of 1968–69 knew the size of it on its first night.

#### Commando Hunt begins
*15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · changed from history · EV000-55*
*Initiative: allied*

On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night²⁹. In this war the Global Hawk's panhandle watch was the campaign's third sensor from its first night, and the AC-130s of the 16th Special Operations Squadron were sent to radar tracks as well as to sensor activations¹⁰.

The B-52Js and the coordinate cell were its fourth instrument. Historically Commando Hunt's interdiction points, the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes and the Ban Laboy ford, were struck by B-52 boxes and fighter-bombers night after night to crater them²⁹. Here they were cut with GBU-31s on measured coordinates, and the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Kong below them were sown with Quickstrikes set for the steel pontoons the North had brought in since 1966. The tankers freed by the J's range refuelled the F-4s working the passes, which flew about a quarter more sorties a night over the panhandle than the historical force could keep in the air¹². The interdiction points did not close. The enemy had spent two and a half years building a net with no single point of failure; at Ban Laboy the cell now counted fourteen fords and two underwater causeways, and on any night the J could cut the ones it had measured and the trucks crossed at the ones it had not¹¹.

Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000²⁹. Here they were not believed by this headquarters either. The radar counted the trucks entering the panhandle at Mu Gia and Ban Karai and leaving it toward the South on Routes 92 and 96, and by that measure Phase I destroyed or disabled about 2,600–3,200 trucks and reduced the tonnage reaching the South by about a sixth, not the third the claims implied. The J's crossings added little to that sixth, perhaps a fiftieth part of the flow, because the net they cut had been built to be cut¹¹. MACV J-2 sent the figure to Washington in May 1969 beside the Air Force's, and from then on the two numbers travelled together¹⁰. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it; this time the growth was counted.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent⁴.

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that. About 900,000 hectares have been titled, the Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier⁶. The American decision of 1968 is unchanged, and so is its timing.

The National Police numbers about 95,000 and the Special Branch has buried its first constables of the Tet battles; the infrastructure in the held belt is weaker than anywhere since 1959, and every file it keeps is now held under a destruction plan¹³.

The enemy's rear enters Phase IV counted: the surge after the halt is measured, the first dry season of Commando Hunt is scored against the radar as well as the claims¹⁰, and the American decision of 1968 is unchanged by any of it.

The RVNAF ends 1968 larger and armed throughout with weapons the historical one was still waiting for; its new territorials have rifles and too few sergeants¹⁴. The gap the rearmament opened over history is now closing from the historical side, as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969.

The PAVN leaves 1968 having learned that it does not lay siege to a position the Americans can measure; that is the one way of choosing its battles it has lost¹¹. It chooses all the others. The panhandle is harder to cut than history's, because the bombers taught its engineers how.

Phung Hoang enters 1969 with five years of files, an audited count the press has learned to quote, and no quotas; the Front's Delta committees have lost a third of the leaders they sent into the towns¹⁵. The Hue district files have shown what the national file would be worth to the enemy. The selected advisers are in every field post, the first of them on their second tours, and the American battalions have fought the year as units.

The ARVN's battalions come out of 1968 with commanders who have held them through Tet and a board that has not stopped working; the mobilization's platoons have lieutenants who were sergeants and squads that have no sergeants⁷. COSVN has opened a campaign among the officers the board passed over, and the Military Security Service has a second file on the board's officers, this time as targets.

The RVNAF ends 1968 with the mobilization's soldiers trained to the same standard as the regulars of 1957, and about a month late in the field for it; the People's Self-Defense Force, which no course reached, has paid for the difference in Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong. About 2,150 rather than 2,300 ARVN soldiers died in the first wave, and the hospitals of both armies are full of men who would have died on the historical course and are alive without a leg¹⁶.

The RVNAF ends 1968 with its new platoons led, in about fourteen cases in fifteen, by a man who has passed the leader's course, against about four in five on the course of memorandum 009 and about two in three historically⁸. The academy's course is four weeks for the duration and its field week intact. The posts of Cai Lay held Tet by not being attacked.

### Mechanism

The phase changes in one event, and the change is a stipulation: on 16 March 1968 the massacre at Son My does not happen. The ruling made the removal isolated, with no cause inside the war¹. So the chronicle shows what does not follow, not why it does not happen. Task Force Barker's sweep entered the 11th Brigade's log as a dry hole. There was no report of 128 Viet Cong killed, no concealment to come apart and nothing for Ridenhour to write about in 1969. The conditions historians found behind the massacre stay in the province: the body count, the free-fire areas and the harassment fire, and every other killing of civilians in Quang Ngai that year stays where history put it.

The second change in the phase is in the end vector and not in an event. American political tolerance moves from the historical state to 0..+1, with the public as the binding facet, because the volunteers' effect on the draft peaks in 1966–68: about 70,000–95,000 fewer induction notices a year, fewer families coerced and fewer draft trials. Public opinion binds because the administration's tolerance in 1968 was set by the public's reaction to Tet and the casualty lists, not by Congress, which had not yet refused anything. The facet moves to 0..+2 and the parent to 0..+1, because the gain on the public was too small to reach the decision of 31 March.

My Lai adds nothing to tolerance in 1968, and that is not an oversight. Historically the massacre was concealed until November 1969. Its removal acts on the American public only from that month, in Phase IV. In 1968 its only effect is in Son My itself, where about four to five hundred more people are alive at the end of the day, under the Front.

Two findings are carried here. F011-11 says that no massacre means no reckoning. F011-10 says that the attention of 1969–71 moves elsewhere. The first is the cost the phase plants. The Americal Division's command climate goes unexamined through the drawdown, and the war loses the one event that historically forced the Army to look at how a company could do what Charlie Company did.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy detected nothing, because nothing happened. The 48th Local Force Battalion had left Son My before the helicopters landed, as it left most sweeps in Son Tinh District that spring, and it came back to a village whose people were alive and still under its administration. Its recruiters and tax collectors had four to five hundred more people to work on. The Front's province committee lost the story it would have told in every other village of the district about what the Americans did. I judge that cost as political, unmeasurable in the phase's variables and larger than the recruits it gained. Hanoi's decisions of 1968, the second and third offensives, the talks in Paris and the acceptance of the bombing halt, were taken on Tet's losses and the American election. Nothing in the package reached them.

### Causal chains

The chain that begins here, C011-2, lands in Phase IV and is written there.

### Downstream

Phase IV loses the revelation of November 1969, the Peers inquiry and the Calley trial, and with them the national argument of 1971. It also loses the inquiry into the command climate that the scandal forced. Phases V and VI are untouched by this removal. Son Tinh District stays an ordinary contested district of Quang Ngai, and in March 1975 it is overrun with the province in the collapse of the 2nd Division.

### Clock

The clock in 1968 ran on Tet, the casualty lists and the request for 206,000 more men, and the President stopped it on 31 March. The volunteers took some heat out of the draft and changed none of those three. The 53% who called the war a mistake in August 1968 were history's 53%³. The phase's floor is not met in events. One event changes against a floor of three, and the reason is the provision's shape. The package changes one event of 1968 by stipulation and acts otherwise only through the draft. Every other event of the phase turns on things the package does not reach, and writing others would be padding.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +2..+4 · POP.gvn_legitimacy +2..+4 · VCI.strength -4..-2 · ENEMY.engagement_control -4..-2 · FIND.main_force +2..+4 · FIND.vci +5..+7 · ARVN.effectiveness +5..+7 · US.political_tolerance +0..+2 · US.political_tolerance.public +1..+3 · US.political_tolerance.congress +0..+1.*

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971⁴. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest²⁴. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle⁶.

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses. The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective; Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season; and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically.

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open. Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy. In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home.

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless. The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election. And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute. The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

The officer reforms reach their limit in this phase, and it is the limit their memorandum named: the American lever. As the aid conditions are dropped with Vietnamization, the palace makes the board's general-officer list advisory in January 1970, keeps the board for everything below colonel, and in December 1970 answers a petition of colonels of the classes of 1957–59 by moving about forty of the army's best regimental and battalion commanders to staff and schools. Lam Son 719 goes into Laos weeks later with new commanders in two of the 1st Division's regiments and an Airborne brigade, and fails for the historical reasons, which were decided at corps and in Saigon. Thieu's election of 1971 is run against the background of the colonels' petition, read into the record of the Lower House. The battalions themselves are the best-led the Republic ever had.

Under the withdrawal, the Republic's own plants close the loop that Vietnamization would otherwise have left open: the American equipment handed over in 1969–71 goes into rebuild works and a signal depot that have run since 1966, and by the end of 1971 the RVNAF makes about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, most of its batteries and all of its wire, filled and powered from abroad. In the Delta the subsector advisers hand the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets before they leave. And the Directorate of Defence Industry goes to the palace with the Board, the list, the file and the Commission, keeping its output and losing its honesty.

The schooled sergeant meets the drawdown in both armies. In the American units that stay longest, the sergeant who enforces standards is the man the worst soldiers resent and some of them try to kill, and the schooled sergeant holds more squads together than history's without stopping the decay. In the Republic the gate on NCO promotion is the first of the army's merit rules that the palace breaks, by the National Day decree of 1 November 1970, in the same months it takes the board and the Directorate⁸. The academy survives the advisers, because it was Vietnamese from the start, and the territorial platoon leaders who learned to call fire on a grid call Vietnamese guns when the Americans go.

In this war the home front of 1969–71 has no My Lai and a smaller draft. The Moratorium and the Mobilization of the autumn of 1969 come on their dates without Seymour Hersh's story in their week, and the Peers inquiry, the Calley trial and the national argument of the spring of 1971 do not happen. A quarter of the men the boards would have called have enlisted before their notices came, the boards send about 70,000 fewer notices in 1969 and about 40,000 fewer in 1970, and about one in four of the Army's draftees in the rifle companies of the drawdown chose to be there². None of it changes the withdrawal's schedule, which Washington set in 1969 for its own reasons; it changes the bitterness of the years in which the schedule ran.

#### ◆ The sergeants of the drawdown
*1 Jan 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · US Army and Marine Corps units in I, II and III Corps, especially the 23rd (Americal) Infantry Division at Chu Lai and LZ Baldy, Quang Tin and Quang Nam Provinces; the NCO academies in the United States · new · EV010-5*
*Initiative: neither*

The American NCO corps that fought the drawdown was schooled for its grades to the man and too thin to hold the army it led. The sergeants first class and master sergeants of 1969 were on their second or third tours; the staff sergeants and sergeants of the rifle companies were men of two or three years' service, many of them graduates of the NCO Candidate Course, who had taken the Basic Leader Course's lanes as its last module or at Bien Hoa⁸. In the units that stayed longest, the war they were asked to lead was one their soldiers had stopped believing in. Drug use, racial violence and refusals rose through 1969–71 on their historical course, and the Army and the Marine Corps recorded about 600–800 fraggings and attempted fraggings in those three years, most of them against officers and NCOs³⁰.

The schooled sergeant was both the man who held a squad together and the man its worst soldiers resented most. On 14 March 1970 Staff Sergeant Raymond L. Ochoa, a platoon sergeant of a battalion of the 196th Infantry Brigade at LZ Baldy, who had passed the Advanced Leader Course and had used its counselling and its standards to take four men of his platoon off the line for heroin, was wounded by a fragmentation grenade rolled under his bunk; he lived, and two of the four were convicted. The 23rd Division's inspector general counted in 1970 that the platoons whose sergeants held the course and had led them for six months or more had about a third fewer drug cases and refusals than those whose sergeants had been waived or deferred. That is the provision at its true weight: it held some squads together, and it did not stop the decay, which came from the war's politics and the draft rather than from the sergeant. The MACV academy at Bien Hoa graduated its last class on 30 June 1971 and was handed to the ARVN as a division school⁸.

In this war the draft half of that sentence was smaller. A quarter of the men the boards would have called had enlisted before their notices came, and in a rifle company of the 196th Brigade in 1970, of about 40–60 men who were draftees on the historical course, about 10–15 had chosen to come. The division's inspector general, asked in 1971 to break the 1970 figures down by how men had entered the Army, found the volunteers among the former draftees' cohort about as likely as the volunteers of every year to be on the drug and refusal lists, and about half as likely as the men still drafted; in the platoons that had a schooled sergeant, a battalion that had trained and deployed together and three or four such volunteers, he counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer cases than in Ochoa's platoon². It changed no fragging that the record names and saved no squad that the war's politics had already lost. The units that went home in 1971 went home as history's did, from a war their soldiers knew was being given up.

#### Nixon takes office; Vietnamization
*20 Jan – Mar 1969 · Washington and Saigon · historical · EV000-56*
Richard Nixon was inaugurated on 20 January 1969 with a promise to end the war with honour and no published plan to do it. His national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, commissioned a survey of the agencies' views of the war, which showed deep disagreement about almost everything except that the South could not yet stand alone. At a National Security Council meeting on 28 January the Secretary of Defense, Melvin Laird, proposed calling the policy "Vietnamizing" the war; its two parts were to strengthen the RVNAF to take over the fighting and to extend pacification. Henry Cabot Lodge replaced Harriman in Paris²¹. Laird, who visited Saigon in March, pressed for withdrawals sooner and larger than the military wanted, and made "Vietnamization" the measure of the South's progress³¹. The Improvement and Modernization programme behind it re-equipped the RVNAF from top to bottom: M16 rifles for the regulars and then the territorial forces, M60 machine guns, artillery, helicopters, aircraft, and the training base to use them.

#### The post-Tet offensive
*23 Feb – Mar 1969 · Saigon, Da Nang and installations throughout the South · historical · EV000-57*
On the night of 22–23 February 1969 the PAVN and VC opened a countrywide offensive of attacks by fire and sapper raids against more than a hundred targets, including Saigon, Da Nang, Hue and US bases, the heaviest since 1968. It was smaller in scale and ambition than Tet: the ground attacks were mainly against bases and firebases, not into the cities, and the population did not rise. It cost the Americans heavily in the first weeks, and it was taken in Washington as a breach of the understanding that had accompanied the bombing halt. Nixon's answer, in the third week of March, was the secret bombing of the Cambodian base areas³².

#### ◆ Operation Menu
*18 Mar 1969 – 26 May 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, eastern Cambodia · changed from history · EV000-58*
*Initiative: allied*

On the night of 18 March 1969, 60 B-52s took off from Guam on a mission ostensibly against targets in South Vietnam; 48 were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, where COSVN was believed to be, and dropped about 2,400 tons that night. In this war the 48 were Js, and their unguided carpet was half as dense; about 1,600 tons fell in the boxes, and twelve of the aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s aimed at the coordinates the U-2S had measured over five years in the Fishhook: storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop with a generator, and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload by night. The campaign that followed, codenamed Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner, Supper and Dessert after the base areas it struck, flew about 3,800 B-52 sorties and dropped 108,823 tons until 26 May 1970. Here it dropped about 75,000 tons, a fifth of the sorties carrying JDAM against measured structures¹¹. It was concealed by dual reporting: an overt channel ordered strikes near the border inside South Vietnam, while a back channel ordered the strikes in Cambodia, whose records were destroyed after each mission; neither the Secretary of the Air Force nor its chief of staff was told³².

The measured structures died; the base areas did not. In the first six weeks of Breakfast and Lunch about 300 structures that the U-2S had photographed were destroyed, most of them sheds and workshops in clearings. By June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, and the clearings were empty when the U-2S next imaged them; the J's precision had a season's targets and then none. The rear services cadres, by the account of a COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970, blamed spies in the base areas and searched for beacons in the forest; they found neither, and did the right thing anyway, which was to own nothing that stood in the open. COSVN itself, a mobile headquarters of a few hundred people under the canopy, was never located and never struck, as it was not historically¹¹.

Sihanouk, whose territory it was and whose government had tolerated the base areas and the supply line from Sihanoukville, did not protest publicly. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969; Nixon ordered the leak traced, and the telephones of 13 officials and four journalists were tapped. Historically those wiretaps were the first of the abuses that led to Watergate; in this war there is no Watergate, the burglary of 1972 never happens, and the wiretaps of 1969 are never exposed by an investigation of it². The bombing forced the PAVN and VC to disperse and camouflage their Cambodian bases, here a few months earlier and more completely, but did not stop their offensives, and its effect on the war in the South is disputed³²¹¹.

#### Hamburger Hill
*13–20 May 1969 · Ap Bia Mountain (Hill 937), A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · historical · EV000-59*
In May 1969 the 3rd Brigade of the 101st Airborne Division (Airmobile), under the division commander Major General Melvin Zais, went into the A Shau valley in Operation Apache Snow to destroy the PAVN base areas there. On Ap Bia Mountain, Hill 937, about 1.9 km from the Laotian border, the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry under Lieutenant Colonel Weldon Honeycutt met the 7th and 8th Battalions of the PAVN 29th Regiment, dug in. Ten days of frontal assaults followed, with the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry, the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry and the ARVN 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment joining for the final attack on 20 May. The Americans lost 56 killed, 367 wounded and 7 missing; they counted 630 PAVN dead on and around the hill, many in tunnels, and captured 89 individual and 22 crew-served weapons and 3 prisoners. On 5 June the hill was abandoned³³.

"That hill had no military value whatsoever," Zais said. Senators Edward Kennedy, George McGovern and Stephen Young attacked the battle as senseless; on 27 June *Life* published the photographs of 242 Americans killed in one week. Abrams turned from "maximum pressure" to a posture designed to keep American casualties down, and Nixon announced the first troop withdrawal within weeks³³.

#### Midway, the first withdrawals and the Nixon Doctrine
*8 Jun – Dec 1969 · Midway Island; Guam; South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-60*
*Initiative: allied*
On 8 June 1969 Nixon met Thieu on Midway Island and announced the first withdrawal of US troops, 25,000 men, to be completed by the end of August. Elements of the 9th Infantry Division left the Mekong Delta in July, and the whole 3rd Marine Division had left I Corps by the end of the year³¹. On 25 July, at Guam, Nixon set out what became the Nixon Doctrine: the United States would honour its treaty commitments and provide a nuclear shield, but would look to its Asian allies to provide the manpower for their own defence. Further withdrawals were announced in September and December. For the troops still in Vietnam, the withdrawals made every American death harder to justify. Discipline in the US Army declined through 1969–71; drug use, racial violence and "fragging", attacks on officers and NCOs with grenades, became endemic in rear areas and in some combat units. The withdrawals were faster than the RVNAF's improvement, and they ran on a timetable set by American politics, not by the war.

In this war the withdrawals are of units, and they are cleaner for it. Under the individual system the first increment, Keystone Eagle, took the 9th Division's units home with whichever men had the least time left, and filled the units that stayed with the rest, so that the withdrawal broke up every company it touched. Here the 9th Division's battalions and the 3rd Marine Division's had each arrived as units and left as units, whole, on the dates their tours ended; no company that stayed received the short-timers of one that went, and the commanders of the battalions that stayed kept men they had trained. The cost was arithmetic: an increment had to be made of units whose tours ended in the window, so the withdrawal schedule set by Washington had to be met by shortening some tours and lengthening none, and about 12,000 men in 1969–70 went home one to four months early with their battalions. The breakdown of discipline was not prevented. Fragging, drugs and racial violence rose in 1970–71 in battalions that had trained together, as the best study of the war had led this headquarters to expect; they rose somewhat less inside the rifle companies and as much in the rear¹⁵. Unit rotation preserved the unit and could not preserve the war's purpose in the minds of men who knew it was ending.

#### COSVN Resolution 9 and the death of Ho Chi Minh
*Jul – 2 Sep 1969 · COSVN headquarters and Hanoi · changed from history · EV000-61*
In July 1969 COSVN issued Resolution 9, which accepted that the 1968 offensives had not produced the uprising and directed a return to protracted war: small-unit and sapper attacks, economy of force, the rebuilding of the infrastructure, and a campaign against Vietnamization and pacification, while main forces were preserved in the sanctuaries. It was a decision taken in the light of losses and on the movement's own reading of its position, and it set the pattern of the enemy's war in the South until 1971. On 2 September 1969 Ho Chi Minh died in Hanoi. His testament called for the war to continue until the Americans had left and the country was reunified. Power passed to a collective leadership in which Le Duan, as First Secretary, was the strongest figure, with Le Duc Tho, Truong Chinh and Pham Van Dong. Ho's death did not change the policy; it removed the one figure whose authority stood above the factions, and left the militants who had planned Tet in charge.

In this war Resolution 9 is issued on the same date with the same direction. Its section on the countryside is different in one respect. On the historical course the Front could still campaign in 1969 against a government that had not given the peasants their land. Here the government had been titling since 1964, and the resolution's instruction to the Delta's party committees was to stop contesting the titles, to treat a titled hamlet the government held at night as lost for the present, and to concentrate cadres and the reduced tax effort on the hamlets the government held only by day. That was a realistic instruction and a costly admission: it surrendered the issue on which the Front had been built in the Delta, and it left the movement there with nothing to offer the peasant but reunification and the end of the American war⁶.

The resolution's instruction on the infrastructure was the Front's admission of what the police had done to it. It told the party committees of the Delta and the provinces around Saigon to stop keeping cadres resident in villages with settled police posts, to govern them by visiting cadres who slept elsewhere, to concentrate the reduced effort on the hamlets the government held only by day, and to put the movement's best security cadres into penetration of the Special Branch and of the provincial administration rather than into the villages. A movement that had lived among its people since 1960 now visited them, and visiting cadres taxed less, recruited less and governed less¹³.

#### The closed loop
*1 Jul 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · The Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap signal depot; the ARVN depots at Long Binh and Da Nang; the 1st and 3rd Area Logistics Commands · new · EV008-6*
*Initiative: allied*

Vietnamization handed the RVNAF the equipment of an American army in three years, and in this war the Republic already had the shops to keep it running. From the summer of 1969 the departing American units turned in their vehicles, radios and weapons for transfer, and the Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap signal depot, which had rebuilt the RVNAF's own engines, carriers and sets at the domestic share since 1966, took the American articles into the same loop: in 1970 the works rebuilt about 4,500 engines, 2,000 transmissions and about 550 M113s and M41s, and the depot about 45,000 radios, most of them PRC-25s and PRC-77s, which the communications plant had built under licence since 1969. The crash depot programme that the historical RVNAF needed in 1970–71, when it found itself owning more equipment than it could repair, did not have to be built, and the American logistics staff that would have built it spent the time on the ports³⁴³⁵.

The loop was tested in the spring of 1971. Lam Son 719 lost about a third of the 1st Armored Brigade's carriers and tanks in Laos, most of them abandoned on Route 9 where no rebuild works could reach them. Of those that came back damaged, the works at Da Nang and Bien Hoa returned about 60 to the brigade by August, and its squadrons were at about four-fifths of their establishment again by the end of the year³⁵. The brigade's losses in Laos were history's, and the reason was the operation, not the depot. What the depot changed was how long it took to stand the brigade up again.

By the end of 1971 about two-fifths of the RVNAF's small-arms cartridge cases, about a third of its 105mm bodies, most of its mortar bodies, most of its radio batteries and all of its field wire came from its own plants, filled and powered with American propellant, explosive and semiconductors bought with aid dollars. It was the largest step toward a self-supplying army the Republic ever took, and in 1971 nobody could see it, because the American stocks were still free and still flowing. The Joint General Staff's J-4, which in 1970 would historically have been absorbed by the turnover, spent part of that year drafting the first plan for an RVNAF that would buy its own ammunition with its own money; it was filed and not funded³⁵.

#### The secret Paris channel opens
*4 Aug 1969 – Feb 1970 · Paris · historical · EV000-62*
In June 1969 the National Liberation Front formed the Provisional Revolutionary Government of the Republic of South Vietnam, to claim governmental status at the talks. On 4 August Kissinger began secret talks in Paris with Hanoi's delegation, first with Xuan Thuy, and from February 1970 with Le Duc Tho, a member of the Politburo. The formal sessions at the Hotel Majestic became a forum for public statements; the substance moved to the secret channel. The core issues were fixed from the start and remained so for three years: Hanoi demanded the withdrawal of all US forces and the removal of Thieu as the price of a settlement, and would not discuss the withdrawal of its own forces from the South; Washington would not overthrow its ally²¹.

#### ◆ The Moratorium; no My Lai to reveal
*15 Oct – 24 Nov 1969 · Washington and the United States · changed from history · EV000-63*
*Initiative: neither*
On 15 October 1969 the Moratorium brought hundreds of thousands of Americans out against the war in cities across the country, and on 15 November the Mobilization brought a march of several hundred thousand to Washington. On 3 November Nixon appealed on television to "the great silent majority" to support his policy of withdrawal on his terms, and the speech was well received. Historically, on 13 November Seymour Hersh published the story of the My Lai massacre, the photographs taken by the Army photographer Ronald Haeberle appeared on 20 November, and on 24 November the Army appointed Lieutenant General William Peers to investigate the cover-up¹⁹.

In this war there was nothing to publish. No massacre had been committed at Son My on 16 March 1968, Ridenhour had written no letters, and Haeberle's photographs of Task Force Barker's sweep showed soldiers searching empty bunkers. The Mobilization of 15 November marched in a week whose news was the President's speech and the first troop withdrawals, not a massacre. The antiwar movement's case was the war itself, the casualty lists of 1969 at about 9,400 American dead⁴ and the lack of an end, and it did not need My Lai to make it; it lost the war's single most powerful image of what the fighting did to the villages, and its moral argument was weaker for the loss while its political argument was untouched. In the press the incidents that history overshadowed with My Lai, the free-fire areas of Quang Ngai and the refugee camps on Route 1, were reported as they were found, one at a time and smaller. No Peers inquiry was appointed. The Army was not made to look at the command climate of the Americal Division, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 for a theater-wide inquiry into how the body count shaped the conduct of operations was not taken up in Washington, because without a scandal no one above MACV wanted it². Gallup's share of Americans calling the war a mistake had been 53% in August 1968 and averaged about 55% in 1969–70³; that verdict was in before November 1969, and it was the verdict the President's withdrawal answered.

#### The Board and the Directorate in the palace
*Jan 1970 – Sep 1971 · Saigon and the provinces · new · EV002-6*
*Initiative: allied*
The Provincial Administration Selection Board had outlived four governments because each had found it cheaper to keep than to break, and because a province chief appointed outside the list could not sign a budget, a title or a counterpart-fund disbursement while the Aid Compliance Board stood behind the rule³⁶. By 1970 the American lever was lapsing with the withdrawal, and Thieu, preparing for the election of 1971, wanted the provinces. He did not abolish the Board. As the terms of its two general officers expired in January 1970 he replaced them with officers of his own staff, and from that spring his Prime Minister, General Tran Thien Khiem, appointed the third name on the list for about half the province vacancies and for most of those in the provinces with the largest electorates. The list still excluded the incompetent and the bought; it no longer excluded the loyal mediocre¹³.

The Inspectorate's reports, which had gone to the Board and the Commission since 1964, were routed through the presidential Inspectorate General from July 1971 under the decree that subordinated the Commission. The National Police had followed the same road earlier: its Director General had been the President's appointee in fact since 1966, and in 1970–71 the Special Branch's Saigon headquarters spent a growing share of its effort on the opposition, the An Quang Buddhists and the Ky camp, as historical police chiefs always had. The rural posts, the census and the district sections went on much as before, because they were too many and too local for the palace to want them. In September 1971 province chiefs campaigned for Thieu as they did on the historical course; the difference was that about two-thirds of them were men who had passed an examination and served a district first, and the villages they governed were governed better than they were represented¹³.

The palace took Phung Hoang's national file the same way, without abolishing anything. In February 1970 Khiem, who was Minister of the Interior as well as Prime Minister, moved the national centre from the Directorate of National Police to the Central Intelligence Organization's building and replaced Hieu, who resigned rather than serve under the new chairman, with a Military Security colonel of the President's staff. The file held about 210,000 names by then, every one entered to the three-source standard and many with fingerprints and photographs, and it now served a second purpose: from the spring of 1970 the national centre vetted every candidate for province and district office and every officer above major against it, and its analysts were asked to chart the An Quang Buddhists' committees and the Ky camp's officers by the same method they had used on the Front. For the election of 1971 the provincial copies of the file were called in to Saigon "for consolidation", and about two-thirds of the PIOCCs were left with district extracts; from September 1971 the complete national file existed in one building. Ashby's successor protested in writing in October 1970 and was told, correctly, that the decree of 1964 placed the committee under the Minister of the Interior¹⁵. The district centres went on working the Front as before, and their audited count did not fall; what had changed was who else the file was for.

The Directorate of Defence Industry went the same way in the same months, and for the same reason. In February 1970, as the Aid Compliance Board was reduced to an office reporting to the Embassy, Thieu replaced Vuong Dinh Toan with Brigadier General Vu Xuan Dac of his own military staff, and the Directorate's contracts, its scrap and import licences and its deferment list became the palace's to allot. The domestic share survived, because the Joint General Staff now wanted the plants for its own reasons and the Americans were leaving, and the lines ran as before. What did not survive was the acceptance the Phu Cat lot had made honest: from 1970 the proof-firing at Long Thanh was signed more often than fired, the share of lots rejected fell from about one in sixteen to about one in a hundred, and the field reports of duds and misfires from domestic mortar rounds roughly doubled by 1971. The scrap brass of the arsenal's case lines went to Singapore through a firm whose partners were related to the President's staff, and the Commission, under the Inspectorate from July 1971, could not open the file³⁵.

Taken together, the five institutions this headquarters built behind an American lever in 1964 fell to that lever's lapse in eighteen months: the Commission, the Selection Board for the provinces, the general-officer list, the national Phung Hoang file and the Directorate³⁵. Each lost its reach upward and kept its work below the palace, and the net is the same in each case. The posts, the chiefs' experience, the battalion commanders, the district centres and the plants' output stayed with the Republic; the honesty above them went to the palace. Of the five, the Directorate lost least in output and most in what output is worth.

#### The list in the palace and the colonels' petition
*12 Jan 1970 – 20 Jan 1971 · The Presidential Palace and the Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters · new · EV007-5*
*Initiative: allied*

The ranked list for generals lost its lever in the same month as the Selection Board for the provinces, and in the same way. On 12 January 1970, with the aid conditions of 1964 being folded into Vietnamization's programmes and the Aid Compliance Board reduced to an office that reported to the Embassy rather than to this headquarters, Thieu decreed that the board's general-officer list would be "advisory to the Supreme Commander". He did not abolish the board, which went on choosing battalion and regimental commanders, promoting captains and majors and relieving for cause below the rank of colonel; it had become the army's machinery, and he had no other. Above it the divisions and the corps returned to the palace. In 1970 four division commands were filled, and none of them from the list's first name⁷.

The colonels answered. The officers of the classes of 1957–59, who had been promised a career on merit when they were captains, were by 1970 lieutenant colonels and colonels commanding regiments, serving as division chiefs of staff and teaching at Dalat, and about 30 of them signed a petition to the Chief of the Joint General Staff in September asking that the list be restored, drafted by Colonel Nguyen Dac Khiet, commanding a regiment of the 9th Division. It was a petition, not a plot. The Military Security Service and the Central Intelligence Organization reported it to the palace as a "colonels' group" with contacts among Ky's supporters and Minh's, which was true of four of its signers, and with the intention of a coup, which no evidence ever showed⁷.

Thieu acted in December, before the dry season. Between 5 December 1970 and 20 January 1971 about 40 regimental and battalion commanders, the petition's signers and the officers the CIO's report placed near them, were moved to staff posts, the schools and the provinces' sector headquarters, nine of them in I Corps; two of the 1st Division's regimental commanders and the commander of an Airborne brigade were among them. Khiet went to Dalat as an instructor. Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had commanded a regiment of the 5th Division since 1968 and had refused to sign, was kept in command and transferred to the new 3rd Division forming in Quang Tri in 1971, where the palace wanted a commander whose loyalty its report had cleared⁷. The petition's effect in Saigon was the one the decree's critics had feared from its first day: an educated officer corps whose promotion was blocked had become, in the palace's eyes, a faction, and the palace had treated it as one.

#### The handover of the call at Long My
*1 Feb 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · Long My District, Chuong Thien Province; the Vi Thanh sector operations centre; the district's territorial artillery platoon · new · EV008-7*
*Initiative: allied*

When the Americans left the districts of the Delta, the call for help stayed behind, and Long My is the place to see it. Major Huynh Cong Tri, a list chief who had held the district since 1966, had forty-one PF posts and two Regional Force companies along the Cai Lon and the roads to Vi Thanh, every post with a set on the district net and every set made at Go Vap. Since 1967, under the rules that came out of Cai Lay, each post's first call had gone to a gun: until 1970 most often to an American battery or helicopter fire team through the subsector adviser's radio, and from February 1970 to the district's own two-gun 105mm territorial artillery platoon, formed that month on howitzers rebuilt at Bien Hoa and fed with Bien Hoa's rounds³⁵.

Major Daniel R. Kearsley, the selected adviser who held the subsector from 1969, spent his last year handing the call over. Every post's concentrations were re-plotted for the district platoon and the 21st Division's guns at Vi Thanh; the post commanders called them in Vietnamese, on Vietnamese sets, to Vietnamese gunners, with Kearsley listening and not speaking; and from September 1970 the American fire teams were called only through the district operations room, at Tri's request. On the night of 23–24 February 1971, two months before Kearsley left, a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion came against the post at Xeo Mat at 0110; the post called at 0112, the district platoon fired its first round at 0117, and by 0140 it had put about sixty rounds on the post's three concentrations and the company had withdrawn, leaving four dead in the canal³⁵. Nobody in the chain was American.

The handover worked because what it handed over was Vietnamese at both ends: the set, the battery, the gun and the round. It did not work for anything that had been American at one end. The B-52Js and the coordinate cell that had broken the sieges of the larger war were not handed to anyone, and no district could call them. When the advisers left Long My in June 1971, the district kept every post it had and the Front kept the canal at night between them, as it had before³⁵. Tri's returns, which kept all forty-one posts through the offensive of 1972, began in that handover.

The post commanders who called the district platoon in Vietnamese had all passed the platoon leader's course at the Chuong Thien and Phong Dinh centres, which taught a sergeant to call fire on a grid fixed by map and compass, and on the night at Xeo Mat Sergeant Danh Son, a Khmer Krom platoon leader of the post, gave the district platoon its adjustment in two corrections. The course was the American one, and it taught the American habit of fixing the enemy and calling the guns. At Long My the habit found a Vietnamese gun at the other end, which is the only place in this war where the dependence the course carried down to the squad was answered by the Republic's own fire⁸.

#### Sihanouk falls; the PAVN turns on Cambodia
*18 Mar – Apr 1970 · Phnom Penh and eastern Cambodia · historical · EV000-64*
On 18 March 1970, while Sihanouk was abroad, the Cambodian National Assembly deposed him, and General Lon Nol became the head of a government hostile to the Vietnamese communists. Lon Nol closed the port of Sihanoukville to the supplies that had reached the PAVN and VC base areas through it. From 29 March the PAVN attacked the Cambodian army and within weeks overran much of eastern and northeastern Cambodia, protecting its bases and opening the country to the Khmer Rouge, whom it armed and trained³⁷. Cambodia, a sanctuary since 1965, became a battlefield. The loss of Sihanoukville threw the whole burden of supplying the southern half of South Vietnam onto the Trail, which in turn was extended through Laos and northeastern Cambodia.

#### Land to the Tiller
*26 Mar 1970 · Republic of Vietnam · no longer happens · EV000-65*
The Land to the Tiller law of 26 March 1970 is not enacted in this war, because there is nothing left for it to do. On the historical course it limited holdings of rice land to 15 hectares, gave tenanted land to the tenants who tilled it, confirmed title for peasants who had received land from the Viet Cong, and cost 441 million dollars, 339 million of them American; historians debate whether it came "too little, too late"³⁸. Here the decree of 1 January 1964 had done all of that six years earlier, with a lower ceiling, and by March 1970 about 1,050,000 hectares had been titled to about 800,000 families³⁹. What happens instead on 26 March 1970 is a smaller act: Thieu signs a decree converting the remaining provisional titles to final ones and extending the programme to the communal rice lands of the villages, the last large category of tenanted land, about 200,000 hectares let each year by village councils⁶. It draws no headlines in Washington, because the programme it completes had been reporting monthly for six years. Its effects had accrued over those years, not over the last five of the war, in the attitude of the rural population of the Delta towards a government that had given them something before it asked them for anything.

#### The Cambodian incursion
*29 Apr – 30 Jun 1970 · The Parrot's Beak and the Fishhook, eastern Cambodia · changed from history · EV000-66*
On 29 April 1970 ARVN forces of III and IV Corps under Lieutenant General Do Cao Tri attacked into the Parrot's Beak west of Saigon, and on 1 May the US 1st Cavalry Division and 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment, with ARVN airborne units, attacked into the Fishhook north of it; the 25th and 4th Infantry Divisions followed³⁷. In this war the attack went in on a map. Five years of the Sihanoukville count and the U-2S's radar change detection had located the depots where the convoys stopped, the road net that joined them and the clearings they hid in, and the 1st Cavalry's brigades were landed on the largest of them on the first two days rather than finding them by chance. The great complex that the 1st Cavalry found on 4 May historically, and that the troops called "the City", was its objective on 1 May here¹⁰.

Nixon announced the incursion on television on 30 April as an attack on COSVN. MACV had told CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs on 24 April that the U-2S had heard COSVN's transmitters move west and north out of the Fishhook from about 19 April, and advised against naming it as an objective. The speech was written in Washington, and it named it¹⁰. COSVN had moved before the attack. The PAVN and VC mostly avoided battle, leaving rearguards, and withdrew westward³⁷.

In the base areas the allies found the logistics of the war in the South: about 22,900 individual and 2,500 crew-served weapons, 7,000–8,000 tons of rice, about 1,800 tons of ammunition, 431 vehicles and 55 tons of medical supplies. Here most of it was found in the first ten days rather than through May and June, and the total was larger by about a tenth, because the depots on the map were searched first and the time left over went to the ones that were not¹⁰. The allies claimed 11,369 PAVN and VC killed and 2,509 captured; I make no adjustment to those claims, which carry the usual inflation. US forces were out by 30 June; the ARVN stayed on³⁷.

On 4 May Ohio National Guardsmen shot dead four students at Kent State University, and protests closed hundreds of campuses. On 30 June the Senate passed the Cooper–Church amendment barring US ground forces from Cambodia. The incursion bought time: the loss of the caches and of Sihanoukville delayed the PAVN's recovery in III and IV Corps, and the southern half of the country was quieter for more than a year. It did not destroy the sanctuary. The PAVN rebuilt its supply routes, seized Attapeu and Saravane in Laos, opened a "Liberation Route" through northeastern Cambodia to the Mekong, and in 1972 would attack An Loc from Cambodia³⁷. The radar watched the Liberation Route being built from the summer of 1970, as it had watched Sihanoukville; it could not stop it either.

#### Fire Support Base Ripcord
*1–23 Jul 1970 · Fire Support Base Ripcord, near the A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · historical · EV000-67*
In the spring of 1970 the 101st Airborne Division reopened Fire Support Base Ripcord on the eastern edge of the A Shau valley for an offensive against the PAVN base areas. From 1 July the PAVN 324B Division, with its 803rd Regiment at Ripcord, its 29th Regiment on the nearby hills and a battalion of the 6th Regiment of the Tri-Thien command in support, besieged the base, held by the 2nd Battalion, 506th Infantry of the 3rd Brigade under Colonel Ben Harrison. On 18 July a CH-47 hit by small-arms fire crashed into the ammunition storage area, killing four and destroying six M102 howitzers and 2,238 rounds of 105mm ammunition. The command judged the base indefensible and evacuated it on 23 July under fire; the battalion commander, Lieutenant Colonel Andre Lucas, was among the last killed and received the Medal of Honor posthumously. The Americans lost 75 killed and claimed 422 PAVN dead. It was the last major battle between US ground forces and the PAVN. Whether it crippled the PAVN in the region, as Harrison believed, or achieved the PAVN's purpose by forcing the Americans out, is disputed⁴⁰.

#### Commando Hunt and the growth of the Trail
*10 Oct 1970 – Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle and northeastern Cambodia · changed from history · EV000-68*
*Initiative: both*

The dry-season phases of Commando Hunt were the main US effort against the North's logistics after 1968. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks destroyed, a figure Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; in Phase VII the AC-130E gunships were credited with 7,335 of 10,689 trucks claimed. The CIA estimated that the entire North Vietnamese truck inventory was about 6,000, and a Defense Intelligence Agency model reached zero trucks remaining fourteen times in 1969. Over the campaign the Air Force claimed 51,000 trucks and 3,400 anti-aircraft guns destroyed²⁹.

In this war a second ledger stood beside the claims from 1969, and it was kept by the radar. Its Phase V figure was about 4,500–5,500 trucks destroyed or disabled, and its estimate of net loss to the throughput was about 8–10 percent, against the phases' own historical estimate falling to about 2 percent²⁹. The DIA model's zero did not reach the Secretary of Defense's desk in 1969 because the radar showed, every night, the trucks that the model said did not exist. The claims went on being made; the AC-130E crews were not lying, and a burning truck at night is easy to count twice. What changed was that no one in Washington or Saigon could believe them for long¹⁰.

The J and its weapons were a smaller part of this war than its planners had expected, and the reason is in the panhandle's geography. By 1970 the cell's folders for the panhandle held about 4,000 crossings, of which on any night perhaps a third were in use; the J cut what it could measure, and the Quickstrike fields on the Xe Kong and the Se San below Attopeu kept the steel pontoons and motor barges off the rivers, so the fuel drums floated down them on bamboo rafts instead. The petroleum pipeline from Vinh toward the A Shau was the one target in the panhandle that could not move; each of its pumping stations was measured and cut with GBU-31s, about eighty times between October 1970 and the end of 1971, and each was bypassed within two to four days by a length of flexible pipe laid around the crater. The radar's estimate is that the J's work raised the net loss to the throughput in Phase V by about one or two points, from about 8 to about 10 percent at the season's peak¹¹.

The Trail grew regardless. After the PAVN's offensives in Laos in 1970 its corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles; the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads and paths with bulldozers and graders, and floated fuel drums down the rivers. The U-2S's radar mapped each new road within days of its cutting, and the Global Hawk counted its traffic; the count rose through 1971 to about 150–200 southbound truck movements a night at the dry season's peak¹⁰. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving: the trucks destroyed and the crossings cut rose, and what arrived in the South rose with them. In this war that sentence was written in 1969 by this headquarters, from its own counts, and not by historians. Over the war more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos²⁹.

#### The National Day decree and the gate
*1 Nov 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · The Presidential Palace and the Joint General Staff, Saigon; the National NCO Academy at Dong De, Nha Trang; the corps and division headquarters · new · EV010-6*
*Initiative: allied*

The gate on NCO promotion was the first of the army's merit rules that the palace broke by decree, and it broke it for the soldiers' gratitude. On 1 November 1970, National Day, President Thieu promoted every enlisted man of the RVNAF one grade, regular and territorial, without exception for the courses, as the government had done on national occasions before (M010·S11). The decree came in the same months as the general-officer list's demotion to advice and the Directorate's capture, and it was judged in Saigon as they were, as a matter of the President's gift. It made about 40,000–60,000 men sergeants or senior sergeants without the course for the grade they now held⁸.

The training command, under Brigadier General Phan Van Huong and with Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc's academy behind it, did not contest the stripes. It contested the next promotion: on 20 November it ruled, under the 1957 regulation the decree had not repealed, that no man promoted by the decree could be promoted again until he had passed the course for the grade the decree had given him, and it opened the academy's second shift to them. The Joint General Staff let the ruling stand because the corps commanders, who wanted the stripes for their men, did not care about the next board. By June 1971 about half the men the decree had made sergeants had passed their course, and the other half held a rank they had not been schooled for and could not rise beyond. The gate survived the palace as a Vietnamese rule, enforced by the training command and the boards below colonel, after the American lever behind the board, the Directorate and the Commission had lapsed. Of the four institutions that fell to the palace in 1970–71, it was the one whose damage was repaired within a year, because nobody in the palace wanted it and nobody in the army needed it gone⁸. The academy's last American advisers left Dong De in March 1971; Rourke's successor reported that the course was taught as he had found it.

#### The Son Tay raid
*21 Nov 1970 · Son Tay prison camp, about 37 km west of Hanoi · historical · EV000-69*
On the night of 20–21 November 1970 a joint task force under Brigadier General LeRoy J. Manor and Colonel Arthur D. Simons landed 56 Special Forces soldiers by HH-53 and HH-3E helicopters, guided by C-130 Combat Talons, at the prison camp at Son Tay to rescue about 61 American prisoners. Navy aircraft from USS *Oriskany* and USS *Ranger* flew a diversion over Haiphong, and F-105G Wild Weasels suppressed the SA-2 sites. The camp was empty: the prisoners had been moved in July, which intelligence learned only days before. The raiders lost two wounded, an HH-3E deliberately crash-landed inside the compound and an F-105G to a SAM; they reported 42 guards killed, a Vietnamese source six guards and seven civilians. The raid failed in its purpose but succeeded in another: Hanoi consolidated its prisoners in central prisons in Hanoi, and many returning prisoners said their treatment and morale improved⁴¹.

#### The Cooper–Church amendment
*29 Dec 1970 · Washington · historical · EV000-70*
After the House had rejected the version passed by the Senate in June, the Cooper–Church amendment was enacted in December 1970 as part of the Supplementary Foreign Assistance Act and signed on 29 December. It barred US ground troops and advisers from operations in Laos and Cambodia³⁷⁴². It wrote into law the sanctuary constraint that had been policy since 1965, at the moment the administration was planning to attack the Trail on the ground. Any operation into Laos would now be the ARVN's alone, without the American advisers who called in the air support and coordinated the helicopters on which ARVN operations depended.

#### Lam Son 719
*8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · changed from history · EV000-71*
*Initiative: allied*

In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery⁴². MACV J-2's estimate on 4 February, built from the U-2S's signals and the panhandle watch, put the enemy's strength within reach of Tchepone at about 35,000–40,000 men and rising, about twice the historical estimate given to the planners; the operation went ahead on its date¹⁰.

In this war the operation carried a siege box with it. Sorrell's detachment at Quang Tri, a dozen Americans who could not cross the border, received the Global Hawk's imagery of every firebase north and south of Route 9 each night and passed measured positions to the Js on Guam and at U-Tapao; the ARVN battalions on the hills passed nothing, because they had no means to give a coordinate the bombers could use, and no American advisers were there to give it for them¹². The box worked where the enemy dug and failed where he closed. At Fire Support Base Lolo and at Sophia, south of the road, the PAVN 2nd Division's regiments dug in around the hills from late February, their trenches were imaged and struck each night at 400–700 metres, and the bases held until they were evacuated in the withdrawal. North of the road, Ranger North and Ranger South fell on 20–21 February and Fire Support Base 31 on 25 February much as they fell historically, to infantry and tanks of the 308th and 320th Divisions that came straight at the wire in daylight and were inside 400 metres before they could be measured; on 25 February the radar tracked about twenty tanks forming up north of the hill at about 0900, strike aircraft destroyed six before they reached it, and the base fell that afternoon all the same¹⁰¹¹.

On 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world. Along Route 9 the radar showed the PAVN columns closing on the retreating units, and the Js with GBU-38s were put on the anti-aircraft guns the PAVN had dug in around the landing zones, which the cell measured each night; about 110 gun positions were destroyed in the last two weeks, and I estimate that the US Army lost about a sixth fewer helicopters than the historical 108 destroyed⁴²¹¹.

The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the landing zones and the command, not by what anyone could see or strike. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded". Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after⁴². The ARVN's I Corps read the operation in a way history's did not: the bases it had held were the bases where the American bombs had come every two hours, and it began to build its plans for the defence of the North around them¹¹.

The officer reforms went into Laos with the battalions and stopped at the corps. Two of the 1st Division's three regiments and one Airborne brigade had changed commanders in the six weeks before the operation, in the palace's answer to the colonels' petition, and their new commanders met their staffs in January. The battalions under them had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and it showed in the withdrawal: the battalions that came down Route 9 as units, carrying their wounded and their radios, outnumbered those that came out on the helicopters' skids by more than on the historical course, though the pictures that reached the world were the same pictures. It did not show in the outcome, which was decided by the plan's limits, Thieu's instruction to stop the operation if losses reached about 3,000, Lam's command of divisions whose commanders reported to Saigon over his head, and the enemy's five divisions, none of which any board had touched⁷. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses here either. The reform had made the battalions better than the operation they were sent on.

#### ◆ The war on trial at home
*29 Mar – 30 Jun 1971 · Washington and New York · changed from history · EV000-72*
*Initiative: neither*
Historically on 29 March 1971 a court-martial at Fort Benning convicted Lieutenant Calley of the premeditated murder of at least 20 people at My Lai and sentenced him to life imprisonment; two days later Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest pending appeal¹⁹. In April 1971 Gallup found 11% of Americans approving the verdict, 70% calling Calley a scapegoat and 79% calling the life sentence too harsh³. In this war there is no massacre, no Peers inquiry and no trial, and the spring of 1971 has no argument about a lieutenant that divided the country along lines that did not run between the war's supporters and its opponents. In April veterans opposed to the war demonstrated in Washington and threw their medals onto the Capitol steps; they came without Calley's name in every speech, and their protest's weight came, as it had historically, from who they were. On 13 June the *New York Times* began publishing the Pentagon Papers, the Defense Department's secret history of the war's decisions to 1968, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg; on 30 June the Supreme Court refused to stop publication. The administration's response was the "plumbers" unit formed to stop leaks; in this war its burglaries are never exposed, because the burglary of 1972 that exposed them never happens². By mid-1971 a majority of Americans told pollsters the war had been a mistake, 60% on Gallup's question³, as historically.

In this war the Phoenix programme comes before Congress on the historical date, in the hearings of the House Government Operations subcommittee in July 1971, at which, on the historical course, William Colby testified that more than 20,000 people had been killed under Phoenix since 1968. Here Colby gave the audited figures instead: since January 1965 about 58,000 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 36,000 tried or held under review, about 15,000 rallied and about 7,000 killed, about 2,600 of them in the programme's own operations and the rest in military operations against men whom the centres had named. The hearings were damaging and less damaging than they were historically. The story had been told since Duc Thanh in 1967; the Commission's quarterly samples since 1965 gave the administration an independent count of unsupported neutralizations, about a tenth in the held belt and a quarter in the contested belt across six years, and the magistrate review and capped detention gave it a legal frame to describe. The witnesses who hurt the programme most were former Americans of the PRUs' contested-belt advisory teams of 1967–68, and the subcommittee's report singled out the eleven districts the Commission had already named¹⁵. The Con Son "tiger cages" of July 1970 had happened as they did historically, in a prison the statute did not reach. The Public Safety appropriation for 1972 passed with a cut of about a tenth rather than the larger cuts its critics proposed, and the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers were kept¹³. In this war the subcommittee heard Colby without the shadow of Son My over the hearing room, and its members, who historically asked whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means, asked instead whether the audit could be trusted; the audit could answer that question, and MACV's word in 1971 was worth a little more than history's².

#### The Commission placed under the Inspectorate
*14 May – 9 Jul 1971 · Saigon · new · EV001-8*
On 14 May 1971 the Commission Against Corruption opened an inquiry into the sale of import licences for fertilizer and motor vehicles under the Commercial Import Program, which its auditors had traced through three Saigon firms to a relative of a member of the President's staff. It was the first inquiry in seven years to reach the presidential palace, and it reached it in the year Thieu stood for re-election. The American lever that had protected the Commission since 1964, the Aid Compliance Board's power to suspend counterpart funds to a named command, importer or province, had not been used since 1969: the Mission was managing a withdrawal, and Washington would not risk a quarrel with the government it was leaving over a licence case. Commissioner Khoa's second term, from January 1970, had been confirmed with the American co-chair's concurrence; his removal still required it³⁹⁶.

Thieu did not remove him. On 9 July 1971 he issued a decree placing the Commission, the Special Court's prosecutors and the Pay and Muster Verification Service under a new Inspectorate General of the Presidency, with authority to approve or suspend any inquiry touching an official of the rank of minister, general officer or province chief and above. The licence inquiry was suspended the same day. The Deputy Chief of Mission protested in writing; the Embassy did not invoke the Board. Khoa stayed in office and the Commission's work below that rank went on: the musters, the pay at the parade, the land court cases and the Phoenix audits continued through 1971 at about their earlier rate. What ended was the reach upward that had convicted a general in 1967. An anti-corruption body that had survived seven years with a patron and an American lever lost the lever first, and the patron followed his own interest⁶.

#### Thieu's unopposed re-election
*Oct 1971 · Republic of Vietnam · historical · EV000-73*
*Initiative: neither*

In the presidential election of October 1971 Thieu ran alone. Ky was at first disqualified under an election law requiring endorsements from deputies or provincial councillors, then reinstated by the Supreme Court, and withdrew; Duong Van Minh withdrew, charging that the election was rigged. Thieu received all the valid votes, 5,971,114, and the reported turnout was 87.97 percent of 7,192,660 registered voters; valid ballots were 94.37 percent of those cast⁴³. A government that had won with a third of the vote in a contested election in 1967 was now returned in a referendum on itself. The army and administration remained Thieu's, and the regime was more stable than at any time since 1963; but its claim to stand for the whole non-communist South had narrowed, at the moment American forces were leaving it to stand alone.

In this war the election runs as it did historically, with the same withdrawals, the same single candidate and the same returns. Its context is different in one respect and the same in another. Thieu's government could claim in the countryside what it could not claim on the historical course, that it had given about 800,000 families their land seven years before the election, and its province chiefs campaigned on it. But the subordination of the Commission in July had removed the one body with the standing to hear the opposition's complaints about the endorsement law, and Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard in Saigon as a description of a government that had shut down its own inspector in the year it chose to run alone⁶.

The colonels' petition entered the election in August, when a deputy of the Lower House read its text into the record and asked why its signers had been removed from their commands before an operation in which the army had lost so much. Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard by the officer corps alongside it. The board went on promoting captains and majors through the campaign as if nothing had happened, which was in its way the strongest thing it ever did; the officers it promoted that autumn knew that the list above colonel was now the President's⁷.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving⁴. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before²⁴. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it⁶. These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal⁴². The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent¹³. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

The radar's ledger has made the interdiction debate honest and changed nothing it was about: the Trail is larger at the end of 1971 than ever, the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia is mapped and open, and the aircraft that count both will leave with the Americans¹⁰.

The ARVN's I Corps ends the phase believing, on the evidence of Lolo and Sophia, that a besieged base under American bombers holds. It is true, and it is the belief that will cost it most when the bombers go¹¹.

Phung Hoang ends 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon, serving the President as well as the war. About 3,500 selected advisers remain, with the territorials and the province teams more than with the divisions; the American battalions have gone home whole and their discipline has decayed on its historical course¹⁵.

The board ends 1971 as the machinery of the army below colonel and nothing above it: battalions and regiments are commanded by officers it chose, for terms it sets, and the divisions and corps by the President's choices⁷. The colonels who asked for the list back are in the schools and the staffs. The battalions that went into Laos came out of it better than the operation did.

The RVNAF enters 1972 with a closed rebuild loop and a domestic share that covers about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its batteries, under a Directorate the palace now runs³⁵. The districts of the Delta call Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets. Nobody can see the base's weight in a year of free American supply, and it has not yet been tested by a year without it.

The NCO academy at Nha Trang ends 1971 Vietnamese, as it began, with its American advisers gone and its course whole; the gate on promotion has been broken once by decree and half restored by the training command, and about half the sergeants the decree made have taken their course since⁸. The American NCO corps leaves Vietnam schooled and spent, its best sergeants among the dead of the fraggings as well as the fighting.

### Mechanism

This is where the volunteers and the removal of My Lai act together, on the public, while American troops are still in the field. The Moratorium marched without a massacre to reveal in its second month. The spring of 1971 had no Calley verdict to divide the country along lines that did not run between the war's supporters and its opponents. The Pentagon Papers took the attention instead, on their historical dates. The public's tolerance moves to +1..+3. It moves through the absence of a scandal and through a draft a quarter smaller, and its binding facet is still the public. It binds because the President's schedule of withdrawal was set by his reading of the public and not by Congress, which in 1969–71 constrained only Cambodia and Laos. The parent moves to 0..+2 and the schedule does not move, because Nixon was already withdrawing as fast as Vietnamization allowed and slower than the public wanted.

The verdict did not change, and this is the most important fact in the phase. Gallup's share calling the war a mistake averaged about 55% in 1969–70 and reached 60% in 1971³. It had passed 50% by August 1968, more than a year before Hersh's story. My Lai historically confirmed a verdict that was already in. Removing it takes away the confirmation and not the verdict.

The volunteers' second effect is in the companies. EV010-5 carries it at length and embodies F011-19. In a rifle company of the 196th Brigade in 1970, ten to fifteen of the men who would have been draftees had chosen to come. The division's inspector general found the volunteers of that cohort about half as likely as the men still drafted to be on the drug and refusal lists. Where a schooled sergeant and a battalion that had trained together were already holding the line, he counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer cases. It saved no squad the war's politics had already lost.

The Watergate removal touches the phase at one point. The wiretaps of May 1969, ordered to trace the leak about Menu, are never exposed by an investigation of a burglary that never happens. The "plumbers" of 1971 are formed on the historical date and their burglaries are never exposed either. Nothing in 1969–71 turns on them. Their exposure, which mattered, would have come in 1973–74.

### Enemy response and initiative

Hanoi read the American public through the same press it had always read, and found the antiwar movement as strong in October 1969 as history's. The movement's strength came from the casualty lists and the war's lack of an end, which the package did not touch. COSVN's Resolution 9 of July 1969 and the North's turn to protracted war after Tet were taken on the losses of 1968 and the American withdrawal, and their authors had no reason to revise them. The Front's propaganda lost Son My as an example and used the free-fire areas and the refugee camps of Quang Ngai, which history's My Lai had overshadowed. They were reported in the American press one at a time and smaller.

### Causal chains

**C011-2** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase III, EV000-46) No massacre is committed at Son My on 16 March 1968, so there is nothing for Ridenhour to report, Hersh to publish or Peers to investigate in 1969 → the Army is never made to look at the Americal Division's command climate, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 for a theater-wide inquiry into how the body count shaped operations dies in Washington for want of a scandal → in July 1971 the House subcommittee hears Colby's audited Phoenix figures without Son My's shadow over the room, and asks whether the audit can be trusted rather than whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means → MACV's word in 1971 is worth a little more than history's, so the removal of an American atrocity ends by making the Republic's own intelligence programme easier to defend in Congress, while the body-count culture that produced the atrocity goes unexamined.

### Downstream

Phase V receives a public a little less hostile than history's and a President no stronger, because his support in 1972 rested on the withdrawal and the prisoners and not on the public's view of the war. Phase VI receives the volunteers' last effect: the draft ended in early 1973³ with fewer draft-evasion cases to resolve. About 210,000 men were accused historically³, and here about a quarter fewer were ever called. It also receives a war that Americans remember a little more as fought by men who chose to go. Neither changes a vote of 1973–75. The vote that does change is the Watergate removal's, and it begins in Phase VI.

### Clock

The clock ran on withdrawal, and withdrawal was the President's answer to it. The package wound it back a little in 1969–71, through a quieter November 1969, a spring of 1971 without Calley and draft calls a quarter smaller, and it did not change the rate at which the troops came home. A provision that makes the public less angry about a war it has already judged a mistake buys the President time he had decided not to use for escalation. Nixon spent it on the withdrawal's pace and on Cambodia and Laos, which ran on their historical dates.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

Phase IV's tolerance moved, by about a band on the public, and it does not alter 1972. The offensive was met with American air power the President ordered on his own authority, and the last ground battalion left in August. The two break-ins of 28 May and 17 June are removed, but historically Watergate cost Nixon nothing in 1972: he won forty-nine states. The letter of 14 November to Thieu is written as history's was³. Its change is that its author stays in office, which matters only from 1973.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +6..+9 · POP.displacement +0..+4 · POP.gvn_legitimacy +3..+6 · GVN.political_stability +5..+8 · VCI.strength -6..-3 · ENEMY.engagement_control -2..+1 · TRAIL.tonnage_arriving +2..+5 · ARVN.effectiveness +3..+6 · ARVN.self_sufficiency +2..+4 · ARVN.self_sufficiency.logistics +2..+4 · ARVN.self_sufficiency.fire_support +1..+3 · US.political_tolerance +2..+4 · US.political_tolerance.public +0..+1 · US.political_tolerance.congress +2..+4 · US.political_tolerance.administration +5..+8 · NEGOT.position +2..+5.*

Phase VI is the war without the Americans. The Paris Agreement of January 1973 takes US forces out and returns the prisoners; it leaves the PAVN in the South, the Thieu government in Saigon, and the war to be decided between them. The ceasefire never holds. In 1973 Saigon is on the offensive, recovering hamlets and roads, while the North rebuilds; in 1974 the two are evenly matched, and the ARVN, short of ammunition, fuel and spare parts as American aid falls, bleeds in a war of attrition it cannot sustain; in January 1975 the North tests whether the United States will respond to the seizure of a province, and when it does not, it commits its army to the Highlands⁴⁴.

The shape of the phase is three slow years and a fast spring. The constraints that bind the American role are now law, not policy: the Case–Church amendment ends all US combat activity in Indochina from 15 August 1973, and Congress cuts military aid. In Hanoi, the 21st Plenum of October 1973 returns to revolutionary violence, and the PAVN builds a road network east of the mountains, a fuel pipeline into the South and an army of corps. In Saigon, the economy, hit by the oil shock and the departure of the Americans whose spending had sustained it, and a government that has narrowed its base, face the offensive of 1975 with an army spread thin across the whole country.

In this war the army that fights the last three years has been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the abundance of American ammunition, and the ration of 1974 finds it more dependent on volume than the historical one. It fights the attrition of 1974 with better weapons than its enemy and too few rounds to use them, and the hoards it builds against the ration are lost with Hue and the Highlands.

The bombers leave in August 1973 without bombing Neak Luong, and the coordinate cell is disbanded with nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to. The ARVN fights 1974 and the defence of Phuoc Long with plans written for an air force that no longer comes, and loses Thuong Duc and Phuoc Long as history did, with its officers knowing exactly what they lack.

Phung Hoang outlives the Americans as a Vietnamese programme and helps the Republic's offensive of 1973 in the Delta; its national file, consolidated in Saigon since 1971, is still in the Central Intelligence Organization's building at the end of 1975, and the province files of Quang Ngai and Quang Tin are taken when those provinces fall in March. The advisers are gone in March 1973, and nothing of their eighteen months survives them but the counterparts who remember them.

The officer reforms leave the Republic in its last three years an officer corps that knows precisely what it lacks. Its battalion and regimental commanders, educated on a doctrine built around American fire, plan the battles of 1974 for a weight of fire the ration no longer allows. Its staff college writes, in November 1974, the only serious plan the Republic ever had for a defence on reduced resources; in March 1975 the President rejects it entirely and orders everything held, and its method is used a year late, for the evacuation of Da Nang in May.

In this war the last ration also fell on an army that made two-fifths of its own small-arms ammunition, a third of its 105mm rounds and nearly all of its radio batteries, from imported powder and semiconductors. The domestic plants stretched each ammunition and communications dollar, made the attrition of 1974 somewhat cheaper and changed no position the PAVN chose to take, gave Phuoc Long more rounds than it could fire, and in 1975 were still the Republic's.

In this war there is no Watergate, and the President who wrote Thieu his promises is in office to the end of the record. Case–Church passes as historically, so that the bombers he promised cannot lawfully return, but the War Powers Resolution fails in the House, the Republicans of 1974 do not desert him, the midterm costs his party about 22 House seats instead of 49, and the fiscal 1975 appropriation is about $950 million, which the Republic's own plants stretch to buy about what $1.2 billion bought historically. The Republic fights 1974 near the 1973 allowance. In March 1975, with a year's fuel and a President who has asked Congress for its bombers and been refused, Thieu refuses the Dalat staff college's withdrawal and orders the Highlands and I Corps held; the Highlands are lost in five weeks instead of ten days and Hue in an ordered withdrawal in April, and on 31 March the Politburo, which historically resolved on 25 March to take Saigon before the rains, has no double collapse to resolve on and puts Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76. The Republic ends 1975 smaller, holding III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, with its general reserve brought back from Da Nang by sea and its fall expected in the first months of 1976².

#### The Paris Agreement
*27 Jan 1973 · Paris · historical · EV000-83*
On 27 January 1973 the Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Vietnam was signed in Paris by the United States, the Democratic Republic of Vietnam, the Republic of Vietnam and the Provisional Revolutionary Government. A ceasefire in place took effect on 28 January Saigon time. The United States and its allies would withdraw all forces within 60 days, and prisoners would be exchanged in the same period. Nothing required the PAVN to leave the South, where by US estimates about 150,000 of its troops remained. The Thieu government remained, and a National Council of National Reconciliation and Concord was to arrange elections, which it never did. An International Commission of Control and Supervision of Canadian, Hungarian, Indonesian and Polish personnel, and a Four-Party Joint Military Commission, were to supervise the ceasefire, and both sides could replace worn-out armaments one for one. Thieu signed under American pressure, with Nixon's written promises of continued aid and of a forceful response to a major violation²¹. The agreement was close to the terms of October 1972.

#### The ceasefire that never held
*28 Jan – Dec 1973 · The countryside of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-84*
*Initiative: allied*
In the last days before the ceasefire both sides grabbed for hamlets and roads, raising flags to claim territory, the "war of the flags"; the ARVN recovered most of what the PAVN and VC had seized. Fighting resumed in earnest by March²¹. Through most of 1973 the Saigon government was on the offensive: the Communists, rebuilding supply lines and wary of provoking the return of American air power, largely stayed on the defensive, while the ARVN and territorial forces pushed into PRG-held areas. Both sides violated the agreement from the start; the ICCS could not stop it, and Canada left the commission in July 1973. From late 1973 the PAVN grew bolder as the American political system turned on itself, and by early 1974 the two sides fought roughly on equal terms⁴⁴.

In this war the Republic's offensive of 1973 had one instrument the historical one lacked: district centres nine years old in the Delta, with files the American departure did not take with it. When the American advisers left the centres in March 1973, the centres stayed, under the police statute, as they had always been; the Phung Hoang name was retired in December 1972, as historically, and the work went on under the Special Branch. In the "war of the flags" of January–February and the push into PRG-held hamlets that followed, the IV Corps provinces moved first into villages whose infrastructure the centres had charted, and the Front's district committees in Chuong Thien, Vinh Binh and Kien Hoa lost about a fifth of their members in 1973, about twice the historical rate¹⁵. It was a gain in the hamlets and not on the frontiers, and it changed nothing in the balance of 1974, when the PAVN's divisions decided what the hamlets were worth.

#### Operation Homecoming and the last American troops
*12 Feb – 29 Mar 1973 · Hanoi, Clark Air Base and Saigon · historical · EV000-85*
On 12 February 1973 the first American prisoners were flown out of Hanoi to Clark Air Base in the Philippines. By 29 March, 566 US servicemen, 23 American civilians and 5 foreign nationals had come home through Clark⁴⁵. On 29 March 1973 the last US combat and support troops left South Vietnam, and MACV was disestablished. A Defense Attaché Office under Major General John E. Murray, with a few dozen military personnel and civilian contractors, took over the management of American military aid. The United States still maintained air power in Thailand and the Seventh Fleet offshore, and continued to bomb in Cambodia. The question of Americans missing in action, about whom Hanoi gave little information, would keep the war alive in American politics for two decades.

#### ◆ Case–Church and the end of American combat in Indochina
*Jun – 7 Nov 1973 · Washington; Cambodia · changed from history · EV000-86*
*Initiative: neither*

In June 1973 Congress passed the Case–Church amendment, which barred any US military activity in or over Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia without congressional approval from 15 August 1973²¹. It passed the House 325–86 on 26 June and the Senate 73–16 on 29 June, and the President signed it on 1 July. In this war it passed in the same words and by about the same margins. Historically Republicans relented on support for South Vietnam under the pressure of Watergate³; here there was no Watergate, the Senate's televised hearings never opened on 17 May, and the President's own party stood with him on every vote that was about him³. But this vote was not about him. The prisoners were home, the troops were out, and 60% of Americans called the war a mistake³; the amendment's margins were veto-proof before a single Republican defection is subtracted, and the Republicans who historically voted for it voted for it here, because their voters wanted Indochina finished². Until 15 August the B-52s went on bombing Cambodia, which had continued since the Paris Agreement, in support of Lon Nol's forces around Phnom Penh. Historically on 6 August a B-52 dropped its bombs on the town of Neak Luong on the Mekong, killing about 137 people and wounding about 268, because its navigator had failed to set the offset for the radar beacon on which the strike was aimed. In this war no beacon was used: the J navigated and released on its own GPS, and the strike that night fell on its intended box in the forest east of the river. Neak Luong was not bombed, and the American ambassador in Phnom Penh did not have to pay its families a hundred dollars a death¹¹.

On 15 August the bombing of Cambodia ended, and with it the last American combat missions in Indochina. The Js went home to the Strategic Air Command's alert pads, and in this war they stayed on alert at Andersen and U-Tapao through each dry season as the visible instrument of the President's promise to Thieu; Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut, which had been folded into the Defense Attaché Office's intelligence branch in March, was disbanded in September, and its folders for every firebase and district town in the South went to Clark Air Base and then to the Defense Mapping Agency. The Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell of its own; there was nothing for such a cell to send coordinates to, since no aircraft the VNAF flew could carry a guided bomb, and the request was not pursued¹²¹¹. Historically on 7 November Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto, eighteen days after the Saturday Night Massacre of 20 October³. In this war there was no Saturday Night Massacre, and the override failed in the House by a handful of votes, the Republicans who had historically broken with a President they were about to impeach holding with one they were not. It changed less than it seemed. Nixon's promise to Thieu of a forceful response to a major violation was beyond the President's power to keep without Congress, not because of the War Powers Resolution but because of Case–Church, and Congress would not give it². The sanctuary constraint, which had shaped the war since 1964, now covered the whole of Indochina and was written into law.

#### Resolution 21 and the North's rebuilding
*Oct 1973 – 1974 · Hanoi; the Laotian panhandle and the western Highlands · changed from history · EV000-87*
*Initiative: enemy*

In October 1973 the 21st Plenum of the Party Central Committee in Hanoi resolved that the revolution in the South must continue by the path of revolutionary violence, and that the PAVN should respond to Saigon's operations and prepare for a decisive offensive. The PAVN rebuilt through 1973 and 1974: it improved the strategic road along the western side of South Vietnam east of the Truong Son, extended the fuel pipeline into the South, built up stocks of ammunition and armour in the base areas and grouped its divisions into army corps, the 1st Corps in October 1973 and the 4th Corps on 20 July 1974 among them⁴⁶. It was the North's own decision, on its own reading of the American political collapse and Saigon's weakness, and it was taken while Saigon still held the initiative in the countryside.

In this war the rebuilding had about a tenth more to make good from 1972, most of it in the divisions that had been struck at An Loc and Kontum. It made it good on the historical schedule, and the schedule is the point. The corps were formed on their historical dates because what governed them was the Politburo's decision of October 1973 and the American political collapse it was reading, not the number of men in the B-2 Front's regiments; the North's flow of replacements to the South in 1973 covered the difference in men within a season, and the B-2 Front's command filled the 7th and 9th Divisions' companies with new conscripts under the surviving cadre. What the difference bought was quality, not time: the 9th Division that attacked Rach Bap in May 1974 and the 7th that went into Phuoc Long in December had fewer company commanders who had fought in 1972 than history's, and their first assaults in each battle were cruder⁴⁷. The PAVN's losses of 1972 therefore left the date of every 1974 and 1975 operation where history put it, and made a few of them dearer.

#### Saigon declares the war resumed; the Paracels
*Jan 1974 · Saigon; the Paracel Islands · historical · EV000-88*
In January 1974 Thieu announced that the war had resumed and that the Paris Agreement no longer bound his forces²¹. On 19 January a Chinese naval force defeated South Vietnamese ships in the Paracel Islands, and China took control of the islands⁴⁸. The United States did not intervene. The clash showed how isolated Saigon had become: its ally would not act, and a great power on its northern horizon was willing to take territory from it.

#### ◆ The aid cuts and the shortages
*25 Jan – 9 Aug 1974 · South Vietnam and Washington · changed from history · EV000-89*
*Initiative: neither*

On 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel to all ARVN units were cut because of budget limits. Historically US military aid fell from about $1.1 billion in fiscal year 1974 to an appropriation of about $700 million for fiscal year 1975, the July 1974–June 1975 year, the figure given by source as $700–750 million⁴⁴. Major General Murray of the Defense Attaché Office warned that cuts in support could roughly be equated with loss of territory, and that aid of $600 million would mean writing the South off⁴⁸. The ARVN rationed artillery rounds, grounded aircraft and helicopters for lack of fuel and parts, and cut patrols; the soldiers' pay lost most of its value to inflation, and desertion rose. The economy suffered from the world oil shock of late 1973 and the loss of American spending.

In this war the ration fell on an army that had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical one. The Joint General Staff's directive of February 1974 limited each infantry squad to its basic load for a month's operations, and the squad gun went first: about 200 rounds a gun for a month, which is what an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes. Battalion commanders who had learned their trade breaking contacts with the squad gun's volume found that the 1974 ration let them do it about twice in a month, and the advisers' successors at the Defense Attaché Office reported that the infantry, rather than fight with aimed rifle fire as their fathers had fought with the Garand, avoided contacts it could not break with volume. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had a separate line, were rationed harder still; by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms for want of ammunition. Divisions began to hoard linked 5.56mm in their own ammunition points against the ration, against JGS orders, and by the end of the year the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Divisions held about 9 million rounds outside the corps depots between them¹⁴.

The vote was where this war parted from history's. In the spring of 1974 the President was not fighting impeachment; the House Judiciary Committee voted no articles in July, because there was nothing to vote on, and on 9 August, the day history's Nixon resigned and Gerald Ford succeeded him⁴⁸³, this war's Nixon was at San Clemente drafting the message on the fiscal 1975 budget that he sent to Congress on 14 August. He made Murray's warning his own argument, in a joint appearance with the Secretary of Defense before the House Appropriations Committee's defense subcommittee on 21 May, and he had the votes of about 15–30 Republicans in the House whom history's Watergate summer had sent home to defend themselves. The House cut the request anyway, because the war was unpopular and the recession was beginning, and the conference report of the autumn gave the RVNAF about $950 million for fiscal 1975, against history's $700 million and fiscal 1974's $1.1 billion. In the House debate the Bien Hoa arsenal was cited by the advocates of the cut as proof that the Republic could make its own ammunition, which was half true, since it made the brass and the steel and not the powder³⁵; here the President answered it, and the answer bought about $250 million that history's Congress did not vote².

That was the ration on American stocks, and in this war it was not the whole of the ration. Since 1965 the Aid Compliance Board's domestic share had made the RVNAF buy from its own plants, and by 1974 the Bien Hoa arsenal made about two-fifths of the RVNAF's 5.56mm and 7.62mm cartridges, about a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its mortar rounds, every one of them filled with imported propellant and explosive bought out of the same appropriation. A dollar of propellant, primers and explosive loaded at Bien Hoa bought about one and a half times the rounds of a dollar of finished American ammunition, and the battery works and the signal depot stretched the communications dollar about twice³⁴. Across the whole of the RVNAF's programme the fiscal 1975 appropriation of about $950 million bought about what $1.2 billion had bought on the historical course, with the upper bound marked because it multiplies two judgments that lean the same way. From March 1974 the Joint General Staff raised the squad gun's monthly ration to about 400 rounds and the 105mm battery's daily allowance by about a third, and the PRC-25 and PRC-77 batteries that the historical RVNAF rationed through 1974 were issued at the full rate from Go Vap; the territorial posts' radios, which history's district chiefs found going silent in the second half of the year, stayed on the air³⁵. From the autumn, when the larger appropriation began to arrive, the margin went where the domestic stretch had never reached: aviation fuel and imported parts for the VNAF, diesel for the relief columns and the trucks of the corps' logistics commands, and 155mm and 175mm rounds that Bien Hoa could not forge. The VNAF's grounded aircraft, which history counted in hundreds by the end of 1974, were about a third fewer by December².

The plants also paid wages, and in 1974 that mattered in the households of the army. About 60,000–80,000 people worked in the Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap works, more than half of them women on the battery and assembly lines, and perhaps one ARVN or RF family in eight in the provinces of III Corps had a factory wage in it. A private's pay in 1974 bought a fraction of what it had bought in 1971; a wife's wage at the battery works roughly doubled what the household had. The factory families of Bien Hoa were the one part of the army's families that the inflation of 1974 did not reach in full, and they lived beside the plants, not beside the units. The larger appropriation did not reach the soldier's pay, which was the Republic's own budget and was paid in piasters the oil shock was eating; the economic aid voted with it, about a tenth larger than history's, held the import programme's rice and fertilizer a little nearer their 1973 level and slowed the piaster's fall in the second half of the year without stopping it². Rice was the other side of the household: the pumps the agricultural machinery shops had sold since 1964 had put a dry-season crop on about 300,000 hectares of the held Delta by 1974, and the Delta's surplus that reached Saigon in 1974 was somewhat larger than history's, which held the price of rice in the capital a little below history's in a year when everything else rose³⁵.

#### The ARVN's last offensives
*12 Feb – 2 May 1974 · Tri Phap, Dinh Tuong Province; Svay Rieng, Cambodia · historical · EV000-90*
In the spring of 1974 the ARVN still had the strength to attack. From 12 February to 4 May it struck the PAVN base at Tri Phap in Dinh Tuong Province in the Delta. From 27 March to 2 May, in the last major ARVN offensive of the war, III Corps attacked the PAVN base areas around Svay Rieng in Cambodia, claiming more than 1,200 PAVN killed against fewer than 100 ARVN⁴⁸. They were the work of an army still able to win local battles when it had the ammunition and air support to do so. They did not change the balance, and after the spring the ARVN was on the defensive everywhere.

#### ◆ The war of attrition of 1974
*16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · changed from history · EV000-91*
*Initiative: enemy*

From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed⁴⁸.

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were¹⁴.

They bled in a second way that history's Airborne did not. Since An Loc, Kontum and the Lam Son bases the ARVN's I Corps had been taught, by success, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege. At Thuong Duc the district town's garrison, the 79th Ranger Battalion, dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, for a siege box that no longer existed; the 304th Division's regiment dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them, and the town fell on 7 August. The Airborne's counter-attack on the heights was planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia in 1971 under the Js, and its first assaults on Hill 1062 went forward in the expectation that the PAVN's positions on the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in the monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it. I estimate that the Airborne Division's losses in the heights were perhaps a tenth higher than historically, a judgment I hold loosely¹¹. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

The officer reforms made the attrition of 1974 more legible and no cheaper. The battalion and regimental commanders who fought at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc had been educated on a doctrine in which the infantry finds the enemy and the fires destroy him, and from January 1974 the fires were rationed; their reports to the Joint General Staff in the summer, collected by the Central Training Command, are the most exact account anywhere of what the ration did to each kind of fight, and none of them could change it. At Duc Duc the 3rd Division's battalions, under board commanders, held their companies together through 4,700 casualties with fewer men lost to desertion than history's, and held no more ground⁴⁸⁷.

The Bien Hoa rounds changed which guns could fire, and the PAVN's choice of ground decided how much that was worth. Through the summer the 105mm batteries of the 3rd Division at Duc Duc and the Airborne on the Thuong Duc heights fired about a third more than the historical ration allowed, on domestic rounds, and every post and company on the ridges kept its radio on the air. It did not change the arithmetic of the artillery duel. The PAVN fought these battles where its 130mm M-46 guns, ranging 27 kilometres, could reach the ARVN's 105mm positions at 11 kilometres from beyond their range, and the guns the domestic rounds fed were the guns its choice of ground made least decisive. Thuong Duc still fell on 7 August, Duc Duc still cost the 3rd Division its 4,700, and Phu Loc was recovered in December as history's was; at Rach Bap the III Corps battalions, which drew directly from Bien Hoa, retook the post in the last week of October rather than in November. I judge that the domestic rounds and the radios saved perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead in 1974, resting on a third more 105mm fire and a doubled squad-gun ration in the fights the army did accept³⁵.

In this war the strategic raids of the autumn met an army whose larger appropriation had begun to arrive, and they met it less often. The Politburo had watched a President who did not fall all summer, and the B-2 Front's and the Tri-Thien front's plans for raids in III Corps and on the Phu Loc hills in September were cut back by Hanoi to the Phu Loc operation alone, on the reasoning that a President in full possession of his office might find a way round the statute if provoked hard enough before the North was ready. At Phu Loc from 28 August the 1st Division's counter-attack on the hills had VNAF UH-1Hs for its lift and resupply on most days instead of on some, because the helicopters had fuel, and the 155mm batteries at Phu Bai fired at about the 1973 allowance from October; the hills were retaken in the third week of November instead of on 10 December. I judge that the larger appropriation and the President's restraint on Hanoi's raids together saved perhaps 1,500–4,000 more of the Republic's dead in 1974, a judgment resting on a rate of 5–13% fewer losses in the fights the army accepted with fuller supply and on raids not made, and I keep the reported figure of about 30,000 dead for the year, since the two savings together lie at the edge of its uncertainty⁴⁸². What absorbed the rest of the difference was the PAVN's choice of ground and the ARVN's lack of a reserve: a better-supplied army that could not move its divisions still lost the positions it was tied to.

#### ◆ The midterm of 1974
*5 Nov 1974 · Washington and the United States · new · EV011-1*
*Initiative: neither*
The congressional elections of 5 November 1974 were held in a recession, a year after the oil embargo, in the sixth year of a Republican presidency, and they went against the President's party as such elections do. Historically the Democrats gained 49 House seats and 5 Senate seats, in the autumn after the resignation and the pardon, and the freshmen of 1975 were the most hostile class to the war Congress ever seated³. In this war there was no resignation and no pardon. The Democrats gained about 22 House seats and 3 Senate seats, the ordinary price of a bad economy and an unpopular war's long tail. The ninety-fourth Congress that met in January 1975 was still Democratic in both houses and still wanted Indochina finished; it had about 27 more Republicans in the House than history's, enough to sustain a veto and not enough to pass anything the Democratic leadership opposed. The President had lost no authority he had not already lost to the statute of 1973. In the Politburo's assessment of December, read to the strategy conference that sat from 18 December, the election was noted as a defeat for Nixon smaller than the Americans' own press had predicted, and the conference's conclusion that the United States would not return rested on the law, not on the President².

#### ◆ The fall of Phuoc Long
*12 Dec 1974 – 6 Jan 1975 · Phuoc Long Province (Phuoc Binh and Song Be), III Corps · changed from history · EV000-92*
*Initiative: enemy*

On 12 December 1974 the PAVN 4th Corps under Hoang Cam, with the 7th Division, the new 3rd Division and independent regiments including the 141st, 165th, 201st and 271st, and two armoured companies with 14 T-54s, attacked Phuoc Long Province on the Cambodian border. It was defended mainly by Regional Force battalions, reinforced by the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 7th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 9th Regiment of the 5th Division and the 81st Airborne Ranger Battalion⁴⁶. The province chief's plan for the defence of Phuoc Binh, drafted in 1973 by officers who had served at An Loc, was a plan to hold the town for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege; it named the bomber cells by the call signs Hollingsworth's men had used in 1972. On 13 December the province chief asked III Corps for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan", and III Corps forwarded the request to the Joint General Staff, which forwarded it to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it¹¹.

 In this war the attack was the B-2 Front's decision more than Hanoi's. With a President in Washington who had not fallen, the Politburo was more cautious through the autumn than history's, and the General Staff's plan for the 1974–75 dry season, approved at the conference of 30 September – 8 October under Le Duan, held the main blow for the Highlands and left the B-2 Front a supporting role. Lieutenant General Tran Van Tra, the B-2 Front's commander, argued in Hanoi in November for the attack on Phuoc Long as a test of the American response, as he had historically, and against a Politburo that had more reason to fear the answer; he won, on his front's own reckoning that the province could be taken before Washington could decide anything, and on the B-2 command's long rivalry with a General Staff that had kept the decisive operations for the northern fronts since 1968². The test was his, and it was taken on his timetable.

On 18 December, in the meeting at the Independence Palace where Thieu and the Joint General Staff decided not to send the general reserve to Phuoc Long, the request was on the table, and the decision taken was historical: the Airborne Division was committed in I Corps, and Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers. In this war the judgment was put in those words, and with more conviction, because the Republic's officers had seen at An Loc and Kontum what American bombers could do for a besieged town and knew exactly what they were not getting¹¹. The last ARVN post fell on 6 January 1975, the first provincial capital lost permanently since the Paris Agreement. The ARVN lost more than 1,000 killed and 2,444 captured; the PAVN about 1,300 killed and wounded. Saigon did not try to retake it. Washington made no military response⁴⁶. In Hanoi, where a strategy conference sat from 18 December to 8 January, Phuoc Long answered the question the leadership had set itself: the United States would not return⁴⁸. The plan for a two-year campaign to end the war in 1976 was adapted to strike in the Central Highlands in 1975.

In this war the defence of Phuoc Binh had more rounds and a radio to the end, and neither reached the reasons it fell. The province's ammunition point held about a third more 105mm rounds than history's, most of them from Bien Hoa, and the Regional Force battalions' posts in the province reported on the air until they were overrun, because their batteries were fresh. The PAVN's 130mm guns and its T-54s decided the siege, as they had historically: the province's two 155mm and four 105mm howitzers were silenced by counter-battery fire in the first week, and the rounds that would have fed them were still in the ammunition point when the 7th Division took it⁴⁶. What absorbed the difference was the decision of 18 December not to send a reserve and the PAVN's artillery, which no rounds for 105mm guns could answer. One thing went to Hanoi that had not gone historically. The 4th Corps' artillery staff reported that the ARVN's guns had fired in the first week at a rate the General Staff's estimate of the 1974 ration did not allow, and the B-2 Front's intelligence section, asked where the rounds came from, answered with the name of the Bien Hoa arsenal and the tonnage of propellant the port of Saigon had landed for it in 1974³⁵. It was the first time the North's planners counted the Republic's own industry as part of its strength. Washington's answer came after the fall, and it is the next event.

#### ◆ The message on Phuoc Long
*7 Jan – 13 Mar 1975 · Washington, Hanoi and Saigon · new · EV011-2*
*Initiative: allied*
On 7 January 1975, the day after the last post at Phuoc Binh fell, the President sent Congress a message that history's President never sent. It asked for two things: a supplemental appropriation of $300 million for the RVNAF's ammunition and fuel, and authority under the Case–Church amendment to use American air power in Indochina "in the event of a major offensive by the forces of North Vietnam in violation of the Agreement". Historically President Ford asked for the same $300 million in January 1975 and was refused. Here the request for money went to the appropriations committees and the request for authority to the Foreign Affairs and Foreign Relations Committees, and the second was dead within the month: the Senate committee did not report it, and the House leadership, with the votes of the Democratic majority behind it, refused to schedule it. The supplemental fared better. A Congress that would not let the bombers fly would pay for rounds, and in the second week of March it voted about $150 million of the $300 million, most of it for fuel and 105mm and 155mm ammunition².

The message was read in three capitals, and each read it right. In Hanoi the General Staff's assessment, which reached Le Duan before the final session of the strategy conference on 8 January, said that the President wanted to return and that the Congress would not let him; it was the conclusion Phuoc Long had been fought to obtain, and the plan for the Highlands went forward on it. In Washington the President and Kissinger concluded that the promise of 1972 could not be kept with bombers and would be kept with money. In Saigon Thieu read the refusal of authority as Hanoi did and the vote of $150 million as the President's word kept as far as Congress allowed, and he concluded that the money was coming and that he should not give up any province to make it go further². That conclusion was in his mind on 11 March, the day after Ban Me Thuot fell.

#### The fall of Ban Me Thuot
*10–12 Mar 1975 · Ban Me Thuot, Darlac Province · historical · EV000-93*
For Campaign 275 the PAVN Central Highlands Front under Lieutenant General Hoang Minh Thao, directed by the chief of the general staff, General Van Tien Dung, massed the 10th, 316th and 320A Divisions with the 3rd and 968th Divisions, tanks, artillery and anti-aircraft units. Diversionary attacks on Pleiku and Kontum, false radio traffic and local rumours drew the attention of the II Corps commander, Major General Pham Van Phu, who discounted repeated warnings and kept his forces in the north. At 02:00 on 10 March the PAVN attacked Ban Me Thuot, defended mainly by the 53rd Regiment of the 23rd Division with Rangers and Regional Forces; the town was in PAVN hands by 11–12 March. The counter-attack by the rest of the 23rd Division, lifted in by helicopter, was broken. The PAVN admitted 600–800 killed and 2,416 wounded; the Vietnamese official history counts 28,514 ARVN put out of action, 4,502 killed and 16,822 captured. II Corps lost more than three-quarters of its combat units⁴⁹. Ban Me Thuot was the strategic surprise of the war's last campaign.

#### ◆ The defence of I Corps
*13 Mar – 18 Apr 1975 · Quang Tri, Hue, Quang Ngai, Quang Tin and Da Nang · changed from history · EV000-94*
*Initiative: enemy*
Historically, on 13 March Thieu ordered Truong to give up most of I Corps and withdrew the Airborne Division towards Saigon as a reserve; on 20 March he ordered him to hold only Da Nang; on 29 March he ordered him to turn back and retake it, which Truong refused. The contradictory orders, given as the PAVN 2nd Corps with the 304th, 324th and 325th Divisions attacked from the north and west and the 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade struck in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin, broke I Corps; Hue was abandoned on 25 March, about 6,000 soldiers of the 2nd Division deserted on 28 March, and the PAVN entered Da Nang on 29 March. Three ARVN infantry divisions and the 147th Marine Brigade ceased to exist, about 58,722 soldiers were captured at Hue, and the PAVN took 129 aircraft, 179 armoured vehicles and 327 artillery pieces⁵⁰.

In this war no order came on 13 March. Thieu's meeting of 11 March at the Independence Palace, with Vien, Lieutenant General Dang Van Quang and Prime Minister Tran Thien Khiem, heard Vien present the Dalat study for a withdrawal to a defensible Republic, and Thieu refused it: with the fuel and rounds of a fiscal 1975 appropriation near the 1974 level, the March supplemental voted and a President in Washington who had asked Congress for the bombers, he would not give up provinces the aid could still defend. Truong was told to hold I Corps with what he had, the Airborne Division included. He had planned for it since the autumn, and his plan began with the families: from 15 March the Vietnamese Navy's LSTs and the requisitioned coasters of the Saigon lines carried the dependants of the 1st Division and the Marines from Tan My and Thuan An to Da Nang and on to Cam Ranh, about 70,000 people in three weeks, so that the soldiers on the My Chanh line and in the hills west of Hue had their families behind the Hai Van pass and not on the road².

The 2nd Corps attacked on 19 March as it had historically, and northern Quang Tri was given up to the My Chanh line on the 19th–21st in an ordered withdrawal, the line the Marines had held in 1972. In the south of the corps the defence broke as history's did. The 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin, whose families lived in the dependants' quarters at Quang Ngai and Tam Ky and had not been moved, came apart under the PAVN 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade between 22 and 28 March; Quang Ngai city and Tam Ky fell, about 5,000 of the division's men left their units for their families, and the remnants were taken off the beach at Chu Lai on the 30th. On the My Chanh and west of Hue the line held. The 1st Division and the Marine Division, with the VNAF's A-37s flying from Da Nang on fuel the appropriation had bought and the 175mm guns at Camp Evans firing on rounds Bien Hoa could not have made, held the 324B and 325th Divisions short of Hue for three weeks. When the 2nd Corps cut Highway 1 at Phu Loc behind them in the first week of April, Truong withdrew Hue's garrison by sea from Tan My and over the Hai Van between 14 and 18 April, in an ordered movement with the families already gone; he lost about a third of the 1st Division and most of its heavy equipment, and the divisional hoard at Hue, about 3 million rounds of linked 5.56mm, was destroyed in place on the 17th rather than captured². Da Nang was held by the Airborne, the Marines, the 3rd Division and the 1st Division's survivors, behind the Hai Van and the Thu Bon.

In this war the Special Branch's files went as the provinces went. The Quang Tri and Thua Thien centres burned their files on the 19th and the 16th of April under the destruction plan of 1968, because the withdrawal was ordered and the regional chief gave the order; the Quang Ngai and Quang Tin centres, overrun in the collapse of the 2nd Division, did not, and the PAVN's security troops took their district files, about 9,000 names, when they entered Quang Ngai on 25 March¹⁵². The I Corps regional office in Da Nang, with its district extracts for all five provinces, was still in the Republic's hands. That the collapse of a corps would deliver its files with it was the risk the memorandum that created them had named; in this war only one province's collapse was a rout, and only its files were lost.

#### ◆ The battle for the Highlands
*14 Mar – 19 Apr 1975 · Pleiku and Kontum; Phuoc An on Route 21; Route 19 through the Mang Yang pass to An Khe · changed from history · EV000-95*
*Initiative: enemy*

Historically, on 14 March at Cam Ranh, Thieu ordered Phu to abandon Pleiku and Kontum and pull II Corps back to the coast; the chief of the Joint General Staff, General Cao Van Vien, warned against Route 19 because of the French disaster at the Mang Yang Pass in 1954, and Thieu chose Route 7, a disused road in poor condition. The retreat was ordered verbally and kept secret; some 400,000 civilians joined the columns, the PAVN ambushed them near Cheo Reo, and only the 6th Ranger Battalion and the 19th Armoured Cavalry Squadron arrived in good order; an estimated 155,000 civilians were killed or abducted on the "convoy of tears"⁴⁹.

In this war the plan for a withdrawal existed, and it was refused. In November 1974 a seminar of the Command and General Staff College at Dalat, under Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had gone to its faculty after Quang Tri, wrote a study for the Joint General Staff titled "The Defence of the Republic on Reduced Resources". It concluded that with the aid then expected the RVNAF could not hold the whole country against the forces the North was building, and proposed a withdrawal from Kontum, Pleiku and northern Quang Tri over about three months of the dry season, before any offensive, with routes secured in advance, the families of the soldiers moved first, the province chiefs told, and the Airborne and Marines rebuilt as a reserve. Vien sent it to the palace in January 1975, after Phuoc Long, and Thieu did not answer it⁷. On 14 March at Cam Ranh he did not take even its conclusion. With a year's fuel and ammunition near the 1973 allowance and a President who had asked Congress for the bombers, he ordered Phu to hold Pleiku and Kontum and to retake Ban Me Thuot, and the study went back into Vien's safe².

The counter-attack on Ban Me Thuot went in from Phuoc An on Route 21 on 15–18 March with the 23rd Division's 45th Regiment and the 21st Ranger Group, lifted by VNAF CH-47s and UH-1Hs that had fuel to fly, and the PAVN 10th Division, dug in on the high ground west of Phuoc An with the 320A in reserve, broke it on the 18th as it had broken history's 23rd Division counter-attack on the same ground. From 20 March the 320A and 968th Divisions closed on Pleiku from the south and west and the PAVN 3rd Division's 12th Regiment and the 95B Regiment cut Route 19 at the Mang Yang pass, and II Corps in the Highlands, about 25,000 regulars and Rangers and the province's territorials, was besieged at Pleiku and Kontum with the Cu Hanh airfield its only line to the coast. Kontum was given up on 27 March in an ordered withdrawal down Route 14 by the 22nd and 23rd Ranger Groups, under fire and in good order, because Phu, ordered to hold, chose to hold one town rather than two².

The siege of Pleiku lasted three weeks, and it was fought on what the appropriation had bought. The VNAF's C-130As and C-123Ks flew from Nha Trang into Cu Hanh by day until 9 April, about 25–35 sorties a day with ammunition, fuel and the wounded out, and the A-37s of the 6th Air Division flew from Pleiku itself until the PAVN's 130mm guns closed the runway on 9 April. The 320A's assault battalions came at the town's southern perimeter on 2 and 5 April across open ground and were broken there by the 44th Regiment and the 4th Ranger Group's squads, with the squad guns fed and the artillery firing at the 1973 allowance; the PAVN's own account puts the cost of the two assaults at about 1,200 killed and wounded, and I judge it at perhaps twice that. It was the defence's best fight and it did not change the siege. The 10th Division came up from Ban Me Thuot, the 968th's guns had the airfield, and with Cu Hanh closed the town could be fed for about a week. On 14 April Phu broke out down Route 19 with the garrison and about 60,000 civilians, the families of the soldiers at the head of the column under the plan he had written for it in March, and fought through the Mang Yang between 15 and 19 April against the 3rd Division's blocking positions. About 11,000 of the 25,000 regulars and Rangers reached An Khe and Qui Nhon with their weapons; the column lost about 9,000 civilians killed or taken, against history's estimate of 155,000 on Route 7⁴⁹².

It was a defeat, and a different defeat from history's. II Corps lost the Highlands on 19 April instead of 25 March, five weeks later and with about half its Highland force still in being rather than two units⁴⁹². The President's refusal of the Dalat study turned a plan that would have saved the Highlands force without a fight into a siege that saved half of it after one; and the siege held the 320A, 10th and 968th Divisions in the Highlands until the third week of April, which is what reached Hanoi's calendar. The best piece of staff work the reforms ever produced was refused by a President who believed, on the evidence of the money and the message, that he did not need it.

#### ◆ The Politburo's calendar: Saigon after the rains
*25 Mar – 14 Apr 1975 · Hanoi and the roads south · changed from history · EVMT01-4*
*Initiative: enemy*

Historically the date of the fall of Saigon was set in Hanoi between 25 March and 14 April, and it was set by the rains and by the road. On 25 March, with Hue abandoned and the Highlands column destroyed, the Politburo resolved to take Saigon before the rainy season, which in the southern provinces begins in May and turns the lowlands around the capital to mud; on 31 March, with Da Nang taken, it decided to do it in April; and on 14 April it named the operation the Ho Chi Minh Campaign. The 1st Corps, the strategic reserve, was brought south from the North by road, a march of more than 1,500 kilometres, the 2nd Corps came down the coast behind the collapse of I and II Corps, and General Van Tien Dung's plan set the assault for the last days of April because that was when the last corps would be in place and the first rains still a fortnight off⁵¹.

In this war the Politburo met on 25 March with a different map. Ban Me Thuot had fallen and Quang Ngai was falling, but Pleiku was besieged and holding, Kontum's garrison was fighting its way down Route 14, Hue was held on the My Chanh, and Da Nang and the coast from Qui Nhon south were the Republic's. Le Duan's resolution of that day was the one the October conference had prepared, not the one history's collapse made possible: finish the Highlands and the central coast in the dry season, move the 1st Corps south, and prepare the general offensive on Saigon for the dry season of 1975–76. Tran Van Tra argued in the Politburo's meeting of 31 March, as the B-2 Front's commander, for a thrust on Saigon in May with the 4th Corps, Group 232 and whatever the 1st Corps could bring, on the reasoning that the Republic's reserve was in I Corps and Saigon had nothing behind it; Dung, who had come back from the Highlands front, answered that the 1st Corps could not be at the capital before the third week of May, that the 2nd Corps was held on the My Chanh and the 3rd Corps' divisions at Pleiku, and that an assault into the rains with two corps against five divisions was the error of 1968. Le Duan took Dung's view. The 1st Corps was ordered south by the strategic road to assembly areas in the base areas north of Saigon, to arrive by June and wait².

The critical path that MT01 named ran through this meeting, and in this war it bent. Historically only a delay of about three weeks or more in the collapse of I and II Corps would have put the assault into the rains and forced Hanoi to choose between fighting in the mud and waiting for the dry season. The Highlands held until 19 April, about five weeks past history's 14 March decision and three and a half weeks past the 25 March resolution it made possible; Hue held until 18 April, about three and a half weeks past history's 25 March; Da Nang did not fall in the spring at all². On 25 March the Politburo had no double collapse to resolve on, and by mid-April, when the Highlands opened, the calendar left two weeks before the rains for a campaign that needed five. What delayed the collapse was not the arsenal or the officers, which the critical path had already absorbed, but a decision at the top that history's President took one way and this war's took the other: the order of 14 March, given by a man with a year's fuel and a President's message in hand. It is the first change to the critical path in this record, and it bought a season, not a war.

The base still changed one line of Dung's plan. After Phuoc Long the General Staff counted the Bien Hoa works as part of the Republic's strength, and the order for the general offensive, when it was written in the autumn for the dry season of 1975–76, named the arsenal, the steelworks and the Go Vap plant among the objectives to be taken intact rather than shelled, on the Politburo's instruction that the South's industry be preserved for the reunified country³⁵².

#### ◆ Xuan Loc
*9–21 Apr 1975 · Xuan Loc, Long Khanh Province, northeast of Saigon · no longer happens · EV000-96*
*Initiative: enemy*

Historically on 9 April the PAVN 4th Corps under Major General Hoang Cam, with the 6th, 7th and 341st Divisions and tanks and artillery, attacked Xuan Loc, the road junction northeast of Saigon on Route 1, held by the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao; the division held for twelve days and was withdrawn on 19–21 April to defend Bien Hoa and the capital⁵². The battle was the gate of the Ho Chi Minh Campaign, fought on a calendar that wanted the 4th Corps at the gates of Saigon on the day the 1st Corps arrived.

In this war there was no such calendar in April, and Xuan Loc was not attacked. The resolution of 25–31 March put Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76, and the 4th Corps' task for April and May was to take the approaches to the capital that the next offensive would need, not the capital's gate. Between 10 and 30 April Hoang Cam's 6th and 341st Divisions took Dinh Quan and Route 20 north to the Lam Dong border and the district towns of Binh Tuy, against the 18th Division's 52nd Regiment and the territorials, who gave them up in ordered withdrawals to Xuan Loc and Route 1. The 18th Division held Xuan Loc through the rains with its squads, its marksmen and the ammunition the Long Binh depot could send it, which in this war was more than history's April could find, because the divisional hoards of I and II Corps had not been abandoned to the enemy in March¹⁴. The 3rd Battalion of the 1st Airborne Brigade, destroyed in history's withdrawal at Suoi Ca, was at Da Nang⁵²². On 17 April Phnom Penh fell to the Khmer Rouge, as it did historically.

#### ◆ The President's request; Thieu stays
*10–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon and Washington · changed from history · EV000-97*
*Initiative: allied*
Historically on 10 April President Ford asked a joint session of Congress for $722 million in emergency military aid and $250 million in economic and humanitarian aid for South Vietnam by 19 April; Congress would not vote the military aid; on 21 April Thieu resigned in a televised address denouncing the United States, and on 28 April General Duong Van Minh became President in the hope that Hanoi would negotiate with him³⁵³.

In this war the request was Nixon's, on the same day, and it was smaller and earlier in the collapse: $400 million for the RVNAF's ammunition, fuel and the re-equipment of the divisions that had come out of Quang Ngai and the Highlands, and $200 million for refugees. The Republic was still fighting at Pleiku and on the My Chanh when he asked, and the Congress elected in November 1974 voted the refugee money in full on 23 April and about $175 million of the military request in the second week of May, enough for the fuel and ammunition of the summer and nothing for re-equipment; the vote was taken with the House Armed Services Committee's report that the RVNAF could hold what it still held through the rains and could not retake what it had lost. Thieu did not resign. He spoke on 22 April, the day after history's resignation, to announce the evacuation of the Highlands' survivors to the coast and the defence of "every province the Republic holds", and the Senate in Saigon gave him its confidence². Minh stayed out of office. The President's restored authority, which could not bring back the bombers, had brought the Republic a season's money and kept its government in place.

#### ◆ The fall of Saigon
*29–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon · no longer happens · EV000-98*
*Initiative: enemy*
Historically by late April five PAVN corps, Group 232 and the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th Corps, with 15 infantry divisions and three tank regiments, encircled Saigon in the Ho Chi Minh Campaign. Rockets and artillery hit Tan Son Nhut on the morning of 29 April, killing two US Marine guards, Corporal Charles McMahon and Lance Corporal Darwin Judge, the last American servicemen killed in action in Vietnam; Operation Frequent Wind lifted about 1,373 Americans and about 5,100 Vietnamese to the Seventh Fleet; at 10:24 on 30 April Minh announced an unconditional surrender, and PAVN tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace⁵³.

In this war Saigon was not attacked in April 1975. The Ho Chi Minh Campaign was not named, because the Politburo's resolution of 25–31 March put the offensive on the capital back to the dry season of 1975–76. On 29 April 1975 Tan Son Nhut was not shelled, McMahon and Judge stood their watch at the Defense Attaché Office compound and went off it, and Frequent Wind remained a plan in the Seventh Fleet's safe. The Defense Attaché Office's evacuation of American dependants, begun in early April, went on through May at the pace of the commercial flights².

In this war the Central Intelligence Organization's building still held the national Phung Hoang file at the end of 1975, about 240,000 names with photographs and fingerprints for about 90,000, consolidated there since 1971¹⁵. The Director General ordered its index microfilmed in May and the film sent to Can Tho, against the day the capital fell, and the order was carried out. The Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap works were still the Republic's, and in the summer of 1975 they were the Republic's largest source of small-arms rounds and batteries for an army that had lost its northern hoards³⁵².

#### ◆ Operation SOUTHERN WALL and the rains
*1 May – 31 Dec 1975 · Da Nang, Qui Nhon and the central coast; Saigon; Hanoi and the strategic road · new · EV011-3*
*Initiative: both*
The Republic that entered the rains of 1975 had lost the Highlands, Quang Tri, Thua Thien, Quang Ngai, Quang Tin and Phuoc Long, about a third of its army's infantry and most of the heavy equipment of II Corps and the 1st and 2nd Divisions; it held III and IV Corps whole, Da Nang and the Quang Nam lowlands, and the coast from Qui Nhon south. Its general reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, was at Da Nang, five hundred kilometres from the capital the next offensive would aim at. On 6 May Vien sent the Dalat study back to the palace with a covering note that it was now a plan for the defence of Saigon, and Thieu, who had refused it in March, took its method this time².

Operation SOUTHERN WALL, the evacuation of Da Nang, ran from 20 to 31 May under a plan the Joint General Staff staffed for a month from the Dalat study's annex on movement. The families went first, from 8 May, by the Vietnamese Navy's LSTs, the Saigon coasters and two chartered American freighters; the territorials of Quang Nam held the Thu Bon and Dien Ban for ten days while the 3rd Division, the Marines and the Airborne thinned the perimeter brigade by brigade and embarked at Tien Sa and the Han River docks. The PAVN 2nd Corps, which had been ordered to take the city before the northeast monsoon and was not ready to assault it until June, pressed the perimeter with the 304th and 325th Divisions from 24 May and took the airfield on the 30th, as the last Marine battalion went aboard. About 70,000 soldiers and about 250,000 civilians were lifted, against history's chaos at the same docks in March; about 4,000 men of the rear guard and the territorials were lost². By the end of June the Airborne and Marine Divisions were rebuilding at Vung Tau and Long Binh as the reserve the Dalat study had asked for in November 1974.

The coast went as the season allowed. Qui Nhon was given up on 12 June after the 22nd Division, which had held it since March, was outflanked by the 3rd Division and the 968th coming down Route 19; the division came out by sea, as it had historically in April, with about two-thirds of its battalions. Tuy Hoa fell in July. The line on the central coast stood at the Khanh Hoa border north of Nha Trang through the rains, held by the 22nd Division's survivors, the 3rd Airborne Brigade and the territorials of Khanh Hoa and Ninh Thuan².

In Hanoi the Politburo spent the rains preparing the offensive it had put off. The 1st Corps reached its assembly areas north of Saigon by the strategic road in June, the pipeline was extended to Loc Ninh, and the Soviet deliveries of 1975, which had been planned for a campaign ending in 1976, were brought forward: the General Staff asked Moscow in May for the 130mm ammunition and the tank replacements of 1976 to be shipped in the autumn of 1975, and Moscow, which had its own reasons to see the war ended before the American election year, shipped most of them. Beijing, which had no wish to see Hanoi's victory hastened by Soviet guns, sent rice and trucks and no more. The resolution of October 1975 set the general offensive on Saigon for the first months of 1976, with five corps, as soon as the ground in the eastern provinces was dry².

In Saigon and Washington the rains were spent on money and on the families. The fiscal 1976 appropriation was voted in November at about $800 million, less than the Republic needed to replace what it had lost in the spring and more than it would have received from a Congress that had seen it collapse; the RVNAF's ration in the autumn of 1975 was near the 1974 level, and the Bien Hoa arsenal ran three shifts. The soldiers' families from I and II Corps, about 400,000 people, were in camps around Saigon, Vung Tau and Cam Ranh; the government paid the dependants' allowance in rice from the Delta's 1975 crop, which the Republic still held and which was about history's 1974 harvest, and the piaster lost about a third of its value between April and December. Desertion in the units whose families were in the camps was about twice the rate in the units whose families had stayed in III and IV Corps². At the end of 1975 the Republic of Vietnam stood, smaller by half its territory and a third of its army, with its reserve rebuilt, its capital unattacked and an enemy of five corps preparing to attack it in the dry season.

#### How the phase ends
In this war Phase VI does not end with the fall of Saigon. On 31 December 1975 the Republic of Vietnam holds III and IV Corps, the coast from Nha Trang south and about half the population of 1974; the PAVN holds the Highlands, I Corps and the central coast north of Khanh Hoa, and five corps are assembled for an offensive on the capital in the first months of 1976. That is a conclusion and not an assumption. The date of 30 April 1975 was set in Hanoi by a resolution to finish before the rains, and the resolution was made possible by the double collapse of I and II Corps in the second half of March; in this war Thieu's decision of 11–14 March went the other way, the Highlands were lost in a five-week siege instead of a ten-day rout, Hue in an ordered withdrawal on 18 April instead of a collapse on 25 March, and the Politburo, without a collapse to resolve on, put Saigon back to the dry season². The mechanism was political and at the top: a President in Washington who did not fall, an appropriation near the 1974 level, and a President in Saigon who believed, on that evidence, that he need not give anything up. It bought a season, and it was bought at a price. The refusal of the Dalat study cost the Republic half its Highland force and the 2nd Division, which a planned withdrawal in the dry season would have saved, and its reserve spent the spring at Da Nang, five hundred kilometres from the capital, until SOUTHERN WALL brought it home in May.

The Republic that enters 1976 is not the historical Republic, and it is not a Republic that has survived. Its fall is expected in the dry season of 1976, by my estimate between February and May, from an offensive on the capital by five corps that it has a rebuilt reserve, a full arsenal, its Delta and its capital's belt to meet, and no American air power². Rung (b) by the test of rules/RUNGS.toml, because Saigon stands past 30 April 1975; the outcome in its own terms is a reprieve of about a year, and the record ends before it is spent. The Paris Agreement had removed the American forces and kept the PAVN in the South; Congress had written the end of American combat into law and would not unwrite it for a President who asked; American military aid had fallen less than history's but had fallen; and the government in Saigon, more stable than at any time since 1963, faced an enemy that had rebuilt after 1972 and chose its moment, and then chose to wait.

At the end of 1974, on the eve of Phuoc Long, the held belt of the Delta and the Saigon region was held at night more firmly than history's, and the Front's district committees there had lost about twice the historical share of their members in 1973¹⁵; the Special Branch's knowledge of the infrastructure was still the better for its ten years of files; the government's standing in the hamlets was history's, after the decrees of 1972; the army's battalions were better led and its divisions and corps no better; and the army as a whole was more dependent than history's on American fire it no longer had, and knew it⁴⁷. In this war the army had also fought 1974 near the 1973 allowance, with its aircraft flying and its relief columns fuelled, and lost perhaps 2,500–6,500 fewer men in the year than history's 30,000, a judgment on the two rates named in EV000-91³⁵². At the end of 1975 the held belt and the files are still the Republic's, and the end vector measures a state that still exists.

Hanoi's posture never changed. It was committed to armed reunification throughout, from the Ninth Plenum of December 1963 to the resolution of October 1975 that set the offensive on Saigon for 1976, through the losses of 1965–68 and 1972 and the delay of 1975, and it accepted the Paris Agreement as a step towards that end. The timing of its campaign was its own, set by its army's road, the season and, in March 1975, by a map that did not offer it the collapse history had. The US war in Vietnam, from the advisory years to 1973, cost more than 58,000 American dead. The Vietnamese dead, military and civilian, northern and southern, are counted in the millions by most estimates, and the record of them is less precise than the record of the Americans.

The squad guns and the marksmen's rifles of the ARVN's II Corps and of the 1st and 2nd Divisions are in the PAVN's hands by the end of the spring, with the hoards of Quang Ngai and the Highlands; the hoard at Hue was destroyed in place, and the hoards of III and IV Corps are still the Republic's¹⁴².

The Republic's defence of every besieged town had been built since 1971 around American bombers and American coordinates, and both were gone by the end of 1973¹¹. At Pleiku in April 1975 it fought a siege without them, on fuel and rounds, and held three weeks.

The officers the reforms made fought 1975 as well as any part of the RVNAF fought it. Their best staff plan was refused in March and used in May, for SOUTHERN WALL and the rebuilding of the reserve. The board's files, with the evaluation report of every officer since 1964, are in the Joint General Staff's compound at the end of 1975⁷².

The Republic's own plants stretched its aid dollar by about a third to two-fifths, kept its radios on the air through 1974, and in 1975 were the arsenal of an army that had lost its northern hoards; they shortened no collapse and lengthened none, because the spring of 1975 was decided by orders, families and an enemy who chose his ground, and at the end of the year they are still the Republic's³⁵².

### Mechanism

The phase turns on one removal and the chain it starts. The break-in of 1972 never happens, so there are no Senate hearings in May 1973, no Saturday Night Massacre, no impeachment and no resignation. The President who wrote the letters of 1972 is in office until January 1977. Three things follow, and only the third reaches the war.

First, the law that ended American combat in Indochina passes as history's did. Case–Church carried the House 325–86 and the Senate 73–16 in June 1973, before the Watergate hearings had made any Republican defect, and its margins were veto-proof without a single Republican defection³. What absorbs the change is the voters of the Republicans who voted for it, who wanted Indochina finished whoever was President. The War Powers override fails, and it changes little, because Case–Church already denied the President the bombers. The binding constraint on American support moves from the President to Congress. That is why `US.political_tolerance` binds on Congress in Phase VI and not on the public. The public's verdict stood at 60% "mistake" and the President's will to support the Republic was at its strongest, so the facet that limited what reached Saigon was the one that held the purse and the statute.

Second, the appropriation of 1974 is fought for by a President who is not fighting impeachment, and it is larger. In fiscal 1975 the RVNAF received about $950 million against history's $700 million. Through memorandum 008's domestic stretch that bought what about $1.2 billion had bought historically. The upper bound is marked, because it multiplies two judgments that lean the same way. The money reached what the stretch never had: aviation fuel and parts, diesel for the relief columns, and the heavy rounds Bien Hoa could not forge. That is the strongest positive effect, F011-21, and it is grounded in two dated events. The first is Phu Loc: the 1st Division retook the hills in the third week of November 1974 instead of on 10 December, with helicopters that had fuel on most days. The second is Pleiku: on 2 and 5 April 1975 the 44th Regiment and the 4th Ranger Group broke the 320A's assault battalions on the southern perimeter, with the squad guns fed and the artillery firing at the 1973 allowance. The binding facet of `ARVN.self_sufficiency` is still fire support. The money fed the 105s and 155s, and the PAVN's 130mm still outranged every gun the Republic could feed. The gain on logistics is therefore a gain on the parent only within the range the binding facet allows.

Third, and against the provision's direction, the money and the message made Thieu plan for a rescue the law forbade. On 7 January 1975 Nixon asked Congress for $300 million and for authority to use air power, and received half the money in March and none of the authority. Thieu read the refusal correctly and the money as the President's word kept as far as Congress allowed. On 11 March he refused the Dalat study, and on 14 March he ordered Phu to hold Pleiku and Kontum and retake Ban Me Thuot. This is the counter-directional finding at work, and in this war it is also the mechanism of the gain. A President who believed he need not give anything up gave no order to give up the Highlands by Route 7 and no contradictory orders to Truong. Without those orders the collapse of I and II Corps came three to five weeks late. The net, judged in both directions as DIRMT01-5 requires, runs as follows. The refusal cost half the Highland force, the 2nd Division and the reserve's spring at Da Nang, all of which a planned withdrawal in the dry season would have saved. It gained a season, a capital not attacked in April, a government still in office, and 146,000 fewer civilians lost on the Highland road than history's convoy of tears, a figure resting on two estimates of very different quality. I judge the gain the larger, because the alternative to the refusal in this war was not the Dalat study carried out but history's order of 14 March. Thieu was the same man in both wars, and only the money and the message changed his answer.

The PAVN's 1972 losses are restated under DIRMT01-6. They were about a tenth above the historical figure, concentrated in the divisions struck at An Loc and Kontum, and were replaced within the North's 1973 flow. The 1974–75 divisions were worse led and on time. They are not part of this memorandum's mechanism. The delay of 1975 came from the top of the Republic, not from the bottom of the PAVN.

The Republic's state at the end of 1974, before the last offensive, is judged in the snapshot. Night control in the held belt of the Delta and the Saigon region was about one to three steps better than history's, and the Front's district committees there had lost about twice the historical share of their members in 1973. The government's standing in the hamlets was history's, after the decrees of 1972. The army's battalions were better led, its divisions and corps no better, and its reserve was tied to I Corps. It had fought the year near the 1973 allowance and lost perhaps 2,500–6,500 fewer dead than history's 30,000 on the two rates of EV000-91. The President was more confident in his ally than history's, and his base was no broader.

By region. In the Delta the effect is economic and not military. IV Corps was never attacked in 1975, and the dry-season crop on about 300,000 hectares of the held Delta, with the economic aid about a tenth larger than history's, held the price of rice in Saigon a little below history's in 1974; at the end of 1975 the Delta is the Republic's whole and is its granary. In the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps the effect is mixed. Quang Ngai and Quang Tin collapsed in March as history's did, because the 2nd Division's families had not been moved. Hue was given up in order on 18 April, Da Nang was evacuated under SOUTHERN WALL in May, and Qui Nhon and Tuy Hoa went in June and July. In the Highlands the effect is a siege instead of a rout. Pleiku held three weeks and about 9,000 civilians were lost on Route 19 instead of 155,000 on Route 7. The record does not say how the Jarai and Bahnar of Pleiku and Kontum fared under the PAVN after April, and I name that as a gap rather than fill it. In the Saigon belt the effect is the whole of the gain: the capital was not attacked, Xuan Loc held through the rains, and the Bien Hoa arsenal ran three shifts.

The soldier's family is where the spring of 1975 was decided, as POSMT01-6 found, and this memorandum confirms the finding from the other side. In I Corps Truong moved the dependants of the 1st Division and the Marines first, about 70,000 people by sea in three weeks from 15 March, and those divisions held the My Chanh and withdrew in order. The 2nd Division's families lived in the dependants' quarters at Quang Ngai and Tam Ky and were not moved, and about 5,000 of its men left their units for them. At Pleiku the families went at the head of the breakout column. At Da Nang they went from 8 May, twelve days before the soldiers. What reached POSMT01-6's mechanism was not a reform of the army. It was time, and a corps commander's plan that time allowed him to carry out. The soldier's pay was the Republic's own budget, in piasters the oil shock was eating. The larger appropriation did not reach it, and the economic aid voted with it slowed the piaster's fall in the second half of 1974 without stopping it.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's decisions in this phase had authors, and they disagreed. The Politburo under Le Duan watched a President who did not fall and was more cautious through 1974 than history's. The General Staff's plan for the dry season, approved at the conference of 30 September – 8 October, held the main blow for the Highlands, and Hanoi cut the B-2 Front's and the Tri-Thien front's autumn raids back to Phu Loc alone. That was reaction: the threat worked while it was untested.

 The test was the B-2 Front's own decision. Lieutenant General Tran Van Tra argued in Hanoi in November 1974 for the attack on Phuoc Long, as he had historically, and he won against a Politburo with more reason to fear the answer. Three things drove him. The first was his front's own reckoning of what the B-2 needed to know before it could plan its part of the 1975–76 campaign. The second was the southern command's long view that Hanoi's caution had cost it 1968's chances. The third was a reading of American politics that was right: the President wanted to return and Congress would not let him. Nothing the Republic or the Americans did provoked it. Tra chose the province, the moment and the test, and Hoang Cam's 4th Corps carried it out with the 7th and 3rd Divisions. The test cost the North about 1,300 killed and wounded and answered the question the conference of 18 December – 8 January had set itself.

The second disagreement decided the date. On 31 March 1975 Tra argued for a thrust on Saigon in May with the 4th Corps, Group 232 and what the 1st Corps could bring, on the reasoning that the Republic's reserve was in I Corps. Van Tien Dung answered that the 1st Corps could not reach the capital before the third week of May, that two corps would be attacking five divisions in the rains, and that this would repeat the error of 1968. Le Duan took Dung's view. The North's decision to wait was its own, taken out of its own doctrine and the memory of 1968, on a map the Republic's March had drawn.

The adaptation cost the North a year and nothing more. It spent the rains bringing the 1st Corps south, extending the pipeline to Loc Ninh and asking Moscow to ship the 130mm ammunition and the tanks of 1976 in the autumn of 1975. The Soviet and Chinese supplies behaved as variables with their own politics. Moscow shipped most of what was asked, because it wanted the war ended before the American election year. Beijing, which had taken the Paracels in January 1974 and had no wish to see Hanoi's victory hastened by Soviet guns, sent rice and trucks and no more. The October 1975 resolution set the general offensive on Saigon for the first months of 1976.

### Downstream

No phase follows in the record. The war does not end with it, and §4.N proposes a redraw. The downstream of this phase is the offensive of 1976. The Republic meets it with III and IV Corps, a rebuilt reserve, a full arsenal, its Delta and the capital's belt, a ration near 1974's and no American air power. The North meets it with five corps and the Soviet deliveries of 1976 brought forward.

### Clock

In Phase VI the clock is the appropriation cycle, and it has two hands. The President's hand runs slow. He stays in office and spends his capital on the Republic, and the administration's tolerance is at +5..+8 at the end of 1975. Congress's hand runs as history's did on combat and more slowly on money. Case–Church stands, the bombers' authority is refused in January 1975, and every dollar after it is voted for fuel, rounds and refugees. The public's hand does not move: 60% called the war a mistake in 1973 and nothing in the package changed that³. A provision that keeps a President in office does not make a war popular. What it does is put the question of April 1975 to a Congress through a President who can put it. Congress answered with money and not with power, and the money bought a season.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the cumulative force now changes the war's end as well as its interior. This memorandum makes the change, and it makes it in 1974–75.

The Republic of Vietnam stands at the end of 1975, against a fall on 30 April 1975. It has lost the Highlands, Quang Tri, Thua Thien, Quang Ngai, Quang Tin, Phuoc Long and the central coast north of Khanh Hoa, and about a third of its army's infantry. It holds III and IV Corps whole, the coast from Nha Trang south, about half the population of 1974, its Delta, its capital and its plants. Its government is Thieu's, with the Senate's confidence. History had Minh's surrender. Saigon was not attacked in April and Xuan Loc was not fought. The capital's fall is expected eight to thirteen months after history's.

Formations: the Airborne and Marine Divisions, whose brigades history's spring broke at Hue, Da Nang and Suoi Ca, come out of Da Nang under SOUTHERN WALL and are rebuilt at Vung Tau and Long Binh as a general reserve. The 1st Division loses about a third of itself at Hue rather than ceasing to exist. Of II Corps' Highland force, about 11,000 of 25,000 regulars and Rangers reach the coast with their weapons, against history's 6th Ranger Battalion and 19th Armoured Cavalry Squadron. The 2nd Division and the Highland force's other half are lost as history's were.

Lives. In 1974 the Republic lost perhaps 1,500–4,000 fewer dead on this memorandum's account, and 2,500–6,500 fewer with memorandum 008's, against about 30,000. On the Highland road about 9,000 civilians were lost against history's estimate of 155,000, a difference that rests on a contested historical figure and my own judgment of a column a fifth the size. At Son My between 347 and 504 people were not killed on 16 March 1968¹⁹. Corporal McMahon and Lance Corporal Judge live. The American dead of the war are otherwise history's.

Money: about $950 million for fiscal 1975 against $700 million, $150 million in March and $175 million in May 1975 against nothing, and about $800 million for fiscal 1976.

What has not changed is as concrete. The Paris Agreement and Case–Church passed as they did historically. No American aircraft flew in combat over Indochina after 15 August 1973. The PAVN rebuilt on its historical schedule, took Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot on their historical dates, and kept the initiative in every fight but the siege of Pleiku. The 2nd Division dissolved toward its families as history's I Corps did. Hanoi never wavered in its aim. The margin over history is a year, and it is a large margin for a provision that put no American soldier, aircraft or dollar into the war except through a vote.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The outcome in its own terms comes first. It is a reprieve of about a year. The Republic survives the spring offensive of 1975 with its capital, its south and its government. It does so because a President in Washington who did not fall got Congress to pay for most of a year's war, and a President in Saigon, holding that money and that promise, refused to give anything up. The record ends on 31 December 1975 with five PAVN corps assembled for an offensive on Saigon in the first months of 1976 and no American air power to meet them. On the ladder it is rung (b), dependent survival. It is the first change of rung in this record.

**The rung, and why (b) and not (a).** DIRMT01-1 warned that a change of date does not by itself move the rung between (a) and (b) "while the ladder's overlap stands". The test of v1.0.6 removed the overlap. Condition b2 names the date: Saigon stands past 30 April 1975. Its twin, US combat forces staying, describes a different war. Saigon stands at the end of 1975, eight months past the date, and the script requires the rung the test supports. So the move to (b) is the test's verdict and not a date carried into a rung. I hold it on the war as well. The Republic that enters 1976 has fought an offensive without American air and kept its heartland, which history's never did. Part 2 expected that the provision would not reach the critical path. The test in the chapter found that it did, through the one premise Part 2 had not traced to the end: Thieu's decision of 11–14 March 1975 was itself a function of the money and the message. I report that as a correction of Part 2's expectation, not as a confirmation of it.

**Why not (c).** Rung (c) needs the Republic to hold the countryside in every corps region without American ground troops, and to stand at the end of the record with American air power available. It holds neither. I and II Corps are lost, northern Binh Dinh was lost in 1972, and Case–Church forbade the air power from August 1973 and was not lifted when the President asked. The rung test also passes c1 on 1972, when the RVNAF stopped the Easter Offensive at An Loc and Kontum with American air power and no American ground units. That was true historically too, so it is not this memorandum's gain. POSMT01-1 said the rung would not move until a provision reached an air arm the Republic owns, sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation, or the choice of the corps commanders. This provision reached the appropriation itself. It moved the rung one step and no further, because it reached neither the air arm nor the corps commanders. The corps commanders in this war were the same men; what changed was the order they were given.

**The date of the fall.** Saigon was not attacked in 1975, and my estimate of its fall is between February and May 1976, in the dry season, from an offensive by five corps. Four mechanisms in the cumulative force pull it later. The first is the rebuilt reserve, the Airborne and Marines at Vung Tau and Long Binh. The second is the arsenal running three shifts, with the ration near 1974's on a fiscal 1976 appropriation of about $800 million. The third is the Delta's rice and posts. The fourth is a government still in office whose Senate gave it confidence. Four mechanisms pull it earlier. The Soviet deliveries of 1976 were brought forward into the autumn of 1975. The Republic lost a third of its infantry and the heavy equipment of II Corps and the 1st and 2nd Divisions. The PAVN's 130mm still outranges every gun the Republic can feed. And there is no American air power. The second set dominates, because the 1976 offensive meets the Republic on ground it must hold, the capital's approaches, with no mechanism in the force that changes the artillery duel. An offensive of five corps is the one the Republic could not stop in 1975 when it came, and nothing it gains in the rains changes the arithmetic. The reprieve could stretch past the dry season of 1976 only if Congress paid for re-equipment, which the May vote refused. It could also stretch if the Politburo waited again, which its October resolution rules out.

**Is the 1974 vote still history's?.** No. Under DIRMT01-2 the appropriation of 1974 and the refusal of April 1975 were to be treated as events a provision can change, and this provision changes both. POSMT01-5 held the vote as history's until a provision changed what the Republic needs Congress to pay for. This one changed Congress's paymaster instead, and the position is reversed below. The April request is now Nixon's, smaller and earlier, and Congress voted the refugee money and $175 million of $400 million.

**The running sums.** American dead saved: memoranda 001–010 judged about 3,700–7,800 (memorandum 010 §4.N). This memorandum adds the two Marines of 29 April 1975 and nothing else. The volunteers die at the draftees' rate, so the sum stays at about 3,700–7,800, six to thirteen percent of the historical dead. It supports the lower half of the range of `US.casualties` it already sits in, and it does not move tolerance. The Republic's soldiers saved: memoranda 009 and 010 judged about 8,000–23,000 in 1964–72. Memorandum 008 judged 1,000–2,500 in 1974 at a third more 105mm fire, and this memorandum adds 1,500–4,000 in 1974 at a rate of 5–13% fewer losses in the fights accepted with fuller supply and on raids not made. That gives about 10,500–29,500 to the end of 1974. It is shown at work at Rach Bap and Phu Loc in 1974 and at Pleiku in 1975, as DIRCP01-2 requires, and it is a judgment on its rates. For 1975 I give no sum. The soldiers lost at Pleiku and in the Mang Yang, at Hue and at Da Nang must be set against those history's spring lost and those it never fought at Xuan Loc and Saigon, and the record does not count either side closely enough to subtract. Civilians: the 347–504 of Son My¹⁹, and about 146,000 fewer lost on the Highland road, a difference whose historical term is itself an estimate.

**Floors not met, and why.** Part 2 of this memorandum finished with two warnings that its text did not explain. It had four employment concepts against a floor of six, because a political provision has few ways to be employed: the letters, the supplemental, the volunteer and the district exhaust them, and three more were abandoned for stated reasons. It had about thirteen equipment designations against twenty, because the package's instruments are votes, letters and induction notices, and the designations it names are the ones its downstream effects reach (the B-52J, the GBUs, the C-130A, the UH-1H, the 105mm, 155mm and 175mm). In Part 3, Phase III changes one event against a floor of three, for the reason stated in its Clock. These are the provision's shape and not shortfalls of the analysis.

**Phase redraw.** Phase VI ends on 31 December 1975 with the war unfinished, and the record cannot say how the Republic falls or whether it does. I propose that the next checkpoint add a Phase VII, "The last dry season (Jan 1976 –)", and move EV011-3's preparations into its opening. Until then the end state is open on its date, and every later memorandum tests its effects against the offensive of 1976 as this one tested its own against the critical path of 1975.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **Saigon stands at the end of 1975.** The Republic is not attacked at its capital in April 1975, Thieu does not resign and Minh never takes office. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps, the coast from Nha Trang south and about half its 1974 population, a reprieve of about a year over history's 30 April.
- **The critical path of 1975 bent for the first time.** I and II Corps collapsed three to five weeks later than history's: the Highlands on 19 April and Hue on 18 April instead of 25 March, with Da Nang held through May. On 25 March the Politburo put the assault back to the dry season of 1976. That had been the threshold since MT01.
- **The Republic fought 1974 on about the 1973 allowance.** It had about $950 million for fiscal 1975 against $700 million, buying about what $1.2 billion had bought, with fuel, aircraft and heavy rounds reached and 1,500–4,000 fewer dead.
- **The Highlands were a siege, not a rout.** Pleiku held three weeks and broke two assaults. About 11,000 of 25,000 soldiers reached the coast, and about 9,000 civilians were lost on Route 19 against history's estimate of 155,000 on Route 7.
- **The Republic carried out its only planned withdrawal.** SOUTHERN WALL lifted about 70,000 soldiers and 250,000 civilians out of Da Nang in May, families first, and rebuilt the Airborne and Marines as a reserve.
- **No massacre at Son My.** No revelation, no Calley trial and no national argument in 1971. A quieter draft took 70,000–95,000 fewer notices a year in 1966–68, and the public's tolerance was a little higher in 1968–71.
- **The rung moved from (a) to (b),** by the test of rules/RUNGS.toml and on the war.

### What has not changed

- **American combat in Indochina ended by law in August 1973.** Case–Church passed as history's did, and Congress refused the President's request for the bombers. No American aircraft returned.
- **Hanoi's aim and its initiative.** It rebuilt on schedule, chose Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, and in October 1975 set the offensive on Saigon for 1976.
- **The cost of Thieu's refusal.** The Dalat study was refused in March. The 2nd Division dissolved toward its families, and half the Highland force and the heavy equipment of II Corps were lost, all of which a planned withdrawal in the dry season would have saved.
- **The public's verdict.** 53% in 1968, about 55% in 1969–70 and 60% from 1971 called the war a mistake, as history's did. The package removed two emblems and not the argument³.
- **The American dead, and the command climate behind My Lai.** The volunteers die at the draftees' rate. With no scandal, no one examined how the body count shaped the conduct of operations.
- **The artillery duel and the soldier's pay.** The PAVN's 130mm outranged every gun the money fed, and the larger appropriation never reached the soldier's piasters.
- **The end is expected.** The Republic is expected to fall in the dry season of 1976 to five corps and no American air power (§4.N).

### Trajectory Log entry

Memorandum 011 changed the war from Washington. In this war the Watergate burglary never happened, a quarter of the draftees volunteered, and nobody was killed at Son My. The volunteers and the missing massacre quieted the draft and the courtrooms of 1966–71 without changing the public's verdict or any battle. The removal of Watergate did what ten theater provisions could not: it reached the level at which the war was lost. Case–Church passed anyway, and no bomber returned. But a President who did not resign got the Republic about $950 million for fiscal 1975 instead of $700 million, with fuel and aircraft, and after Phuoc Long he asked Congress for more. Thieu, holding that money and that promise, refused on 11–14 March 1975 to give anything up. The Highlands fell after a three-week siege at Pleiku, Hue was withdrawn on 18 April and Da Nang was held. With no double collapse in March, the Politburo put Saigon back to the dry season of 1976. The end state moves for the first time, from rung (a) to (b): a reprieve of about a year, with the Republic's fall expected between February and May 1976.
