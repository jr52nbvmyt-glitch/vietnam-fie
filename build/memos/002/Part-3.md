## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I now command is the same war on its borders and a different war in its villages, and the difference has grown. The first memorandum gave the Republic something to defend in the hamlets. This one gives it a policeman who lives there, a Special Branch that knows who the Party's men are, and a district chief who stays long enough to learn his district. Together they give the Republic, in the part of the country it holds at night, something it never had on the historical course until 1969: a government in the district. They do not give it a single battalion, and they do not move the date on which Saigon falls.

The war opens on 1 January 1964 with four decrees instead of two. Minh's council promulgates the land reform and the Commission Against Corruption, and with them a National Police rebuilt around the village and a Provincial Administration Selection Board that will choose every province and district chief by examination from a ranked list. The decrees are read on Radio Saigon that evening. The landlords protest the land decree; the officers who will lose their provinces say nothing yet, because the list does not exist yet.

Thirty days later Khanh takes power, as he did historically, and does what every coup-maker did: he detains the police chief and names his own men as acting chiefs in eleven provinces and districts. The difference is that this time the Americans have something to pull. The Aid Compliance Board gives notice that a province whose chief is not taken from the list will get no counterpart funds, the Director General, Vo Dinh Cuong, is back at his desk in three days, and Khanh decides that a Board he cannot staff is worth more to him as a reputation for reform than as a fight with the Embassy. The first list is published on 27 April. Nine of Khanh's eleven acting chiefs are gone by June. It is a partial failure of the reform's first test, and it sets the pattern that will hold for six years: each government that inherits the Board finds it cheaper to keep than to break.

Through 1964 and the first half of 1965 the Republic comes apart in Saigon exactly as it did. Khanh's charter, the September coup attempt, the High National Council, the fall of Khanh, Quat, and the arrival of Thieu and Ky all run on their historical dates. Two things in that sequence are new. In September the plotters' broadcast promises to restore the corps commanders' authority over the provinces, which means the end of the Board, and its failure within a day attaches the provincial reform, like the land decree, to the Young Turks who defeated it. And the Special Branch, which watches the generals as well as the Front, tells Khanh and the Embassy two days ahead that the 7th Division's battalions are moving. A police that is not one faction's instrument raises the price of a coup by a little. It does not stop one.

In the countryside the posts follow the titling line. The first is settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964; by July 1965 there are about 1,100, each a brick house with ten constables, a radio and, by rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village. The Front reads them correctly within weeks and tells its district committees to kill constables before they learn the village. Where the rule is broken it works: about 85 posts fall and about 410 constables die by July 1965, almost all of them in posts settled ahead of night security. Where the rule is kept, four posts in five that are attacked hold. The first harvest of the Special Branch is about 1,900 arrests on evidence, and the magistrates let a third of them go, which annoys the Branch and reassures me. The Front's own agent copies the Dinh Tuong informant cards for half a year before he is found, and eleven men die for it. The programme works where the government holds the night and is a list of targets where it does not, which is what its design said.

None of this touches the main-force war. Binh Gia, Song Be, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai go as they went. The PAVN regiments come down the Trail on the schedule their training set. Pleiku, Flaming Dart, Rolling Thunder and the Marines at Da Nang follow, and in July the President commits the Army. I would like to say that a better district government in the Delta changed that decision. It did not. The decision was made on the destruction of ARVN battalions in Phuoc Long and Quang Ngai, and nothing in this memorandum saved one of them.

The Front takes a year to understand the provincial reform. It reads the first list as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, which on the record of 1963–64 is a reasonable reading. By the summer of 1965 it can see that the new chiefs have stayed through three governments, and it draws the conclusion that hurts: the chiefs who keep their Popular Forces in the hamlets are its most dangerous enemies. Between August 1965 and June 1966 it kills nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs. The Board answers each death in about three weeks, from the same list, and the districts do not drift back to the towns while they wait. The Front never again kills chiefs at that rate, because it has killed the easy ones and the rest have learned.

The American war begins its full stride at the Ia Drang, and behind it the police war has its first complete success. Operation NIGHT LANTERN goes back in November 1965 to Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, the Long An districts where the Front shot title holders off a posted list in 1964. A province chief and a district chief from the list, Long An's own Regional and Popular Forces, two Police Field Force companies, 140 constables and a Special Branch section of 45 settle 14 village posts in five months. On the night of 19 January 1966 the 506th Local Force Battalion comes back to Phuoc Lai, where the killings began, and is beaten off by a Popular Forces platoon, ten constables and an RF company that comes down the canal in sampans before two in the morning. Three weeks later a night check in Long Huu finds a card made out for an older man, and the thread leads to the district committee's security chief. By April the committee has moved to the edge of the Rung Sat and visits instead of living among its people, its tax take is a third of the year before, and the 400 titles held in the district office since 1964 are handed to their families in their own hamlets. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 lives. It did in six months what no sweep of Long An had done in six years, and it is the clearest single case of what these provisions are for.

The cost of the instrument comes due in the spring of 1966. Ky moves against Thi, the Struggle Movement takes Da Nang and Hue, and Ky removes Cuong by a decree his statute forbids and gives the police to Nguyen Ngoc Loan. The Embassy protests and does not pull the lever, because the Directory is the government the President has just embraced at Honolulu. Loan takes the Field Force into Hue with the census of the pagoda quarter in his hands, and about 1,300 people are detained. The magistrates' review, the one safeguard he cannot suspend without a decree the Americans have priced at the whole Public Safety programme, releases most of them by October. But the first American stories about an "American-built secret police" date from that June, three years ahead of the historical Phoenix stories, and a few dozen of Hue's student leaders, knowing that the census found them, go to the Front's city committee. That is the counter-directional finding made real. I had asked in January 1964 for a line drawn and held at the first test; it held in the provinces and failed in Saigon.

Through 1966 and 1967 the large operations run as they did: Masher/White Wing, Hastings, Attleboro, Cedar Falls, Junction City, Con Thien, Dak To. Each wins its battles and leaves the war as it found it. Beneath them the held belt is cleared. By 1967 the Special Branch's arrests there have fallen to about 40 percent of their peak, and the systems analysts in Washington read it as failure and ask for quotas. I tell them in writing that the count is falling because there is almost no one left in the villages to arrest. We win half the argument: quotas only beyond the posts. Where they run, false arrests follow, about ten complaints to the Commission for every one from the districts without them. And the cadres who left the villages have not vanished. They have gone to Can Tho, My Tho and the edges of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knows far less than one in a village of 2,000. When CORDS is created in May 1967 it finds a police of about 70,000 and a Special Branch with three years of files, and ICEX is built on the Branch's district offices instead of beside them.

Tet comes on the historical night and is decided where it was decided: in Saigon, at Hue, at Khe Sanh, and in Washington. In the held belt of the Delta the police changes the night in the way the land programme did, and along the same line. The urban sections know about a third of the legal cadres who surface to lead the uprising, and take about 1,700 of them in the first fortnight; the guides who were to bring the battalions into Can Tho do not arrive, because the night check took them two weeks before; and the 21st Division goes into the battle nearly at strength, because the census has made its deserters findable at home. Hue is the other side of the coin. The Front's lists there are better than they were historically, because its agent copied the Republic's own census and the Struggle's student leaders knew how it was kept, and police officers, constables, census clerks and their families are about a fifth of the identified dead. I do not raise the total. I say only that the record we built to find the Party's men was used to find ours, as I warned it would be, and that the Directorate orders the destruction plan for every file in the country within six weeks.

The President's decision of 31 March, Abrams, Paris, the bombing halt and Nixon's election run on their dates. Phoenix arrives in this war as a new name over an old institution: in the districts where the Special Branch has run target folders for three years or more, the Commission's audit finds about one claimed neutralization in ten unsupported; where the Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the sector intelligence officers run them, about one in two. Phoenix is the instrument of feud its critics described where the institution is youngest, and a refinement of four years' work where it is oldest.

In 1969 the Front concedes the villages as it conceded the land. COSVN's Resolution 9 tells the Delta's party committees to govern villages with settled posts by visiting cadres who sleep elsewhere, and to put their best security men into penetrating the Special Branch and the provincial administration. The countryside of 1969–71 is the best the government ever has, as it was historically, and better than that in the held belt. But the historical police grows into the same villages after 1969, and the gap this memorandum opened in 1964 narrows to experience and files. The American lever lapses with the withdrawal. Thieu does not abolish the Board; he replaces its two generals with his own officers and has his Prime Minister appoint the third name on the list for half the province vacancies. The list still excludes the incompetent and the bought. It no longer excludes the loyal and mediocre. The Phoenix hearings of July 1971 are less damaging than they were, because the Commission's audits and the magistrates' review give the administration something lawful to describe, and the Public Safety appropriation survives with a cut of about a tenth.

From there the war runs on its historical rails. The Easter Offensive of 1972 is fought by PAVN divisions with T-54s and 130mm guns against ARVN divisions and American air power, at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc, and a village constable is no factor in it. The Paris Agreement leaves the PAVN in the South. Congress writes the end of American combat into law and cuts the aid. In 1975 the blow falls at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, Thieu orders the Highlands abandoned, and on 30 April the PAVN enters Saigon. The Republic falls on the historical date, rung (a), collapse.

What this memorandum has changed is what the Republic was in the years it lived: a state that could govern its own belt at night five years earlier than it historically did, with a police that arrested on evidence and chiefs who were chosen for ability. What it has not changed is that the Republic died of a conventional invasion after the Americans left, and that no policeman in any village could have stopped the tanks. When the files of 1964 are captured in the province capitals in 1975, whether the thermite charges are fired will decide how many of the people who trusted the police survive the peace. That question lies after the war's last date, and I record it here because I signed the order that created those files.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.gvn_legitimacy +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · FIND.vci +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4).*

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964, none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year¹. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon, and at Binh Gia, Song Be, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they destroy ARVN battalions in set-piece battles. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

One thing in this phase is new. On the first day of 1964 the junta promulgates a land reform that ends rent on tenanted rice land, gives the land free to the man who works it and confirms to him whatever the Viet Minh or the Front had already given, together with an independent commission against corruption backed by an American power to suspend aid. Neither touches the main force, and the double collapse in Saigon and in the field runs much as before. What changes, slowly and only where the government holds the hamlets, is what the government's arrival means to a tenant: by the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the first musters have shown the RVNAF that it is about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls². The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

A second change runs beside the first. The same decrees of 1 January gave the Republic a rural police and a system of choosing its province and district chiefs by examination, from a ranked list, for at least two years. Khanh's coup tested both within a month and both survived it. By July 1965 about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have been appointed through the list, and the Special Branch has begun to take the Front's village committees on evidence; the Front has killed about 410 constables, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security³. None of it touches the main-force war or Saigon's coups.

#### The Land-to-the-Tiller Decree and the Commission Against Corruption
*1 Jan 1964 · Saigon · new · EV001-1*
On 1 January 1964 the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh, with Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho's cabinet, promulgated two decree-laws drafted with the US Mission over the preceding weeks. The first ended the collection of rent on tenanted rice land from the coming main-season harvest, made every present tiller the provisional owner of the land he worked up to 3 hectares in the Delta and 1 hectare in the Central Lowlands, set the landlord's retention of tenanted land at zero and his compensation at 2.5 times the land's annual paddy yield, 20% in cash and the rest in eight-year bonds indexed to the paddy price, and confirmed by name every distribution the Viet Minh or the Front had made to the man now tilling the land. The second created the Commission Against Corruption under a Commissioner appointed for six years, Judge Truong Vinh Khoa of the Saigon Court of Appeal, a Special Court for Corruption, a Pay and Muster Verification Service, and a Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board whose American co-chair, the Deputy Chief of Mission, could suspend aid to a named command, importer or province and whose concurrence was needed to remove the Commissioner⁴.

Minh read the land decree on Radio Saigon that evening; its first sentence was the confirmation of the Front's distributions. Within a week the Vietnamese Information Service's loudspeaker teams and leaflets had carried it to the district towns of the Delta and the coastal Centre. The landowners' associations of Saigon and Can Tho protested to the council within days, and the protest was carried by officers of landed families who sat on it. Two USAF-contracted survey aircraft began photographing the rice lands from Tan Son Nhut on 13 January, tied to ground control laid by an Army topographic detachment from the old French triangulation; they finished the Delta in early April and the coastal Centre in May².

#### ◆ The Police and Provincial Administration decrees; the first list
*1 Jan – 27 Apr 1964 · Saigon · new · EV002-1*
On 1 January 1964, in the same session that promulgated the land and anti-corruption decrees, the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh promulgated two more decree-laws drafted with the US Mission and the aid mission's Public Safety Division. The first rebuilt the National Police around the village: a resident post of 8 to 12 constables in every village the government held, a Police Special Branch with sole charge of intelligence on the Viet Cong infrastructure, a Police Field Force to be raised at once rather than in 1966, a national identity card and family census, a pay and career statute, and magistrate review of every arrest within 72 hours, with detention without trial capped at two years and reviewed every six months. The Directorate General was given to Vo Dinh Cuong, a career inspector of the old Sûreté who had served in the police under the French and under Diem and had kept out of Nhu's political service, for a fixed term removable only for cause³. The second decree created the Provincial Administration Selection Board, seven members under Judge Dinh Quang Tuyen of the Court of Cassation, and an Inspectorate of Provincial Administration, and made unlawful the appointment of any province chief, district chief or deputy for administration who was not taken from the Board's ranked list⁵.

The two decrees were read on Radio Saigon on the evening of 1 January after the land decree, and drew less notice than it did; the landowners protested the land decree, and the officers who would lose their provinces said nothing yet, because there was nothing yet to lose. The British Advisory Mission under R. G. K. Thompson, in Saigon since 1961, lent four Special Branch officers from the Federation of Malaysia to write the Special Branch's first manuals; the first 2,500 recruits a month entered the police training centres at Vung Tau and Da Lat from February. The Board's written examination was sat on 2 March at the National Institute of Administration by about 1,640 officers and civil servants, of whom about 1,000 were judged credible after the three-day assessments held through March and April³.

On 27 April 1964 the Board published its first list in the official gazette: ranked candidates for 31 posts, the 6 province chiefs and 25 district chiefs whom the Inspectorate's first sweep had found worst, nine of them in Khanh's "acting" appointments of February. Khanh's government appointed 27 of the 31 from the first name on each list, 3 from the second and 1 from the third; it refused no list. By 31 December 1964 about 95 of the Republic's roughly 290 chiefs had been appointed through the list, a third of the whole, and the Board's register of candidates had become the first record the Republic had ever kept of what its officers could actually do³.

#### ◆ Khanh's coup
*30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · changed from history · EV000-1*
Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh without firing a shot. He acted with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were put under house arrest on a charge of plotting a French-backed neutralist settlement, and Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho was detained. The one death was Major Nguyen Van Nhung, Minh's aide, executed after the takeover. Khanh kept Minh as a figurehead chief of state. The generals were tried in closed session on 28–29 May and found guilty only of "lax morality"⁶.

The coup was not caused by the decrees of 1 January; it was the product of the generals' rivalries and Khanh's ambition, and it came on the historical date. But the reforms shaped what followed. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay gave Khanh their quiet support in the hope of a government that would let the decrees lapse. In February Khanh asked Commissioner Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals; Khoa replied that the Commission had no evidence against them and would bring no case without it. Khanh moved to replace him, and the American co-chair of the Aid Compliance Board refused the concurrence the decree required². The generals went before a military court instead, on the "lax morality" charge of the historical record.

Washington was surprised but quick to back the new man; President Johnson sent Khanh a personal note on 2 February⁶, which in this war carried the condition, at the urging of the US Mission and this headquarters, that the United States would support no government that repealed either decree². Khanh, who needed American backing more than he needed the landlords, accepted it and kept both. The coup mattered less for what Khanh did than for the precedent it set: a second violent change of government within three months, carried out by officers against officers, and accepted by the United States. Khanh moved, as every coup-maker had, to place his men in the provinces, and the decree of 1 January now stood in his way. In March, Secretary of Defense McNamara visited and endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 of 17 March 1964 committed the United States to the survival of an independent, non-communist South Vietnam. In June 1964 General William C. Westmoreland, Harkins's deputy since January, took command of MACV, and General Maxwell D. Taylor replaced Henry Cabot Lodge as ambassador in July.

In this war Khanh's coup was the first test of the police and provincial decrees, and it came thirty days after them, before the Board had published a list. On the morning of 30 January Khanh's officers detained the new Director General of National Police, Vo Dinh Cuong, as they had detained the police chief on the historical course, and in the first week of February Khanh named "acting" chiefs in eleven provinces and districts. The US Mission and this headquarters treated the two decrees as part of the condition Johnson's note attached to the land and anti-corruption decrees: the Aid Compliance Board gave notice that a province whose chief was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds once the first list was published, and Cuong was released on 2 February and restored to his office. Khanh kept the Board, because he had no candidate for its chairmanship and saw a reputation for reform in it, and kept his acting chiefs until the list replaced nine of the eleven in April and May; the other two placed well enough to be appointed from it³. It was a partial failure of the reform's first test and a partial success, and it set the pattern that held until 1970: the Board survived each government because the government that inherited it found it cheaper to keep than to break.

#### ◆ The first posts and the Front's answer
*1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · new · EV002-2*
The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads⁵. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered³.

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected. A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about four in five held³.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed³. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

#### The first titling teams
*16 Mar – 31 Dec 1964 · An Giang, Phong Dinh, Vinh Long, Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · new · EV001-2*
The first 40 mobile titling teams of about 25 men each took the field on 16 March 1964, working outward from province capitals and main roads into villages the district could reinforce by day, each escorted by a Regional Forces company armed with M1 and M2 carbines and M1918A2 automatic rifles. The first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen in An Giang on 21 April, in the Hoa Hao country where the Front was weakest, and the first in a contested district at Phung Hiep in Phong Dinh on 11 May. By July 70 teams were in the field, the most the provinces could escort without stripping their daytime reaction forces. They moved by road, in the LCM-6 and LCVP craft of the River Assault Groups and by hired sampan, and to villages beyond both by CH-21C and UH-1B helicopters when the operations staff would release them².

By 31 December 1964 titles had been issued for about 210,000 hectares to about 170,000 families, about a fifth of the target, almost all of it in hamlets held by the government at night or contested only lightly: An Giang, the riverine districts of Phong Dinh and Vinh Long along the Bassac, the inner districts of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and the coastal plain around Hoi An and Tam Ky in Quang Nam. The rent stop held at the main-season harvest of December 1964 in most districts the government controlled; landlords' agents collected under Regional Forces escort in parts of Vinh Long, Vinh Binh and Dinh Tuong, which led to the first suspension a few months later. Twenty-three team members were killed by mines and ambush during the year². The figure the Mission reported to Washington each month was titles issued; this headquarters reported it with the warning that it measured the government's paperwork, not the peasants' allegiance.

#### The dawn musters
*7 Apr – 31 Dec 1964 · ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions and the Regional and Popular Forces of the Delta, then countrywide · new · EV001-4*
The Pay and Muster Verification Service's first 20 teams began unannounced dawn musters of the ARVN 21st Division's 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments around Bac Lieu and Soc Trang on 7 April 1964, and of the 7th and 9th Divisions in Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long in May; by the autumn 60 teams were mustering about 480 units a month across all four corps. Each man was enrolled on an identity card with his photograph and fingerprints, and from August the Service's paymasters paid the enrolled units of IV Corps at the parade rather than through their commanders. By 31 December about 41,000 names had been struck from the rolls of a force carried at about 514,000, about 8%; the rate ran from about 4% in the Airborne and Marine battalions to about 15% in some Regional Forces companies of the Delta². These are counts of men present against names on the roll, not estimates, and they are the best figures this headquarters had ever had for the army it advised.

Washington read them as a smaller army. Secretary McNamara, visiting in May, was briefed on the first returns and told the President that the RVNAF's real strength was below its reported strength by a margin that would grow as the musters spread. In the units mustered twice, the second muster found few ghosts, because commanders had struck them before the teams came. The Commission opened 412 cases by the end of the year, almost all against company and battalion commanders and province finance officers, and brought 61 to trial².

#### The Can Giuoc killings
*9 Jun – 14 Jul 1964 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · new · EV001-3*
In May 1964 titling teams from Tan An began work in the villages of Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, the rice districts of Long An between Saigon and the sea, where the survey of December 1963 had found most of the strategic hamlets destroyed and the Front's infrastructure intact. The province chief, under pressure from Saigon for a showcase close to the capital, sent the teams into hamlets the government held by day only and posted the claimants' names in the village offices, as the procedure then required². Each team worked under the escort of a Long An Regional Forces company that withdrew to its post at dusk.

On the night of 9 June armed propaganda teams of the Can Giuoc district committee, with the Long An provincial security section and screened by a company of the provincial 506th Local Force Battalion, entered three hamlets of Phuoc Lai village, read out names from the posted list, and shot four men who had claimed titles and the village land committee's secretary. Over the following five weeks the same pattern was repeated in eleven hamlets of the two districts: by 14 July 37 title holders and 9 members of village land committees had been killed, and several hundred families had brought their titles back to the district office at Can Giuoc rather than keep them in the house. The Regional Forces companies, posted in mud forts along Route 5 and the canal, did not move at night, and no reaction force came. The figures are this headquarters' count from district and province reports, cross-checked by the American province adviser, and are a floor².

The killings were the land programme's first lesson in what it did not change. Teams stopped posting names in hamlets not held at night, and titles in contested hamlets were thereafter held at the district office until the hamlet was secured or the owner asked for his. A COSVN directive captured near Duc Hoa in August told district committees not to obstruct the titling, to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper", to keep collecting the Front's agricultural tax, and to make examples only where a hamlet's people began to inform². In Can Giuoc the titling was suspended until the following spring. It was the first evidence that the reform's value in any district was exactly the value of the security behind it.

#### The battle of Nam Dong
*6 Jul 1964 · Nam Dong CIDG camp, about 50 km west of Da Nang near the Laotian border · historical · EV000-2*
At 02:30 on 6 July 1964 a force that the Americans estimated at about two battalions of Viet Cong and PAVN, some 800–900 men, attacked the CIDG camp at Nam Dong in the mountains west of Da Nang, near the Laotian border. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-726 under Captain Roger H. C. Donlon, 12 Americans in all, with one Australian warrant officer of the Australian Army Training Team Vietnam, 311 Montagnard strikers, 50 Nung mercenaries and seven Vietnamese Special Forces. The attack opened with mortar fire on the command post and a sapper assault on the main gate; the outer perimeter was penetrated and many strikers deserted or went over to the attackers⁷.

The inner perimeter, held by the Americans and the Nungs, survived about five hours of close fighting until the attackers withdrew at dawn. Two Americans and the Australian adviser were killed; 58 strikers and Nungs died and 57 were wounded, and more than 100 strikers defected. Fifty-five to sixty-two attackers' bodies were counted around the wire, a figure more reliable than most body counts because the dead lay inside a perimeter the defenders held. Donlon, wounded several times, became the first American awarded the Medal of Honor in Vietnam⁷. Nam Dong is the pattern of the border camp war: an isolated garrison of irregulars whose loyalty is uncertain, a night assault by a force that has chosen its moment, no reinforcement before daylight, and a defence that survives on the quality of a handful of men.

#### The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution
*2–10 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · historical · EV000-3*
On 30–31 July 1964 South Vietnamese commando boats under the covert OPLAN 34A programme, run by MACV's Studies and Observations Group, raided the North Vietnamese islands of Hon Me and Hon Ngu. On 2 August the destroyer USS *Maddox*, on a DESOTO signals intelligence patrol under Captain John J. Herrick, was attacked in daylight by three P-4 torpedo boats of the 135th Torpedo Squadron. The *Maddox* fired, four F-8 Crusaders from USS *Ticonderoga* attacked the retiring boats, and the North Vietnamese lost four killed and six wounded with three boats damaged; the *Maddox* was struck by one machine-gun round and suffered no casualties⁸.

On the night of 4 August the *Maddox* and USS *Turner Joy*, in rough weather, fired for about two hours at radar contacts they took to be a second attack. Herrick cabled that the "entire action leaves many doubts". No wreckage or bodies were found, and the National Security Agency's own history, written in 2001 and declassified in 2005, concluded that no attack took place and that signals intelligence had been presented selectively. Johnson decided on retaliation within thirty minutes⁸. On 5 August, in Operation Pierce Arrow, 64 sorties from USS *Ticonderoga* and USS *Constellation* struck torpedo-boat bases and the oil storage at Vinh. Two aircraft were lost; Lieutenant (jg) Richard Sather was killed and Lieutenant (jg) Everett Alvarez became the first American naval aviator taken prisoner by North Vietnam.

On 7 August Congress passed the Southeast Asia Resolution, 416–0 in the House and 88–2 in the Senate, authorizing the President to take "all necessary steps, including the use of armed force"⁸. Johnson signed it on 10 August. It was the only legislative authority for the war that followed. B-57B bombers were moved to Bien Hoa in the aftermath⁹, within mortar range of the countryside around it.

#### ◆ The Vung Tau Charter crisis and the September coup attempt
*16 Aug – 14 Sep 1964 · Saigon, Hue and Da Nang · changed from history · EV000-4*
Khanh tried to use the Tonkin crisis to make himself president under a new constitution, the Vung Tau Charter of 16 August 1964. Students and the Buddhist movement led by Thich Tri Quang answered with demonstrations and riots in Saigon, Hue and Da Nang, and Khanh conceded the repeal of the charter. At a Military Revolutionary Council meeting on 26–27 August he promised to resign, but the generals could agree on no successor, and a triumvirate of Khanh, Minh and Khiem was formed to rule for two months; Khanh dominated it. Khiem was sent abroad as ambassador to Washington and Minh on a "goodwill tour"¹⁰.

Before dawn on 13 September Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc, with about ten battalions recruited among them and four moving up from the Mekong Delta, seized key points in Saigon and announced a new government on the radio while Khanh took refuge in Da Lat. The coup collapsed within a day. US officials publicly backed Khanh; Air Commodore Nguyen Cao Ky, commanding the Vietnamese Air Force, flew jets low over the city and brought loyal marines and paratroopers into Tan Son Nhut, and General Nguyen Chanh Thi supported him; Phat and Duc backed down on the morning of 14 September. No one was killed¹⁰. In this war the coup-makers had a programme as well as grievances. General Duc, the IV Corps commander, had learned in August that the Commission had opened a case on the ghost payrolls the musters had found in his corps, and Phat's broadcast of 13 September promised to "suspend the land decree pending review" and to dissolve the "foreign-controlled" Aid Compliance Board. It was the first time a coup had announced a policy toward the countryside, and the policy was the landlords'; the same broadcast promised to "restore the authority of the corps commanders over the provinces", which meant the abolition of the Selection Board³. Its failure within a day attached the reforms to the side that won: the Young Turks who had saved Khanh took up the land decree as their own cause, and Ky spoke of it in his first public statements as an argument for their claim to govern².

The lasting result was a new power centre: the younger officers, the "Young Turks" around Ky and Thi, who had saved Khanh and now knew they could unmake him¹⁰. The war in the countryside was left to commanders whose first concern was the next coup.

The police played a part in this coup that it had never played before. The Special Branch, which watched the generals under its statute as well as the Front, reported to Cuong on 11 September that battalions of the 7th Division were being readied at My Tho without orders, and Cuong passed the report to Khanh and to the US Mission the same night. The warning did not stop the coup, which came on schedule, but on 13 September the Saigon police stayed in their stations and the Field Force companies in their barracks, on Cuong's order, rather than joining either side, and the Mission's knowledge of the movement two days in advance let Ambassador Taylor tell the coup-makers within hours that the United States would not recognise them³. A police that was not one faction's instrument had raised the cost of a coup by a little, and the plotters' programme of abolishing the Board had made the Young Turks who defeated them its defenders, as it had made them the land decree's.

#### ◆ The Hop Tac programme
*Sep 1964 – mid-1965 · Saigon and the surrounding provinces · changed from history · EV000-5*
In September 1964, at the urging of the US Mission and Westmoreland, the government launched Hop Tac ("cooperation"), a plan to pacify a ring of territory around Saigon outward in phases, the "oil spot" concept applied to the capital region. The priority was explicit: concentrate resources on the Saigon area and accept little progress elsewhere for the time being. By then the trend elsewhere was plain. Ambassador Taylor's mission reported on 6 October that the provinces rated unsatisfactory for pacification had risen from 7 in July to 14 in August, and named the diversion of resources to Hop Tac as one possible cause. The mission nonetheless recommended unanimously that Hop Tac be given full support¹¹.

The programme never reached its schedule. Its ARVN units were repeatedly pulled away by coups and coup alerts, its Vietnamese chain of command changed with each government, and the Viet Cong infrastructure in Gia Dinh, Long An and the provinces north of the capital was left largely intact. Hop Tac carried the titling teams into the inner ring of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and by mid-1965 about 38,000 hectares there had been titled; in the hamlets of the inner ring the Front's tax collectors found fewer families willing to pay without coercion, and the province advisers' reports of volunteered information rose from a trickle to a few dozen reports a month². The provincial reform reached the ring at the same time: Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An received province chiefs from the list between May and July 1964, and about 160 police posts were settled in the inner districts by mid-1965, with a Special Branch section in each district town. In the inner ring of Gia Dinh the Special Branch broke two of the Front's village committees and the courier line that ran from the Hoc Mon rice fields into the city's northern wards, and the hamlets around the posts were held at night through the coups of 1964–65 even when the ARVN battalions assigned to Hop Tac were pulled away³. That did not save the programme. By mid-1965 Hop Tac was effectively moribund. Its failure foreshadowed the problem of the whole war: pacification required a stable government and a security force that would stay in the hamlets at night, and in 1964 the Republic of Vietnam had neither.

#### Hanoi commits the regulars; the Trail and the first air war in Laos
*Sep 1964 – Apr 1965 · North Vietnam, the Laotian panhandle and the Central Highlands · changed from history · EV000-6*
The Ninth Plenum of the Lao Dong Party Central Committee, in December 1963, had resolved to seek a decisive military victory in the South before the United States could intervene in strength. Through 1964 the Politburo under Le Duan acted on it. Infiltration down the Trail, managed since 1959 by the PAVN's Group 559, shifted from regrouped southerners to northern-born conscripts, and in the autumn of 1964 the first complete PAVN regular regiments were ordered south. The 95th Regiment of the 325th Division arrived in Kontum Province in December 1964; the 101st Regiment followed in February 1965 with the division commander, Colonel Nguyen Huu An; the 18th Regiment came a few months later. The division was then broken up among the B3 (Central Highlands) Front and Military Region 5¹². These were the first regular North Vietnamese formations committed to the war, and by the US estimate of 1965 about 4,500 PAVN troops and 300 tons of materiel moved through Laos each month¹³. Both figures are intelligence estimates of their time; the movement was disciplined, at night, under canopy and through a country that US ground forces could not enter.

The decision to commit the regulars had been taken before the land decree existed, and it was not caused by it. The decree gave it a second argument. Party reporting from the Delta through 1964 described the "land trick" as the most dangerous of the enemy's measures, because it threatened the movement's base in exactly the provinces where it was strongest, and Le Duan's line that victory must come before the Americans and before the political base eroded drew strength from it. The schedule of the 325th Division's regiments was set by their training and by the Trail, not by the reform, and the regiments came when they would have come².

The American answer in Laos began on 14 December 1964 with Operation Barrel Roll, a small programme of armed reconnaissance by US aircraft over northern and eastern Laos. On 3 April 1965 the 2nd Air Division opened Operation Steel Tiger against the Trail in the southeastern panhandle¹³. Both were bound by the fiction of Laotian neutrality under the Geneva Accords of 1962, by the ambassador in Vientiane's control of targets, and by the bar on ground operations across the border. The Trail at this stage was still largely foot and bicycle traffic with truck routes being built; its capacity was growing faster than anything flying over it could reduce.

#### The attack on Bien Hoa air base
*1 Nov 1964 · Bien Hoa air base, Bien Hoa Province · historical · EV000-7*
On the night of 31 October–1 November 1964, two days before the American presidential election, a Viet Cong mortar team fired on Bien Hoa air base, about 30 km northeast of Saigon, for about thirty minutes. The B-57B Canberras moved there after the Tonkin crisis were parked wingtip to wingtip on the ramp. Five B-57Bs were destroyed and 13 damaged; with helicopters and other aircraft, as many as 30 aircraft were destroyed or damaged⁹. Four Americans were killed and 72 people wounded¹⁴. The attackers escaped.

It was the first of at least 475 attacks on the primary air bases in South Vietnam during the war¹⁴. Ambassador Taylor and the Joint Chiefs pressed for reprisal bombing of the North; Johnson, on the eve of the election, declined. The attack exposed what American airpower in South Vietnam would cost: every base needed a ground perimeter that the ARVN could not or would not hold, and the question of who would guard the bases was the question that would bring the first US combat troops ashore four months later.

#### The Brink Hotel bombing and the purge of the High National Council
*19–24 Dec 1964 · Saigon · historical · EV000-8*
In the week before Christmas the Young Turks, with Khanh's acquiescence, dissolved the High National Council, the civilian body set up to give the government a constitutional face, and arrested several of its members. Ambassador Taylor summoned the officers and dressed them down; Khanh answered by attacking Taylor publicly, and relations between the US Mission and the junta reached their low point¹⁰. The civilian prime minister, Tran Van Huong, who had held office since early November, was left without authority.

At 17:45 on 24 December a charge of about 90 kg of explosive in a car parked beneath the Brink Bachelor Officers' Quarters in central Saigon exploded during the evening gathering in the officers' bar. Two Americans were killed, Lieutenant Colonel James R. Hagen and a Navy Department civilian, David M. Agnew; the wounded are given variously as 38 American officers and 25 Vietnamese civilians, 58 people, or 98, and are best stated as about 60–100. Two Viet Cong operatives, disguised as a driver and an ARVN major, placed the car and escaped. Taylor and Westmoreland urged reprisal strikes; Johnson again refused, doubting that the public would accept Viet Cong responsibility and saying that he had "never felt that this war will be won from the air"¹⁵. Within six weeks he had changed his mind.

#### The battle of Binh Gia
*28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · historical · EV000-9*
Binh Gia, a Catholic village of northern refugees in Phuoc Tuy Province east of Saigon, was the site of the Viet Cong's first large set-piece battle. The attackers were the 271st and 272nd Regiments (then called the 1st and 2nd), the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, with the 80th Artillery Detachment and local battalions of the B-2 Front, an estimated 1,800 men. They took the village on 28 December and then waited for the relief. The ARVN committed the 30th, 33rd, 35th and 38th Ranger Battalions and the 4th Marine Battalion of the general reserve, with two artillery platoons and a section of M24 tanks, about 4,300 men, lifted and supported by the US 118th Aviation Company and the gunships of the 68th Assault Helicopter Company¹⁶.

The relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal, the artillery badly positioned and air support thin. On 30 December a 68th Company gunship was shot down near Quang Giao and its four crewmen killed; on 31 December the 4th Marine Battalion, going to recover the bodies, was ambushed in a rubber plantation and destroyed, losing 122 killed including its commander, Major Nguyen Van Nho, and 71 wounded. MACV counted allied losses from 28 December to 6 January at 201 killed, 192 wounded and 68 missing, including five Americans dead and three missing or captured, among them Captain Donald Cook, the Marine adviser, who died in captivity. MACV reported four helicopters shot down. The VC claimed 1,783 killed and captured and 35 helicopters destroyed, which is not credible; their own losses are given only as at least 32 confirmed dead, which is a floor and not an estimate¹⁶.

The significance was not the arithmetic but the form. A Viet Cong formation had held ground for four days against armour, artillery and helicopters, defeated the general reserve, and withdrawn intact. The 271st took the honorific "Binh Gia Regiment"¹⁶. US observers concluded that the ARVN could not defeat main-force units of this kind without American firepower and, increasingly, without American troops.

#### The fall of Khanh
*27 Jan – 25 Feb 1965 · Saigon · historical · EV000-10*
On 27 January 1965 Khanh and the Armed Forces Council removed Prime Minister Huong after another wave of Buddhist protest. On 16 February, to stop Khanh installing a prime minister of his own choosing, the council appointed Dr Phan Huy Quat, a civilian physician and former foreign minister. On 19–20 February Colonel Pham Ngoc Thao and General Lam Van Phat attempted another coup; it failed, but in its confusion the Armed Forces Council, with American encouragement, voted Khanh out¹⁷¹⁰. He left the country on 25 February with the title of roving ambassador. Thao, who went into hiding, was a communist agent, as later became clear.

Quat's civilian cabinet governed under the oversight of a military council in which Ky and Thieu were now the strongest figures¹⁷. The government that would receive the American ground commitment was a caretaker administration whose authority depended on generals who had just shown that they would remove anyone, and whose own legitimacy with the population of the countryside was close to nil.

#### Pleiku, Qui Nhon and Flaming Dart
*7–11 Feb 1965 · Camp Holloway and Pleiku, Pleiku Province; Qui Nhon, Binh Dinh Province; southern North Vietnam · historical · EV000-11*
At 01:50 on 7 February 1965, while the President's national security adviser McGeorge Bundy was in Saigon and Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin was in Hanoi, about 300 men of the 30th Company of the VC 409th Sapper Battalion, reinforced by local engineers and a local force company and issued four 81mm mortars, attacked the US Army helicopter base at Camp Holloway and the advisers' compound at Pleiku. Seven Americans were killed and 104 wounded; 10 aircraft were destroyed and 15 damaged. Bundy recommended reprisal from Saigon, and about twelve hours after the attack 49 aircraft from USS *Coral Sea* and USS *Hancock* struck the barracks at Dong Hoi just north of the 17th parallel, the first strike of Operation Flaming Dart¹⁸.

On 10 February the VC blew up the enlisted men's billet at Qui Nhon, killing 23 Americans. On 11 February about 160 US and Vietnamese aircraft struck the barracks at Chanh Hoa and Chap Le in the southern North¹⁸. Flaming Dart was still framed as reprisal for specific attacks, but Bundy's report of 7 February argued for "sustained reprisal" against the North as the best available way to affect the war, and the administration accepted it. Pleiku is the hinge of the phase: within four weeks the reprisal policy had become a continuing bombing campaign.

#### Rolling Thunder begins
*2 Mar 1965 · North Vietnam, south of the 20th parallel · historical · EV000-12*
On 2 March 1965 Operation Rolling Thunder opened with a strike by 104 USAF aircraft, F-105s, F-100s and B-57s, on the ammunition storage area near Xom Bang, while 19 RVNAF A-1 Skyraiders struck the naval base at Quang Khe¹⁹. It was conceived as a graduated campaign: a slowly rising pressure on the North, moving northward a step at a time, meant to signal resolve, raise the cost of supporting the insurgency and bring Hanoi to negotiate, while avoiding Chinese or Soviet intervention.

The constraints were part of the design. Targets were chosen in Washington, approved individually by the President and the Secretary of Defense at the weekly meetings of the "Tuesday" group, which also fixed timing, numbers of aircraft and ordnance. Strikes were barred within 30 nautical miles of Hanoi and 10 of Haiphong and in a 30-mile buffer along the Chinese frontier. The North was divided into route packages assigned to the Air Force and the Navy. North Vietnam began the campaign with about 1,500 anti-aircraft guns and a few dozen MiG-17s; the United States estimated more than 5,000 guns within a year. Through 24 December 1965 the USAF flew 25,971 sorties and dropped 32,063 tons and the Navy flew 28,168 sorties and dropped 11,144 tons, at a cost of 180 US aircraft¹⁹. The campaign that began on 2 March would last, with pauses, until November 1968.

#### The Marines land at Da Nang
*8 Mar – 6 May 1965 · Da Nang, Quang Nam Province · historical · EV000-13*
On 8 March 1965 Battalion Landing Team 3/9 under Lieutenant Colonel Charles E. McPartlin came ashore over Red Beach 2 northwest of Da Nang, an hour late because of surf, and the first C-130s carrying BLT 1/3 under Lieutenant Colonel Herbert J. Bain landed from Okinawa at about 13:00; the airlift halted and resumed, and 1/3 was complete by 12 March. The 9th Marine Expeditionary Brigade under Brigadier General Frederick J. Karch numbered nearly 5,000. Its mission was the security of Da Nang air base, which Rolling Thunder now needed and the Bien Hoa attack had shown to be vulnerable²⁰. The Joint Chiefs' orders were that the Marines "will not, repeat will not, engage in day-to-day actions against the Viet Cong"²¹.

The restriction lasted three weeks. At a National Security Council meeting on 1 April the President approved the use of Marines "in active combat under conditions to be established and approved by the Secretary of Defense" and an increase of 18,000–20,000 in US support forces. In April further battalion landing teams arrived; on 6 May the brigade became the III Marine Amphibious Force²⁰. The enclave concept, US forces holding coastal bases and patrolling outward, was the first form of American ground strategy and lasted only until the summer. By the end of 1965 III MAF would number more than 38,000²¹.

#### The bombing of the US embassy
*30 Mar 1965 · Saigon · historical · EV000-14*
On 30 March 1965 a car packed with about 300 pounds of plastic explosive was parked outside the US embassy; a Vietnamese policeman who argued with the driver was shot by a second VC who drove up alongside, before the charge exploded. Twenty-two people were killed, two Americans, among them Barbara Robbins of the CIA, 19 Vietnamese and a Filipino serving in the US Navy, and 183 were wounded. Johnson rejected calls for a specific reprisal; Rolling Thunder was already running. Congress voted one million dollars for a new, fortified embassy, which opened on 29 September 1967²². The bombing confirmed that the VC could strike anywhere in Saigon and that its urban networks were intact after a decade of police effort.

#### The Thanh Hoa Bridge and the northern air defences
*3 Apr – 24 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong), Thanh Hoa Province, and the Hanoi region · historical · EV000-15*
On 3 April 1965 a 79-aircraft package led by Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner, 46 F-105s with F-100 escorts, RF-101Cs and KC-135 tankers, attacked the Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa; 16 F-105s carried AGM-12 Bullpup missiles and the rest 750 lb bombs. The bridge was hit repeatedly and stood; traffic stopped for a few hours; an F-100 and an RF-101 were lost. On 4 April 48 F-105s returned with 750 lb bombs. Eight MiG-17s of the VPAF 921st Fighter Regiment, half acting as decoys, attacked the F-105s and shot down two, killing Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson; a third F-105, Captain Carlyle Harris's, was lost and its pilot captured. The VPAF lost three MiG-17 pilots that day; how each was lost is disputed. The bridge, struck by more than 300 bombs, still stood; it would stand until laser-guided bombs dropped its span in 1972²³.

On 24 July an SA-2 Guideline missile shot down an F-4C near the munitions depot at Dien Bien Phu, the first US aircraft lost to a surface-to-air missile¹⁹. Within five months the North's air defence had become an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles, and the restricted zone around Hanoi had become its sanctuary.

#### The Vinh Long suspension
*6 Apr – 11 May 1965 · Vinh Long Province · new · EV001-5*
At the December 1964 harvest landlords' agents collected rent in four villages of Vinh Long's Tam Binh and Vung Liem districts under the escort of Regional Forces platoons the province chief had lent them, and tenants who refused were taken to the district headquarters. The Commission documented 26 cases from the tenants' complaints to the land court on circuit and from the American province adviser's reports, and on 2 February 1965 sent them to the Ministry of the Interior with 30 days to act. Nothing was done; Saigon was occupied with the fall of Khanh. On 6 April the Aid Compliance Board suspended the release of counterpart piasters to Vinh Long Province. On 28 April the Quat government replaced the province chief, and on 11 May, after the Commission certified that the escorts had ended and the collected rents had been returned, the Board restored the funds².

It was the only suspension the Board made against a province in the war's first two years, and it worked as designed: the next province chief stopped the escorts, and in the rest of the Delta the threat was enough. It also cost what this headquarters had warned it would cost. Liberation Radio broadcast for a month that the "American masters" had dismissed a "puppet" who displeased them, and in Saigon the officers who resented the Board pointed to the same fact².

#### The battle of Song Be
*10–12 May 1965 · Song Be (Phuoc Long province capital) and Phuoc Binh, Phuoc Long Province · historical · EV000-16*
The 173rd Airborne Brigade, the first US Army combat formation in the country, began arriving at Bien Hoa and Vung Tau on 5 May 1965 to guard the air bases. Five days later the VC opened their summer offensive. In the early hours of 11 May B-2 Front forces, identified variously as the 763rd Regiment with the 840th Battalion or as elements of the 271st–273rd Regiments, with two sapper platoons and artillery, attacked Song Be, capital of Phuoc Long Province, 119 km northeast of Saigon, and the district town of Phuoc Binh. The defence rested on the 36th Ranger Battalion, about 250 men, about 150 territorials, and about three dozen American advisers, mostly of Special Forces Detachment B-34 under Lieutenant Colonel Alton E. Park, who was badly wounded; Major Mitchell A. Sakely took over. Gunships of the US 197th Aviation Company supported them, and a relieving Ranger battalion reached the town by midday on 11 May²⁴.

The town was held. The ARVN lost 58 killed, 96 wounded and 83 missing; five Americans were killed and 13 wounded; a B-57 was shot down; civilian dead are given as 12–80. The advisers counted 297 VC dead, and MACV estimated up to 1,000 VC killed and wounded, which is an estimate of the kind A13 warns against. The communist history claims 1,389 allied killed, including 30 Americans, which is fiction²⁴. Song Be was a battle the government won, narrowly and with American firepower; the next two were not.

#### The battle of Ba Gia
*28–31 May 1965 · Ba Gia, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · historical · EV000-17*
On 29 May 1965 the VC 1st Regiment of the forming 2nd Division (40th, 60th and 90th Battalions, with the 45th Independent Battalion and local force battalions), directed by PAVN Major General Chu Huy Man, overran the outpost at Loc Tho near Ba Gia, about 10 km from Quang Ngai, and ambushed and destroyed the ARVN 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry Regiment as it came to help. Major General Nguyen Chanh Thi, commanding I Corps, sent a task force under the 51st Regiment: its 2nd Battalion, the 3rd Marine Battalion, the 37th and 39th Ranger Battalions, two 105mm artillery battalions and an M113 squadron. On 30 May the 39th Rangers fought the VC 45th Battalion on Mount Chop Non, and the 2nd Battalion, 51st and the Marines were hit near Phuoc Loc; US fighter-bombers and UH-1 gunships, delayed because forward air controllers could not find the fight, prevented a rout. On the night of 30–31 May the 3rd Marines held a moat line through repeated assaults; the 39th Rangers were reduced to a single company²⁵. The VC withdrew at dawn on 31 May.

The US Army's history gives ARVN losses of 107 killed, 123 wounded and 367 captured or missing, with 384 individual and 10 crew-served weapons lost; 84 VC bodies were found and MACV estimated up to 826 VC casualties. The Vietnamese official account claims 915 ARVN killed or wounded and 270 captured. Ba Gia was the first time the VC destroyed an ARVN regimental task force, and by 3 June they controlled much of northern Quang Ngai. On 5 July they overran Ba Gia again, and the ARVN abandoned the post²⁵. In Washington it was read as proof that the ARVN could no longer cope alone.

#### The battle of Dong Xoai
*9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · historical · EV000-18*
Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers. The camp was largely overrun during the night, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overwhelmed; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it and only about 150 came back. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had²⁶.

The allied figures are 416 South Vietnamese killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing; 18 Americans killed; about 300 civilians killed and 2,500 made refugees. More than 300 VC bodies and 104 weapons were found and the allies estimated 625 more VC dead; the Vietnamese official account gives 134 killed and 290 wounded. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had one reserve battalion left²⁶.

#### Quat resigns; the Thieu–Ky government
*12–19 Jun 1965 · Saigon · historical · EV000-19*
On 12 June 1965 Prime Minister Quat, deadlocked with the chief of state Phan Khac Suu over a cabinet reshuffle, handed power back to the military. On 14 June the generals formed a National Leadership Committee with Major General Nguyen Van Thieu as its chairman and chief of state, and on 19 June Air Vice Marshal Nguyen Cao Ky became prime minister as chairman of the Central Executive Committee¹⁷. American officials expected it to last no longer than its predecessors. It lasted the war, in the sense that Thieu would govern until April 1975; but it began as a junta of officers in their thirties and forties with no political base outside the armed forces.

#### The first Arc Light strike
*18 Jun 1965 · Near Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · historical · EV000-20*
On 18 June 1965 Strategic Air Command flew its first B-52 mission of the war. Thirty B-52s of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings were launched from Andersen Air Force Base, Guam. Over the sea, while circling to meet their KC-135 tankers, two B-52Fs collided and fell into the sea, killing eight crewmen. Twenty-seven aircraft struck a suspected Viet Cong base area near Ben Cat, about 40 miles north of Saigon, with 750 lb and 1,000 lb bombs²⁷²⁸. Ground follow-up found little: the enemy had left, or had not been there in strength. The mission was widely ridiculed as a sledgehammer swung at flies. Within five months B-52s would be striking within a few kilometres of US troops in the Ia Drang; Arc Light would continue until August 1973 and fly about 126,615 sorties²⁹. What its first strike showed is the find-and-fix problem in its purest form: unlimited striking power, aimed at a target located by intelligence that was hours or days old.

#### The July decision
*28 Jul 1965 · Washington · historical · EV000-21*
On 7 June 1965 Westmoreland asked for a major reinforcement to stop what he judged a coming collapse; by 22 July the President had authorized US combat strength of 44 battalions²⁵. On 28 July, at a midday press conference rather than in an address to Congress, Johnson announced that US forces in Vietnam would rise immediately from 75,000 to 125,000, with more to follow as requested, that monthly draft calls would rise over time from 17,000 to 35,000, and that the Airmobile Division would deploy³⁰. The reserves were not called up and no new taxes were sought. The decision was presented as a continuation of policy and made, in effect, without a public debate. Its strategy, Westmoreland's three-phase concept of halting the losing trend in 1965, taking the offensive in high-priority areas in 1966 and destroying the enemy's main forces and base areas thereafter, committed the United States to a war of attrition against an enemy who controlled his own losses by deciding when to fight.

#### How the phase ends
By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has destroyed ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security forces it needs. One thing is different from the historical summer of 1965: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's². Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

The police and the chiefs have begun to change the same districts. About 1,100 village posts are settled, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure in the villages around the posts has begun to leave them³. The posts that fell were the posts settled ahead of security, and the Front has learned to kill constables before it has learned that the new chiefs are staying.

### Mechanism

The police and the provincial reform change Phase I only in the districts the government already holds, and only from the spring of 1964. The bottleneck was always going to be the first coup: a decree thirty days old, with no list yet published, met a coup-maker whose first instinct was to put his men in the provinces. The decree survived because the Aid Compliance Board's power over counterpart funds gave it the one thing a Board without troops needed, a consequence for ignoring it. The interaction with the first memorandum is what makes these provisions work at all: the land programme's titling teams chose the villages, the posts settled behind them, and the Commission's lever protected the list. Where all three met, as in the inner ring of Gia Dinh under Hop Tac, the hamlets held through the coups of 1964–65 even when the ARVN battalions assigned to the ring were pulled to Saigon.

The cost is shown in the casualty returns of the posts, and it falls exactly where the negative specification said it would. A post settled without its Popular Forces platoon, like Long Dinh on Route 4 on 3 October 1964, is a gift of nine carbines to the Front's guerrillas. A post settled with its platoon and a reaction within the hour usually holds. The programme did not make the Popular Forces stay in the hamlets; it made the price of their absence visible within weeks, and the province chiefs from the list paid attention to that price, which the patronage chiefs had not.

The strongest effect in this phase is invisible in its own time. By the end of 1964 the Board's register had become the first honest record of what the Republic's officers could do, and by July 1965 about two-thirds of the chiefs had come through it. No battle in Phase I turned on it. Every district in Phase II did.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy answered the police quickly and correctly. The COSVN directive of March 1964 named the constables a priority target above the Popular Forces, and its three instructions, kill them early, penetrate the Special Branch, confiscate the cards, were the right counters. The Dinh Tuong penetration, found in February 1965 after eleven informants were dead, showed the second working. Card confiscation worked in the villages the Front held at night, where a family without a card could not travel to the market town without being stopped, and so tied the population to the Front a little more tightly; in the held villages it was not possible.

The enemy answered the provincial reform slowly and wrongly for a year, because it read the list as another reshuffle. That error was reasonable. It cost the Front a year in which the new chiefs learned their districts unmolested.

The great decision of the phase was Hanoi's own and owed nothing to the police: the Ninth Plenum's line that victory must come before the Americans could intervene in strength, and the commitment of the 325th Division's regiments down the Trail. The provisions did not hasten it. The Front's district committees noticed the posts. The Politburo did not need to.

### Causal chains

No chain terminates in Phase I. C002-1 begins here, with the first list of 27 April 1964 that gave Long An a province chief from the examination; it lands in Phase II.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a police of about 45,000 growing to 70,000, about two-thirds of its chiefs from the list, and an enemy who has learned to kill constables and has not yet learned to kill chiefs. It also inherits the precedent of the first test, which is double: the Board survives a coup if the American lever is pulled at once, and the police chief can be detained by the coup's first patrol. The first half of that precedent holds until 1970. The second comes back in March 1966.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · POP.gvn_legitimacy +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · VCI.strength -2 (old band ≈ -7..-5) · FIND.vci +2 (old band ≈ +5..+7).*

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967¹. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 hectares titled to about 640,000 families, most of the Delta's tenanted rice land in hamlets the government holds at night and a great deal of the coastal Centre's. Its first large clearing operation built around titling, GOLDEN SHEAF in the western Delta in the first half of 1966, shows both what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of the territory the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark. The Commission Against Corruption survives the Buddhist crisis and the election under the protection of the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 its Special Court convicts the first officer of general rank. None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live². It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

A fifth war, the police's, runs inside the fourth. With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages, and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it: NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case. The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and by moving into the towns, and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before.

#### ◆ The war on the district chiefs
*Aug 1965 – Jun 1966 · The Delta, the provinces around Saigon and the coastal Centre · new · EV002-3*
For its first year the Front read the Provincial Administration Reform as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, and it took until the summer of 1965, when the list-appointed chiefs had stayed through the fall of Khanh, the Quat government and the arrival of Thieu and Ky, for the provincial committees to report that the new chiefs were not changing. The conclusion COSVN drew in August 1965 was the one that hurt: the chiefs who kept their Popular Forces in the hamlets at night and directed the police were the enemy's most dangerous officials, and the district committees were to kill them. Between August 1965 and June 1966 nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs were killed by mines, ambush and assassination, sixteen of them appointed from the list. On 14 November 1965 Major Tran Van Sang, district chief of Mo Cay in Kien Hoa since June 1964, was killed with his driver and two bodyguards by a command-detonated mine on the road from Mo Cay to Ben Tre; the provincial committee's own report, captured in 1966, called him "the most harmful of the puppet district chiefs in the province" because the hamlets of his district no longer sheltered its cadres at night³.

The killings did not empty the districts, and that was the reform's quiet gain. A dead chief's deputy for administration acted at once, and the Board sent a ranked list for the vacancy within an average of about three weeks; Mo Cay had a new chief, a captain who had served as the district's deputy for security, on 6 December. Under the patronage system a vacant district had waited months for a patron's choice, and the Popular Forces had drifted back to the district town while it waited⁵. Each killing cost the Front a security cell's best men, because the chiefs now travelled with Field Force escorts and varied their roads, and five of the twenty-one attacks were traced and their teams taken by the Special Branch within the year³. The Front went on killing chiefs for the rest of the war. It never again did so at the rate of 1965–66, because the chiefs it could kill easily had been killed and the rest had learned.

#### Operation Starlite
*18–24 Aug 1965 · Van Tuong peninsula, south of Chu Lai, Quang Ngai Province · historical · EV000-22*
On 15 August 1965 a deserter from the VC 1st Regiment surrendered to the ARVN and, under interrogation at General Thi's I Corps headquarters, gave away the regiment's base on the Van Tuong peninsula, about 12 miles south of the new Marine airfield at Chu Lai, and its plan to attack the field. III MAF had corroboration from another source. On 18 August the Marines attacked by land, sea and air: the 3rd Battalion, 3rd Marines came across the beach from the amphibious ships USS *Bayfield*, *Cabildo* and *Vernon County*; the 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines came in by UH-34 helicopters of HMM-261 and HMM-361 to block inland; the 1st and 3rd Battalions, 7th Marines were committed in support and reserve, all under Colonel Oscar F. Peatross of the 7th Marines. The cruiser USS *Galveston* and destroyers *Orleck* and *Prichett*, A-4s and F-4s of Marine Aircraft Groups 11 and 12 and the 3rd Battalion, 12th Marines supported them. The VC 1st Regiment, with its 60th and 80th Battalions, a company of the 45th Weapons Battalion and the 52nd Company, under Le Huu Tru, fought from prepared hamlets and ambushed a supply column³¹.

The Marines lost 45 killed and 203 wounded. They reported 614 VC killed, 9 prisoners, 42 suspects and 109 weapons; the gap between bodies counted and weapons taken is the first clear instance in this war of a count inflated by its counters. VC accounts admit about 50 killed and some 200 killed, wounded or captured, which is too low. Corporal Robert E. O'Malley and Lance Corporal Joe C. Paul received the Medal of Honor³¹. Starlite was the first regimental-sized US battle of the war and a clean tactical victory, won on a piece of human intelligence the war would rarely provide again so cleanly. The 1st Regiment was rebuilt and back in the field within months.

#### Plei Me and the Ia Drang
*19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · historical · EV000-23*
On 19 October 1965 the PAVN B3 Front under Major General Chu Huy Man attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, south of Pleiku, and the next day ambushed the ARVN relief column; the relief reached the camp at dusk on 25 October and the PAVN withdrew west towards the Chu Pong massif on the Cambodian border. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), newly arrived, was turned loose in pursuit. On 14 November the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray at the foot of the Chu Pong, into the assembly area of the 66th Regiment. For three days the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th, with elements of the 33rd, assaulted a perimeter held with the help of the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert Tully, massed tube artillery, tactical air and, from 15 November, B-52s striking about 7 km to the west, the first tactical use of the bombers, in five missions and 96 sorties over the campaign. American losses at X-Ray were 79 killed and 121 wounded; Moore's count of PAVN dead, reduced by him from 834 to 634, and an estimate of 1,215, are the figures of record³².

On 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade, marching overland to Landing Zone Albany, was ambushed in column in tall grass by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd Regiment. The PAVN, following Colonel Nguyen Huu An's order to "grab them by the belt", fought inside the column where American artillery and air could not reach them, for about sixteen hours. The battalion lost 155 killed, 124 wounded and 4 missing, the worst American loss of a single day so far. Over the campaign the United States counted 237 dead and claimed a kill ratio of nearly ten to one; a PAVN report of 1966 gives 559 killed and 669 wounded. Both sides inflated the other's losses³².

Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success, and each drew the lesson that confirmed its strategy: Westmoreland that airmobile forces with heavy fire support could destroy PAVN regulars at a favourable rate; the PAVN that by closing to close quarters and choosing when to fight they could neutralize American firepower and withdraw across the Cambodian border to rebuild³². McNamara, visiting after the battle, advised the President at the end of November that even a doubling of US forces in 1966 might produce only a military stalemate at a higher level.

#### ◆ Operation NIGHT LANTERN
*15 Nov 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · new · EV002-4*
NIGHT LANTERN went back to the districts where the land programme had learned its first lesson. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings, the titles of about 400 families were still held in the district office, because no one could protect the families who kept them. Long An had been given a province chief from the first list in June 1964, Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, an engineer officer who had placed third in the country in the examination, and Can Giuoc a district chief from the second, Major Le Cong Phuong, who had grown up in Can Duoc. In September 1965 they proposed to the American province adviser an operation built not around a regiment but around the settled post, the census and night check and the target folder, in the 14 villages of the two districts between Route 5 and the Vam Co³.

The forces were Long An's own: eight Regional Forces companies, 26 Popular Forces platoons raised in the villages themselves, two Police Field Force companies of about 150 men each with M1 and M2 carbines, M1918A2 automatic rifles and 60mm mortars, 140 rural constables for the 14 posts, the province Special Branch section of about 45, and four census teams. Fire support was two M101 105mm howitzers at Can Giuoc and an AC-47 of the USAF 4th Air Commando Squadron on call at night; lift was the RF's sampans, two LCVPs of the River Assault Group at Nha Be, and UH-1B helicopters when III Corps would release them. The rule was Lan's: no post settled until its PF platoon slept in the same hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour, and no arrest without three sources³.

The posts went in from 15 November, one village at a time, working out from Can Giuoc town along the canals. The turn came in two steps. On the night of 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th Local Force Battalion attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, the village where the killings of June 1964 had begun. The PF platoon and the ten constables held the post and the hamlet behind it for three hours; an RF company came down the canal in sampans and reached the post at 01:40, and the AC-47 kept the attackers off the paddy until it arrived. The 506th left 38 dead and 14 weapons; the defenders lost 9 killed, 3 of them constables. On 4 February a night check in Long Huu found a young man whose card had been made for someone two years older; his booklet did not match the house, and within a week the courier route he served had led the Special Branch to the chief of the Can Giuoc district committee's security section, arrested on 11 February with his files³.

By 30 April all 14 posts were settled and held. The Special Branch had arrested 212 people on three-source evidence, of whom the magistrates released 71, and 64 cadres and guerrillas had rallied to the police posts, which they found easier to approach than an army camp. The district committee moved south to the edge of the Rung Sat and visited its villages instead of living in them, and the Can Giuoc tax ledger captured in June showed collections for the first quarter of 1966 at about a third of the same quarter of 1965. The about 400 titles held since 1964 were handed to their families in April, in their hamlets³. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 RF, PF and police dead. It was the first operation in the war in which the province chief, the police and the land programme acted as one district government, and it did in six months what no battalion sweep of Long An had done in six years.

#### Operation GOLDEN SHEAF
*10 Jan – 30 Jun 1966 · Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien Provinces, along the Xa No canal from Can Tho to Vi Thanh · new · EV001-6*
GOLDEN SHEAF was the first operation of the war planned around the land programme rather than around an enemy unit. Its object was to carry the titling line west from the Bassac along the Xa No canal, the main waterway from Can Tho to Vi Thanh, into about 60 villages of Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien that the government held by day only or not at all, and to leave behind hamlets held at night by their own titled families. IV Corps gave it to the ARVN 21st Division, with its 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments, two Ranger battalions, about 40 Regional Forces companies of the two provinces, 24 titling teams and 6 muster teams; the 21st's advisory team and the American province senior advisers at Can Tho and Vi Thanh planned it with the division staff in November and December 1965. Fire support was the division's M101 105mm howitzers moved by LCM-6 along the canal, VNAF A-1H Skyraiders on call, and AC-47 gunships at night over posts under attack; lift was the US Army helicopter companies in IV Corps, UH-1B and UH-1D, about 30 aircraft on the operation's busiest days². The dry season set the timing: from January to April the paddies were dry or stubble, the canal and the main arroyos were the only water lines, and troops could move off the dikes; the rains from May slowed every movement after that³³.

The method was the land oil-spot at battalion scale. A regiment cleared a block of four to six villages by day in a sweep of about a week, with Rangers on the far side of the block as a stop line; RF companies, one to each two or three hamlets, stayed in the hamlets overnight rather than withdrawing to posts; the titling teams followed about ten days behind, posting parcel numbers rather than names, and the muster teams recruited a Popular Forces platoon in each titled hamlet from the families who had just received their land, issued M1 carbines and AN/PRC-25 radios through the district, and paid them at the parade. A regiment moved to the next block only when the American province adviser and the district chief rated the last one able to hold at night with its RF company and its new platoon².

The enemy declined battle. The Front's Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien provincial battalions and the regional main-force battalion this headquarters' order of battle placed west of the Xa No withdrew south-west towards the U Minh forest as the first blocks were entered, leaving local guerrillas, mines on the dikes and snipers at the canal bends³⁴. The one serious engagement came on the night of 22–23 March, when about two companies attacked an RF company and a new PF platoon in a hamlet at kilometre 28 on the canal; the post held for four hours under an AC-47 and illumination from the howitzers at Phung Hiep, and the attackers withdrew before dawn leaving 31 bodies and 9 weapons; the defenders lost 14 killed. Across the operation the ARVN, RF and PF lost about 214 killed and 640 wounded, more than half to mines and booby traps. The units reported about 690 enemy killed; the muster teams' audit of the reports, the first ever made of a division's body count, accepted 268 by body and weapon and estimated the true figure at 250–350, and recovered 140 weapons in all². Of the reported figure, I am confident only that it is too high.

By 30 June about 46,000 hectares had been titled to about 38,000 families in 112 hamlets, and 74 of those hamlets were rated by the district and the province adviser as held at night by their own forces. The other 38 were held by day, and the titles for them stayed in the district office at Phung Hiep and Long My. The operation stopped at the line the Regional and Popular Forces could hold, about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh, not at the limit of the titling teams, who were idle for most of June: the bottleneck had moved from the offer to its delivery. In the 74 held hamlets the Front's tax collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn the province recorded the first rice sales through the government's market at Phung Hiep from villages that had never sold there². It is the first place in this war where the government's arrival was what F001-17 said it would be: a guarantee rather than a threat, delivered by the government's own troops to people who had something of their own to defend.

#### Operation Masher/White Wing
*24 Jan – 6 Mar 1966 · Bong Son plain and the Kim Son and An Lao valleys, Binh Dinh Province · changed from history · EV000-24*
In the first large-unit operation of 1966 the 1st Cavalry Division, with the ARVN 22nd Division, an ARVN airborne brigade blocking along Highway 1 and the ROK Capital Division, swept the Bong Son plain and the valleys behind it in northern Binh Dinh, the heartland of the PAVN 3rd (Sao Vang) Division: the 22nd and 12th Regiments and the VC 2nd Regiment, about 6,000 men. The Marines ran Operation Double Eagle in Quang Ngai to the north, and the Koreans Operation Flying Tiger to the south. President Johnson had the name changed from Masher to White Wing because the original sounded too brutal³⁵.

The division lost 228 killed and 834 wounded, and 46 more men died in a separate aircraft crash; Double Eagle cost the Marines 24 killed and 156 wounded. The United States claimed 1,342 PAVN and VC killed and the ARVN and Koreans another 808; 254 weapons were recovered. The disproportion between the two figures drew criticism at the time. US figures counted more than 27,000 civilians displaced, and of about 8,000 people in the An Lao valley, 4,500 left; a mid-1966 Army study found US and South Vietnamese bombing and artillery to be the main cause of refugee movement in the province. Civilian deaths are largely unknown; Korean troops of the Capital Division are reported to have killed more than 1,000 civilians at Binh An/Tay Vinh, a figure from Vietnamese sources that has never been tested in any proceeding³⁵.

The 3rd Division slipped away, much of it before the operation reached it, and intelligence reported its return to the area about a week after the cavalry withdrew. The 1st Cavalry came back in Operation Crazy Horse two months later and again in Operation Thayer in October³⁵. Masher/White Wing is the template of the phase: a tactical success measured by body count, a large refugee population, and an enemy whose infrastructure in the hamlets survived intact.

In this war the operation and its displacement run as they did historically; the bombing and artillery that emptied the valleys were not touched by the provisions. What changed is what the refugees left behind. The coastal plain of northern Binh Dinh had been surveyed in May 1964, and by the end of 1965 the Binh Dinh land service had issued provisional titles for about 9,000 hectares on the Bong Son plain, at the Centre's ceiling of 1 hectare a family, including several thousand parcels the Viet Minh had distributed and the decree confirmed⁴. Under an instruction of the Ministry of Land Reform of February 1966, drafted with this headquarters after the first refugee returns reached Qui Nhon, the parcels of families who fled were recorded in the land court's register as held for the absent tiller and could not be reallotted, sold or reclaimed by a former landlord while the family lived. When the camps at Bong Son and Phu My began to empty in 1967, families who returned to the plain found their parcels recorded in their own names. The Front's cadres, who had told the camps that the Americans had driven the peasants off the land so that the landlords could have it back, lost the sentence that had served them best in Binh Dinh since 1955. The An Lao valley itself, a Front base area since the French war and never surveyed, gained nothing: its 4,500 refugees had no titles to keep².

#### The fall of the A Shau camp
*8–10 Mar 1966 · A Shau Special Forces camp, A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · historical · EV000-25*
The A Shau camp lay about 30 miles southwest of Hue and 2 km from the Laotian border, astride a main infiltration route into I Corps. It was garrisoned by ten Green Berets of the 5th Special Forces Group and about 210 CIDG, reinforced on 7 March by seven more Americans, nine interpreters and a MIKE Force company. Intelligence pointed to four battalions of the PAVN 325th Division, identified as the 95B Regiment¹². The assault came on 9 March under low cloud that kept most tactical air away and prevented reinforcement from Hue and Phu Bai. A-1 Skyraiders and AC-47 gunships flew below the overcast to support the defenders³⁶.

By 10 March the camp had fallen. The Special Forces team lost five killed and twelve wounded; of a garrison of 434, 186 were evacuated by helicopter and the rest were listed as missing, a total accounted differently in every source. On 10 March Major Bernard F. Fisher landed his A-1E on the debris-strewn strip under fire to pick up Major D. W. Myers, who had crash-landed there, and received the Medal of Honor. The valley was not retaken. The PAVN made it a base area and a terminus of the Trail for the rest of the war, and US and ARVN forces never re-established a permanent presence there³⁶.

#### ◆ The Buddhist uprising
*10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · changed from history · EV000-26*
At the Honolulu conference in February 1966 Johnson embraced Ky publicly, and Ky came away believing he had American backing to move against his principal rival, Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps. On 10 March the junta dismissed Thi. When Ky let him return to Da Nang and Hue on 16–17 March he was met by crowds, and the "Struggle Movement" of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers, led by Thich Tri Quang, took control of the two cities and called for civilian government. Ky's first attempt to reassert control, landing marines at Da Nang on 3–5 April, was blocked by I Corps troops and withdrawn; on 9 April US Marines interposed on a Da Nang bridge to stop a rebel convoy³⁷.

On 15 May Ky airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang and took the city centre; the I Corps commander, General Ton That Dinh, fled to the US Marine headquarters, and III MAF found itself between two factions of its ally, preventing a bridge and an ammunition dump being blown and readying jets to intercept Vietnamese aircraft if Ky's air force struck the rebels. Thi agreed to go into exile at the end of May; on 26 May a funeral riot burned the US Information Service library. In June the Struggle supporters blocked Hue's streets with family altars until police removed them; Tri Quang was arrested and placed under house arrest in Saigon, and government control of Hue was restored by 19 June. About 150 people were killed and more than 700 wounded on both sides, including 23 Americans wounded³⁷.

The uprising ended the Buddhist movement as an organised political force and confirmed Thieu and Ky in power³⁷. It also suspended operations in I Corps for three months at the height of the American buildup, and was answered with a political concession: elections for a Constituent Assembly, held on 11 September 1966.

In this war the uprising runs on the historical dates and ends as it did, and the police that Ky used against it was the one the decree of 1964 had built. On 12 March 1966, two days after Thi's dismissal, the Directory removed Director General Cuong by a decree "reorganising" the Directorate, which his statute did not allow, and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, already director of the Military Security Service. The US Mission protested and did not pull the lever; the Directory was the government the Americans had just embraced at Honolulu, and Washington would not quarrel with it over a policeman in the month the Struggle Movement began. In May and June Loan took Field Force companies and Saigon police into Da Nang and Hue, and in Hue the Special Branch's provincial section gave him the family census of the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university, compiled in 1965 for another purpose. About 1,300 people were detained in Thua Thien and Quang Nam by the end of July. The magistrates' 72-hour review was the one part of the statute Loan could not suspend without a further decree, and the Mission made that decree the price of the Public Safety programme; by October about 1,000 of the detained had been released by the courts³.

The repression was more efficient than the historical one and it cost more. The first American newspaper stories about "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, three years earlier than the Phoenix stories of the historical course. Several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders in Hue, knowing that the census had found them, went to the Front's Hue city committee in the summer of 1966, as some of their number did historically. They took with them what they knew of how the census was kept and where its copies were held. The rural sections of the Special Branch were not used against the pagodas, because the Mission's line held in the provinces where it had failed in Saigon, and in the villages the police went on as before³.

#### The POL strikes
*29 Jun 1966 · Hanoi and Haiphong petroleum storage areas · historical · EV000-27*
On 28 June 1966, after weeks of postponement for weather and leaks, McNamara asked the President for authority to strike the two principal petroleum, oil and lubricant storage installations in the Hanoi–Haiphong area; Johnson, worried among other things about hitting a Soviet tanker in Haiphong harbour, assented, and the strikes went in on 29 June. McNamara's argument was that the North's ability to support the war in the South depended on its fuel³⁸. The strikes destroyed most of the bulk storage the North then had. They did not affect its war effort: North Vietnam had already begun dispersing fuel into drums and small caches across the countryside, and imports through Haiphong continued. By the autumn the intelligence community concluded that the POL campaign had not measurably reduced the flow of men and supplies to the South. It was the most important target system Rolling Thunder was allowed to attack before 1967, and the clearest demonstration that an agrarian economy supplied by its allies could absorb strategic bombing directed at a modern economy's vulnerabilities.

#### Operation Hastings
*15 Jul – 3 Aug 1966 · Song Ngan valley, the Rockpile and Hill 208, Quang Tri Province, south of the DMZ · historical · EV000-28*
In mid-1966 the PAVN 324B Division crossed the DMZ into Quang Tri Province in strength, treating the demilitarized zone as no barrier at all. The 3rd Marine Division under Major General Wood B. Kyle formed Task Force Delta under Brigadier General Lowell English, with the 2nd Battalion, 1st Marines, 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines, 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines and 3rd Battalion, 4th Marines, the 3rd Battalion, 12th Marines in support, and later the Special Landing Force battalion, 3rd Battalion, 5th Marines, from Operation Deckhouse II. The ARVN 1st Division under Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong ran Lam Son 289 alongside with an airborne task force. About 8,000 Americans and 3,000 ARVN faced 8,000–10,000 PAVN, including the 90th Regiment, about 1,500 men, and the 812th Regiment³⁹.

The opening air assault into the Song Ngan valley on 15 July lost three CH-46s in a collision and another to fire, killing 13 Marines; the valley was named "Helicopter Valley". On 18 July the 3rd Battalion, 4th Marines, pulling out of Landing Zone Crow, lost 14 dead and 49 wounded to a PAVN assault and counted 138 PAVN bodies. On 24 July the 6th Battalion, 812th Regiment hit Company I, 3rd Battalion, 5th Marines on Hill 362, killing 18 and wounding 82. The operation ended with 126 Americans and 21 South Vietnamese killed and about 700 PAVN claimed killed and 17 captured. It pushed the 324B Division back across the DMZ. Its lasting effect was to draw the Marines north: from 1966 to 1968 the 3rd Marine Division was built up along the DMZ, away from the coastal pacification on which III MAF had wanted to concentrate³⁹.

#### The battle of Long Tan
*18 Aug 1966 · Long Tan rubber plantation near Nui Dat, Phuoc Tuy Province · historical · EV000-29*
On the afternoon of 18 August 1966 D Company, 6th Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment, about 108 men under Major Harry Smith, sweeping east of the 1st Australian Task Force base at Nui Dat after a mortar attack the night before, met the VC 275th Regiment of the 5th Division and the provincial D445 Battalion, possibly with a PAVN battalion, in the Long Tan rubber plantation. Monsoon rain cut visibility to about 50 metres. For about three hours the company, scattered in platoons, held against repeated assaults with the support of the 1st Field Regiment, Royal Australian Artillery, including the New Zealand 161st Battery, and the US A Battery, 2nd Battalion, 35th Artillery; two RAAF No. 9 Squadron UH-1Bs dropped ammunition through the rain; at dusk A Company, 6 RAR arrived in the M113s of 3 Troop, 1st APC Squadron under Lieutenant Adrian Roberts and the VC broke contact⁴⁰.

Australian losses were 18 killed and 24 wounded. The Australians counted 245 enemy dead on the field in the following days and claimed 350 wounded and 3 captured; VC and PAVN sources give 47 killed and 100 wounded and about 700 men engaged, against Australian estimates of 1,500–2,500⁴⁰. The truth lies somewhere between the two accounts, closer to the Australian body count than to the Vietnamese admission, since that count was of bodies found. The battle secured Nui Dat and gave the Task Force freedom of action in Phuoc Tuy for the rest of its tour.

#### Operation Attleboro
*14 Sep – 25 Nov 1966 · War Zone C, northwest of Dau Tieng, Tay Ninh and Binh Duong Provinces · historical · EV000-30*
Attleboro began on 14 September 1966 as a brigade shakedown by the newly arrived 196th Light Infantry Brigade under Brigadier General Edward de Saussure, searching for caches northwest of Dau Tieng. In late October and early November it found more than it was looking for: the VC 9th Division, with its 271st, 272nd and 273rd Regiments and the PAVN 101st Regiment on loan from the 7th Division. The brigade, combing the jungle with rifle companies, was caught in a three-day fight that cost it 60 dead and 159 wounded. II Field Force, Vietnam took over and turned it into a corps operation: the 1st Infantry Division under Major General William E. DePuy assumed command, with the 25th Infantry Division under Major General Frederick C. Weyand, a brigade of the 4th Infantry Division and ARVN and Nung units⁴¹.

The operation cost the Americans 155 killed and 494 wounded. The US claimed 1,016 enemy dead; the PAVN admitted about half that, and an independent estimate gives about 500. Some 127 individual and 19 crew-served weapons were captured; 843 tons of rice were uncovered early and more than 1,300 tons in six caches near Ben Cui. Captured documents later showed that DePuy had overestimated the damage: the 101st was badly hurt, the 271st and 272nd took moderate losses and the 273rd was untouched. Attleboro became the model for the multi-division search-and-destroy operations of 1967, and like them it left VC political control of the province intact once the Americans had gone⁴¹.

#### Operation Bolo
*2 Jan 1967 · Over the Hanoi area, North Vietnam · historical · EV000-31*
By late 1966 the MiG-21s of the VPAF were attacking F-105 strike formations from bases the rules of engagement placed off limits. On 2 January 1967 the 8th Tactical Fighter Wing under Colonel Robin Olds flew F-4C Phantoms along the routes and at the altitudes and speeds of an F-105 strike, using F-105 call signs and electronic countermeasures pods. The MiG-21s rose to meet what they took for bomb-laden Thunderchiefs, and seven were shot down in twelve minutes with no US loss. It was the most successful single air battle of Rolling Thunder for the United States, and it bought a few months' relief; the VPAF stood down to retrain and came back with better tactics, and its strength grew from about 100 interceptors in 1967 to 180 MiGs by 1968¹⁹.

#### Operation Cedar Falls
*8–26 Jan 1967 · The Iron Triangle and Ben Suc, Binh Duong Province · historical · EV000-32*
Cedar Falls attacked the Iron Triangle, the VC base area between the Saigon and Thi Tinh rivers about 40 km northwest of Saigon that held the headquarters of VC Military Region 4, the 272nd and 165th Regiments and the Phu Loi Local Force Battalion. On 8 January a battalion of the 1st Infantry Division air-assaulted into the village of Ben Suc; the 1st and 25th Infantry Divisions, the 196th Light Infantry Brigade, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment then sealed and searched the triangle with ARVN units, Rome plows clearing the jungle. The whole population of Ben Suc and the surrounding hamlets, about 6,000 people, two-thirds of them children, was removed to a camp at Phu Loi and the village was destroyed; 106 were detained after screening. Infantry "tunnel rats" went into the tunnel complexes for the first time; 25 tunnels, about 100 bunkers and more than 500 structures were destroyed⁴².

The United States lost 72 killed and 337 wounded and the ARVN 11 killed and 8 wounded. The US claimed about 720–750 VC killed, 280 captured and 540 Chieu Hoi defectors, and captured rice enough to feed 13,000 men for a year. Within two days of the operation's end VC forces were back in the triangle, and within ten days a US report described it as "literally crawling" with them. A year later it was a staging area for the attack on Saigon at Tet⁴². Cedar Falls embodies the phase's dilemma in the countryside: the population could be moved and the base destroyed, but a base area emptied of people was not held by anyone.

#### ◆ The arrest count falls
*Feb – Nov 1967 · Saigon; Phong Dinh, Long An and Vinh Long Provinces; and the towns of the Delta · new · EV002-5*
In the first quarter of 1967 the Special Branch's arrests of infrastructure in the held belt of the Delta fell to about 40 percent of their peak of the second quarter of 1966. The Office of the Secretary of Defense's systems analysts read the decline as a programme losing its grip, and in February Robert Komer, then the President's special assistant for pacification, proposed monthly arrest targets by province as the measure of the attack on the infrastructure. This headquarters answered in writing on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working: in the districts with posts three years old there was almost no one left in the villages to arrest, and the measures the enemy could not fake, captured tax ledgers, the count of functioning village committees in captured documents, and the age of captured guerrillas, all showed the infrastructure shrinking there. The Director General of National Police, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, wanted the targets; Commissioner Khoa opposed them on the record³.

The compromise of May 1967 set targets only for the contested belt beyond the posts, and measured the held belt by the captured documents. That was half a victory, and the half that was lost mattered. Where the targets ran, they produced what quotas always produced: in the second half of 1967 the Commission received about 430 complaints of false arrest from the districts with targets and about 40 from those without. And the infrastructure that had left the held villages had not disappeared. The Special Branch's provincial sections reported through 1967 that legal cadres and their families were settling in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the outskirts of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000³. The Branch built urban sections to follow them, slowly. The war against the infrastructure had moved toward the towns, and so, as the Branch's own reports warned in November, had the Front's means of acting there.

#### Operation Junction City
*22 Feb – 14 May 1967 · War Zone C, Tay Ninh Province, to the Cambodian border · historical · EV000-33*
Junction City, under II Field Force (Lieutenant General Jonathan O. Seaman, then Lieutenant General Bruce Palmer from 18 March), sent the 1st and 25th Infantry Divisions, the 173rd Airborne Brigade, the 196th Light Infantry Brigade, the 3rd Brigade of the 4th Infantry Division and the 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment with ARVN forces into War Zone C to destroy the VC 9th Division and find COSVN, the Central Office for South Vietnam, which American analysts imagined as a fixed headquarters. On 22 February 845 men of the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry and Battery A, 3rd Battalion, 319th Artillery jumped from 13 C-130s near Katum, the only major US combat parachute assault of the war⁴³.

The enemy chose to fight several times, always at night or at dawn, against prepared positions: on 28 February the 101st Regiment hit a company of the 1st Division's 1st Brigade at Prek Klok and lost 167 dead to the Americans' 25; on 10 March the 272nd Regiment attacked Fire Support Base II at Prek Klok and lost 197 to 3; on 19 March the 273rd attacked Fire Support Base 20 at Ap Bau Bang and lost 227 to 3; on 21 March the 272nd and U-80 Artillery assaulted Fire Support Base Gold at Suoi Tre and were broken by artillery firing beehive rounds at point-blank range and the arrival of armour, losing 647 dead to the Americans' 31 killed and 109 wounded; and on 31 March the 273rd and a battalion of the 70th Guard Regiment attacked Landing Zone George at Ap Gu, losing about 600 to 17. The operation used 366,000 artillery rounds and 3,235 tons of bombs⁴³.

The United States lost 282 killed and 1,576 wounded and claimed 2,728 enemy killed. The Vietnamese Ministry of Defence gave 255 killed and 1,275 casualties in all in 2017, which is too low for the battles recorded. COSVN was not found; it moved, and the 9th Division withdrew its main forces into the Cambodian sanctuary, which expanded. Junction City showed that the VC would lose heavily when they attacked American firebases, and that they would decide for themselves whether to do so; after it, the enemy's main forces in III Corps fought from Cambodia, and the base area returned to VC control when the Americans left⁴³.

#### The Special Court convicts the IV Corps quartermaster
*6 Mar – 18 Apr 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province, and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · new · EV001-7*
On 6 March 1967 the Commission Against Corruption arrested Brigadier General Phan Duy Khiem, IV Corps deputy commander for logistics since 1965, with eleven officers of the corps quartermaster and finance staffs at Can Tho. The case was documentary and had been built for fourteen months. The Pay and Muster Verification Service's parade payments had removed ghost pay from the corps' units in 1964–65, and the graft had moved, as this headquarters had expected, to rations and fuel. The Service's auditors matched the corps' monthly ration draws against the strength the musters had established and found the corps drawing rice, canned fish and salt for about 9,000 more men than it had; a parallel audit by the Joint Aid Compliance Board traced about 2.1 million litres of motor fuel issued to RF companies with no vehicles and sold through two Cholon dealers. The total over two years came to about 310 million piasters, about $2.6 million at the official rate².

The arrest was made by the Commission's own judicial police, Vietnamese officers under Commissioner Khoa, with no American present. General Khiem was the first general officer the Commission had charged, three years after it was founded; it had refused the junta's invitations to begin at the top in 1964 and had worked upward from battalion and district. His patron in the Directory tried to have the case moved to a military court in March; the Deputy Chief of Mission, as American co-chair of the Board, told the Directory's chairman, General Thieu, that the US Mission would suspend counterpart funds to IV Corps under the Board's authority if the case was moved, and Thieu, then preparing his presidential candidacy and wanting no quarrel with the Embassy, let it stand. The Special Court tried the case in public in Saigon over nine sitting days and on 18 April 1967 sentenced Khiem to eight years and confiscation of property, and nine of the eleven officers to terms of two to six years².

The conviction mattered more in the officer corps than in the countryside. It established that the Commission's reach now ran to the rank below the Directory and no higher; that it reached that rank only on documentary evidence and with the American lever behind it; and that a general who stole from his troops' rations could go to prison. The American press reported it as a sign that the Saigon government was cleaning itself up, and for a few weeks the corruption stories of the spring were about a conviction rather than a scandal. The officers of the IV Corps staff who replaced the convicted were chosen in part from the Commission's own list of officers with clean records, and there were not enough of them².

#### The Hill Fights at Khe Sanh
*24 Apr – 11 May 1967 · Hills 861, 881 South and 881 North, northwest of Khe Sanh, Quang Tri Province · historical · EV000-34*
On 24 April 1967 a Marine patrol from Khe Sanh ran into PAVN troops on Hill 861, setting off three weeks of fighting for the hills that dominate the Khe Sanh plateau. The PAVN 325C Division had six battalions on the hills, preparing the ground for an attack on the Khe Sanh base. The 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 3rd Marines, with companies of the 9th Marines, took the hills one at a time against bunkers built to survive bombing: 861 on 28 April after the PAVN withdrew, 881 South on 2 May, 881 North on 5 May, with B-52 strikes and heavy artillery fire⁴⁴. The new M16 rifles carried by the Marines jammed repeatedly in the fighting, and Marines were found dead beside rifles they had been trying to clear; the failures led to a congressional inquiry and changes in ammunition and maintenance.

The Marines lost 155 killed and 425 wounded and reported 940 PAVN killed. About 1,500 Marines had fought about 2,000 PAVN⁴⁴. The attack on Khe Sanh was forestalled for the year, and the hills were garrisoned. The fighting confirmed the PAVN's interest in the northwest corner of Quang Tri, and fixed Westmoreland's attention on it for the next nine months.

#### ◆ CORDS is established
*9 May 1967 · Saigon · changed from history · EV000-35*
On 9 May 1967 the President placed the American pacification effort, civil and military, under MACV as Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support, with Robert W. Komer as one of Westmoreland's deputies, holding ambassadorial rank and authority over military personnel and resources, the first civilian to serve in such a role inside a US military command. It followed the failure of the Office of Civil Operations, set up in November 1966 to coordinate the civil agencies under civilian leadership. CORDS was about 85 percent military. Each corps had a CORDS deputy, usually civilian, under the corps senior adviser, and each province an American province senior adviser with civilian and military staffs alongside the Vietnamese province chief, usually an ARVN colonel⁴⁵.

Komer's priorities were the territorial forces and the attack on the VC infrastructure. The Regional and Popular Forces numbered about 300,000 in 1967; their advisory presence was later multiplied through Mobile Advisory Teams⁴⁵. In mid-1967 CORDS created the Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme, ICEX, to target the VCI, which became the Phoenix programme at the end of the year. The Hamlet Evaluation System, introduced in January 1967, began rating every hamlet monthly on a scale reported by the American district advisers; its figures were at once an indispensable record and a measure produced by officers rated on the result. CORDS solved the American management problem of pacification. It did not solve the Vietnamese one, that security in the hamlet at night depended on Vietnamese forces and officials who had to stay there.

In this war CORDS is established on the historical date and in the historical form; the provisions did not create a single manager. What CORDS inherits is different. The American land advisers, about 260 in 1964 and about 340 by 1967, pass into it as a land reform division under the CORDS deputy, and the provincial land services' register of titled parcels, which by May 1967 lists about 740,000 hectares and the households that hold them, becomes the first hamlet-by-hamlet household record the American pacification effort has ever had⁴. Komer adds titled hectares as a factor in the Hamlet Evaluation System's economic rating from its first revision. This headquarters objected in writing on 22 June 1967 that a title measured the government's paperwork and not the peasant's allegiance, that the Front had told its cadres to let peasants take titles and keep paying the Front's tax, and that titles issued would rise fastest where they meant least. The objection was recorded and the factor kept, at half the weight Komer had proposed. ICEX, from July, used the land register to locate the families of known cadres; the Commission, from the same month, began to receive complaints about it².

In this war CORDS inherits a police as well as a land register. The aid mission's Public Safety Division, about 300 advisers in 1966, passes into CORDS as its Public Safety Directorate, and with it a National Police of about 70,000, about 1,500 village posts, and a Special Branch with district sections in about 200 districts and files on the infrastructure kept since 1964⁵. ICEX, from July 1967, is built on those district sections rather than beside them: its district intelligence and operations coordinating centres sit in the Special Branch offices, under the district chief, with the Military Security Service, the sector S2 and the CIA's Provincial Reconnaissance Units feeding them. The PRUs remain an American instrument outside the statute, and their methods outside its safeguards; that is the gap in the design this headquarters had warned of in 1964 and could not close. What the American pacification effort created in 1967 on the historical course, here it found already running, and the province senior advisers found a counterpart chief who had been in his post for two or three years³.

#### Operation Buffalo and the siege of Con Thien
*2 Jul – 31 Oct 1967 · Con Thien and "the Marketplace", Quang Tri Province, south of the DMZ · historical · EV000-36*
Con Thien, a low hill 2 miles south of the DMZ and the western anchor of the planned strongpoint barrier, was the most exposed Marine position in the country, within range of PAVN artillery north of the Ben Hai. On 2 July 1967, at a place the Marines called the Marketplace, PAVN forces ambushed and nearly destroyed Company B, 1st Battalion, 9th Marines; it was the single worst day for the Marines in Vietnam, with 86 killed. The resulting Operation Buffalo, 2–14 July, cost 159 Marines killed and 345 wounded; US sources claimed 1,290 PAVN killed. In August there were 60 Arc Light strikes around the position⁴⁶.

From September the PAVN laid Con Thien under siege by fire. Shelling ran at 200 rounds a day and more, rising to about 1,200 rounds on 25 September; between 19 and 27 September more than 3,000 rounds hit the base. The Marines called it "the Meat Grinder". The answer was firepower: Operation Neutralize brought B-52s, tactical air and naval gunfire onto the PAVN artillery and assembly areas, with 12,577 Marine artillery rounds, 6,148 naval rounds and more than 5,200 Marine and USAF sorties⁴⁶. The siege lifted in October. Con Thien was held, but the Marines along the DMZ had become a static garrison under artillery they could not reach on the ground, because north of the Ben Hai was North Vietnam.

#### Hanoi decides on a general offensive
*6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · changed from history · EV000-37*
In April 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive in the South, and in May sent the plan to Hanoi. Thanh briefed the Politburo in July and died there on 6 July, by the most common account of a heart attack after a party, by another of wounds from a US bombing raid. The plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho; General Vo Nguyen Giap, who had favoured protracted war, did not originate it and modified it reluctantly. Beginning on 27 July, hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals were arrested in the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair", with a second wave in November and December, which removed opposition to the offensive and much of Giap's staff⁴⁷.

The premise was that the ARVN was no longer effective and the Saigon government unpopular, and that a general offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising. In October the Politburo chose the Tet holiday of 1968 as the date; it reaffirmed the decision in December and formalized it as Resolution 14 in January 1968⁴⁷. The border battles of the autumn, at Con Thien, Loc Ninh, Song Be and Dak To, were part of the preparation, drawing US forces away from the cities⁴⁸. This was a decision taken in Hanoi for Hanoi's reasons: the stalemate in the South, the factional struggle in the Party and a reading of the South that proved wrong.

In this war the decision is taken on the same dates by the same men, and for the same reasons; the provisions did not make Le Duan a militant or remove Giap's staff. They added an argument to the militants' case and subtracted one from its premise. COSVN's reports of 1966–67 recorded that the Front's agricultural tax in the Delta was falling, that recruitment in the titled districts along the Bassac was harder each season, and that the "land trick" had not been exposed as the cadres had promised; Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait. At the same time the premise that the Saigon government was unpopular in the countryside was weaker in the Delta than anywhere else, and COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 in the autumn said so; it was overruled². The urgency rose and the forecast of a general uprising was carried forward unchanged, so that in this war the offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than the historical one.

#### The constitution and the presidential election
*3 Sep 1967 · Republic of Vietnam · historical · EV000-38*
The Constituent Assembly elected in September 1966 produced a constitution, promulgated on 1 April 1967. In the presidential election of 3 September 1967, held with the Senate election, Thieu ran for president with Ky as his running mate, after the generals forced Ky to stand aside. Eleven tickets ran. Thieu and Ky won 34.83 percent, 1,649,561 votes; the runner-up, Truong Dinh Dzu, a lawyer campaigning on negotiations with the NLF, won 17.26 percent, 817,120 votes. The reported turnout was 83.17 percent of 5,853,251 registered voters. There were credible reports of soldiers showing voters how to mark their ballots, and of a secret military committee that would govern behind the elected government; on 10 August 57 members of the US House had condemned the conduct of the campaign. Dzu was later imprisoned. Thieu was inaugurated on 31 October and the lower house was elected on 22 October⁴⁹.

The election gave the Republic of Vietnam an elected government for the first time since 1963 and, under Thieu, the stability that the previous four years had lacked. It did not give it legitimacy in the countryside: two-thirds of the voters chose someone else, and a civilian peace candidate had come second.

#### The barrier and Muscle Shoals
*8 Sep – Dec 1967 · The DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand · historical · EV000-39*
In 1966 the Jason Division of the Institute for Defense Analyses, at McNamara's request, studied an anti-infiltration barrier of mines and sensors, and on 15 September 1966 McNamara placed Lieutenant General Alfred D. Starbird at the head of a Defense Communications Planning Group to build it. Johnson authorized construction on 13 January 1967. Its eastern, conventional part, a cleared strip with strongpoints at Gio Linh, Con Thien and the bases west of them, was built under fire by the Marines in 1967; its western, electronic part across the Laotian panhandle was named Muscle Shoals on 8 September 1967⁴⁶. Air-delivered seismic and acoustic sensors, the ADSID and the ACOUSID among them, developed in part from the Navy's sonobuoys, would be dropped by OP-2E Neptunes of the Navy's VO-67 squadron and relayed by EC-121R aircraft to an Infiltration Surveillance Center, Task Force Alpha, at Nakhon Phanom in Thailand, where IBM 360 computers analysed the signals. Field testing began in southeastern Laos on 25 November 1967. Construction cost $1–1.7 billion and operation about $1 billion a year⁵⁰. The system, renamed Igloo White in 1968, would find trucks on the Trail at night as nothing before had; whether it reduced what arrived in the South is a question for later phases.

#### The battle of Dak To
*3–23 Nov 1967 · Dak To and Hills 1338, 724 and 875, Kontum Province · historical · EV000-40*
In late October and early November 1967 the PAVN 1st Division, with its 24th, 32nd, 66th and 174th Regiments and the 40th Artillery Regiment, moved into the hills around Dak To near the tri-border with Laos and Cambodia, and a defector gave away much of its plan. The 4th Infantry Division and the 173rd Airborne Brigade, with the 1st, 2nd and 4th Battalions, 503rd Infantry, the 3rd Battalion, 12th Infantry, the 3rd Battalion, 8th Infantry and two battalions of the 1st Cavalry Division, and the ARVN 42nd Regiment and 2nd, 3rd and 9th Airborne Battalions, fought for the ridges in three weeks of bunker fighting in double- and triple-canopy jungle. The 3rd Battalion, 12th Infantry took Hill 1338 through a bunker complex linked by field telephone; on Hill 724 the PAVN struck the laager of the 3rd Battalion, 8th Infantry, killing 18 and wounding 118. On 15 November mortar fire set off the ammunition dump at Dak To, destroying more than 1,100 tons of ordnance and two C-130s⁴⁸.

From 19 to 23 November the 2nd and 4th Battalions, 503rd Infantry fought for Hill 875; on the first evening a misdirected US bomb fell on the 2nd Battalion's perimeter, killing many of its men. By the time the paratroopers took the crest on 23 November the PAVN had withdrawn. The Americans lost 361 killed, 15 missing and 1,441 wounded, and about 40 helicopters; the ARVN 73 killed. US figures for PAVN dead run from about 1,000 to 1,644, with allegations of inflation; a PAVN report gives about 450 killed and 1,089 wounded. The battle consumed 151,000 artillery rounds, 2,096 tactical air sorties, 257 B-52 strikes and 2,101 Army helicopter sorties. Westmoreland called it a defeat for the enemy. It was also exactly what Hanoi intended: one of the border battles that, with Loc Ninh, Song Be and Con Thien, drew US forces to the frontiers, so that by January 1968 about half the US manoeuvre battalions in South Vietnam were in the border regions⁴⁸.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year¹. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December¹⁹, and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000⁴⁷, and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view"⁴⁷. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside².

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest³. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

### Mechanism

Phase II is where these provisions do their work, and the work is the clearing of the government's own belt. On the historical course the infrastructure in districts the government held by day taxed, recruited and organised under the noses of the district towns until 1968–69. Here it is driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, because the means that historically reached it after Tet, a police in the village, a Special Branch that owns the files and a chief who stays, exist from 1964–65. NIGHT LANTERN is the case in miniature, and the strongest positive effect of the memorandum grounded in a dated event: on 19 January 1966 at Phuoc Lai a Popular Forces platoon and ten constables, with a reaction that came down the canal in the dark, held the village where the Front had shot title holders eighteen months before, and in June the district committee's own ledger showed its tax take cut by two-thirds.

The interaction with the land programme is what turns this from a police success into a political one. A titled family in a village with a post and a chief who keeps the platoons in the hamlets has a reason to keep its title and someone to tell when it is threatened. GOLDEN SHEAF in the western Delta had stopped at the line the Regional and Popular Forces could hold; with posts and the Special Branch behind it the line holds more firmly, though it does not move much further, because the police follows security and does not create it.

The bottleneck moved, as the second part said it would, from identifying the infrastructure to doing something lawful with it: trained Special Branch handlers, magistrates and detention under review. Then it moved again, to the towns. The infrastructure that left the villages went to Can Tho, My Tho and the outskirts of Saigon, and the arrest count in the held belt fell by 60 percent from its 1966 peak. That is an improvement in the war that makes the metric worse, and I said so in writing on 3 April 1967 against the request for quotas. We won half of that argument. The half we lost produced the false arrests that quotas always produce, in the contested belt where the institution was youngest.

The counter-directional finding came true in the Buddhist crisis. The police that Ky used in Hue was the one this decree built, with a census that told it who lived in the pagoda quarter. The repression was more efficient than the historical one, and its costs, the "secret police" stories in the American press and the students who went to the Front, were paid in 1966 and again in Hue in 1968.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's answer to the provincial reform, once it understood it, was the war on the district chiefs of 1965–66. It cost the Republic nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs in eleven months, and cost the Front its best security cells, five of whose teams the Special Branch took within the year. It did not empty the districts, because the list filled each vacancy in about three weeks.

Its answer to the police was to leave the villages, which is the infrastructure's way of declining battle. That was expensive. An infrastructure that visits instead of living among its people taxes less, recruits less and governs less, and its cadres spend their nights moving rather than organising. It was also an investment the Front would draw on at Tet, because the cadres who left went to the towns.

 The Front's largest decision of the phase was not a response to anything this headquarters did. In December 1965 the Twelfth Plenum in Hanoi resolved to meet the American intervention with a main-force war and to seek decisive victories in the South, and through 1966 COSVN under Nguyen Chi Thanh carried that line out in its own way, upgrading guerrilla units to local-force battalions and local-force battalions to main-force regiments, and stripping village cadres and guerrillas to fill them. It was driven by doctrine and by the politics of the Party: Thanh's conviction, against Giap's caution, that the Americans could be beaten in large battles in the South, and Le Duan's need to show Hanoi's allies a war that was being won. In this war the upgrading had a consequence the Plenum did not intend. It took from the villages of the Delta, in 1966, the young guerrillas who were the infrastructure's protection against the posts, in the year the posts were being settled, and the 506th's failure at Phuoc Lai was in part a failure of a battalion that had lost its best companies to a regiment in War Zone C³.

### Causal chains

**C002-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV002-1) The first list gives Long An a province chief and Can Giuoc a district chief chosen by examination, who stay → they plan NIGHT LANTERN around settled posts, the census and the target folder, and the Special Branch breaks the Can Giuoc district committee's security section in February 1966 → the district committee, unable to protect its cadres, calls on the 506th Local Force Battalion to destroy the posts by force, and the battalion fights at Phuoc Lai on ground the province had prepared, against a platoon with a reaction behind it → the one main instrument the Front had in Long An's rice districts was spent defending the infrastructure rather than preparing for the offensive, and the province committee moved it out of Can Giuoc to the edge of the Plain of Reeds, leaving the rice districts with no local-force cover at all.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits a held belt with almost no resident infrastructure, a Special Branch with four years of files and new urban sections that know a part of the cadres who have moved to the towns, a police of about 80,000, and chiefs who have mostly held their districts for two or three years. It also inherits three liabilities: a police directed since 1966 by a general who wanted quotas, a census of Hue that the Front has copied, and Hue students in the Front's city committee who know how the census is kept. The first two are mine to manage. The third I could not.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · POP.gvn_legitimacy +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · VCI.strength -2 (old band ≈ -7..-5) · FIND.vci +2 (old band ≈ +5..+7).*

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war, and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end¹.

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone⁴⁷.

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way; Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington. The difference is in the Delta and in the hamlets. The Front enters the offensive with a smaller base in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre, recruits fewer men there in 1968, and loses the towns of the western Delta faster because the hamlets around them warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers. When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled, and the Commission audits the new Phoenix programme against the land register². The American political calendar is not changed by any of it.

The police changes Tet in the same places and in the same way: in the held belt of the Delta the Front's urban cadres are known and its guides do not arrive, and in Hue its lists are better because the Republic's own census fed them. Phoenix, here, is a new name over a Special Branch that had run target folders since 1964.

#### The siege of Khe Sanh
*21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province · historical · EV000-41*
In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion. The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", answered with Operation Niagara: intelligence collection by sensors and reconnaissance, followed by a campaign in which about 60 B-52s attacked on an average day and more than 100,000 tons of bombs fell by mid-April, with more than 158,000 artillery rounds fired in the first three months⁵¹.

On 7 February twelve PT-76 amphibious tanks of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, overran the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei, the first PAVN tank attack of the war; at least five tanks were knocked out, but of about 500 CIDG some 200 were killed or missing, and of 24 Americans ten were killed and eleven wounded. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, much of it by C-130s using the Low Altitude Parachute Extraction System, and from late February the "Super Gaggle" of A-4s suppressing flak let helicopters supply the hill outposts. The air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters⁵¹.

Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April; it cost 92 Americans and 33 ARVN killed. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July; US intelligence counted 1,602 bodies and estimated 10,000–15,000 dead, while a secret MACV report estimated 5,550 to the end of March. Whether Hanoi ever meant to take the base, or used it to fix American attention on the border while the cities were attacked, is disputed to this day; a captured PAVN study of 1974 said the PAVN would have taken it if it could, at a price it was not willing to pay⁵¹.

#### ◆ The Tet Offensive: the first wave
*30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-42*
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital⁴⁷.

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed⁴⁷.

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces. In IV Corps it goes differently in two of them. At Can Tho, the corps headquarters on the Bassac, the battalions that attacked the town and the Binh Thuy air base moved up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh that GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left in the hands of their own Popular Forces; PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, two of them fought, and the 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters, and Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, with far less of the town destroyed than on the historical course. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days. At Ben Tre, in Kien Hoa, the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta, where the government had titled little outside the town because it held little outside the town, the battle and the destruction were as they were historically. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964. Allied and enemy losses for the country as a whole are not materially changed by it, and I do not adjust them².

The police and the chiefs changed the first wave in the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon, and changed it less than they might have, because the infrastructure they had driven from the villages had gone to the towns. In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced on 31 January to lead the uprising, and in the first two weeks of February arrested about 1,700 of them on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs. Where the Front's guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later: the battalions that attacked Can Tho waited on the night of 30–31 January for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal who never arrived, because both had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier. The ARVN 21st Division, whose desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its strength on the muster roll, and the half of the ARVN that was on Tet leave elsewhere was a smaller half in IV Corps. In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands, where the police counted for little against PAVN regiments and VC main-force battalions, the battle went as it did historically³.

#### The battle for Saigon and the attack on the US embassy
*31 Jan – 7 Mar 1968 · Saigon, Cholon and Tan Son Nhut · historical · EV000-43*
At 02:45 on 31 January a 19-man team of the VC C-10 Sapper Battalion blew a hole in the wall of the new US embassy compound in Saigon and fought inside the grounds for more than six hours without entering the chancery; the compound was secured by 09:20, and five Americans were killed. The sappers were all killed or captured. Other VC units attacked Tan Son Nhut air base with three battalions, the Joint General Staff compound, the Presidential Palace and the naval headquarters, and held the national radio station for about six hours, though they could not broadcast⁴⁷. The image of the embassy under attack, broadcast in the United States hours after Westmoreland had declared the enemy's offensive capacity broken, did more political damage than any battle of the war.

The fighting in Saigon lasted weeks in Cholon, the Chinese quarter, where VC battalions held out around the Phu Tho racetrack. On 4 February residents were ordered out and the area declared a free-fire zone; the last battle in Cholon was fought on 7 March⁴⁷. On 1 February the national police chief, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shot a captured VC officer in the head in a Saigon street before a photographer and a film camera. The image became, with the embassy, the American public's picture of Tet.

#### ◆ The battle of Hue
*31 Jan – 2 Mar 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · changed from history · EV000-44*
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division⁵².

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west and did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February⁵². The ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel; the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March.

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800⁵². Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war.

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966, and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor³.

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order that this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred: every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a rehearsal every quarter³. In the provinces the plan was rehearsed. Whether it would be carried out on the day it was needed was a question for another year.

#### The reinforcement request and the decision of 31 March
*20 Feb – 31 Mar 1968 · Saigon and Washington · historical · EV000-45*
On 12 February Westmoreland asked for 10,500 more troops; on 13 February a brigade of the 82nd Airborne Division and a Marine regimental landing team, already authorized, were sent. On 20 February the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Earle Wheeler, went to Saigon, and returned with a request for 206,000 more men, framed by the Chiefs to force a mobilization of the reserves. On 28 February McNamara left office and on 1 March Clark Clifford succeeded him, charged with reviewing the request⁴⁷. On 10 March the *New York Times* reported it. On 12 March Senator Eugene McCarthy, running against the war, took 42 percent of the vote in the New Hampshire primary; on 16 March Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race. On 25–26 March the senior outside advisers, the "Wise Men", told the President that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.

On 31 March Johnson announced in a televised address that he was stopping the bombing of North Vietnam except in the area just north of the DMZ, that he would send only about 13,500 more troops, that the United States was ready to talk, and that he would not seek or accept his party's nomination for another term¹⁹. The speech capped the American commitment. Westmoreland's strategy of attrition by American forces had been judged by the President's own advisers to have no end that the country would accept, and from this point the questions were how to get out, and how to leave the South able to stand.

#### The My Lai massacre
*16 Mar 1968 · Son My village (My Lai 4 and My Khe), Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · historical · EV000-46*
On the morning of 16 March 1968 Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Brigade, Americal Division, operating under Task Force Barker, was landed by helicopter at the hamlet of My Lai 4 in Son My village, in an area of Quang Ngai long held by the VC 48th Local Force Battalion. It met no resistance. Its men, led among others by Second Lieutenant William L. Calley, killed the unarmed villagers they found, mostly old men, women and children, in their homes, on the paths and in an irrigation ditch; Company B, 4th Battalion, 3rd Infantry killed civilians at My Khe nearby. The dead number from 347, the US Army figure, to 504, the Vietnamese figure. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson, flying an OH-23 scout helicopter overhead, landed between the soldiers and a group of villagers, threatened to fire on his own side, and evacuated survivors; his crewman Glenn Andreotta pulled a wounded child from the ditch⁵³.

The Army reported the operation as a victory over 128 Viet Cong, and the brigade's investigation conceded about 20 accidental civilian deaths. The cover-up held for a year and a half, until Ronald Ridenhour's letters to Congress in March 1969 and Seymour Hersh's report of 13 November 1969⁵³. My Lai belongs to this phase as an event of the war in the countryside: a company of a division assembled hastily, under a command climate that measured success by bodies, in a province where the population was treated as the enemy's. Its effect on the war came later, when it became known.

#### The battle of Dai Do
*30 Apr – 3 May 1968 · Dai Do and the hamlets north of Dong Ha on the Cua Viet, Quang Tri Province · historical · EV000-47*
At the end of April, as the second wave of the offensive began, battalions of the PAVN 320th Division, two of them identified as belonging to the 48th and 52nd Regiments, moved into the hamlets of Dai Do, Dinh To and their neighbours north of Dong Ha, threatening the Marine base and the Cua Viet supply route. The 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines under Lieutenant Colonel William Weise attacked across open paddies from amphibian tractors and boats, with the 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines, ARVN units of the 2nd Regiment and a mechanized battalion, and from 1 May the US 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry. The Marines lost 81 killed and the ARVN 5; the Americans claimed more than 600 PAVN killed, and the PAVN has not released its losses. By 3 May the PAVN had gone. Whether Dai Do was a main thrust against Dong Ha or a diversion is not settled; the frontal assaults of 1 and 2 May were costly enough to be criticized by later historians⁵⁴.

#### The Paris talks open
*May 1968 · Paris · historical · EV000-48*
After weeks of argument over a site, talks between the United States and North Vietnam opened in Paris in May 1968, with W. Averell Harriman leading for the United States and Xuan Thuy for Hanoi. They stalled at once: Hanoi demanded a complete and unconditional end to the bombing of the North before substantive talks, and refused to recognise the Saigon government; Saigon refused to sit with the Front⁵⁵. Through the summer the talks were a forum for statements, while both sides fought for position on the ground. Their opening nonetheless changed the war's logic. From May 1968 both governments were fighting to shape a settlement as well as to win, and every offensive that followed had a negotiating purpose.

#### The May offensive in Saigon
*5 May – 15 Jun 1968 · Saigon and its outskirts, and towns throughout the South · historical · EV000-49*
On 5 May the PAVN and VC launched the second phase of the general offensive, which the Americans called "mini-Tet", with attacks by fire on more than 100 towns and installations and a renewed assault on Saigon. VC battalions fought their way into the southern and western districts of the capital and into Cholon, where they were destroyed or driven out by the ARVN and US forces over several weeks, with air strikes and artillery used inside the city; much of District 8 was destroyed and tens of thousands were made homeless. The phase lasted until 15 June⁴⁷. Its losses fell heavily on the same local forces and cadres who had survived February, and many of the units that fought in Saigon in May were filled out with northern replacements. American deaths in May 1968 were the highest of any month of the war.

#### The evacuation of Kham Duc
*10–12 May 1968 · Kham Duc Special Forces camp and Ngok Tavak, Quang Tin Province · historical · EV000-50*
On 10 May the 40th Battalion of the PAVN 2nd Division's 1st Regiment overran the outpost of Ngok Tavak, held by an Australian-led company of the 11th MIKE Force and a battery of the 2nd Battalion, 13th Marines. The PAVN 1st and 21st Regiments then closed on the camp at Kham Duc, held by Special Forces Detachment A-105, CIDG and a SOG detachment, reinforced by the 2nd Battalion, 1st Infantry of the Americal Division. On 11 May Westmoreland approved evacuation, and on 12 May, under about 350 air sorties, roughly 1,500 soldiers and civilians were flown out. A C-130 flown by Major Bernard L. Bucher was shot down just after take-off, killing all 183 Vietnamese civilians aboard, a Special Forces officer and the five crewmen; a second C-130 was wrecked on the runway. Lieutenant Colonel Joe M. Jackson landed a C-123 on the abandoned strip to recover a three-man combat control team left behind, and received the Medal of Honor. The Americans lost about 42–46 killed and 31–33 missing and twelve aircraft; about 220 CIDG were killed or missing; the US claimed about 345 PAVN killed, and a later study estimates 1,500–2,000. The abandonment of the camp removed one of the few allied positions between the Laotian border and the lowlands of Quang Tin and Quang Nam, and its loss was one of the few clear PAVN victories over a US-defended position in 1968⁵⁶.

#### Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned
*10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · historical · EV000-51*
On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy⁵⁷.

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969⁵⁸. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh, which Westmoreland had held through the siege; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July⁵¹. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ, but it was read by both sides as an admission of what the base had cost.

#### The third offensive
*17 Aug – 23 Sep 1968 · Provincial towns and bases, especially in III Corps and the Central Highlands · changed from history · EV000-52*
The third phase of the general offensive began in mid-August with attacks by fire and ground assaults on towns and bases, including attacks around Tay Ninh in III Corps. It was weaker than its predecessors: the local forces that had carried the attacks of February and May were largely gone, and the main forces would not commit themselves against American firepower in the same way again. It ended in late September without lasting gains. Taken together, the three offensives of 1968 cost the VC about 50,000 dead by one estimate against about 4,000 US and 6,000 ARVN. Their recruitment in the South collapsed: in Region 5, 1,700 recruits in 1969 against 8,000 in 1968, in the Mekong lowlands 100 against 16,000. By the end of the year about 70 percent of the main-force troops in the South are reported to have been northerners. MACV estimated total PAVN and VC strength at 287,000 in January 1968 and 251,000 at its end⁴⁷. The losses forced a reassessment in Hanoi that would carry into 1969.

In this war the third offensive runs as it did historically, in a part of the country the provisions barely touched. What changes is the arithmetic behind it. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968, 16,000 on the historical course, is about 11,000 here, because about a third of the tenant families who would have supplied the recruits had been titled by the government in districts it held at night, and the Front's own conscription in those districts had become coercion that the PF platoons could resist. The 1969 collapse comes from a lower base and is no less complete. The consequence is that COSVN begins filling its Delta main-force battalions with northern replacements in the summer of 1968, several months earlier than on the historical course, and the northerners who arrive in Chuong Thien and Kien Giang are strangers in hamlets where the Front's strength had always been that its soldiers were local. The proportion of northerners in the main forces at the year's end, about 70 percent historically⁴⁷, is a few points higher here².

#### The bombing halt and the American election
*31 Oct – 5 Nov 1968 · Washington, Paris and Saigon · historical · EV000-53*
In October Hanoi signalled that it would accept the Saigon government at the table if the bombing stopped. On 31 October Johnson announced the end of all bombing of North Vietnam from 1 November, ending Rolling Thunder¹⁹. Thieu refused to join the talks on the terms agreed. Through Anna Chennault the Nixon campaign had encouraged him to wait for a better deal, and on 2 November she told the South Vietnamese ambassador to "hold on a while longer"; Johnson learned of it through intercepts and ordered the campaign watched, but did not make it public. Historians disagree on whether the channel changed anything, since Thieu had his own reasons to refuse. On 5 November Richard M. Nixon was elected president by a narrow popular margin. Talks in Paris did not resume in substance until the new year, after a dispute over the shape of the table was settled with a round table for the governments⁵⁵.

Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder had flown 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine attack sorties, and lost 506 USAF, 397 Navy and 19 Marine aircraft over or near the North¹⁹. It ended without having achieved its purposes of breaking Hanoi's will or stopping the flow of men and supplies south.

#### ◆ The Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix
*1 Nov 1968 – Jan 1969 · The countryside of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-54*
Phung Hoang, the Vietnamese programme to coordinate the attack on the VC infrastructure, had been established by decree in December 1967 out of the American ICEX programme of June 1967, and Thieu put his own decree behind it in July 1968. Its instruments were district intelligence centres, the CIA-run Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the police; its method was to identify, capture, turn or kill the cadres of the parallel administration⁵⁹. On 1 November 1968, at the urging of Komer and his successor William E. Colby, the government launched the Accelerated Pacification Campaign, a three-month drive to raise about 1,000 contested hamlets to relative security before any ceasefire could freeze the map, using RF and PF, Revolutionary Development cadre and the new People's Self-Defence Force, with regular forces screening.

The campaign met its targets by the Hamlet Evaluation System, a scale filled in by American district advisers under pressure to show progress, and in many places those hamlets did become more secure after dark, because the VC local forces that had protected the infrastructure had died in the offensives. Phoenix's own figures, 81,740 people "neutralized" between 1968 and 1972, 26,369 of them killed, were produced against monthly quotas that encouraged false arrests and padding, and about 87 percent of the killings credited to it were by ordinary military operations. Communist cadres later described 1968–72 as their hardest period, and some American advisers concluded that Phoenix had been a mortal threat in some provinces and a paper programme in others⁵⁹.

In this war the campaign and Phoenix are launched on the historical dates, under the same men, with the same quotas. Three things are different. The district intelligence centres start from the land register, which by November 1968 records about 900,000 titled hectares and the households that hold them; for the first time a district knew who lived in each titled hamlet, and who had left it for the Front, from a record compiled for another purpose and not for the quota. And from December the Commission Against Corruption's inspectors, under an agreement Colby made with Commissioner Khoa, sampled the neutralization reports of 40 districts in IV and III Corps each quarter and checked each name against the register, the province prison rolls and the PRU and police reports. The first sample, reported in February 1969, found about a third of the claimed neutralizations unsupported: names that appeared on no record, persons arrested for debt or feud, and killings in ordinary operations credited to Phoenix. The quotas were not abolished, but in the sampled districts the padding fell, false arrests for ransom became cases before the Special Court, and the district chiefs learned that a name would be checked. The register had been made public in 1964 only as parcel numbers, after Can Giuoc; in 1968 it was used, for the first time, as a list of persons, and the Front's propaganda said so².

The third is that Phung Hoang was not a new instrument here but a new name over an old one. The district intelligence and operations coordinating centres had sat in the Special Branch's district offices since ICEX in 1967, and the Special Branch had been running target folders in the held belt since 1964. The Commission's sample of February 1969 found about a third of the claimed neutralizations unsupported across the 40 districts; in the districts where a Special Branch section three or more years old ran the folders, the unsupported share was about a tenth, and in those where the Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the sector S2 ran them, about a half. The quotas fell hardest where the institution was youngest, which was in the contested belt and the districts recovered after Tet, and there Phoenix was the paper programme and the instrument of feud its critics described³. In the held belt it was a refinement of what the police had done for four years, and the Front's cadres there had left before it began.

#### Commando Hunt begins
*15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · historical · EV000-55*
On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night. Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000⁶⁰. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent¹.

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that. About 900,000 hectares have been titled, the Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier². The American decision of 1968 is unchanged, and so is its timing.

The National Police numbers about 95,000 and the Special Branch has buried its first constables of the Tet battles; the infrastructure in the held belt is weaker than anywhere since 1959, and every file it keeps is now held under a destruction plan³.

### Mechanism

Tet is decided by the main forces and by Washington, and the police changes neither. What it changes is the offensive's grip on the towns of the held belt and the composition of the dead in Hue. In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the cadres who surfaced to lead the uprising had settled in the towns after leaving the villages, and the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of them. The displacement that had made the arrest count fall in 1967 handed the Branch a harvest in February 1968. Where the guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later, and at Can Tho two of them had been taken in the night check a fortnight before. The census also made the Republic's own army a little stronger where it mattered: the 21st Division, whose deserters could now be found at home, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its roll.

In Hue the mechanism ran the other way. The census and the Special Branch files were a list of loyalties, and the Front read it. Its agent in the Hue identity office and the student leaders who had gone over in 1966 gave its security section houses rather than neighbourhoods, and police officers, constables and census clerks made up about a fifth of the identified dead. I do not count it as more killing. I count it as a more exact choice of whom to kill, made with the Republic's own record.

Phoenix comes into a countryside where its work had been done for four years in the held belt. The Commission's audit shows that the programme was as good as the institution beneath it: one unsupported neutralization in ten where the Special Branch was old, one in two where the PRUs and the S2s ran the folders.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's urban network, swollen by the cadres who had left the villages, was its means of acting in the towns at Tet, and it was more exposed for having moved. Its Hue security section used what it had stolen with precision. After the offensives COSVN's own reporting began to describe the held belt as a region where the movement had "no resident apparatus" and could act only by visiting teams from the forest, a description that historically fitted only some provinces and only after 1969³.

The third offensive of August–September found fewer guides in the held belt still. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands, already lowered by the land programme, fell further where the census showed which sons had gone to the forest and the family was visited the next morning.

### Causal chains

**C002-2** [war-shaping] The police reform builds a family census of Hue that records every household, including the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university → in the Buddhist crisis of 1966 Loan uses it to detain about 1,300 people in Thua Thien and Quang Nam, and several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders, knowing they have been found, go to the Front's Hue city committee with what they know of how the census is kept → in 1967 the city committee places an agent in the identity office's records room and copies the census → in February 1968 the execution lists in Hue are drawn by house, and the Republic's own police, constables and census clerks are about a fifth of the identified dead, the instrument built to find the Party's men having been turned, through the Buddhist repression it enabled, against the men who built it.

**C002-3** [months] The family census reaches the home districts of the 21st Division's soldiers in 1966 → a deserter at home is listed in a booklet, checked at night and known to a constable, and the division's desertion rate falls → the division is close to its muster-roll strength in January 1968 and its reaction company is full → when the Popular Forces on the Xa No canal report the columns on the night of 30–31 January, there are men to send, and Can Tho, the corps headquarters, is cleared in about four days with far less of the town destroyed than historically.

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits a held belt without resident infrastructure, a Phoenix programme whose quality is measured by an outside auditor, a police of about 95,000 with every file under a destruction plan, and an urban Front apparatus badly reduced by its own surfacing. It also inherits the gap's narrowing: from 1969 the historical police grows into the same villages, and what this memorandum keeps after that is experience and files, not institutions the Republic would otherwise lack.

### Clock

The provisions' effect on American tolerance in 1968 is small and runs both ways. The image of Tet in the United States was the embassy wall and Loan's pistol in a Saigon street, and Loan in this war is the Director General of the police this headquarters asked Congress to fund; the "secret police" stories that began in 1966 had prepared the ground for that photograph. Against that, the province reporting that reached Washington in 1966–67 had been truer, because chiefs who did not expect to lose their posts for bad news reported it, and the credibility gap that Tet opened was opened a little less widely by the reports that had preceded it. Neither effect moved a vote in Congress or a date in the President's calendar. The decision of 31 March stands on its date.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · POP.gvn_legitimacy +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4) · VCI.strength -1 (old band ≈ -4..-2) · FIND.vci +1 (old band ≈ +2..+4).*

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971¹. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest⁵⁸. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The revelation of My Lai, the Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the trial of Calley and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle².

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses. The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

#### Nixon takes office; Vietnamization
*20 Jan – Mar 1969 · Washington and Saigon · historical · EV000-56*
Richard Nixon was inaugurated on 20 January 1969 with a promise to end the war with honour and no published plan to do it. His national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, commissioned a survey of the agencies' views of the war, which showed deep disagreement about almost everything except that the South could not yet stand alone. At a National Security Council meeting on 28 January the Secretary of Defense, Melvin Laird, proposed calling the policy "Vietnamizing" the war; its two parts were to strengthen the RVNAF to take over the fighting and to extend pacification. Henry Cabot Lodge replaced Harriman in Paris⁵⁵. Laird, who visited Saigon in March, pressed for withdrawals sooner and larger than the military wanted, and made "Vietnamization" the measure of the South's progress⁶¹. The Improvement and Modernization programme behind it re-equipped the RVNAF from top to bottom: M16 rifles for the regulars and then the territorial forces, M60 machine guns, artillery, helicopters, aircraft, and the training base to use them.

#### The post-Tet offensive
*23 Feb – Mar 1969 · Saigon, Da Nang and installations throughout the South · historical · EV000-57*
On the night of 22–23 February 1969 the PAVN and VC opened a countrywide offensive of attacks by fire and sapper raids against more than a hundred targets, including Saigon, Da Nang, Hue and US bases, the heaviest since 1968. It was smaller in scale and ambition than Tet: the ground attacks were mainly against bases and firebases, not into the cities, and the population did not rise. It cost the Americans heavily in the first weeks, and it was taken in Washington as a breach of the understanding that had accompanied the bombing halt. Nixon's answer, in the third week of March, was the secret bombing of the Cambodian base areas⁶².

#### Operation Menu
*18 Mar 1969 – 26 May 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, eastern Cambodia · historical · EV000-58*
On the night of 18 March 1969, 60 B-52s took off from Guam on a mission ostensibly against targets in South Vietnam; 48 were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, where COSVN was believed to be, and dropped about 2,400 tons that night. The campaign that followed, codenamed Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner, Supper and Dessert after the base areas it struck, flew about 3,800 B-52 sorties and dropped 108,823 tons until 26 May 1970. It was concealed by dual reporting: an overt channel ordered strikes near the border inside South Vietnam, while a back channel ordered the strikes in Cambodia, whose records were destroyed after each mission; neither the Secretary of the Air Force nor its chief of staff was told⁶².

Sihanouk, whose territory it was and whose government had tolerated the base areas and the supply line from Sihanoukville, did not protest publicly. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969; Nixon ordered the leak traced, and the telephones of 13 officials and four journalists were tapped, the first of the abuses that led to Watergate. The bombing forced the PAVN and VC to disperse and camouflage their Cambodian bases but did not stop their offensives, and its effect on the war in the South is disputed⁶².

#### Hamburger Hill
*13–20 May 1969 · Ap Bia Mountain (Hill 937), A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · historical · EV000-59*
In May 1969 the 3rd Brigade of the 101st Airborne Division (Airmobile), under the division commander Major General Melvin Zais, went into the A Shau valley in Operation Apache Snow to destroy the PAVN base areas there. On Ap Bia Mountain, Hill 937, about 1.9 km from the Laotian border, the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry under Lieutenant Colonel Weldon Honeycutt met the 7th and 8th Battalions of the PAVN 29th Regiment, dug in. Ten days of frontal assaults followed, with the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry, the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry and the ARVN 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment joining for the final attack on 20 May. The Americans lost 56 killed, 367 wounded and 7 missing; they counted 630 PAVN dead on and around the hill, many in tunnels, and captured 89 individual and 22 crew-served weapons and 3 prisoners. On 5 June the hill was abandoned⁶³.

"That hill had no military value whatsoever," Zais said. Senators Edward Kennedy, George McGovern and Stephen Young attacked the battle as senseless; on 27 June *Life* published the photographs of 242 Americans killed in one week. Abrams turned from "maximum pressure" to a posture designed to keep American casualties down, and Nixon announced the first troop withdrawal within weeks⁶³.

#### Midway, the first withdrawals and the Nixon Doctrine
*8 Jun – Dec 1969 · Midway Island; Guam; South Vietnam · historical · EV000-60*
On 8 June 1969 Nixon met Thieu on Midway Island and announced the first withdrawal of US troops, 25,000 men, to be completed by the end of August. Elements of the 9th Infantry Division left the Mekong Delta in July, and the whole 3rd Marine Division had left I Corps by the end of the year⁶¹. On 25 July, at Guam, Nixon set out what became the Nixon Doctrine: the United States would honour its treaty commitments and provide a nuclear shield, but would look to its Asian allies to provide the manpower for their own defence. Further withdrawals were announced in September and December. For the troops still in Vietnam, the withdrawals made every American death harder to justify. Discipline in the US Army declined through 1969–71; drug use, racial violence and "fragging", attacks on officers and NCOs with grenades, became endemic in rear areas and in some combat units. The withdrawals were faster than the RVNAF's improvement, and they ran on a timetable set by American politics, not by the war.

#### ◆ COSVN Resolution 9 and the death of Ho Chi Minh
*Jul – 2 Sep 1969 · COSVN headquarters and Hanoi · changed from history · EV000-61*
In July 1969 COSVN issued Resolution 9, which accepted that the 1968 offensives had not produced the uprising and directed a return to protracted war: small-unit and sapper attacks, economy of force, the rebuilding of the infrastructure, and a campaign against Vietnamization and pacification, while main forces were preserved in the sanctuaries. It was a decision taken in the light of losses and on the movement's own reading of its position, and it set the pattern of the enemy's war in the South until 1971. On 2 September 1969 Ho Chi Minh died in Hanoi. His testament called for the war to continue until the Americans had left and the country was reunified. Power passed to a collective leadership in which Le Duan, as First Secretary, was the strongest figure, with Le Duc Tho, Truong Chinh and Pham Van Dong. Ho's death did not change the policy; it removed the one figure whose authority stood above the factions, and left the militants who had planned Tet in charge.

In this war Resolution 9 is issued on the same date with the same direction. Its section on the countryside is different in one respect. On the historical course the Front could still campaign in 1969 against a government that had not given the peasants their land. Here the government had been titling since 1964, and the resolution's instruction to the Delta's party committees was to stop contesting the titles, to treat a titled hamlet the government held at night as lost for the present, and to concentrate cadres and the reduced tax effort on the hamlets the government held only by day. That was a realistic instruction and a costly admission: it surrendered the issue on which the Front had been built in the Delta, and it left the movement there with nothing to offer the peasant but reunification and the end of the American war².

The resolution's instruction on the infrastructure was the Front's admission of what the police had done to it. It told the party committees of the Delta and the provinces around Saigon to stop keeping cadres resident in villages with settled police posts, to govern them by visiting cadres who slept elsewhere, to concentrate the reduced effort on the hamlets the government held only by day, and to put the movement's best security cadres into penetration of the Special Branch and of the provincial administration rather than into the villages. A movement that had lived among its people since 1960 now visited them, and visiting cadres taxed less, recruited less and governed less³.

#### The secret Paris channel opens
*4 Aug 1969 – Feb 1970 · Paris · historical · EV000-62*
In June 1969 the National Liberation Front formed the Provisional Revolutionary Government of the Republic of South Vietnam, to claim governmental status at the talks. On 4 August Kissinger began secret talks in Paris with Hanoi's delegation, first with Xuan Thuy, and from February 1970 with Le Duc Tho, a member of the Politburo. The formal sessions at the Hotel Majestic became a forum for public statements; the substance moved to the secret channel. The core issues were fixed from the start and remained so for three years: Hanoi demanded the withdrawal of all US forces and the removal of Thieu as the price of a settlement, and would not discuss the withdrawal of its own forces from the South; Washington would not overthrow its ally⁵⁵.

#### My Lai revealed; the Moratorium
*15 Oct – 24 Nov 1969 · Washington and the United States · historical · EV000-63*
On 15 October 1969 the Moratorium brought hundreds of thousands of Americans out against the war in cities across the country, and on 15 November the Mobilization brought a march of several hundred thousand to Washington. On 3 November Nixon appealed on television to "the great silent majority" to support his policy of withdrawal on his terms, and the speech was well received. On 13 November Seymour Hersh published the story of the My Lai massacre, and on 20 November the photographs taken by the Army photographer Ronald Haeberle appeared. On 24 November the Army appointed Lieutenant General William Peers to investigate the cover-up; his report of 17 March 1970 found that at least 175–200 Vietnamese had been killed and condemned the concealment⁵³. My Lai became, in American opinion, the symbol of the war in the countryside, and in South Vietnam a weapon for the Front's propaganda in a province that had suffered it.

#### ◆ The Board and the Directorate in the palace
*Jan 1970 – Sep 1971 · Saigon and the provinces · new · EV002-6*
The Provincial Administration Selection Board had outlived four governments because each had found it cheaper to keep than to break, and because a province chief appointed outside the list could not sign a budget, a title or a counterpart-fund disbursement while the Aid Compliance Board stood behind the rule⁵. By 1970 the American lever was lapsing with the withdrawal, and Thieu, preparing for the election of 1971, wanted the provinces. He did not abolish the Board. As the terms of its two general officers expired in January 1970 he replaced them with officers of his own staff, and from that spring his Prime Minister, General Tran Thien Khiem, appointed the third name on the list for about half the province vacancies and for most of those in the provinces with the largest electorates. The list still excluded the incompetent and the bought; it no longer excluded the loyal mediocre³.

The Inspectorate's reports, which had gone to the Board and the Commission since 1964, were routed through the presidential Inspectorate General from July 1971 under the decree that subordinated the Commission. The National Police had followed the same road earlier: its Director General had been the President's appointee in fact since 1966, and in 1970–71 the Special Branch's Saigon headquarters spent a growing share of its effort on the opposition, the An Quang Buddhists and the Ky camp, as historical police chiefs always had. The rural posts, the census and the district sections went on much as before, because they were too many and too local for the palace to want them. In September 1971 province chiefs campaigned for Thieu as they did on the historical course; the difference was that about two-thirds of them were men who had passed an examination and served a district first, and the villages they governed were governed better than they were represented³.

#### Sihanouk falls; the PAVN turns on Cambodia
*18 Mar – Apr 1970 · Phnom Penh and eastern Cambodia · historical · EV000-64*
On 18 March 1970, while Sihanouk was abroad, the Cambodian National Assembly deposed him, and General Lon Nol became the head of a government hostile to the Vietnamese communists. Lon Nol closed the port of Sihanoukville to the supplies that had reached the PAVN and VC base areas through it. From 29 March the PAVN attacked the Cambodian army and within weeks overran much of eastern and northeastern Cambodia, protecting its bases and opening the country to the Khmer Rouge, whom it armed and trained⁶⁴. Cambodia, a sanctuary since 1965, became a battlefield. The loss of Sihanoukville threw the whole burden of supplying the southern half of South Vietnam onto the Trail, which in turn was extended through Laos and northeastern Cambodia.

#### Land to the Tiller
*26 Mar 1970 · Republic of Vietnam · no longer happens · EV000-65*
The Land to the Tiller law of 26 March 1970 is not enacted in this war, because there is nothing left for it to do. On the historical course it limited holdings of rice land to 15 hectares, gave tenanted land to the tenants who tilled it, confirmed title for peasants who had received land from the Viet Cong, and cost 441 million dollars, 339 million of them American; historians debate whether it came "too little, too late"⁶⁵. Here the decree of 1 January 1964 had done all of that six years earlier, with a lower ceiling, and by March 1970 about 1,050,000 hectares had been titled to about 800,000 families⁴. What happens instead on 26 March 1970 is a smaller act: Thieu signs a decree converting the remaining provisional titles to final ones and extending the programme to the communal rice lands of the villages, the last large category of tenanted land, about 200,000 hectares let each year by village councils². It draws no headlines in Washington, because the programme it completes had been reporting monthly for six years. Its effects had accrued over those years, not over the last five of the war, in the attitude of the rural population of the Delta towards a government that had given them something before it asked them for anything.

#### The Cambodian incursion
*29 Apr – 30 Jun 1970 · The Parrot's Beak and the Fishhook, eastern Cambodia · historical · EV000-66*
On 29 April 1970 ARVN forces of III and IV Corps under Lieutenant General Do Cao Tri attacked into the Parrot's Beak west of Saigon, and on 1 May the US 1st Cavalry Division and 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment, with ARVN airborne units, attacked into the Fishhook north of it; the 25th and 4th Infantry Divisions followed. Nixon announced it on television on 30 April as an attack on COSVN. COSVN had moved before the attack. The PAVN and VC mostly avoided battle, leaving rearguards, and withdrew westward. In the base areas the allies found the logistics of the war in the South: about 22,900 individual and 2,500 crew-served weapons, 7,000–8,000 tons of rice, about 1,800 tons of ammunition, 431 vehicles and 55 tons of medical supplies. The allies claimed 11,369 PAVN and VC killed and 2,509 captured. US forces were out by 30 June; the ARVN stayed on⁶⁴.

On 4 May Ohio National Guardsmen shot dead four students at Kent State University, and protests closed hundreds of campuses. On 30 June the Senate passed the Cooper–Church amendment barring US ground forces from Cambodia. The incursion bought time: the loss of the caches and of Sihanoukville delayed the PAVN's recovery in III and IV Corps, and the southern half of the country was quieter for more than a year. It did not destroy the sanctuary. The PAVN rebuilt its supply routes, seized Attapeu and Saravane in Laos, opened a "Liberation Route" through northeastern Cambodia to the Mekong, and in 1972 would attack An Loc from Cambodia⁶⁴.

#### Fire Support Base Ripcord
*1–23 Jul 1970 · Fire Support Base Ripcord, near the A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · historical · EV000-67*
In the spring of 1970 the 101st Airborne Division reopened Fire Support Base Ripcord on the eastern edge of the A Shau valley for an offensive against the PAVN base areas. From 1 July the PAVN 324B Division, with its 803rd Regiment at Ripcord, its 29th Regiment on the nearby hills and a battalion of the 6th Regiment of the Tri-Thien command in support, besieged the base, held by the 2nd Battalion, 506th Infantry of the 3rd Brigade under Colonel Ben Harrison. On 18 July a CH-47 hit by small-arms fire crashed into the ammunition storage area, killing four and destroying six M102 howitzers and 2,238 rounds of 105mm ammunition. The command judged the base indefensible and evacuated it on 23 July under fire; the battalion commander, Lieutenant Colonel Andre Lucas, was among the last killed and received the Medal of Honor posthumously. The Americans lost 75 killed and claimed 422 PAVN dead. It was the last major battle between US ground forces and the PAVN. Whether it crippled the PAVN in the region, as Harrison believed, or achieved the PAVN's purpose by forcing the Americans out, is disputed⁶⁶.

#### Commando Hunt and the growth of the Trail
*10 Oct 1970 – Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle and northeastern Cambodia · historical · EV000-68*
The dry-season phases of Commando Hunt were the main US effort against the North's logistics after 1968. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks destroyed, a figure Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; in Phase VII the AC-130E gunships were credited with 7,335 of 10,689 trucks claimed. The CIA estimated that the entire North Vietnamese truck inventory was about 6,000, and a Defense Intelligence Agency model reached zero trucks remaining fourteen times in 1969. Over the campaign the Air Force claimed 51,000 trucks and 3,400 anti-aircraft guns destroyed⁶⁰.

The Trail grew regardless. After the PAVN's offensives in Laos in 1970 its corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles; by 1970 a petroleum pipeline from Vinh reached towards the A Shau; the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads and paths with bulldozers and graders, and floated fuel drums down the rivers. The phases' own estimates of net loss to the enemy's throughput fell from about 13.5 percent in Phase III to about 2 percent in Phase VI. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving: the trucks destroyed rose, and what arrived in the South rose with them. Over the war more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos⁶⁰.

#### The Son Tay raid
*21 Nov 1970 · Son Tay prison camp, about 37 km west of Hanoi · historical · EV000-69*
On the night of 20–21 November 1970 a joint task force under Brigadier General LeRoy J. Manor and Colonel Arthur D. Simons landed 56 Special Forces soldiers by HH-53 and HH-3E helicopters, guided by C-130 Combat Talons, at the prison camp at Son Tay to rescue about 61 American prisoners. Navy aircraft from USS *Oriskany* and USS *Ranger* flew a diversion over Haiphong, and F-105G Wild Weasels suppressed the SA-2 sites. The camp was empty: the prisoners had been moved in July, which intelligence learned only days before. The raiders lost two wounded, an HH-3E deliberately crash-landed inside the compound and an F-105G to a SAM; they reported 42 guards killed, a Vietnamese source six guards and seven civilians. The raid failed in its purpose but succeeded in another: Hanoi consolidated its prisoners in central prisons in Hanoi, and many returning prisoners said their treatment and morale improved⁶⁷.

#### The Cooper–Church amendment
*29 Dec 1970 · Washington · historical · EV000-70*
After the House had rejected the version passed by the Senate in June, the Cooper–Church amendment was enacted in December 1970 as part of the Supplementary Foreign Assistance Act and signed on 29 December. It barred US ground troops and advisers from operations in Laos and Cambodia⁶⁴⁶⁸. It wrote into law the sanctuary constraint that had been policy since 1965, at the moment the administration was planning to attack the Trail on the ground. Any operation into Laos would now be the ARVN's alone, without the American advisers who called in the air support and coordinated the helicopters on which ARVN operations depended.

#### Lam Son 719
*8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · historical · EV000-71*
In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery⁶⁸.

The advance stalled within days. Firebases north of Route 9 held by Rangers and Airborne were overrun or abandoned; on 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world. The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing, and 108 helicopters destroyed and 618 damaged. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded". Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after⁶⁸.

#### ◆ The war on trial at home
*29 Mar – 30 Jun 1971 · Fort Benning, Washington and New York · changed from history · EV000-72*
On 29 March 1971 a court-martial at Fort Benning convicted Lieutenant Calley of the premeditated murder of at least 20 people at My Lai and sentenced him to life imprisonment; two days later Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest pending appeal. His sentence was later reduced, and he was paroled in 1974⁵³. In April veterans opposed to the war demonstrated in Washington and threw their medals onto the Capitol steps. On 13 June the *New York Times* began publishing the Pentagon Papers, the Defense Department's secret history of the war's decisions to 1968, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg; on 30 June the Supreme Court refused to stop publication. The administration's response, the "plumbers" unit formed to stop leaks, led to Watergate. By mid-1971 a majority of Americans told pollsters the war had been a mistake.

In this war the Phoenix programme comes before Congress on the historical date, in the hearings of the House Government Operations subcommittee in July 1971, at which William Colby testified that more than 20,000 people had been killed under Phoenix since 1968. The hearings were damaging and less damaging than they were historically. The Commission's quarterly samples since 1968 gave the administration an independent count of unsupported neutralizations, falling from about a third in 1969 to about a sixth in 1971, and the Special Branch's magistrate review and capped detention gave it a legal frame to describe. The Con Son "tiger cages" of July 1970 had happened as they did historically, in a prison the statute did not reach. The Public Safety appropriation for 1972 passed with a cut of about a tenth rather than the larger cuts its critics proposed, and the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers were kept³.

#### The Commission placed under the Inspectorate
*14 May – 9 Jul 1971 · Saigon · new · EV001-8*
On 14 May 1971 the Commission Against Corruption opened an inquiry into the sale of import licences for fertilizer and motor vehicles under the Commercial Import Program, which its auditors had traced through three Saigon firms to a relative of a member of the President's staff. It was the first inquiry in seven years to reach the presidential palace, and it reached it in the year Thieu stood for re-election. The American lever that had protected the Commission since 1964, the Aid Compliance Board's power to suspend counterpart funds to a named command, importer or province, had not been used since 1969: the Mission was managing a withdrawal, and Washington would not risk a quarrel with the government it was leaving over a licence case. Commissioner Khoa's second term, from January 1970, had been confirmed with the American co-chair's concurrence; his removal still required it⁴².

Thieu did not remove him. On 9 July 1971 he issued a decree placing the Commission, the Special Court's prosecutors and the Pay and Muster Verification Service under a new Inspectorate General of the Presidency, with authority to approve or suspend any inquiry touching an official of the rank of minister, general officer or province chief and above. The licence inquiry was suspended the same day. The Deputy Chief of Mission protested in writing; the Embassy did not invoke the Board. Khoa stayed in office and the Commission's work below that rank went on: the musters, the pay at the parade, the land court cases and the Phoenix audits continued through 1971 at about their earlier rate. What ended was the reach upward that had convicted a general in 1967. An anti-corruption body that had survived seven years with a patron and an American lever lost the lever first, and the patron followed his own interest².

#### Thieu's unopposed re-election
*Oct 1971 · Republic of Vietnam · historical · EV000-73*
In the presidential election of October 1971 Thieu ran alone. Ky was at first disqualified under an election law requiring endorsements from deputies or provincial councillors, then reinstated by the Supreme Court, and withdrew; Duong Van Minh withdrew, charging that the election was rigged. Thieu received all the valid votes, 5,971,114, and the reported turnout was 87.97 percent of 7,192,660 registered voters; valid ballots were 94.37 percent of those cast⁶⁹. A government that had won with a third of the vote in a contested election in 1967 was now returned in a referendum on itself. The army and administration remained Thieu's, and the regime was more stable than at any time since 1963; but its claim to stand for the whole non-communist South had narrowed, at the moment American forces were leaving it to stand alone.

In this war the election runs as it did historically, with the same withdrawals, the same single candidate and the same returns. Its context is different in one respect and the same in another. Thieu's government could claim in the countryside what it could not claim on the historical course, that it had given about 800,000 families their land seven years before the election, and its province chiefs campaigned on it. But the subordination of the Commission in July had removed the one body with the standing to hear the opposition's complaints about the endorsement law, and Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard in Saigon as a description of a government that had shut down its own inspector in the year it chose to run alone².

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving¹. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before⁵⁸. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it². These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal⁶⁸. The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent³. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

### Mechanism

In Phase IV the provisions' advantage over history shrinks, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964. The rural police extension, the Field Force, Phoenix and an accelerated police growth to about 100,000 come on the historical course in 1969–71, and the gap narrows to two things: chiefs who have governed their districts for five or six years instead of two, and Special Branch files that run back to 1964. The end vector of the phase reflects that: the infrastructure is weaker and better known than historically, by the same band the first memorandum left it, and no further.

The provisions meet the same limit the Commission met. The Board and the police survived four governments because the American lever made breaking them expensive, and when the lever lapsed with the withdrawal, Thieu captured them without breaking them. The list still excluded the incompetent; it stopped excluding the loyal. The Special Branch's Saigon headquarters turned toward the opposition, as every historical police chief had turned it. Below the palace the posts, the census and the district sections went on, because they were too many and too local to be worth capturing.

### Enemy response and initiative

Resolution 9 conceded the villages with settled posts, as it conceded the land. The Front's answer was to visit rather than live, and to put its best security cadres into penetrating the Special Branch and the provincial administration. That was the cheap counter, and it was well chosen for a war that Hanoi now intended to win by conventional invasion rather than by the infrastructure: an agent in a province Special Branch would be worth more in 1975 than a village committee in 1970.

The decision for the Easter Offensive, taken after Lam Son 719, is Hanoi's own and owes nothing to the police.

### Causal chains

**C002-4** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase II) The arrest count in the held belt falls from 1966 because the infrastructure has left the villages, and Washington asks for quotas → this headquarters and the Commission win quotas only for the contested belt and an outside audit of every neutralization from 1968 → the Commission's quarterly samples show unsupported neutralizations falling from about a third to about a sixth by 1971, and the magistrates' review gives the programme a legal frame → when Phoenix comes before the House subcommittee in July 1971, the administration has an independent count to set against the critics', and the Public Safety appropriation passes with a cut of about a tenth, so that the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers survive into 1972, which on the historical course they barely did.

### Downstream

Phase V inherits a countryside that is better governed and better known than history's, a Board and a police captured at the top by the President, and a Front that has bet on conventional invasion. None of it bears on the armoured columns of 1972. Phase VI inherits the files, under a destruction plan the Directorate has rehearsed since 1968.

### Clock

The provisions slow the clock slightly in these years and cannot stop it. The Phoenix hearings of July 1971 were less damaging than historically, and the police programme kept its appropriation, but the clock in 1969–71 ran on the withdrawal timetable, My Lai, Kent State and the Pentagon Papers, and none of those was touched. A better district government in the Delta did not bring one American home sooner or keep one there longer. It only meant that the country the Americans left was governed better in the villages than the one they left historically.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The provisions do not bite on 1972 because the Easter Offensive is a conventional invasion by PAVN divisions with T-54 tanks and 130mm guns, decided at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc by ARVN divisional leadership and B-52s. A constable has no answer to a tank, and the chiefs' competence does not reach the corps commanders who lost Quang Tri. The countryside's better governance means the Front's local forces add little to the offensive in the Delta, as they added little historically after 1968. Phase IV's end vector is unchanged by this memorandum, so nothing arrives in 1972 for the phase to answer.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The provisions do not change the end because the Republic falls to a conventional offensive after the aid cuts, at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, and to Thieu's order to abandon the Highlands. The police and the chiefs strengthen the Republic's hold on the villages in the ceasefire war of 1973–74, but that war was not where the Republic was lost historically either, and I cannot show it changes a date. Their largest effect falls after 30 April 1975, outside the chapters: whether the destruction plan for the files is carried out decides how many of those who trusted the police survive the peace.

## §4.H Historical comparison

The cumulative force has now changed four things from history, all of them in the countryside the government held at night, and none of them in the war of the main forces.

First, the Republic had a government in the district from 1964–65, four to five years before the historical one. Province and district chiefs came from a ranked list, stayed at least two years, and were replaced from the same list in about three weeks when the Front killed them; historically chiefs changed with every coup, and Thieu could not find competent replacements when he sacked 16 of them in 1968⁵. The held belt was cleared of resident infrastructure before Tet rather than after it, and NIGHT LANTERN shows the difference in one place: a district where the Front shot title holders in 1964 was a district where the Front's local battalion lost a night attack and its tax take fell by two-thirds in 1966³. That is a substantial improvement over history, and in the held belt I think it is the largest single change either memorandum has made.

Second, the Republic's own records improved, and so did its reporting to Washington. The census, the Board's register and the Inspectorate gave the Republic true figures for its population, its officers and its provinces; the Commission's audits put an outside check on Phoenix from 1968, and the unsupported share of its claims fell from a third to a sixth by 1971³. This is an improvement in the honesty of the war's measurements, which on this theater's record is worth a great deal.

Third, the costs were real and specific: about 410 constables killed by July 1965 and more after, nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs in 1965–66, a more efficient repression of the Buddhists in 1966, and in Hue in 1968 the Republic's own census turned into execution lists³. The police that was built to arrest on evidence was captured at the top by Ky in 1966 and by Thieu in 1970–71, exactly as the counter-directional finding predicted.

Fourth, nothing in the main-force war changed. Every battle from Binh Gia to Xuan Loc, every American decision from July 1965 to the aid cuts of 1974, and the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 stand on their historical dates. No life was saved in the Easter Offensive or in the last campaign by these provisions. Lives were saved in the villages of the held belt in 1966–68, where an infrastructure that would otherwise have taxed, conscripted and executed was not there to do it; I cannot count them honestly and I will not invent a figure.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state remains rung (a), collapse, on the historical timeline. The two memoranda together have moved the deficit the Theater Needs Assessment named first, the political one, further than I expected: the Republic now gives its peasants land in 1964, governs its own belt with a police and chiefs chosen for merit from 1965, and has its army counted honestly. Under POSTNA-1 that is the precondition for any rung above (b), and it is now met in the Delta and the coastal lowlands the government holds.

It is not sufficient, and POS001-4 says why. The Republic fell to a conventional invasion after the Americans left, and a provision that clears the infrastructure does nothing to the PAVN's corps, the Trail, the sanctuaries or the American aid appropriation of 1974. Rung (b), dependent survival, would require that the Republic hold while American combat forces are present and fall within about two years of their leaving; it did hold while they were present, historically as here, and fell two years after they left, which is the historical outcome and is still called (a) because the date did not move. To make (b) or (c) reachable the cumulative force needs something these provisions are not: the means to stop the 1975 offensive, by the ARVN's leadership at corps and division, its mobility and its supply after the aid cuts, or by American air power that Congress no longer permits.

The war's shape has not outgrown its phases. The held belt now runs ahead of history inside every phase, but the phase boundaries are set by Hanoi's offensives and Washington's decisions, and neither has moved. I propose no redraw.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The Republic governs its own belt at night four to five years early.** Chiefs from the list, posts beside the Popular Forces and a Special Branch that owns the files cleared the held belt of resident infrastructure by 1967; on the historical course that happened in 1969–71. This is the largest change either memorandum has made, and it is a genuine one.
- **NIGHT LANTERN is the proof in one district.** Can Giuoc, where the Front shot title holders in 1964, was held in 1966 by its own platoons and constables, and the families got their titles back in their own hamlets.
- **The land programme becomes deliverable.** A titled family now has a constable to tell and a chief who keeps the platoon in the hamlet; the land reform of 001 and the police of 002 are one programme in the villages.
- **The provinces survive the coups.** The Board outlived four governments to 1970 and filled vacancies in weeks; the districts no longer stop when Saigon changes hands.
- **The war's numbers are more honest.** The census, the Inspectorate and the Commission's audit of Phoenix gave Washington truer province reporting from 1964 and an outside count of Phoenix claims from 1968.
- **Tet in the held belt of the Delta went better again,** with urban cadres known, guides taken and the 21st Division nearly at strength.

### What has not changed

- **The main-force war,** every battle from Binh Gia to Xuan Loc, and the decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces.
- **Tet's political verdict and the American clock:** the decision of 31 March 1968, the withdrawal timetable and the aid cuts stand on their dates.
- **The coups and the capture of the police at the top:** Ky took the police in 1966 and Thieu the Board in 1970–71, as the counter-directional finding said; a stronger police was used more efficiently against the Buddhists.
- **Hue:** the Republic's own census fed the Front's execution lists in 1968.
- **The end:** the Easter Offensive, Paris, Phuoc Long, Ban Me Thuot and the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975; rung (a).

### Trajectory Log entry

Memorandum 002 gave the Republic a rural police, a Special Branch and province and district chiefs chosen by examination, on the same day as the land reform. Together with 001 they made a government in the district four to five years early: chiefs from the list held their districts through the coups, constables lived beside the Popular Forces, and the infrastructure in the government's own belt was driven out before Tet, as Operation NIGHT LANTERN showed in Can Giuoc in 1966. The costs came as predicted: constables killed in posts settled ahead of security, a war on the best chiefs, Ky's capture of the police against the Buddhists, and in Hue the Republic's own census turned into execution lists. Thieu captured the Board as the American lever lapsed, and the historical police closed the gap after 1969. The main-force war, Tet's verdict in Washington, the Easter Offensive and the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 stand. The end state stays at rung (a): the political deficit is now largely met where the government holds the night, and the conventional one is untouched.
