# Memorandum 013 — Part 2 of 3 · §2 Integration and second-order effects · §3 Changes to existing forces


The setting that changes the answer is stated first. Under CONFIG, `bombing_north` is SUSPENDED on the provision's terms: I choose the targets, restrikes are standing, Hanoi and Haiphong have no zones, Haiphong's port takes one strike, the Chinese buffer is 15 miles¹ and there are no pauses. `haiphong_mining` stays MODELLED until May 1972, and the ground sanctuaries, the DMZ conventions and the Laos rules bind as they did. The provision therefore reaches the North and nothing beyond it, and most of what follows is about what reaching the North does and does not do to the war in the South.

## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

The provision joins STRIKE and INTERDICT. It strengthens no other system directly, and it makes one thing newly viable: a campaign against the North's fixed targets fought as a campaign, from a list chosen in the theater and restruck at the theater's tempo.

**F013-1** (Integration · STRIKE, FIND) The authority is the missing half of an instrument the record already holds. Memorandum 005 named the J's binding constraint as coordinates, kits and "Washington's authority over the B-52", and found that "Thanh Hoa waits on Washington". Memorandum 003 gave the theater the imagery to measure the North. By June 1965 the coordinate cell held about 1,400 folders south of the 20th parallel and none had been struck². The provision supplies the authority, and with it the record's folders and its Js become a campaign. Without the J, the authority would be history's 1972 latitude in the weather of 1965, flown by fighter-bombers that bomb visually under the crachin. Without the authority, the J is what the record shows: a bridge-killer allowed below the 18th parallel from April 1966 and over Hanoi only in December 1972. The compound is the whole of the provision's power, and neither half is worth much alone.

**F013-2** (Integration · STRIKE, SUSTAIN) The authority sits awkwardly in the Pacific command, and the awkwardness is real friction. Historically the air war against the North was CINCPAC's, flown by PACAF's and PACFLT's components, and MACV had a voice and later Route Package 1. Now COMUSMACV selects the targets and Admiral Sharp transmits them to his own components. The Navy's carrier task force takes its targets from an Army general's cell. Strategic Air Command lends the Js and its targeteers to a theater it never trusted with them. I meet this by building the cell as a joint one, with CINCPAC's and CTF 77's officers inside it and a Navy captain as deputy, and by leaving the route packages as the components' means of execution. The friction costs a few weeks in the spring of 1965 and a standing quarrel over sortie allocation between the North and the South³. It does not stop the campaign. It does mean that every failure of the northern campaign is now mine.

### Compound pairings
**Interactions with earlier provisions**

- P013a, P013b × P005a, P005b, P005c: the J with GPS kits flies the opening night in the crachin and restrikes the bridges and power plants at the cell's tempo; the authority turns memorandum 005's bridge night into a campaign
- P013a, P013b × P003a, P003b: the U-2S's change detection and the Gulf watch see each repair, each SAM site under construction and each lighter out of Haiphong; restrike is ordered on measured evidence, and battle-damage assessment has a second ledger
- P013b × P005d: the river and estuary fields south of the 20th parallel and the restruck rail and road north of it squeeze the panhandle's freight from both ends; Haiphong itself stays unmined until May 1972
- P013b × P003b (Sihanoukville count): pressure on the northern routes shows up as more Chinese-flag cargo counted at Kompong Som
- P013a × P012a: the combined command's planning staff gives the VNAF's A-1 squadrons their place in the opening strike on the coastal targets; nothing else in the combined command touches the North

The pairing with memorandum 005's Quickstrike is narrower than it looks. The rivers south of the 20th parallel were mined from February 1967, and mining the ports stays MODELLED. The provision lets me strike everything that moves the cargo after it leaves Haiphong, not the ships that bring it.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

**F013-3** (Integration · FIND, FIX) The provision gives the find-to-fix chain against the main force nothing and takes something from it.

Nothing on the target list is a PAVN or VC regiment in the South, and nothing in the North's fixed economy decides where those regiments are or when they fight. The record's settled position holds: the main force under canopy is found no better by any instrument of the air war.

What the provision takes is reconnaissance time. The U-2S already spends a quarter to a third of its hours re-imaging known targets. A northern campaign with standing restrike needs each major target imaged after every strike and every repair. I judge that it draws a further fifth to a third of the U-2S's hours north from 1965³, and some of the Global Hawk's second orbit over the Red River delta. That time came from the deep look over the border base areas and the panhandle. The direct cost to finding the main force is small, because the main force was not being found from 70,000 ft anyway. The cost to counting the Trail and the Cambodian route is measurable. I protect the panhandle watch and take the hours from the second orbit, not the first.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

The provision does not touch the VCI. The infrastructure lives in the hamlets of the South and depends on the North for direction and cadre, not for anything the target list holds. The record's chain against it, the Special Branch, Phung Hoang and the combined task force of memorandum 012, is untouched. A national radio transmitter struck in Hanoi does not silence a district committee that receives its orders by courier. This is a null I defend rather than assert: the only route by which bombing the North could reach the VCI is a collapse of Hanoi's will, and nothing in the record reaches it.

### Dependence on SECURE

The provision is the one instrument in the cumulative force that does not depend on SECURE: it is fought entirely outside the South's countryside. That independence is also the measure of its limit. The war in the hamlets, which the record shows decides the Republic's strength, is neither helped nor harmed by what falls on the North, except through the tonnage that reaches the South. That tonnage the record judges taxed and not stopped in every phase. The countryside's regions are addressed in §4 under DIRMT01-8. The honest statement here is that the provision has no effect on them except through the panhandle's flow and the attention of this headquarters.

**F013-4** (Integration · ADVISE, STRIKE) The VNAF takes its place in the opening strike, and nothing more. Its A-1H squadrons at Bien Hoa and Da Nang strike the coastal radars and naval bases south of the 20th parallel on the first day, as history's 19 VNAF A-1s struck Quang Khe on 2 March 1965⁴. They then fly the southern route packages under the cell's list in 1965–66, as history's VNAF did until its A-1s were wanted in the South³. The RVNAF gains no capability it can keep: no targeteer, no folder, no aircraft able to drop a GPS weapon. The record's finding stands that none of the air war's instruments belonged to the Republic.

### The ADVISE cluster

The ADVISE cluster is starved slightly and fed not at all. The cell at Tan Son Nhut takes 60–120 American specialists⁵ from the pool that supplies the coordinate cell and the advisers' fire-support training. The record already holds that pool to be tight. It takes this headquarters' attention: a COMUSMACV who chooses the targets in the North spends a morning a week on them and his best staff officers on the cell. The advisory effort's leverage over the RVNAF is unchanged. The one adviser's lesson the provision carries is a dangerous one: that the war's decisive instrument is American air power the Republic will never own. The record has already found that lesson costing the ARVN in 1974–75. An air campaign in the North that looks decisive in 1965 teaches it earlier, to more officers.

### Against the studies

**The air-war study** judges AIR.north_effect a trace in Phases I–III (+0..+1, +1..+2, +0..+1, low) on the ground that the folders went unstruck until 1972. It judges AIR.north_defences and AIR.losses near zero over the North before 1972. The provision's pushes, +3..+5 and +2..+4 on AIR.north_effect, −3..−1 and −2..0 on AIR.north_defences, and −3..0 on AIR.losses, are far above those values. The values do not contradict the pushes, because the study judged a war in which the constraint was MODELLED. Its own reasoning is that the instruments were decisive "where Washington lets it strike fixed targets" and nothing otherwise. The provision changes the condition, not the reasoning.

The study's central judgment is one the provision must fit and does: the air war "taxed, never stopped" the flow, and TRAIL.tonnage_arriving sits a trace below history at most in every phase. My pushes on tonnage (−2..0) and capacity (−2..0) stay inside that judgment. A larger push would contradict the study's measured finding that Group 559's braided net has no single point of failure, and I do not make one.

The study's values for Phases I to III on the three AIR variables will have to be re-judged after Part 3. That is a change of the area, not a contradiction of the study. Part 3 records it in the study's entry.

**The maritime-interdiction study** holds that the provisions changed the route of the enemy's external supply, not its volume. It holds that Sihanoukville opened in June 1965 as the principal line into III and IV Corps, and that Haiphong was closed only by Pocket Money in May 1972. The provision fits this exactly. Haiphong's port is struck once and stays unmined, so the North's imports keep coming by sea. The push I make on the area, SANCT.cambodia 0..+1, adds to the study's own mechanism, which moves cargo to whichever route costs less: a northern route battered without pause makes the southern one more valuable. My Part 1 manifest pushed INFIL.sea for the cargo at Haiphong. That was the wrong variable, because INFIL.sea measures seaborne supply into the South, and I have withdrawn it. The North's own imports belong to INTL.communist_aid.

### Employment concepts

#### EC013-1 · The first night

The opening strike is the Js' night. The cycle runs as follows:
- **Departure.** About 22–24 Js leave Andersen in cells of three from mid-afternoon local time.
- **Transit and release.** They fly about 4½–5 hours to the Gulf of Tonkin entry points, release GPS weapons from 30,000–40,000 ft up to 15 miles from the aimpoint², and recover to Guam after about 9–11 hours.
- **Turnaround.** Twelve to fourteen hours of loading and crew rest, so each aircraft flies once a day for three days with a crew ratio of about 1.5⁶.

Each night's targets come in a set order. First, Phuc Yen's runway and revetments and the early-warning and fire-control radars with GBU-38. Second, the command compounds at Bach Mai and the Ministry of Defence with GBU-31(V)3/B. Third, the power plants, bulk fuel and Red River bridges with GBU-31(V)1/B.

The minimum viable package is a cell of three Js per target complex. A U-2S pass over the same complex within twelve hours shows what stands for the second wave. An EB-66 orbit over the Gulf jams the four fire-control radars⁷ for the first hour, until they are struck. The concept depends on memorandum 005's kits and memorandum 003's measured coordinates. Without them it degrades to history's B-52F with unguided bombs, which could not have flown it at all in 1965: no B-52 struck the North until 1966.

#### EC013-2 · The daylight wave

The second wave goes on the first flyable day after the first night, and on every flyable day for a week:
- **Navy.** Task Force 77's alpha strikes come from Yankee Station: about 30–40 aircraft each, A-4C and A-4E strikers with F-8E flak suppressors and F-4B cover, two per carrier per day⁸.
- **USAF.** The F-105D packages fly from Korat and Takhli with F-100D flak suppression and F-4C cover from April, about 16–24 strikers each, two packages a day⁹. KC-135As refuel them over Laos.

Targets are what the Js could not measure: aircraft dispersed from Phuc Yen to Kep, Gia Lam and Kien An, the guns around the struck targets, shipping in the rivers and the rail yards' rolling stock. The cycle is set by the crachin. In March and April it is perhaps one good day in three over the delta and more over the panhandle¹⁰. On a flyable day the wave puts up about 150–250 strike sorties⁶.

#### EC013-3 · The restrike watch

The cell's daily rhythm:
1. Morning: the U-2S's and RF-101C's take of the previous day.
2. Midday: the repair list, meaning every struck target showing pontoons, scaffolding, new rail or a turbine hall re-roofed.
3. Afternoon: a restrike order to Guam for the night, or to the 2nd Air Division and CTF 77 for the next day.
4. Evening: a weekly review of the standing list of about 200–400 targets¹¹.

The target rate is about 30–60 restrikes a week¹¹. The bridges get about one J sortie each per repair, the power plants a cell each time a turbine runs, and the rail yards a daylight package weekly. The concept depends on the U-2S. Without it, restrike runs on the RF-101C's and RF-8A's daylight photography, at about half the tempo and with blind weeks in the monsoon³.

#### EC013-4 · The missile sites before the missiles

The U-2S's change detection saw the first SA-2 site prepared on 22 March 1965, the first Fan Song on 18 April and eleven sites by 24 July¹². Under the provision each prepared site is struck within 24–48 hours of its first image: by a J cell with GBU-38 on the revetments and the van pads, or by an F-105D package in daylight³. The aim is the site's equipment and its Soviet crews' training cycle, not the empty earthworks alone. Historically SAM sites near Hanoi were barred for fear of killing Soviet technicians¹³. The provision removes that bar, and this concept accepts the risk.

From 1966 the concept joins the Iron Hand and Wild Weasel forces with the AGM-45 Shrike. The concept's limit is the North's answer: by 1967 about 150 launchers rotated among about 150 sites in 25 battalions¹⁴. Most sites are empty at any hour, and the cell's strike on a prepared site becomes a strike on a decoy. It stays worthwhile while the North has fewer launchers than sites. It degrades into an expensive decoy hunt once it has many more sites than launchers³.

#### EC013-5 · Haiphong's one strike

The port takes one strike, and the concept is to make it the opening night's last target.

The package is a cell of six Js with GBU-31(V)1/B. They strike the gantry and portal cranes, the transit sheds and warehouses, the port's power substation and the coal docks, after the harbour's ships have been counted by the Gulf watch at dusk. The berths with foreign ships alongside are left out³. A daylight Navy package follows on the first flyable day against the lighters and the port's fuel.

The timing is chosen because the port's throughput in March 1965 is at its most concentrated: its cranes, berths and warehouses are the whole of its capacity. Every month later, the North adds lighters, anchorages and beach unloading that no single strike touches. The risk accepted is a Soviet seaman killed by a bomb meant for a crane. That was the risk Washington would not take historically, and it remains Washington's to forbid at the decision whether to bomb.

#### EC013-6 · The rail war at the buffer's edge

The northeast railway from Hanoi through Kep to Lang Son and Dong Dang, and the northwest line through Yen Bai to Lao Cai, carry a third of the North's imports¹⁴. They are struck wherever they lie outside the 15-mile buffer¹: the yards at Yen Vien and Kep and the bridges at Bac Giang, Viet Tri and Yen Bai. The Js take the bridges, about two cells a week³. The F-105Ds hunt the rolling stock by day, two packages a week. The line is struck up to the buffer, 10–15 miles nearer the frontier than history allowed¹⁵. Where the cargo crosses from China into the buffer, unloads at the border yards and goes on by truck, the concept has nothing to strike for the last 15 miles.

#### EC013-7 · Decapitation (abandoned)

The opening night's command strikes went against the buildings where the North's leadership was expected to work. The concept of hunting the leaders after the first night lasted about two weeks. The U-2S's signals collection could locate the Ministry of Defence's transmitters to kilometres, not metres¹². The Politburo met where it chose, and no agent in Hanoi reported its movements. The cell struck three suspected command sites on signals evidence in March and found offices, a school and an empty villa³. The concept was abandoned because it named a constraint the dossier had only implied: no instrument in the force can fix a person in a city, and a strike on a guess in Hanoi is a gift to Hanoi's information war.

#### EC013-8 · The dikes (abandoned)

The Red River dike system was examined as a target in April 1965 and set aside³. Breaching the dikes in the flood season of July–August would flood the delta's rice and the Hanoi lowlands. It would not move a ton less to the South that year, because the porters and trucks of the panhandle live on stocks and imports, and it would bring China in faster than any other target on the list. The President keeps the decision whether to bomb, and a strike on the dikes would end the campaign on the day it was reported. The concept is recorded here because the provision allows "anything you see fit", and this is the target I do not see fit, for reasons that are military and political, not moral.

#### EC013-9 · Daylight armed reconnaissance of the roads (abandoned in part)

History's dominant mission over the North was armed reconnaissance of the roads and rail. In 1966 most of the 106,000 sorties went to it¹⁵. Under the provision it is cut back in the summer of 1965 to the panhandle's coastal routes, where the Gulf watch tells the strikers what moves. Elsewhere it is abandoned in favour of the restrike watch³, because the trucks moved at night and the day's armed reconnaissance found few of them. That costs the North less traffic disruption by day than history's campaign imposed. It saves the aircraft that history lost at low level to the guns along the roads.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**F013-5** (Displacement · INTERDICT, STRIKE) The North's fixed economy goes where it cannot be measured, about a year earlier than historically.

- **Fuel.** It goes into drums and small buried tanks in the countryside by the summer of 1965, not after the strikes of June 1966.
- **Power.** It goes to small diesel generators dispersed through the villages and factories¹⁶.
- **Industry.** What there is moves into caves, villages and the hills.
- **The port.** After its one strike Haiphong works by lighter in the roads and over the beaches. Cargo spreads to Cam Pha and Hon Gai, which are not Haiphong and are open to strike, and to the Chinese rail and the roads from Guangxi³.

What the North accepts losing is efficiency: each ton costs more labour and arrives later. What it protects is the flow.

What my reconnaissance loses by pushing it there is the target list itself. In March 1965 the North's war potential sat in perhaps 600–900 measurable aimpoints⁶. By the end of 1965 most of it sits in thousands of places that are each too small to be worth a sortie³.

The record has seen this displacement before, in the panhandle: Group 559 answered HARD CROSSING by owning nothing it could measure. The provision brings the same answer to the whole North a year early. Most of the opening strike's gain is the part that cannot be displaced, which is bridges, ports, rail yards and power plants. These are repaired, not moved, and the restrike watch keeps them down.

**F013-6** (Displacement · INTERDICT) Two smaller displacements leave the North entirely.

- **The southern supply line.** The freight for the B-2 Front and the Delta, already moving by Chinese-flag ships to Sihanoukville from June 1965, gains in Hanoi's planning as the northern rail and road cost more. The Sihanoukville count of 1966–67 should show a larger share of the southern war's arms arriving that way than the 9,000–13,000 tons the record already holds¹². I judge it a tenth to a fifth larger³, and that route cannot be struck at all.
- **The VPAF.** It moves its training and part of its strength to Chinese airfields in Guangxi and Yunnan from 1965, beyond the buffer and beyond the provision. It flies into the North to fight and back to China to rest.

Both are cheap for the enemy and both are permanent. The second makes the MiG force a smaller daily threat and a harder one to destroy.

### Bottleneck migration

**F013-7** (Bottleneck migration · STRIKE, INTERDICT) Before the provision, the binding constraint on the air war against the North was Washington's authority: the Tuesday lunch, the zones and the halts. After it, the constraint on the northern campaign migrates in three steps:
- **First, the weather.** It binds for the fighter-bombers from November to April.
- **Then the kits.** They bind for the Js.
- **Then the North's repair rate against the restrike tempo.** This binds for both.

By 1966 the binding constraint is the third: whether a pontoon bridge rebuilt overnight is struck again within the day.

On the line of effort the provision serves, reducing what the North sends south, the binding constraint does not migrate at all. It is where the record has always put it. The enemy's requirement in the South was about 20 tons a day in 1964 and 70–90 tons a day at the 1966 peak¹². It is carried through Laos by labour that is elastic and over a net with no single point of failure. Nothing in the North's fixed economy limits it.

This is the memorandum's most useful single statement. The provision removes the constraint the theater complained of for four years and leaves the war's constraint exactly where it was.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F013-8** (Own-force opportunity cost · STRIKE, SUSTAIN, FIND) The provision consumes four scarce things.

- **GPS kits.** The record's supply is about 8,000–12,000 GBU-31 and 15,000–25,000 GBU-38 a year, enough for about 400–600 and 400–800 full sorties². A restrike campaign of 30–60 targets a week at four to eight kits a target would use about 6,000–25,000 kits a year by itself. That is between most and more than all of the supply. The cell must ration: the Js take the bridges, the power plants and the command sites, and the fighter-bombers' unguided bombs take the rest. Every GBU-31 that falls on a restruck bridge in the Red River delta is not available for HARD CROSSING in the panhandle in April 1966 or for the siege box at Con Thien and Khe Sanh. I judge that the panhandle crossings and the siege box receive a quarter to a third fewer kits in 1966–68 than the record gave them. I hold the siege box's share first, because it is the one use the record shows decisive.
- **The U-2S's hours.** See F013-3.
- **Targeteers and photo interpreters.** They come from Strategic Air Command and from the coordinate cell, which is the same pool.
- **Carrier time.** Task Force 77's carriers stay on Yankee Station. History's Dixie Station, the carrier that flew close support over the South from May 1965 to August 1966¹⁷, is shorter-lived by perhaps four to six months³.

What is not being done is close support in the South from the sea in 1965, and some of the panhandle's crossing campaign in 1966.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F013-9** (Counterfactual erasure · STRIKE) Two and a half years of the northern air war's history do not happen.

- **The zones and target approval.** Rolling Thunder's first strikes on Xom Bang and Quang Khe are a footnote to a strike on the whole list. Risner's failure at the Ham Rong bridge on 3 April 1965, and the refusal of 13 April to send four Js against it, are gone: Ham Rong is struck on the first night. So is the prohibition on the SAM sites, and the re-targeting of Spring High in July from an empty site to an occupied one.
- **The bases and the fuel.** The two-year sanctuary of the MiG bases is gone. So is the POL strike of 29 June 1966 as a separate event: the fuel is struck in March 1965, a year before history's strike and three months before the dispersal the record dates to mid-1966. The Paul Doumer bridge is struck in March 1965, not August 1967¹⁸.
- **The pauses.** The six days of May 1965, and with them the MAYFLOWER peace probe, do not happen. Nor do the 37 days of Christmas 1965¹⁵ and the diplomacy that went with them, or the Tet truce of February 1967.

The forces and attention those events consumed go elsewhere:
- **Washington's.** The Secretary of Defense's weekly hours on target selection go to the ground war.
- **This headquarters'.** They go to the cell.
- **The North's.** The repair and air-defence effort it spread over two and a half years of increments arrives in a single shock.

**F013-10** (Counterfactual erasure · STRIKE, SUSTAIN) The partial halt of 31 March 1968 does not happen, and with it goes the mechanism that opened the Paris talks.

Historically Johnson ended the bombing north of the 19th or 20th parallel in the speech in which he withdrew from the election. Hanoi answered on 3 April that it would meet, and the talks opened in Paris in May, with Hanoi demanding a complete halt before substantive talks¹³. Under the provision there is no partial halt to offer. The decision whether to bomb at all stays the President's, so what he can offer in March 1968 is the whole campaign or nothing.

I judge three readings of what follows, in order of likelihood³:
- **He offers nothing, and the talks wait** until the full halt that history gave on 1 November in exchange for Hanoi's acceptance of the Saigon government at the table. The talks open about six months late, in November 1968, and the summer's statements in Paris never happen.
- **He offers the full halt in March** on the conditions history agreed in October, Hanoi refuses them as it did until October, and the talks still wait.
- **He ends the campaign outright on 31 March** to get the talks. The northern campaign then ends seven months early, and the provision's last year is lost.

The first is the likeliest, because Johnson's decision on 31 March was driven by Tet and New Hampshire, not by the bombing's results. It is the reading Part 3 tests. The effect is not on the war's battles. It is on the date from which both sides fought to shape a settlement, and on the President's last months in office.

### Perverse effect

**F013-11** (Perverse effect · STRIKE, SUBVERT) Success creates two liabilities.

**The cell's ledger becomes the air war's body count.** The cell that chooses the targets also counts what it destroyed, and a campaign against a dispersing economy produces a rising count of targets struck long after the targets stopped mattering. The record has the antidote in the radar's second ledger, which kept the panhandle campaign honest from 1969. I apply it to the North from the start. The ledger sent to Washington pairs the cell's claims with the Gulf watch's count of what moves on the coastal road and rail and what leaves Haiphong by lighter. The CIA's estimate of the North's imports goes beside them.

**Hanoi's civilian dead are visible from 1965.** There is no prohibited zone around Hanoi, so the bombs that miss and the guns' own falling shells kill civilians in the capital from the first week, with Western correspondents and diplomats there to see it. Historically the North's count of civilian dead from Rolling Thunder ran to perhaps 52,000 casualties including 21,000 deaths by April 1967 on the CIA's estimate¹⁴. Hanoi's own figures came to the American public chiefly through Harrison Salisbury's dispatches of December 1966. Under the provision the capital's dead are a story from the spring of 1965. The GPS weapon's precision narrows the harm at each aimpoint and does nothing for the aimpoints in a city.

This pushes US.political_tolerance.public −1..0 (manifest). It is a real cost and a minor one against the casualty lists from the South, which the record shows spent the clock.

### Anticipation

**F013-12** (Anticipation · STRIKE, INTERDICT) The provision's existence prevents most of the events that would have shown it working.

After the first season the North stops owning anything worth a J sortie except what it cannot move: bridges, the port, rail yards and power stations. Even the power stations are replaced in part by village generators. The cell's measure of success, targets destroyed, rises for three months and then falls month after month. The fall shows the North adapting, not the campaign failing.

The real gain is invisible in the same way: the factories never built in the North in 1965–68, the second power plant never begun and the steel works that never produced. Thai Nguyen's blast furnace was struck in March 1967 historically¹⁸. Under the provision it is struck in March 1965, before its first full year of output³.

The enemy's adaptation here is the war's familiar one, declining battle, translated into an economy: the North declines to be a target. The metrics rise and then fall while the war in the South is unchanged. The memorandum says so now so that no ledger in 1966 is read as the campaign winning or losing.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F013-13** (Enemy detection and learning · STRIKE, SUBVERT) Hanoi detects the provision on the first night, and the decisions that follow have authors.

**Who decides.** The Politburo under Le Duan, with Truong Chinh and Pham Van Dong, takes the political decision. In the spring of 1965 it mobilises the North for a war of the whole people against the air campaign, as it did historically³. The Central Military Commission and the Air Defence–Air Force Command under Phung The Tai take the military one: rebuild the air defence on Soviet missiles and Chinese guns, and disperse it. The General Department of Rear Services and Group 559 under Phan Trong Tue keep the flow south moving. Moscow takes its own decision under Brezhnev and Kosygin, and Beijing its own under Mao and Zhou Enlai; each is treated in F013-15 as a variable with its own politics.

**What Hanoi concludes rightly.**
- The Americans will strike anything fixed, so the North must own nothing fixed.
- The Americans have spent their escalation in a single step and have nothing worse to threaten except an invasion and the ports.
- The war in the South does not depend on the North's economy, and the strategy of protracted war in the South continues unchanged.

The North has drawn the right conclusion about the air war twice in this record. The first time was in 1966, when Group 559 read the J's geometry in a season. This time it reads it in a fortnight.

**What it concludes wrongly.** It believes the strike prepares an invasion of the North. I judge that it holds two to four more PAVN divisions in the North for home and coastal defence through 1965–66 than history's plan required, delaying their dispatch south by months. This is the provision's one channel to PAVN.strength_south. It is a modest, temporary reduction in 1965–66, perhaps a trace to −1 against history's build-up³. It fades in 1967 as no invasion comes.

**How fast it propagates.** The lesson travels from the Air Defence Command to every province's militia within weeks, by the same mobilisation that dug the shelters. It reaches COSVN and the regional committees in the South only as the strategy's confirmation: the North can bear the bombing, so the South keeps fighting.

**What Hanoi gets right that hurts.** The bombing's ceiling is now known, and Washington's threat of the next step, the lever of the whole gradualist theory, is gone.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F013-14** (Enemy adaptation cost · INTERDICT, SUSTAIN) The North's adaptation costs labour, rice and time, and its allies pay most of it.

**Labour.** History's North diverted perhaps 300,000–500,000 full-time workers and many more part-time to repair, transport and air defence¹⁹. Under the provision the diversion comes a year earlier and is larger at the start, perhaps 400,000–600,000 by the end of 1965³.

**Rice.** The labour comes from the North's farms, so the North's rice output falls and its rice imports from China and the USSR rise, by perhaps 100,000–300,000 tons a year from 1966³. That is a cost to the North's people and to its allies' shipping, not to the war in the South.

**Imports.** The Soviet and Chinese aid that replaces the bridges, generators, trucks, guns and missiles is paid in Moscow and Beijing, and both are competing for Hanoi's loyalty.

**Time.** The North loses about a season of its build-up in 1965. Its planned dispatch of PAVN regiments south is delayed in part, and its own industrialisation is put off for the war's duration.

**The exchange rate.** It is not favourable to us, and the memorandum says so. One F-105D lost costs about $2 million and a trained pilot²⁰. The bridge it struck is rebuilt as a pontoon by a few hundred labourers in a night and paid for in Soviet steel. The provision makes the North poorer and more dependent. It does not make the war in the South more expensive for Hanoi by more than the labour it can spare.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F013-15** (Cross-boundary feedback · STRIKE, SUSTAIN) Three loops leave the theater. I follow each until it returns.

**The Soviet loop.** The opening strike lands while Moscow is deciding how far to commit. Kosygin had left Hanoi on 10 February 1965²¹. Brezhnev's leadership wants the North armed and does not want a war with the United States. It sends the SA-2 package faster and larger, perhaps 20–30 battalions' equipment by the end of 1966 rather than history's build-up to 25 battalions by 1967¹⁴, with the MiG-21s and more advisers. The loop returns as AIR.north_defences rising from the spring of 1966 back toward history's density. It returns again in 1967 as AIR.losses rising toward history's over Hanoi, where the provision lets the cell send aircraft into the thickest guns of the war. Over 1965–68 the early saving and the later cost partly cancel. Part 3 must judge the net by year, not as one figure. I judge it a saving in 1965–66 and close to nil in 1967–68³.

**The Chinese loop.** It runs through the 15-mile buffer. Beijing warned Washington in the spring of 1965, through intermediaries, that it would fight if the United States invaded the North or struck China²². Its practical answer was the Chinese support forces: engineer, railway and anti-aircraft troops in the North from June 1965, about 320,000 men over the war on Chinese accounts²³. Under the provision they come earlier, more of them anti-aircraft divisions along the two rail lines, and some within the buffer's last 15 miles, where they cannot be struck.

The loop's danger is an incident: an American aircraft over Chinese territory pursuing a MiG, a Chinese gun crew killed on the northeast line, or a Chinese fighter shot down near the frontier. It returns to Washington as the President's decision whether to bomb. One serious incident could halt the campaign for weeks. The provision forbids pauses, not the President's decision to stop. I judge the risk of such an incident in 1965–67 as real and the risk of Chinese intervention as low and unchanged in kind (INTL.chinese_intervention_risk +1..+3)³.

**The Washington loop.**
- **Aircrew.** Aircraft losses fall in 1965–66, so fewer American aircrew are captured in the first two years. Each prisoner was a hostage to the negotiations of 1968–73. Fewer prisoners in 1965–66 is a smaller hostage, though not a small one.
- **Peace gestures.** The removal of the pauses takes away the Administration's evidence that it sought talks, and the opposition of 1966–67 has one more charge to make.
- **Civilian dead.** Hanoi's civilian dead are on television earlier.

Against these, the campaign's early results are the best news the Administration has from Vietnam in 1965. The net, on the record's own reasoning, is below a band on any facet of US.political_tolerance: the clock was spent by the casualties of the ground war and by Tet, and nothing in the North changes that. The loop returns at the halt of 1 November 1968, which stays Washington's, and at the talks.

### Open channel

**F013-16** (Open channel · STRIKE, SUBVERT) The theater commander now owns the air war's result.

Historically the failure of Rolling Thunder belonged to Washington: to the Tuesday lunch, the zones and the halts. The airmen's argument for twenty years afterwards was that they were never allowed to win. In this war they are allowed, from March 1965, on the theater's own list and at its own tempo. The campaign's limits will be visible by the end of 1966 as limits of air power against this enemy and not of the rules.

That changes this headquarters' standing in Washington. When I report in 1966 that the northern campaign cannot stop the flow, I report on a campaign I chose, measured by the radar's ledger, and the report is believed. The credibility gap narrows on the air war's claims, and the argument that more air power would win the war is settled earlier and inside the government³. The cost is that the failure is mine. The airmen's grievance shifts from Washington to MACV, and the Navy's from the Air Force to the Army.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F013-17 · Counter-directional** The provision spends the American lever on Hanoi in a week and invites the allies in sooner.

The whole theory of gradual pressure, wrong as it proved, held one real asset: the threat of what had not yet been struck. The opening strike strikes all of it in a week. From the spring of 1965 Hanoi knows the ceiling of American punishment short of invasion and the ports. Moscow and Beijing, faced with an all-out blow on a socialist capital, commit faster and more openly than they did to a campaign that escalated by increments. The North's air defence in 1966–67 therefore reaches about history's density a little earlier than history's did, with Soviet missiles and Chinese guns. The provision's later years over Hanoi, with standing restrike into the thickest guns of the war, may cost about as many aircraft as history's did, or more, where the cell sends packages history never sent³.

The halt of March 1968, removed by the provision, takes with it the mechanism that opened the talks. The talks open about six months late on the likeliest reading.

The true weight of this finding is moderate against the provision's primary effect, which is real. The early saving in aircraft and the North's fixed economy destroyed in a week are not undone. They are the part of the campaign that can be won, and they are bought at the price of the threat of anything more.

**F013-18 · Strongest positive** The North's air defence is fought from above and ahead for the first eighteen months of the campaign rather than from below and behind, and that saves aircraft, crews and prisoners.

On 2 March 1965 the North has about 1,500–2,100 guns, about 36 MiG-17s and no SA-2⁷¹⁴¹⁸. The opening night strikes the MiGs on the ground and the four fire-control radars. From April each SAM site is struck while it is built, and the MiG bases are open from the first day rather than the third year.

Historically 180 aircraft were lost to 24 December 1965¹⁴ and 922 over or near the North by November 1968⁴. About 68 percent of losses were to guns, and the SA-2 brought down about 101 aircraft over the campaign¹⁸. The provision does not touch the guns' share, which the restrike campaign over Hanoi may raise. It removes most of the SA-2's first year and much of the MiG's first two.

I judge the saving at about 30–60 aircraft in 1965 and about 50–150 over 1965–68, net of the later cost over Hanoi³. That is perhaps 60–200 American aircrew not killed or captured [D: about 1.2–1.4 crew a lost aircraft].

To this add the fixed economy of the North destroyed in a week, not in two and a half years: power, bulk fuel, the Red River and Ham Rong bridges, Thai Nguyen and the MiG bases. That makes the North import, disperse and mobilise from 1965 at its allies' cost. It is the most significant improvement over history that the provision makes. It is real and lasting in the air war, and it does not reach the war in the South except through the panhandle's flow, which the record measures as taxed and not stopped.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

MACV gains a combined targeting cell of about 60–120 people⁵ and a new responsibility for the northern campaign. The staff work comes from J-2 and J-3, and from the coordinate cell's measurers, the same specialists memorandum 005 made scarce. COMUSMACV's attention is divided between the ground war and the North from the spring of 1965. The advisory effort loses a few fire-support specialists to the cell and is otherwise unchanged.

### US conventional forces

Unchanged in employment. The ground war in the South is fought as the record has it. The one difference is the supply reaching the enemy in I Corps through the panhandle. That is a trace lower in 1965–67 by the record's measure of the panhandle's flow, and the enemy's guns north of the DMZ are fed a little more slowly by a railway and coastal road struck without pause.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

Unchanged. Nothing in the northern campaign touches the border camps, the CIDG or the reconnaissance programmes in Laos, which stay under the ambassador's and the Studies and Observations Group's rules.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

Unchanged in employment. The Joint General Staff is told what has been struck, not what will be (§1.2 D6). The ARVN learns from the northern campaign the lesson that American air power is the war's instrument (§2, ADVISE cluster), and the record already shows that lesson costing it.

### RF/PF and territorial security

Unchanged. The territorial war in the hamlets is untouched by the North's bombing, as the record's SECURE chain shows.

### Rolling Thunder

Rolling Thunder as history knew it does not exist. It is replaced on 2 March 1965 by the opening strike and a campaign under the theater's authority.
- **Shape.** The campaign's sorties shift from armed reconnaissance of the roads to restrike of fixed targets.
- **Duration.** It runs without pause to 1 November 1968, when the President halts it as he did historically.
- **Losses.** They fall in 1965–66 and come close to history's in 1967–68.

The name stays, because the operation stays Washington's in its existence if not its targets.

### Trail interdiction

The panhandle campaign keeps its rules: Steel Tiger and Tiger Hound stay under the ambassador's control in Laos. The North's own panhandle, Route Package 1 to the 20th parallel, is struck as part of the northern campaign without pause. The kit rationing leaves HARD CROSSING in April 1966 with perhaps a quarter to a third fewer GBU-31 than the record gave it, and its six weeks of cut crossings correspondingly shorter³. Group 559's answer is unchanged.

### Arc Light

Arc Light in the South keeps its J force but loses some of its precision cells to the North in 1965–67. The siege box's kits are held first. In March 1965 the Js fly the opening strike, three months before the first Arc Light of 18 June, and the B-52's first mission of the war is over Hanoi, not Ben Cat.

### Airmobile formations

Unchanged. Nothing in the provision touches helicopters, their hours or their employment.

### Riverine and coastal forces

Unchanged in the South. Off the North, the Gulf watch's count of lighters from Haiphong becomes a restrike input. The Navy's surface ships of Sea Dragon, which shelled the coast of the North from October 1966 historically, find fewer fixed targets and more lighters³.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Unchanged. The northern campaign does not touch the VCI (§2 Part A).

### Third-country forces

Unchanged in employment. Thailand's bases carry the USAF's share of the campaign, as historically, and its government's exposure rises with the campaign's intensity. The combined command of memorandum 012 has no part in the North beyond the VNAF.

### Prior provisions

- **P003a · U-2S Dragon Lady.** About a fifth to a third more of its hours go north, to imaging restrike targets and SAM sites under construction. It now flies over a North whose SA-2 sites are struck as they are built, so its own risk from the SA-2 is lower in 1965–66 than the record judged.
- **P003b · RQ-4B Global Hawk.** The Gulf watch counts Haiphong's lighters and the coastal traffic as a restrike input. Some of the second orbit's time goes to the Red River delta. The Sihanoukville count measures the displacement of F013-6.
- **P005a · B-52J Stratofortress.** It flies the opening strike over the North on 2 March 1965 and the restrike campaign after it, three years and nine months before Linebacker II. From 1966 its sorties are divided between the North, the panhandle and the siege box.
- **P005b · GBU-31 JDAM.** Its supply is rationed between the North and the panhandle. Its first operational use is the opening night in March 1965, not HARD CROSSING in April 1966.
- **P005c · GBU-38 JDAM.** It is used on radars, parked aircraft and SAM sites in the North. The siege box's share is held first.
- **P005d · Quickstrike mines.** Unchanged in authority: the ports stay unmined until May 1972 (CONFIG). The river fields south of the 20th parallel are complemented by the struck rail and road north of it.
- **P012a · Combined Operations Command.** Its staff places the VNAF's A-1 squadrons in the opening strike. It has no other part in the North.
