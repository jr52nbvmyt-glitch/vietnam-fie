# Memorandum 013 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


The provision lifts the political constraint on bombing the North, and it does so in a stated form. Under CONFIG the constraint `bombing_north` is now SUSPENDED, with the terms the provision gives it: I choose the targets north of the DMZ; I hold standing permission to restrike; there is no restricted or prohibited zone around Hanoi or Haiphong; Haiphong's port infrastructure may be struck once, and everything else at and around Haiphong is open; a buffer of 15 miles runs along the Chinese border instead of history's 25–30¹; and there are no pauses inside the campaign. Washington keeps the decision whether the bombing happens and when it opens, which is 2 March 1965, history's date, with planning from the Gulf of Tonkin incident. Mining the northern ports stays MODELLED: Haiphong is mined in May 1972, as it was historically². Targets in Laos stay with the ambassador in Vientiane. Every other constraint in CONFIG is unchanged and binds as it did.

I read "no pauses" as removing every stand-down inside the campaign: the six-day halt of May 1965, the Christmas halt of 24 December 1965 to 30 January 1966, the Tet and holiday truces, and the halt of 31 March 1968 north of the 19th or 20th parallel¹³. The halt of 1 November 1968, which ended the campaign in exchange for talks, is a decision whether to bomb at all, and it stays Washington's. That reading is stated here and carried into the manifest. Part 2 examines what removing the March 1968 halt does to the opening of the Paris talks, which history tied to it.

The provision makes two dossiers. P013a is the opening strike, a single compressed blow on the whole target list. P013b is the standing regime that follows it: who chooses the targets, how often they may be struck, where, and without stopping. The first is read as an air operation. The second is read as an institutional provision.

## §1.1 Opening strike of the air campaign against the North, 2 March 1965 — P013a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The provision is an opening strike of the air campaign against the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. It is planned from August 1964 and flown on 2 March 1965². Its model is the opening of the air campaign of DESERT STORM on the night of 16–17 January 1991, which struck the Iraqi communications buildings, sector operations centres and airfield radars in one compressed blow, with F-117s on the control nodes and the centre of Baghdad⁴. There is no manufacturer. The target list is the Joint Chiefs' Target Study for North Vietnam of 94 targets, forwarded on 24 and 27 August 1964 in JCSM-729-64 and JCSM-746-64. Its five categories were airfields, lines of communication, military installations, industrial installations and routes of armed reconnaissance⁵. CINCPAC had planned since March 1964 an eight-week campaign in three stages, published at the end of August as OPLAN 37-64 with the same list⁶. History never flew it: the White House rejected the 94-target list¹, and Rolling Thunder opened on 2 March 1965 against an ammunition depot at Xom Bang and the naval base at Quang Khe⁷.

The quantity is one opening strike on the whole list². The provision allows me to add to the list, and I add four classes of target that the August list held thinly or not at all⁸:
- The North's command and control: the Ministry of Defence and General Staff compound in Hanoi, the Air Defence and Air Force Command at Bach Mai, the national radio transmitters, and the long-haul telephone and telegraph exchanges.
- The early-warning and ground-controlled interception radars.
- The electric power plants and their transformer stations.
- The bulk fuel storage at Hanoi, Haiphong, Nam Dinh and Vinh.

The record already holds folders for the southern half of this list. By June 1965 the coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut held about 1,400 folders, every road and rail bridge between the 20th parallel and the DMZ, measured from the U-2S and Global Hawk imagery to coordinates true to a few metres⁹. The northern half is new work, and it begins on 5 August 1964².

The strike is organised in three waves over the first three nights and days¹⁰:
- **The first wave** is B-52Js from Andersen AFB on Guam with GBU-31 and GBU-38 JDAM. It goes against the fixed targets that do not move and can be measured, in any weather, by night.
- **The second wave** is the Navy's Task Force 77 from Yankee Station and the USAF fighter-bombers from Korat, Takhli and Da Nang. It goes against the targets that need eyes on them: the airfields' aircraft, the guns and the ships.
- **The third wave** is restrike on whatever the U-2S's first-morning imagery shows standing.

Command runs through a combined targeting cell at MACV, described under P013b. Execution stays with the component commanders: the 2nd Air Division for the USAF, CTF 77 for the Navy, and the 3960th Strategic Wing and its provisional wing on Guam for the Js⁹.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The strike uses no new units. It is flown by the aircrews of the air forces in theater on the date. Those are history's, with the record's B-52Js in place of the B-52Fs⁹:
- **The Js:** about 30 in 1965⁹, five crew each against the B-52D's six⁹, about 150 aircrew on Guam.
- **The USAF fighter-bombers in Thailand and at Da Nang:** about 80–120 F-105D, F-100D and F-4C¹¹.
- **Task Force 77:** two or three carriers on Yankee Station, each with about 70–80 aircraft of which about 40–50 are attack and fighter types¹².

The planning staff is the new part. For seven months it numbers about 40–70 officers and analysts at MACV J-2, J-3 and the 2nd Air Division, with CINCPAC's and the Navy's planners¹³. On the strike it is absorbed into the P013b cell.

The ceiling is NONE (CONFIG), so personnel spaces do not bind. The spaces in RVN are about 40–70 planners¹⁴ and none of the aircrews, who are based in Thailand, on Guam and at sea. The cost of the spaces is the cost of the people: a targeting cell good enough for this list needs photo interpreters and weaponeers the Air Force had in 1965 only in Strategic Air Command, which gave them up reluctantly¹⁵.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The opening strike's weight lies in the GPS weapons, because only they work in the weather of March over the Red River delta (D7). On Guam in March 1965 the record holds the stock of fourteen months of kit deliveries, less the training expenditure:
- **GBU-31:** 8,000–12,000 kits a year with no operational release until HARD CROSSING in April 1966, so about 8,000–12,000 GBU-31 kits¹⁶.
- **GBU-38:** 15,000–25,000 a year⁹, so about 15,000–25,000 GBU-38 kits¹⁷.

Each J carries 20 GBU-31 (8 internal, 12 external)⁹ or about 30–40 GBU-38. The GBU-31(V)3/B on the BLU-109 penetrates about 1.8–2.4 m of reinforced concrete, enough for the command bunkers at Bach Mai and the Ministry of Defence as they stood in 1965¹⁸. The GBU-31(V)1/B on the Mk 84 carries about 945 lb of explosive⁹, enough for a power-plant turbine hall, a bridge span or a fuel tank farm. The GBU-38 is the weapon for the radars, the parked aircraft and the gun pits.

The tactical aircraft carry the 1965 inventory and no guided weapon except the AGM-12B Bullpup:
- **F-105D:** up to 16 Mk 82 or Mk 117 bombs on the multiple-ejector racks, or AGM-12B¹⁹.
- **F-100D:** four to six 750 lb or 500 lb bombs and 20mm cannon.
- **The Navy's A-4C and A-4E:** Mk 81, Mk 82 and Mk 83 bombs, Zuni rockets and the Bullpup.
- **A-1H:** a large mixed load of bombs, rockets and 20mm¹⁴.
- **F-8E:** flak suppression and air cover with Zuni rockets and AIM-9B.
- **F-4C and F-4B:** AIM-7D Sparrow and AIM-9B Sidewinder.

There is no anti-radiation missile: the AGM-45 Shrike reaches the theater in March 1966²⁰. Nor is there a laser-guided bomb or a cluster munition built for flak suppression. The CBU-2 dispenser is in the Navy's and the A-1's inventory¹⁴.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The strike sees through the record's two reconnaissance types and through the historical reconnaissance force:
- **The U-2S:** ASARS-2A radar in all weather, SYERS-2C electro-optical and infrared camera, and ASIP signals collection, at more than 70,000 ft and immune to guns and MiG-17s²¹. It is vulnerable to the SA-2 only from April 1965, and on 2 March 1965 there is no SA-2 in the North⁷.
- **The RQ-4B Global Hawk:** its MP-RTIP radar for moving targets and imaging²¹. Under the standing condition E01 it uses satellite links, which reach Hanoi and Haiphong²¹ (E01).
- **The historical force:** the RF-101C and the Navy's RF-8A for low-level photography, and the EB-66 for electronic reconnaissance and jamming²².

Against this target list, canopy is irrelevant. The list's targets are buildings, bridges, power plants, tank farms, radars and airfields in the open delta and the valleys of the northeast. The canopy question belongs to the war in the South and to the Trail.

Night favours the Js. Their AN/APQ-188 radar and the GPS weapons do not need light⁹, and for the North's guns in March 1965 only the four fire-control radars counted in September 1964²³ could direct fire at night. The tactical aircraft of 1965 cannot bomb a point target at night: the night belongs to the J.

Monsoon is the decisive condition, and it decides the shape of the strike. March is the season of the crachin, the low cloud and drizzle of the northeast monsoon's last months over the Red River delta, with ceilings often below 1,000–2,000 ft for days together²⁴. The fighter-bomber of 1965 bombs visually. Under the crachin the second wave flies on the days it can, and the J flies regardless. The opening strike is therefore a bomber's strike with a fighter's follow-up, not the other way round.

### D5 · Performance

The weight of the first three days depends on the Js' sortie rate from Guam. Andersen to Hanoi is about 2,100–2,300 nautical miles²⁵, so a sortie is a round trip of about 9–11 hours unrefuelled²⁶. The J's range makes it possible without tankers⁹. Thirty aircraft at about 75–80% availability²⁷ give 22–24 sorties a day for three days, and 15–18 a day after that as crews rest²⁸. That is about 440–480 GBU-31 or 660–960 GBU-38 a day in the surge.

The 94 targets, with my additions, hold about 600–900 desired points of impact²⁹. Two GBU-31s per aimpoint allow for the 5 m CEP and the 9.6 m found in operational test⁹. On that basis the Js alone can strike every measured aimpoint on the list in about three to four days. That is the compression the provision asks for. History needed from March 1965 to the autumn of 1967 to strike most of the list, and struck it by increments¹⁷.

The second wave sets what happens to everything the Js cannot measure in advance. On a flyable day the tactical force can put up about 150–250 strike sorties against the North³⁰. The first day of Rolling Thunder put 104 USAF aircraft over one target⁷; the second wave may put twice that over a dozen.

The kit stock allows the opening surge with room to spare. Three days at the surge rate use about 1,400 GBU-31, about an eighth of the stock.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The planning chain runs from MACV to CINCPAC and on to the components. The execution chain is the 1965 chain:
- HF and UHF voice between the strike forces and the airborne control.
- The Navy's own net from Yankee Station.
- For the Js, the mission data loaded on Guam from the cell's folders.

The cell passes coordinates by message and courier to Andersen⁹. For the third wave, the U-2S's imagery is read at the ground stations at Bien Hoa and Da Nang²¹, and restrike requests go to Guam by message the same day³¹.

The ARVN has no part in the strike except the VNAF. On the historical first day 19 VNAF A-1s struck Quang Khe³², the only VNAF strike in the North the record names before 1974. The opening strike keeps that: the VNAF's A-1H squadrons go to the naval bases and coastal radars south of the 20th parallel, the targets in reach of Bien Hoa and Da Nang. The Joint General Staff does not see the list before the strike. The record holds that the staff is penetrated²¹, and a strike whose whole value is surprise cannot be briefed to it.

### D7 · Domain conditions

The threat on 2 March 1965 is the North's air defence before the Soviet package:
- **Guns:** about 1,500 anti-aircraft weapons at the campaign's opening, mostly 37mm M1939 and 57mm S-60, or about 2,100 by February 1965 on another count.
- **Radars:** about 22 early-warning radars and four fire-control radars, as counted in September 1964²³.
- **Fighters:** the VPAF's MiG-17F, about 36 aircraft with about as many qualified pilots in 1965, 53 on another count⁶, based at Phuc Yen³³.
- **Missiles:** none. The first SA-2 site was detected on 5 April 1965, and the first missile shot down an F-4C on 24 July⁷.

The 85mm KS-12 and 100mm KS-19 radar-directed guns, more than 5,000 guns in all, came within the year⁶.

Survivability is therefore at its highest on the first day of the war and falls every week after it. Each element of the threat has an answer:
- **The Js:** above the 37mm and 57mm guns' effective ceilings when they release GPS weapons from 30,000–40,000 ft³⁴. They face no missile, and the MiG-17 cannot climb to them in time on a night interception without ground control³⁵.
- **The fighter-bombers:** low-level gunfire over the targets, which killed most of Rolling Thunder's crews: about 68 percent of aircraft losses were to guns⁷.
- **The MiG-17s:** they get one chance. The Js' GBU-38s are on Phuc Yen's revetments and runway in the first hour.

Basing has limits of its own:
- **Andersen:** long enough for the J, unhardened, and beyond the North's reach.
- **Korat, Takhli and Da Nang:** within reach of sappers, as Bien Hoa learned on 1 November 1964.
- **The carriers:** out of the North's reach.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The strike rests on three supply points:
- **Andersen's bomb dump:** the GBU-31 and GBU-38 kits and bodies, built up by the munitions squadrons that M005 placed there with about 150–250 men⁹.
- **Fuel:** for the Js at Andersen and for the fighter-bombers in Thailand, where the fuel came by road from Sattahip and Bangkok in 1965³⁶.
- **Task Force 77's replenishment groups.**

The surge burns about 120,000–140,000 lb of fuel per J sortie³⁷. That is about 3,000 tons a day of jet fuel at Andersen for three days. Andersen's tank farm supported the 1965 Arc Light at a similar rate³⁸.

The specialists that RVNAF cannot supply are all of them: the weaponeers, the photo interpreters, the coordinate measurers, the bomb-builders, the B-52 crews. RVNAF supplies the VNAF A-1H crews and the airfield at Bien Hoa.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

Nothing in the opening strike transfers. It is an American operation built on American bombers, American kits, American satellites and an American targeting staff. The VNAF's part is a squadron of A-1Hs on the coastal targets, as on history's first day³². The planning skill does not pass to the VNAF either. The record already holds that the Joint General Staff asked in 1973 to keep a coordinate cell and that there was nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The strike performs to specification against fixed targets that do not know they are targets. Those are the power plants, the tank farms, the bridges, the airfields with their aircraft, the radars, and the command compounds, in their peacetime places in March 1965. The North's own preparation had barely begun before history's bombing: the bulk fuel was moved into dispersed drums only after the strikes of June 1966. The weather is no bar to the Js and a serious one to the fighter-bombers (D4).

Performance collapses on three axes:
- **Movement.** The GPS weapon goes to a coordinate, and anything that has moved since the image was measured is missed. The MiGs that are airborne, the trucks, the trains, the lighters, the guns that shift by night and the leaders who are not where their offices are all escape.
- **Time.** Every day after the first, the North disperses, hardens and decoys, and the targets that remain are those it cannot move: bridges, power plants, ports, rail yards.
- **Weight against need.** The North in 1965 is an agrarian country whose war in the South needs little from it. J-2 estimated the enemy's need from the North at 70–90 tons a day at the 1966 peak²¹, and its war industry is small enough that its capacity hardly matters against what it imports. The radius from base is no constraint for the J. Tempo over weeks is the subject of P013b.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The decapitation fails first, and in a predictable way.** The Politburo, the Central Military Commission and the General Staff do not work from the buildings on the target list. Le Duan, Vo Nguyen Giap and Van Tien Dung could be anywhere in Hanoi, and the Party's command of the war in the South runs through COSVN and the regional committees, not through a telephone exchange³⁹. Iraq's leadership survived the opening night of 1991⁴. The North's has more places to be and less dependence on a single network. The command strikes are therefore partial at best: they disrupt the North's air defence control and its national communications for days to weeks⁴⁰, and they kill no one who decides the war.

**The second failure is the reaction.** Kosygin was in Hanoi from 6 to 10 February 1965⁴¹, and the Soviet air-defence package followed historically from the spring: 95 SA-2 systems and 7,658 missiles over the war⁶, and 56 SAM sites located by the end of 1965³. A blow on the whole list makes the package larger and faster, not smaller. China's engineer and anti-aircraft troops, which historically entered the North from June 1965⁴², come at least as early.

**The third failure is political.** The strike spends the whole of the escalation in one step. Washington's gradualism was a theory: that each increment was a signal and that the threat of the next was the lever. That theory loses its lever on the first night, and what is left is the campaign of P013b. If the North does not yield in the first week, the Administration has no greater blow to threaten, short of the mining and the ground war it has already decided not to wage.

**A partial failure is a weather failure.** If the crachin holds for the first week, the Js strike everything measured and the fighter-bombers strike little. The MiGs that survive Phuc Yen's runway are dispersed to Kep, Gia Lam and Kien An, and the guns that the second wave should have struck are left standing. The force then fights the restrike campaign against an air defence a fifth or a quarter intact rather than broken⁴³.

**The window when the strike is unavailable** is any day that Washington withholds the order. Until 2 March 1965 the force does in the North what history did: nothing, beyond the reconnaissance of the record and the Gulf watch.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The opening strike's value is greatest on day one and halves within weeks⁴⁴. Measured on the four axes:
- **Target set.** In month one the list is whole and measured. By month three the fixed targets are struck and many restruck. The bridges are down and the power plants out, and the fuel is in drums under trees. What remains are the targets the North can repair and the targets it can hide.
- **Crew proficiency.** The fighter-bomber crews of March 1965 are the best trained the Air Force has in theater. By month twelve the force has lost about 180 aircraft as history did over its first ten months, less what the broken air defence spares (D16), and its replacements come from a training base that cannot keep up⁴⁵.
- **The Js' figures.** These do not decline. The kit stock falls by about an eighth in the surge and refills at 8,000–12,000 a year⁹.
- **The enemy's defence.** This is what rises. About 1,500 guns become more than 5,000 within the year⁶, the first SA-2 sites appear in April⁷, and the MiGs move to China's airfields to train and return⁴⁶.

What declines first is surprise. What declines second is the share of the North's war potential that sits in measurable places.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**The benchmark is history's first year of Rolling Thunder.** From 2 March to 24 December 1965 it flew 25,971 USAF sorties dropping 32,063 tons and 28,168 Navy sorties dropping 11,144 tons, and lost 180 aircraft⁶. It struck targets in Hanoi and Haiphong only from October and November 1965, and it barred the MiG bases for two years⁷. In 1966 only 1,000 of 106,000 sorties went against the 22 fixed targets the White House had authorised. Phuc Yen was struck first on 24 October 1967¹.

**Against that benchmark the margin is about two and a half years on the fixed target list**. In the first three or four days the opening strike puts on the list about 1,400 GBU-31, each with a CEP of 5 m or less⁹. History needed two and a half years and hundreds of thousands of unguided bombs to strike the same list less completely.

**The margin on what the North needed to make war in the South is far smaller.** The record already measures it: the J's precision taxed the flow and never stopped it.

The second benchmark is DESERT STORM's opening. Iraq in 1991 had a centralised air defence, a power grid and a state that depended on them; the coalition lost four aircraft on the first night⁴. The North in 1965 has a primitive air defence, a small grid and a war that depends on neither. The model fits the first half of the problem, the air defence, and not the second.

### D14 · Negative specification
The opening strike cannot decapitate the North. The Politburo, the Central Military Commission and the General Staff do not sit in the buildings on the list, and the Party commands the war in the South through COSVN, not through the Hanoi exchanges⁴⁷. It cannot stop the flow south: J-2 put the enemy's need from the North at 70–90 tons a day at the 1966 peak²¹, and a porter-and-bicycle Trail in 1965 does not depend on a power plant. It cannot strike Laos, where the ambassador keeps the targets, or close Haiphong, which is struck once and mined only in May 1972. It does not stop the third of the North's imports that come by rail from China, and the 15-mile buffer² leaves the last stretch of both rail lines untouched. The fighter-bombers of 1965 cannot bomb a point target at night or under the crachin of March⁴⁸, so on a bad week the strike is the Js' alone. It cannot prevent the Soviet SA-2 package (95 systems over the war⁶) or the Chinese support troops; it can only meet them earlier. And it does not change Washington's decision whether to bomb; it changes how much is struck once that decision is made.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the North the two and a half years of gradualism in which it built the world's densest air defence under sanctuary, from about 1,500 guns and no missiles in March 1965 to more than 5,000 guns within a year and 56 SAM sites by December³. Phuc Yen's MiG-17s (about 36 aircraft⁶) are struck on the ground in the first hour, the four fire-control radars and about 22 warning radars²³ in the first night. It denies the North the use of its fixed infrastructure before it has learned to do without it: the power plants, the bulk fuel at Hanoi, Haiphong, Nam Dinh and Vinh, the Hanoi bridges and the rail yards, all at once and in any weather. And it denies Hanoi the reading of 1965 that the Americans would not dare: the North learns on the first night that the capital is not a sanctuary.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The opening strike is the most efficient blow on the North's fixed targets that any American force in 1965 could deliver. The B-52J with about 1,400 GBU-31 in three days does in the crachin of March what history's fighter-bombers did in two and a half years of weekly packages, and it catches an air defence of guns and MiG-17s before the first SA-2 arrives⁷. That buys a broken air defence for the first months of the campaign, fewer aircraft lost, and a North stripped of power, bulk fuel, bridges and airfields in a week. It does not buy the war. The North's war in the South in 1965 needs tens of tons a day²¹, carried by porters, bicycles and trucks that the list does not hold. Its leaders are not in the buildings, and its allies replace what is destroyed: Moscow ships the missiles faster and Beijing sends the engineers sooner. The strike is the end of gradualism and the start of a test the gradualists never made: whether a North that has lost everything that can be bombed will stop. History's answer, from 1972, is that it negotiates on its own timetable. The record's answer is for Parts 2 and 3.

## §1.2 Standing authorities for the air campaign against the North, from 2 March 1965 — P013b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The provision is a set of standing authorities for the air campaign against the North from 2 March 1965²:
- COMUSMACV selects the targets north of the DMZ in place of Washington's weekly approval.
- Standing permission to restrike any target.
- No restricted or prohibited zone around Hanoi or Haiphong.
- Haiphong's port infrastructure struck once only, with everything else at and around Haiphong open.
- A buffer of 15 miles along the Chinese border.
- No pauses or suspensions inside the campaign.
- Washington keeps the decision whether to bomb at all.
- Targets in Laos stay with the ambassador.
- The mining of the northern ports stays historical, May 1972.

The nearest real precedent is the authority of the 1972 campaigns. In LINEBACKER the theater commanders were given wide latitude over targets and the Hanoi and Haiphong zones were largely lifted, after a war in which, historically, the President chose the targets at a Tuesday lunch¹. The quantity is one authority held by COMUSMACV for the duration of the campaign².

The organisation is a MACV Combined Targeting Cell at Tan Son Nhut. It is built from the coordinate cell that the record already holds there⁹ and from the targeting staffs of the 2nd Air Division and CINCPAC's planners. It has a watch for battle-damage assessment and a standing target list kept against the U-2S's and Global Hawk's daily take. It sets the targets weekly and the restrikes daily. The component commanders, the 2nd Air Division (the Seventh Air Force from April 1966), CTF 77 and the Js' wing on Guam, plan and fly the missions as they did historically.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The cell holds about 60–120 people at full strength⁵¹:
- Targeteers and weaponeers.
- Photo interpreters.
- Coordinate measurers from the existing cell.
- Analysts of the North's railways, power and fuel.
- Liaison officers from CTF 77, Strategic Air Command, CINCPAC and the embassy.

All of them are in RVN, at Tan Son Nhut. With the ceiling at NONE (CONFIG), the spaces do not bind. They cost in the training pipeline, because the Air Force's targeteers in 1965 sat in Strategic Air Command⁵², and in rank: a cell that tells CINCPAC's components what to strike needs a general officer at its head.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The provision brings four authorities.
- **Target selection.** I choose what is struck in the North, and when, without the weekly or monthly packages chosen at the Tuesday lunch. Historically the President chose each target; bridges needed White House authorisation; SAM sites were authorised only once photographs showed them occupied or once they had fired; the MiG bases were barred for two years⁷.
- **Restrike.** Any target may be struck again whenever the cell judges it repaired or reconstituted. Historically the prohibited zones forbade restrike, and time-limited authorisations lapsed if the weather closed in.
- **Geography.** The whole of the North is open except a 15-mile buffer along the Chinese border and the port at Haiphong after its one strike². History's 30-mile and 10-mile circles around Hanoi, its 10-mile and 4-mile circles around Haiphong, and its buffer of 30 miles west of 106° E and 25 miles east of it are gone.
- **Continuity.** The campaign does not stop for diplomacy: no six-day halt in May 1965, no 37 days at Christmas 1965¹, no truces, and no halt north of the parallel in March 1968³.

What the provision cannot compel lies outside these authorities:
- It cannot compel the Navy. CTF 77 is CINCPACFLT's, and my targeting authority over its strikes runs through CINCPAC.
- It cannot compel the ambassador in Vientiane.
- It cannot compel the President to keep bombing when he decides to stop.
- It cannot mine Haiphong before May 1972.

The leverage it gives is over the North's repair crews: each repair is answered at the cell's choosing, not at the next lunch.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.* The cell learns from five sources:
- The U-2S's daily imagery and signals.
- The Global Hawk orbits, including the Gulf watch that has counted the coastal traffic since 1964²¹.
- The historical photographic reconnaissance by RF-101C and RF-8A.
- The EB-66's electronic order of battle.
- The CIA's reporting on the North's economy and imports.

Its knowledge of fixed targets and of their repair is good, and better than history's by the margin of the record's reconnaissance. It sees the reconstitution of a bridge by its pontoons in a day²¹. Its knowledge of the North's will, of its leaders' whereabouts and of what moves at night under the trees and in the cities is no better than history's.

Night and monsoon degrade the photography and not the radar. Canopy hides the dispersed fuel and stores in the countryside, as it hid them after June 1966. The cell's knowledge of what it has destroyed is the cell's own: battle-damage assessment by the organisation that chose the target is a figure produced by the organisation being measured, and is cited as such.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.* The cell can keep a standing list of about 200–400 targets and set about 30–60 restrikes a week⁵³. History's own throughput gives the comparison:
- The White House authorised 22 fixed targets for 1966, against which 1,000 sorties of 106,000 flew¹.
- 304,000 fighter and 2,380 B-52 sorties flew over three years and eight months⁷.

The cell's rate limit is not its own staff. It is the sortie rate the components can generate and the weather over the North. In 1966 history flew about 106,000 sorties over the North¹, and this campaign flies about the same number. It points far more of them at fixed targets, and far fewer at armed reconnaissance along the roads, where history spent most of its effort⁵⁴.

The restrike permission makes the campaign's throughput against the North's repair rate a contest of tempo. A bridge down every two or three days, a power plant restruck at each repair, and the rail yards at Kep, Yen Vien and Thai Nguyen restruck weekly: that is within the force's capacity⁵⁵.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.* The chain changes at the top and not at the bottom.

Historically the air campaign against the North was CINCPAC's: PACAF and PACFLT's components flew it, and MACV had a voice. From 1966 MACV controlled only Route Package 1 nearest the DMZ⁵⁶. The project's own rules give COMUSMACV "a voice, not a veto" in the air campaigns against the North (RULES, ROLE). The provision makes that voice a decision. COMUSMACV now selects the targets, CINCPAC passes them to his components, and the JCS and the White House are told rather than asked.

Admiral Sharp loses the targeting of a campaign he had fought for. The Navy's carrier admirals take their targets from an Army general's cell. I meet both problems by putting CINCPAC's and CTF 77's officers inside the cell, and by leaving the route packages, the components' own system for dividing the North, as the means of execution⁵⁷.

The ambassador in Vientiane keeps the Laos targets. The embassy in Saigon is told of the target list weekly. The Joint General Staff is told what has been struck and not what will be struck, because the record holds that the staff is penetrated²¹. The VNAF's A-1Hs fly the coastal targets in the southern route packages under the cell's list.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.* The reach covers the whole North:
- Hanoi and Haiphong.
- The northeast railway to Dong Dang and the northwest railway to Lao Cai, up to 15 miles from the border².
- The Red River bridges, including the Paul Doumer bridge.
- The Thai Nguyen steel works and the Viet Tri chemical works.
- The MiG bases at Phuc Yen, Kep, Kien An and Gia Lam.
- The SAM sites as they are built.

The Chinese buffer of 15 miles² is half history's 30 miles west of 106° E and three-fifths of its 25 east of it¹. It brings American bombs within about 15 miles of the frontier on the two rail lines that carry a third of the North's imports⁶.

Haiphong's port takes one strike: berths, cranes, warehouses, the coal docks and the port's power supply. After that the port is closed to American bombs until the mining of May 1972². Everything around it stays open: the city's power plant, the fuel storage, the cement works, the airfield at Kien An, the guns and the SAM sites, and the bridges and the road and rail out of the city.

The security precondition is the force's own survival over a North whose air defence grows with every month of the campaign (§1.1 D12). The cell must strike the defences in proportion to the threat they pose or lose the force that strikes everything else.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The cell itself needs little: offices, the imagery link from the ground stations, the message traffic to Guam, Thailand and Yankee Station, and its people. The campaign it directs needs what history's did:
- The Thai bases' fuel and bombs.
- The carriers' replenishment.
- Andersen's kits, in a war that by the record's figures can sustain about 400–600 full GBU-31 sorties a year⁹ alongside the Arc Light and panhandle demands on the same stock.

Restrike permission raises the GPS-weapon demand in the North at the expense of the South and the panhandle. The kit supply becomes a choice between them⁵⁸.

RVNAF cannot supply any part of this: neither the analysts nor the aircrews, except its A-1H crews.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The authority does not transfer: it is an American authority over American forces in a campaign the Republic cannot fly. The skill transfers in part. A Vietnamese officer posted to the cell learns how a target list is built, measured and restruck. The record holds that this skill had nowhere to go in the VNAF of the 1970s. A few Vietnamese officers could be placed there from 1966 without breaking the security of the target list, which is told after the strike, not before⁵⁹.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The authorities perform to specification as long as Washington keeps the bombing on. They need the targets to be fixed and the repair to be slower than the restrike. They need the North's air defence kept below the level at which the cost in aircraft and crews forces the campaign onto the night and the Js alone. And they need the Chinese to read the 15-mile buffer as a limit rather than a probe.

They collapse at four points:
- **When the President stops the campaign.** He keeps that decision.
- **When the targets stop being fixed.** That happens in about a season for fuel and stores, and never for bridges, ports and power plants.
- **When the air defence outgrows the force.** That happened historically by 1967, and the record's Js lessen it but do not prevent it.
- **When the North's import flow comes in faster than the cell can strike it.** Two-thirds of imports by sea through a port struck once, and a third by rail through a buffer the cell cannot touch⁶.

The war's weather does not stop the J and does stop the fighter-bombers for weeks of the northeast monsoon. Tempo is sustainable as long as the kit supply holds (D8).

### D11 · Failure modes

**The first failure is in the port, and it is built into the provision.** Haiphong's port infrastructure is struck once. The Soviet and Chinese ships then unload by lighter in the roads and over the beaches, as the North did whenever the port was congested. The port's imports, two-thirds of the North's⁶, fall for weeks while the cranes are replaced and the lighters gathered⁶⁰, and then come back. The rest of Haiphong's targets are open: the bridges out of the city, the road and rail to Hanoi. So the cell can strike the cargo after it leaves the port, at the price of chasing it through the delta.

**The second failure is China.** A 15-mile buffer puts strikes on the northeast railway at Lang Son and Dong Dang inside the radius in which Beijing warned in 1965 that it would act⁶¹. The likeliest response is not intervention. It is a larger Chinese support force in the North, as history saw from June 1965, and Chinese fighters over the buffer's edge. A partial failure is an incident: an American aircraft over Chinese territory, or a Chinese fighter shot down inside the buffer.

**The third failure is the end of pauses.** Washington's pauses bought nothing militarily: the North used each to repair and resupply¹. But they were the Administration's evidence to Congress, the allies and Moscow that it sought talks. Without them the political tolerance that keeps the bombing on is spent faster. The President, who keeps the decision to stop, uses it sooner or more completely. The halt of 31 March 1968 is the case. It was the price Hanoi set for the talks that opened in Paris that May⁶², and Part 2 has to decide whether the talks open without it.

**The fourth failure is quiet.** The cell's own battle-damage assessment becomes the campaign's measure of success, and the organisation that chose the target reports on it. The record's radar has already made the panhandle campaign honest; the northern campaign must be held to the same standard, or its figures will be the body count of the air war.

**The window when the authority is unavailable** is the window when the President stops the campaign. The record keeps the halt of 1 November 1968 as Washington's decision.

### D12 · Degradation curve

In month one the authority is decisive: the opening strike has broken the air defence, the list is fresh, and restrike keeps the bridges down. By month twelve four things have changed:
- **The air defence.** It has rebuilt with Soviet and Chinese help. SA-2 sites number dozens, as 56 were located by the end of 1965 historically³, and guns number thousands.
- **Losses.** Aircraft and crews are lost at a rate that history's first year set at 180 aircraft in ten months⁶, less what the opening strike spared⁶³.
- **Targets.** The cell is restriking a target list the North has learned to repair: pontoon bridges, dispersed fuel and generators, and the port by lighter.
- **The cell itself.** It improves with experience: its folders deepen, its measurers learn the North's repair rhythms, and its restrikes come sooner.

What declines first is the share of the North's war potential in fixed places. What declines second is Washington's tolerance, which the provision spends faster by removing the pauses.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**The benchmark is history's targeting regime.** The President chose each target at the Tuesday lunch from weekly and later monthly packages. In 1966 only 1,000 of 106,000 sorties went against 22 authorised fixed targets. The MiG bases were barred for two years and Phuc Yen until 24 October 1967. SAM sites were struck only once they fired. Hanoi and Haiphong were struck only from October and November 1965⁷. There were seven halts in three years and eight months¹.

**Against it, the provision changes three things.** It changes the share of sorties against fixed targets from about one in a hundred to the share the cell chooses. It removes the sanctuaries two and a half years early. It removes every pause.

**Against the 1972 benchmark, the latitude of LINEBACKER, the provision is that regime seven years earlier.** It comes without the mining, which LINEBACKER had from May 1972², and with Haiphong's port struck once rather than closed. LINEBACKER's own lesson, in the record, is that the bridges fall on the first night and the North's imports move to the Chinese rail crossings.

### D14 · Negative specification
The authority does not let me stop the bombing being stopped: the President keeps the decision whether to bomb, and the halt of 1 November 1968 stays his. It does not let me close Haiphong. The port is struck once and mined only in May 1972, so two-thirds of the North's imports keep arriving by sea, by lighter if not by crane. It does not let me strike within 15 miles of China², where the rail lines carry the other third⁶. It does not give me the Laos targets, which stay with the ambassador, or the Navy, whose carriers stay CINCPACFLT's and take my targets through CINCPAC. It does not make the North's fuel, stores and trucks fixed: after the first season they are dispersed under trees and in villages, as the record already shows from June 1966. It does not measure itself honestly: the cell's battle-damage assessment is the claim of the organisation that chose the target. And it does not make the North's war in the South depend on what can be bombed in the North.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the North its sanctuaries: Hanoi, the MiG bases, the SAM sites under construction, Haiphong's city and the 30-mile and 10-mile circles in which history let it build, store and train. It denies the repair cycle its respite, because each repair is answered when the cell judges it done, not at the next weekly approval. It denies the pauses, the six days of May 1965 and the 37 days of Christmas 1965¹, which the North used historically to repair, resupply and move forces south. And it denies Hanoi the reading that the war's escalation is a dial in Washington's hand that turns back whenever the President is pressed.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The authority is the change the theater asked for throughout the war, and it buys what was asked for. A campaign against the North's fixed targets is fought as a campaign: targets chosen in the theater, struck when ready, restruck when repaired, without sanctuary and without pause. With the opening strike it makes the North's air defence a lesser and later threat, saves aircraft and crews, and keeps the bridges, power and rail of the North down for as long as Washington allows. It does not buy the end of the North's war in the South. Haiphong's cargo keeps coming by lighter after the port's one strike, and the Chinese rail keeps coming through the buffer. The Trail runs on porters, trucks and fords that the record has already shown no bombing stops. The halt that ends the campaign stays the President's, and removing the pauses spends his tolerance faster. The theater gets the campaign it wanted. The record will show whether that campaign was ever the war's decisive instrument, and history's 1972 suggests it was decisive only for the terms, not for the outcome.
