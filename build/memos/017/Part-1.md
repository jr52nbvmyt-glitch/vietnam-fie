# Memorandum 017 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


## §1.1 US Army Intelligence Support Activity (USAISA, "the Activity", Task Force Orange), 2026 configuration, attached to MACV-JSOC — P017a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The Activity is the unit that finds people for the men who take them, and it has been given to me whole. That is the right way to give it. Delta arrived in this theater a year ago with the hands to take a province committee secretary from his bed and almost no way of learning which bed. The Activity is the other half of that instrument.

**Designation.** United States Army Intelligence Support Activity (USAISA), known in the service as "the Activity" and inside the Joint Special Operations Command as Task Force Orange, and carried under a succession of cover names and special-access programme names: Field Operations Group, Tactical Concept Activity, Mission Support Activity, Gray Fox, Centra Spike, Torn Victor and others, changed about every two years after 1989¹. The unit's real 2026 name and organisation are classified. What follows is built from the open record and is marked as estimate wherever it goes beyond it.

**Origin.** The Field Operations Group was formed in the summer of 1980 under Colonel Jerry King, after the failure at Desert One, to prepare a second attempt to rescue the hostages in Tehran; it was made permanent as USAISA on 3 March 1981, subordinate at first to Army Intelligence and Security Command, and transferred to JSOC in 2003. It was created to fill what the Army's own review called "a serious and persistent information deficiency": the special operations force of 1980 could not find out, on the ground and in time, what it needed to know about its target². It began with about 50 people and passed 100 within its first year¹.

**Mission in this theater, as stipulated.** The Activity is committed undivided to the war from 1 September 1964 and attached to the MACV Joint Special Operations Command, the headquarters formed on 15 August 1964 that already commands Delta. Its first task is finding the targets Delta and the headquarters act on: the senior cadre of the Front and of the Party in the South, COSVN and its sections, the base areas, and the camps where prisoners are held. Its second task is feeding the Combined Joint Interagency Task Force against the infrastructure, whose files drive the attack on the district and village apparatus³. MACV J-2 receives its work on request and does not task it. It crosses into Laos, Cambodia or the North only where and when history's forces did: Laos from October 1965 within the ambassador's limits, Cambodia from September 1967 on foot reconnaissance, and agent operations into the North on the terms SOG and the CIA had⁴⁵.

**What comes with it, and what does not.** The whole unit at full strength: its operations squadron for clandestine human intelligence and close-target reconnaissance, its signals intelligence squadron, its mission support and communications squadron, its analysis, medical and logistics detachments, and its own small fleet of manned special-mission aircraft¹. It brings no 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment, no dedicated JSOC drone wing, and no national collection systems beyond what the standing condition on space constellations already gives every provision built to use it: GPS and military satellite communications⁵. That matches the terms on which Delta came⁴.

**The interpreters.** I read the user's grant as eight people, heritage speakers of Vietnamese holding Top Secret/SCI clearances, attached to the Activity from its first day as members of the unit and not as a separate formation⁵. They are the only people in the theater who are at once fluent in Vietnamese, cleared to the Activity's compartments and owed by no Vietnamese service. Beyond them the Activity works, as every American intelligence unit of 1964 did, through Vietnamese interpreters supplied by the Republic and through the Army Security Agency's Vietnamese linguists trained at Monterey. Eight is a core, not a section. I come back to what that means in D2, D10 and D11.

**Quantity and organisation in theater.** About 360–440 people, plus the eight interpreters⁶; the 2026 figure is classified]. I estimate the organisation as follows.

| Element | Strength | What it does | Where it sits in Vietnam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headquarters and analysis detachment | 40–65⁷ | Targeting, network analysis, fusion with the task force and J-2 | Saigon, beside MACV-JSOC |
| Operations Squadron | 120–140⁸ | Recruiting and running sources, surveillance in the towns, close-target reconnaissance before a raid | Saigon, Da Nang, Nha Trang, Bien Hoa, Can Tho |
| Signals Intelligence Squadron | 100–115⁹ | Intercept, direction finding and geolocation; teams that go forward with Delta | Tan Son Nhut, with teams at the four corps-area task groups³ |
| Mission Support and Communications Squadron | 65–75¹⁰ | The unit's own secure networks, technical surveillance devices, cover documents, vehicles | Saigon |
| Aviation element | 35–45¹¹ | Airborne signals collection and imaging over the South and, from October 1965, the Laotian panhandle | Tan Son Nhut and Nha Trang |
| Eight heritage interpreters | 8⁵ | Translation of the most sensitive sources and intercepts; vetting of Vietnamese interpreters | Saigon, deployed forward in pairs |

**Aircraft.** The Activity's air arm has flown modified Beechcraft King Airs since its first campaign: Seaspray's King Air 100s carried its collection over El Salvador and Honduras from 1982 in an operation planned for one month that ran more than three years¹. I take the 2026 fleet as four to six King Air 350-class twin turboprops fitted for signals collection and geolocation, with an electro-optical and infrared turret¹² and the Army's King Air-based special electronic mission aircraft of the 2010s and 2020s]. These are manned aircraft with Pratt & Whitney PT6A engines, not drones.

**In-service date.** USAISA from 3 March 1981¹; the 2026 unit is the reference configuration⁵. The real-world date is information, not permission: nothing acts before 1 September 1964.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The Activity is small, old in its people and impossible to refill quickly. That is the single most important fact about its complement.

**Complement.** About 360–440 people and the eight interpreters¹³. Each aircraft flies with two pilots and two to three collection operators¹⁴, so the aviation element can crew six to eight sorties a day when every aircraft is up and about four to five a day as a sustained rate. The Operations Squadron fields at any one time about 25–35 teams of two to four people¹⁵. The Signals Intelligence Squadron puts about 10–14 collection teams on the ground and keeps two airborne crews' worth of linguists and analysts per sortie¹⁰.

**The people.** Most of the operators come from Army Special Forces, many from diverse cultural backgrounds that help them blend into foreign settings, and every candidate passes an assessment and selection course, a background investigation and psychological testing before a specialised course in surveillance, tradecraft, infiltration, advanced air operations, driving and signals intelligence². Foreign languages are not required on entry but are required for field work, and the unit's languages in 2026 are those of its wars: Arabic, Persian, Pashto and the like¹. Almost nobody in it speaks Vietnamese.

**Language, as granted.** The eight heritage interpreters are the unit's whole cleared Vietnamese capacity on arrival⁵. Working in pairs for continuity and their own safety, they can cover four tasks at once around the clock, or about eight in daytime. Everything else in Vietnamese passes through South Vietnamese interpreters from the RVNAF and the Central Intelligence Organization, or through the Army Security Agency's own Vietnamese linguists, who are already committed to the 3d Radio Research Unit's intercept¹⁶. A Vietnamese course at Monterey of about 47 weeks¹⁷ means that Americans the Activity sends to learn the language in September 1964 return in the autumn of 1965 at a working level, and only a few of them are ever good enough to run a source alone.

**Personnel spaces.** About 370–450 spaces in the Republic¹⁸. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so they count against nothing. They still cost. The Activity's annual intake in 2026 is small, perhaps 25–35 selected people a year¹⁹, and a unit committed whole to one theater has no rotation base behind it. Delta's record in this war is the warning: committed whole for eleven years, it held its headcount and spent its experience, and about a quarter of its operators by the end of 1967 had been selected after the war began⁴. The Activity's experience is harder to replace than Delta's, because a case officer's value lies in sources he has recruited and who trust him, and those do not transfer to his replacement.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The Activity is not armed for a fight, and its payload is its collection equipment and its people. Read as an institutional provision, this field also covers its instruments and authorities.

**Personal weapons.** Concealable pistols and compact carbines for self-protection: in this record every American handgun is the Glock 21 with tritium night sights and a weapon light from 1964²⁰, and the Activity's surveillance teams carry a smaller pistol of the Glock 19 class²¹, with M4A1 carbines of the record's configuration in vehicles and on close-target reconnaissance²². Its people break contact; they do not hold ground.

**Collection payload, airborne.** Each aircraft carries wideband digital intercept receivers covering the high-frequency, VHF and UHF bands, an interferometric direction-finding and geolocation system, an on-board processing station, and an electro-optical and infrared turret with a laser pointer²³. Two or more aircraft working together can geolocate a single transmission by time and frequency difference of arrival¹⁰.

**Collection payload, ground.** Manpack and vehicle intercept and direction-finding kits; covert cameras and video, including long-lens and thermal surveillance equipment; technical surveillance devices for rooms and vehicles; GPS tracking devices that report by military satellite where they are built to; and the document and media exploitation kits of 2026²⁴. The tracking devices work only where they can report through the satellite channels the space condition provides; a 2026 tracker built to report through a commercial cellular network reports nothing in a country without one.

**Instruments and authorities.** The Activity brings no authority of its own to arrest, detain or interrogate in the South. Its findings become arrests through the task force's police-led teams and warrants under the 1964 police statute³²⁵, or raids through MACV-JSOC and Delta⁴. It runs sources, but clandestine human intelligence by a Defense Department unit in 1964 has to be coordinated with the CIA station, which in this period ran the Republic's own Central Intelligence Organization advisory effort and much of the agent work into the North²⁶. It has a budget for paying sources and maintaining cover; its first budget, in 1981, was $7 million¹. It can compel nothing. It can only find.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

Read for this provision as its information inputs: signals, imagery from its own aircraft, and people. Signals intelligence is indifferent to canopy and to night, which is why it matters here, and dependent entirely on the enemy choosing to transmit, which is why it matters less than a reader of the 2026 record would expect.

**Signals: what there is to hear.** The Activity's signature successes since 2001 came from an enemy who carried telephones. This enemy carries letters. In 1964 the Front and the Party in the South ran their higher command on manual Morse radio nets from COSVN to the regions and to Hanoi, and from regions to provinces and the main-force regiments, with codes and short schedules; below that, most traffic went by courier, by wire and by word of mouth. Battalions and companies seldom used radio at all except in battle. So the Activity's ears reach the top of the enemy's command and his main-force headquarters, and they do not reach the hamlet, the district committee or the battalion moving under canopy. That is not a failure of the equipment. There is nothing in the air to collect.

**Signals: what the equipment does with it.** Against a high-frequency Morse transmitter of a few watts to a few tens of watts, an aircraft at 18,000–22,000 feet can intercept within about 100–150 kilometres line of sight, and somewhat beyond by ground and sky wave²⁷. A single aircraft's interferometer fixes a transmitter to roughly 100–300 metres on a transmission of a minute or two; two aircraft working together can bring it to about 30–80 metres²⁸. History's airborne direction finding, which began in Vietnam in March 1962 with Army U-6A Beavers carrying the set that became the AN/ARD-15, typically gave a fix good to several hundred metres to a kilometre²⁹. Ground direction-finding teams with the AN/PRD-1 had to close to within 5–15 miles of a target to be of use¹⁶.

**Under triple canopy.** Radio passes through canopy with some attenuation, and the fix is as good under the trees as in the open¹⁰. The aircraft's own camera and infrared turret see nothing under triple canopy. The fix therefore gives a point, not a picture: a coordinate where a transmitter was for two minutes, under trees that hide whether it is a headquarters of 300 or a radio squad of six on a hilltop a kilometre from the headquarters it serves.

**At night.** Night changes nothing for the receivers. The enemy's schedules ran day and night. The aircraft can fly at night; the people on the ground move more slowly and less safely in the dark outside the held belt.

**In monsoon.** The aircraft fly in rain and above most cloud, but the monsoon's thunderstorms close the airfields and the low ceilings ground the turret's imagery. High-frequency propagation varies with time of day and season, and the static of the southwest monsoon's storms degrades intercept of weak transmitters¹⁰. I estimate the airborne collection falls by about a quarter in the worst monsoon months³⁰.

**People.** The Operations Squadron's inputs are sources: officials, merchants, drivers, boatmen, prostitutes, prisoners, defectors, the relatives of cadre. They can be recruited and met only where an American, or his Vietnamese cut-out, can go and come back: the cities, the provincial towns, the district towns, the market villages of the held belt¹⁰. They cannot be met in a contested hamlet at night, and the source who lives there is in the hamlet the Front holds after dark. In the countryside the Activity hears the Front only through the people the Front permits to travel.

**Close-target reconnaissance.** The squadron's reconnaissance before a raid uses cameras, thermal sights and observers in place, with the night sights of 2026¹⁰. That is a seeing instrument at a known address, over a few hundred metres. It does not search.

### D5 · Performance

Read as throughput and capacity. The Activity's output is located, identified targets, and its capacity is set by its people, not its equipment.

**Airborne collection.** Four to six aircraft at about 70–80% availability, flying about four to five sorties a day of 5–7 hours each, give about 120–150 sorties a month sustained³¹. Against a disciplined enemy transmitting on short schedules, I expect about two to three useful fixes on identified stations per sortie, or about 250–400 a month at maturity³², scaled for the enemy's larger nets by 1965 and modern receivers]. That is a modest addition in volume to history's own airborne direction finding, which had 30 Army aircraft by 1965 and 41 more ordered after Starlight¹⁶. Its margin is precision and speed: a fix to 30–80 metres in the hands of the targeter 10–20 minutes after the transmission ends, where history's ARDF report took hours to reach a user³³].

**Human intelligence.** Recruited, vetted sources run by the Activity: about 40–60 by the end of 1964, 120–150 by the end of 1965 and 200–240 by the end of 1967³⁴. Of these, about a fifth report on the senior apparatus at province level or above¹⁰.

**Targets.** At maturity, from 1966, about 15–25 complete target packages a month to MACV-JSOC, each a named person, a place and a time window confirmed by at least two independent sources or a source and a signal³⁵. About 20–30 close-target reconnaissances a month before raids. About 30–50 leads a month to the task force's corps-area groups on district-level cadre¹⁰.

**Speed.** From a confirmed sighting to a target package at MACV-JSOC: 1–3 hours inside the South¹⁰. The raid force then needs 2–6 hours from the decision to be on target⁴.

**Protection.** None in any armoured sense. The aircraft are unarmoured civil twins. The people work in civilian clothes, with beards and cover², and survive by not being noticed.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The Activity talks to its own people and to MACV-JSOC on networks the enemy cannot read, and to the Vietnamese only through channels it deliberately keeps narrow. That narrowness is a design choice with a cost.

**Architecture.** Each team carries 2026 multiband radios of the AN/PRC-117G and AN/PRC-163 classes with their own encryption, and satellite terminals on the military constellations the space condition provides³⁶⁵. The aircraft pass fixes and imagery by encrypted datalink to the ground station at Tan Son Nhut and, through it, to MACV-JSOC and Delta's operations centre¹⁰. Nothing the Activity transmits can be read by the enemy's intercept service of 1964–75.

**The enemy listens.** He did listen, and very well. On 20 December 1969 the 1st Infantry Division captured twelve members of an enemy communications-reconnaissance unit in Binh Duong with intercept equipment and thousands of documents, which showed a large and competent network intercepting and analysing allied communications throughout the South³⁷. The American manpack radio of the period, the AN/PRC-25, could not take the scrambler that worked on the vehicle AN/VRC-46, so forward observers and infantry talked in the clear³⁸. The Activity's own traffic is safe. The traffic of every unit that acts on its findings is not. A raid planned over Activity channels is betrayed the moment a supporting company calls its helicopters on an open net. The KY-38 NESTOR secure voice set that could ride on the AN/PRC-77 helps where it is issued and is not issued to every company.

**Talking to the ARVN.** The Activity can speak to the RVNAF and the National Police only through the eight interpreters, through Vietnamese interpreters it does not fully trust, and through the task force's combined fusion centre³. By design it shares product, not sources: a Vietnamese officer receives "a cadre of the province committee will be at this house tonight", never the name of the woman who said so. This is the same compartmentation that cost Delta's headquarters a little advisory leverage when it gave ARVN corps restricted areas without reasons, and it will cost the Activity more, because its whole product is information the Vietnamese would like to see.

### D7 · Domain conditions

Read as reach and security preconditions, with the air conditions for the aircraft. The Activity reaches what Americans can reach safely, and its aircraft reach what they can overfly above the guns.

**Air: basing.** A King Air 350-class aircraft needs about 1,000–1,200 metres of paved runway at operating weight in tropical heat³⁹, which Tan Son Nhut, Bien Hoa, Nha Trang, Da Nang, Pleiku and Binh Thuy all have. The aircraft are valuable, few and soft. They need revetments and guarding at the airfields, which the enemy's mortar and sapper attacks on the airfields reached from the attack on Bien Hoa in November 1964 onward. Losing two on the ground in one night would cut the airborne effort by a third or more.

**Air: survivability against the threat.** Over the South the threat is small arms and 12.7mm machine guns, effective to about 1,500 metres slant range⁴⁰. Above about 15,000 feet the aircraft are safe from it. Over the Laotian panhandle, from October 1965, 37mm and 57mm guns reach effectively to about 3,000–5,000 metres⁴¹, and the Activity's aircraft stay above 20,000 feet there and accept the loss in intercept range on the weakest stations. They do not fly over the North, where the SA-2 and the MiG-21 make an unarmed twin turboprop a loss waiting for a date.

**Ground: the people.** The Operations Squadron's teams can move in Saigon, the provincial capitals, the district towns and the main roads by day. Americans cannot blend into a Vietnamese hamlet at any hour. A team that goes into the countryside goes as an American visitor with an escort, which announces it, or goes by night on close-target reconnaissance behind Delta's security, which makes it part of a raid and not a clandestine collector. The eight interpreters can pass in a town crowd and not in a hamlet, where everyone knows everyone and their Vietnamese is that of the diaspora of 2026, with its southern accent, its own vocabulary and none of the Party's jargon of 1964¹⁰.

**The held belt.** In this record the government holds the hamlets at night in a belt that grew from the edges of the towns and the roads, densest in III and IV Corps³⁴. The Activity's human intelligence inherits that geography exactly as Delta did: sources are recruitable in proportion to the government's presence after dark.

**Monsoon.** The northeast monsoon over I and II Corps from October to March and the southwest monsoon over the south and the Highlands from May to October each cost the aircraft a quarter or so of their effort in the worst months (D4), and turn the roads the ground teams use into mud¹⁰.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The Activity's footprint is light in tons and heavy in things nobody in 1964 can make.

**Fuel.** The PT6A turboprops burn jet fuel, which the theater has¹⁰. Four to six aircraft flying 120–150 sorties a month burn about 300–450 tons a month⁴². That is lost in the theater's fuel flow.

**Spares and electronics.** Everything electronic in the unit is 2026 equipment: receivers, processing stations, computers, satellite terminals, cameras, tracking devices, radios, encryption. None of it can be repaired below board level in 1964, and the components cannot be manufactured anywhere in the world before the 1990s or later¹⁰. The Republic's electronics base of memorandum 008 makes radios, dry cells and field wire of the 1960s, and nothing the Activity's equipment needs⁴³. The unit therefore lives on the stock it brings. I assume, as for Delta, about two to three years of wastage in that stock⁴, so that the electronic edge thins from 1966–67 and is husbanded after that, by cannibalising aircraft and sets to keep the best of them working.

**Batteries.** The 2026 radios, cameras and devices run on lithium cells of the BA-5590 class and on rechargeable packs¹⁰. The record's dry-cell works makes zinc-carbon cells⁴³. Charging is possible wherever there is a generator; replacement cells are not.

**Specialist personnel.** Aircraft and avionics technicians, collection engineers and the unit's own technicians are part of its strength (D1). The RVNAF can supply none of them.

**Money.** Source payments, cover, safe houses and vehicles in piastres, bought at the official rate or on the market, at perhaps $2–4 million a year in 1964 prices⁴⁴]. In an economy where the black-market piastre and American money distort everything they touch, a source network paid in dollars is itself a corruption risk.

**Air-conditioning and power.** The processing stations and the aircraft's equipment need cooled, stable power in a humid tropical climate; the theater's generators and its air-conditioning in 1964 are thin¹⁰. This is a small thing that grounds sets.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The Activity cannot be handed to the Vietnamese. Some of what it knows how to do can be taught to them, slowly, and that is worth doing.

**The equipment.** Nothing electronic in the unit can be transferred. It cannot be maintained by anyone in the Republic, it would be in the enemy's hands within weeks of any defeat, and its methods are among the most closely held in the United States (D8).

**The tradecraft and the method.** The analytic method of 2026, the habit of building a target from two independent sources, and of following a network from its couriers to its leaders, can be taught to the Special Branch of the National Police, the Military Security Service and the Central Intelligence Organization over three to five years⁴⁵. The task force's combined fusion centre is already the classroom for it³, and the Phung Hoang centres are already its customers⁴⁶. A Vietnamese Activity of the Republic's own, perhaps 150–250 people trained from 1967 and independent from 1971–72¹⁰, is a plausible Vietnamization goal, with Vietnamese collection gear of the period, its own sources and its own language, which it would not need to borrow.

**What happens if it is transferred.** The same thing that happened to the national Phung Hoang file in this war: the more accurate it is, the more it is worth to whoever holds Saigon at the end. A Vietnamese source network handed over in 1972 is a list of names in 1975. The honest timeline for transfer is that the method goes in 1969–72, the sources stay with the Americans who recruited them or are retired, and the equipment goes home.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The Activity performs where the enemy talks and where Americans can walk, and it is nearly useless where neither is true.

**Where it performs to specification.**
- Against the enemy's higher command nets: COSVN to the regions and to Hanoi, the regions to the provinces, and the main-force division and regiment headquarters in the field. Each fix is a point on the map within two minutes of a transmission, at any hour and under any canopy (D4).
- In the cities and provincial towns, where the senior cadre of the urban apparatus live under cover, and where couriers from the base areas end their journeys.
- In the held belt, where officials, traders and families of cadre can be recruited and met.
- Before a raid, at a known address, where close-target reconnaissance confirms the target is present and counts the guards.

Consider a district committee secretary in Duc Hue in early 1966. He sleeps in three hamlets in rotation and comes to the district town twice a month to meet a merchant who carries the committee's funds. The task force's file has his name and photograph but not his beds³. The Activity recruits the merchant's driver through a cousin in Bien Hoa. It learns which night the meeting falls on, watches the house with a long lens from a rented room across the canal, and hands MACV-JSOC a package with a time window of six hours. Delta goes in at 0200 and the secretary is in the bed. Nothing in that chain was beyond 1964 except the speed and the care with which it was built. That is the instrument at its best.

**Where performance collapses.**
- **In the contested hamlet at night.** No source can be met there, no American can enter it unannounced, and the enemy who sleeps there does not transmit.
- **Against the courier system.** Most of the Front's traffic never touches the air (D4). The Activity can follow a courier only after a source has named him.
- **Against the main force under canopy.** A regiment's radio station fixes the headquarters, often on a hilltop away from it, not the battalions. The fix is a starting point for reconnaissance, not a target for a B-52J.
- **Against a disciplined enemy who learns.** Within a season of the first fixes being struck, the enemy will move his transmitters away from his headquarters, shorten his schedules and go silent before operations, as he did against history's direction finding⁴⁷. The yield per sortie will fall by half or more from its first months.
- **In the worst monsoon months**, about a quarter of the airborne effort is lost (D4).
- **Beyond the borders**, the aircraft can hear COSVN in Cambodia, but nothing may act on what they hear on the ground until September 1967, and then only on foot, or from the air until the bombing of the Cambodian base areas history began in March 1969⁴⁵.

**Tempo over weeks.** A source network is a slow asset that a fast tempo destroys. Every raid that follows a source's report tells the enemy that somebody near the target talked, and every arrest narrows the circle of suspects. A unit pushed to produce 25 packages a month will burn its best sources within a year; one held to 15 keeps them¹⁰. The sustainable rate is the lower one, and I will hold the Activity to it.

### D11 · Failure modes

The Activity fails quietly. Its typical failure is not a shot-down aircraft but a source who has been working for the other side for a year.

**First to break: the Vietnamese channel.** The Activity's eight cleared interpreters are its only Vietnamese hands outside the Republic's services. Every other interpreter, liaison officer and Special Branch partner is drawn from services the enemy had penetrated deeply. The enemy will feed the Activity false sources through them, as he fed history's agent programmes into the North, more than three-quarters of whose agents were captured and many turned⁴. A turned source is worse than none, because the raid force acts on his word. The defence is the second source, the signal that confirms the human report, and the eight interpreters checking the work of the others. That defence is thin at eight.

**The enemy's own deception.** An enemy who knows his radios are being fixed can use them to lie: a transmitter left in an empty bunker, a net that keeps its schedule after the headquarters has moved, a dummy station on a hilltop to draw a strike⁴⁸]. History's direction finding was fooled this way. A 2026 analyst used to telephones will be fooled at first too.

**The leak at the point of action.** The raid force and the task force act through units whose radios the enemy reads (D6)³⁸. Partial failure looks like a raid that arrives at the right house an hour after the target left.

**Loss of aircraft.** The loss of one aircraft costs a fifth to a quarter of the airborne effort, and there are no replacements of the same equipment (D8). The fleet can survive the loss of two before the airborne collection becomes a once-a-day effort¹⁰.

**Loss of people.** A captured case officer or interpreter is a catastrophe beyond his own fate: he knows sources, methods and capabilities that nothing else in this war reveals. The eight interpreters are the highest-value people in the unit to the enemy and the hardest to protect, because they go where the Vietnamese conversation is. Their capture would compromise every source they handled.

**During the window when it is unavailable.** If the Activity is blinded, by a leak that burns its network or by spares that ground its aircraft, the force falls back on what it had before: the task force's files, history's ARDF, the U-2S signals payload and Global Hawk tracks⁴⁹, and Delta's own reconnaissance⁴. Delta then finds very little itself, and its yield falls to what the task force can hand it.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The Activity's curve is the reverse of a weapon's. Its signals collection is at its best in the first months and declines; its human intelligence starts at nothing and grows for two years.

| Measure | Month 1 (Sep 1964) | Month 12 (Sep 1965) | Month 36 (Sep 1967) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aircraft mission-capable rate | 80–85% | 75–80% | 65–70%⁵⁰ |
| Useful fixes per sortie | 3–4 | 2–3 | 1–2⁵¹ |
| Recruited, vetted sources | 0–10 | 100–120 | 200–240 |
| Americans able to work in Vietnamese | 8⁵ | 8 plus the first 10–15 from Monterey | 8 plus 30–40 |
| Target packages a month to MACV-JSOC | 2–4 | 12–18 | 15–25, held to the low end to protect sources |
| Experienced operators (selected before the war) | 100% | 90–95%¹⁰ | 70–75%⁵² |

**What declines first.** The enemy's radio, as a target. The first months will produce fixes on stations that have never been fixed so precisely or so fast, and some will be struck. The enemy will notice within a season and change his practice, as his engineers on the Trail answered the B-52J in one season in this record. The second decline is the equipment, from 1966–67. The third is the experience of the people. The human intelligence grows until the senior targets it reports on have left for Cambodia, which in this record the raid force's success had largely accomplished by 1967.

**The margin, stated.** Against history's airborne direction finding, the Activity's fix is about five to ten times more precise and its delivery about ten times faster⁵⁷; in volume it adds perhaps a fifth to a quarter to what ARDF already produced by 1966¹⁰. Against Delta's own reconnaissance, which measured 134 coordinates under canopy in 1966–67 in this record, the Activity's close-target reconnaissance adds the confirmation that a named man is present, which a coordinate does not. Against the task force, it adds the senior target the district files cannot reach: by my estimate about 80–100 more cadre at district level or above taken a year by 1967, and about twice that in 1965–66⁵⁸.

### D14 · Negative specification
The Activity cannot hear what is not transmitted, and most of this enemy's command never touches the air: below COSVN, the regions and the main-force headquarters, the Front runs on couriers, wire and word of mouth. Its 2026 craft was built against telephones; there are none here. It brings no national code-breaking: it locates and characterises transmitters, and reads only what the Army Security Agency or its own linguists can read. A fix locates a radio, often on a hilltop away from the headquarters it serves; it never locates the battalions of a regiment under canopy. Its aircraft cannot see under triple canopy, cannot fly over the North, and stay above 20,000 feet over Laos⁵⁶. Its Americans cannot blend into a Vietnamese hamlet, and its eight heritage interpreters speak the Vietnamese of 2026, not the Party's⁵¹⁰. It has no authority to arrest, detain, interrogate or strike; it finds, and others act³. It cannot cross a border before history's forces did, and nothing it hears in Cambodia can be acted on until 1967 on foot and 1969 from the air⁴. Its electronics cannot be repaired or replaced in this war: two to three years of stock⁵⁹. It cannot be handed to the ARVN except as method (D9). And it is small: about 120–140 collectors, refilled at 25–35 a year⁵².

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy's senior command the free use of radio. From late 1964 every transmission from a COSVN section, a region or a main-force headquarters can be fixed to 30–80 metres within minutes⁵⁴, so the enemy must move his transmitters away from his commanders, shorten his schedules and fall silent before operations: slower command at every level that used radio. It denies the senior cadre in the towns and the held belt the safety of a cover identity, and it denies them the assumption that a raid needs months of file work: a package can be built in weeks. It denies COSVN in Cambodia the certainty that it is unlocated, though not that it is unreachable before 1969. It does not deny the enemy his couriers, his main force under canopy, his sanctuaries or his hamlets at night.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The Activity is the finder the raid force lacked, and that matters. Delta could take a man from a guarded house at night; what it could not do was learn which house. From late 1964 a small unit of about 400 recruits the drivers, merchants and relatives around the Front's senior cadre in the towns and the held belt, confirms what they say against the enemy's own radio, and gives MACV-JSOC a name, a place and a six-hour window, 15–25 times a month at maturity⁵⁵. That is perhaps 80–100 more senior cadre taken a year by 1967 and about twice that in 1965–66, which hastens the displacement of the Front's senior apparatus into Cambodia that the raid force had already begun. Its airborne fixes make history's direction finding five to ten times more precise and ten times faster, and that adds a point or two to the share of main-force contacts begun on a located unit¹⁰. It hears COSVN in Cambodia, which nothing else in the record does well, and that will matter when the bombing of the base areas opens in 1969. It is not a transformation. A unit built for an enemy who carries telephones meets an enemy who carries letters, and the war is decided in hamlets it cannot enter, by a main force it cannot see, from sanctuaries it can only listen to. The Activity makes the scalpel sharper and more accurate. It does not make it bigger.
