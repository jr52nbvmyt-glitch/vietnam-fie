# Memorandum 015 — Part 2 of 3 · §2 Integration and second-order effects · §3 Changes to existing forces


The gunships join the war at its most neglected seam. For fourteen memoranda this headquarters has built the post — the land under it, the chief above it, the rifle, the radio, the trained private and the schooled sergeant inside it — and every one of those memoranda has ended at the same sentence: the post can call, and the answer is the weak link. The record put it in the gap register in so many words. Air power in this war has been decisive against the measured siege and the fixed crossing and nothing against an attack on a post at night. The Ghostrider is that answer, from the first night of 1964, and the Spooky II is the first part of it the Republic will own.

That is the headline, and it is first-order. What follows in this section is what the answer then causes: where the Front takes the night attack when the night over the post is no longer its own, what the answer costs the hamlet outside the wire, and what it teaches an army that will have to fight the last two years of the war with a third of it.

## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

**The Ghostrider joins STRIKE, FIX, SECURE and INTERDICT.** In STRIKE it is a new class of fire: neither artillery, which is tied to its range fan, nor tactical air, which comes in the morning or after a 30–60-minute scramble, but a gun that is already overhead. In SECURE it is the reaction that arrives in the dark, the third condition of night control that POSTNA-2 named and that no provision has supplied. In FIX it holds the enemy who attacks at night where he is, under fire, until the morning brings the infantry. In INTERDICT it is a hunter for the trucks the radar of memorandum 003 sees and the strike force of 1965 could not reach.

**The Spooky II joins STRIKE, SECURE and, above all, ADVISE.** It is the first instrument of the air war in this record that the Republic will own. Every provision before it that worked in the air — the U-2S, the Global Hawk, the B-52J, the JDAM, the coordinate cell — went home in 1973, and the meta-checkpoint's finding was that the rung would not move until something the Republic owned did. The U is not that thing on its own. It is the first piece of it.

**It makes newly viable** three things the force could not do: hold an isolated post through a whole night without a relief on the road; cover a relief column from above as it moves; and kill a truck on a track the Global Hawk hands off within minutes rather than the forty minutes that STEADY COUNT showed the strike force needed.

**F015-1** **The post's call now meets an answer.** Memorandum 008 put a radio at every post on the district net, and the Chuong Thien logs of 1966 showed what the radio found: of 23 posts lost from May to August, 19 had called within ten minutes, and the answer was "no company to send, and a battery whose guns reached nine posts in forty-one"¹. The radio moved the post's weakness from the call to the answer. This provision supplies the answer, for every post within the gunship net, from 1964. On a clear night a post in Dinh Tuong that calls at 2237 has a Ghostrider over it by about 2300, and the company forming on the dike to the west is under 105mm fire before it has crossed the paddy². The radio of P008b, the district chief of P002b who answers it, and the Ghostrider together complete the post's chain for the first time in the war: it can call, someone answers, and the answer arrives in the dark. Each piece alone did little; the three together are the condition POSTNA-2 set.

**F015-2** **The radar's track meets a gun already in the air.** In STEADY COUNT, from December 1965, the Global Hawk produced about 1,900 tracks and the strike force reached one in six within forty minutes³. The binding constraint was no longer seeing the truck but having something overhead when the track was fresh. A Ghostrider squadron at Ubon, flying eight to ten sorties a night over the panhandle with a Global Hawk's tracks passed by satellite link to the aircraft, can put a gun on perhaps one track in two or three within ten to twenty minutes. That is a three- to four-fold increase in trucks engaged against the radar's honest count, and it costs Group 559 several hundred trucks a dry season more than history's Commando Hunt cost it at the same date². It reduces the tonnage arriving by much less.

**F015-3** **The siege now has a night shift.** The B-52J's cells, with the GBU-38 on measured trenches, made the measured siege the enemy's worst battle in this war, at Con Thien, Khe Sanh, An Loc and Kontum⁴. They struck at intervals of hours. A Ghostrider over the same siege works between the cells: it kills the sapper teams cutting the wire, the digging parties on the trench heads and the mortar crews firing from the treeline, minute by minute, at night, at 50–200 metres from the defenders². The two together leave the besieger no safe hour. The enemy had already stopped laying measured sieges to American positions after Khe Sanh; this makes the decision firmer and moves his sieges of 1972 to cloud and to the air defences that keep the gunship off.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

The gunships do not find the main force. A PAVN regiment under the canopy of the Chu Pong or War Zone C is as invisible to the MX-25 and the APQ-180 as to every sensor in the record. What changes is the last link of the chain, and only on the enemy's initiative. When a main-force unit attacks at night — a camp, a district town, a battalion's night position — it reveals itself, and for the first time in the war the allies have something that can fight it in the dark from the first quarter-hour. That moves the chain from find–fix–strike to reveal–hold–kill: the enemy finds himself, and the gunship holds him there under fire until the infantry and the guns can come.

It is a real change in the fights the enemy accepts at night, and it does not touch the fights he declines. The main force's control of whether to fight is unchanged. I expect him to accept fewer night attacks on covered positions in clear weather and to move the rest into cloud, into places the net does not cover, and into the day. The gunship's main-force effect is therefore what the enemy stops doing, which the metrics will show as quiet.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

Nothing. A gunship cannot tell the district committee's finance cadre from the farmer, and it does not try. Its one effect on the VCI chain is indirect: a post that holds keeps the village's police post and its Special Branch file alive, and the cadre the task force of P012b and the PIOCC of P006a would lose to a fallen post they keep⁵. That is a gain to the infrastructure war measured in files not burned, and I will not claim more for it. Nor will I claim that a gunship's kills at a post are VCI: the men who assault a post at night are guerrillas and local force, not the cadre who sent them.

### Dependence on SECURE

Everything the gunships do in the countryside depends on the post being there and manned. A gunship can save a post that holds its wall for the first fifteen to thirty minutes; it cannot save one the platoon has abandoned or sold. That makes every earlier provision that keeps the platoon in its post at night — the land of P001a, the chief of P002b, the rifle of P004a, the training of P009c and P010b, the radio of P008b — the gunship's precondition, and in turn makes the gunship the reason those provisions now pay at night. The territorial post, built over fourteen memoranda, now has its last missing piece.

What the gunship does not do is hold the hamlet. POS008-4 recorded that from 1967 the post's first call was for fire rather than men, and that this kept the platoon behind its wire, so that the post held better and the hamlet outside did not. The gunship makes that rule cheaper to follow and the post safer still behind it. The night belongs to the hamlet's people only where the PF go out from the wire into it, and the gunship gives them no reason to.

The regions differ. In **the Delta** the gunship works best: open paddy, little canopy, the south-west monsoon's cloud mostly afternoon convection that clears by midnight, posts at intervals of a few kilometres. In **the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps** the north-east monsoon from October to March closes the sky for days, and the Ghostrider is blind over Quang Ngai and Binh Dinh in exactly the season the Front fights there; the Spooky II's radar is the only gunship fire on those nights after 1967. In **the Highlands** the canopy hides the attacker until he is at the wire and the Montagnard camps and villages are far from the bases; coverage is thinner and the arrival slower. In **the Saigon belt** coverage is densest and clearance hardest, because the hamlets are crowded and the province chiefs closest to the palace.

### The ADVISE cluster

The Ghostrider feeds the ADVISE cluster nothing and draws on it. It is American, it talks to Americans, and an ARVN post that calls it does so through an American adviser or through the combined net of P012a. Like the B-52J's siege box, it makes the Republic's territorial forces stronger while the Americans are there and teaches them to rely on a weapon that will leave.

The Spooky II is the opposite, and it is the most important ADVISE provision in the record since the officer reforms of memorandum 007. Its crews, its maintainers and its squadrons are Vietnamese; it answers a Vietnamese call on a Vietnamese net; and the habit of calling fire from the sky, which the Ghostrider teaches the posts from 1964, has from 1969 a Vietnamese aircraft at the other end. POS010-4 found that the American NCO course carried the habit of fixing the enemy and calling fire down to the ARVN squad, a strength the Republic owned only where the gun at the other end was Vietnamese. From 1969 at night, it is.

**F015-4** **The first instrument of the air war the Republic owns.** The meta-checkpoint held rung (a) on its mechanism: every provision so far was American-owned and withdrawn by 1973, or worked below the level of command at which 1972 and 1975 were decided. The U is owned, and it stays. It works below the level of command that decided 1975 — a gunship does not choose whether the palace abandons the Highlands — but it works at the level of the territorial war of 1973–74, the war of posts and hamlets that history's Republic lost slowly before it lost the conventional war fast. I judge that the U wing at strength gives the Republic its own night fire over about half to two-thirds of the posts the Ghostrider covered in 1969–72, at about a half of its nightly sortie rate, and at about a third of it by the end of 1974². History's Republic had, at night, three to four AC-119 and AC-47 squadrons with no radar that the SA-7 drove high (D13)⁷.

### Against the studies

**The air war.** The study's judgment is that air power in this war is decisive against the measured siege and the fixed crossing and does nothing against a regiment under canopy or an attack on a post at night, and that none of it is Vietnamese. This provision is consistent with the first half and adds to the second: it is the first air instrument in the record aimed at the post at night, and the first owned by the Republic. That is an addition to the area, not a contradiction of the study's account of the war before it. The study's sentence on G-12 will need its second clause changed, and Part 3 records the change in the study's entry. On the variables the study holds: the Ghostrider's Trail effect (−3% to −10% of tonnage arriving by 1971) sits at the edge of what the study allows, a trace below history in P4 on the chapters' judgment of −5% to 0%; I keep the push but at low confidence, and I accept the study's view that the Trail's redundancy, which the B-52J built, absorbs most of it. On *ARVN.self_sufficiency.fire_support*, the study judged −8 to 0 points against history at the end of 1972 because the Republic held its sieges on American coordinates; the U's push of +2 to +6 by 1971 and +3 to +8 at the end of 1974 moves that judgment up, and the study's account of the VNAF in 1974–75 ("it can lift, resupply and strike with unguided bombs, and it cannot break a siege") gains a fourth capability: it can hold a post at night. On *VNAF.capability*, the study's P6 judgment of +3 to +6 with close support binding is consistent with the U's push and will be re-judged in Part 3.

**Maritime interdiction.** The study's area is the sea route, Sihanoukville and the Cambodian supply line. The gunships touch it only at its land end: the convoys of ten to thirty trucks from Kompong Speu toward the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak ran inside Cambodia, where no gunship flew before May 1970 (CONFIG cambodia_air = MODELLED). From May 1970, when the Cambodian roads were open to air, the Ghostrider's Ubon squadron and the Bien Hoa squadron could hunt them; by then Sihanoukville was closed by Lon Nol's coup and the study's account of that route is not changed. Consistent, with nothing to add.

### Employment concepts

#### EC015-1 · The night net over the corps

The Ghostrider's main work. Each in-country squadron of sixteen keeps two to three aircraft airborne over its corps from dusk to dawn in two waves: the first launches at 1830, takes station by 1900 over a pre-planned orbit point 30–60 nautical miles from base, and stays until about 0030; the second relieves it on station and stays until 0600². A third aircraft is on ground alert with engines warm. The orbit points sit over the districts with the most posts and the most attacks in the previous month, set each afternoon by the corps' fire-support centre.

When a post calls — on the district net, relayed by the district or the sector to the corps' direct air support centre, and from there to the aircraft on UHF — the nearest gunship turns toward it. Transit is 5–15 minutes at 250–300 knots. The crew finds the post by the coordinates the district gives and by its flare or strobe, identifies the attackers on the infrared turret, obtains clearance from the province chief through the sector, and fires. A typical engagement lasts 30–90 minutes; the aircraft returns to its orbit or hands off to the second wave².

Cycle: about 45–50 sorties a night across the wing at the sustained rate, of which about 30–36 over the three in-country squadrons' corps and about 8–10 on the Trail; each in-country aircraft about 25–28 sorties a month; about 900–1,100 post engagements a month across the South at the 1967 peak². The minimum package is one aircraft on station per sector of about 50 nautical miles' radius; without the radios of P008b the post cannot call, and the concept degrades to the AC-47's of 1965: an aircraft sent to posts that are already burning.

#### EC015-2 · Over the relief

Developed after the Front began to answer the radio by ambushing the relief. When a district sends its reaction force up a road at night, the gunship over the post detaches or a second aircraft is called, and it flies the road ahead of the trucks at 2–3 kilometres, scanning the cane and the treelines on either side with the infrared turret for men lying in wait². A company in ambush on a dry-season night shows plainly: forty to eighty warm bodies in a line along a culvert. The crew calls the column to halt, reports, and with the chief's clearance fires.

This concept turns the Front's best answer to the radio into a trap for the ambushers, on clear nights. On cloudy nights the Ghostrider cannot see the cane, and the rule of 1967 — fire first, men in the morning — returns. The package is one gunship per relief; at the wing's sustained rate, about one relief in two can be covered on a clear night in the Delta and fewer elsewhere².

#### EC015-3 · Hunter on the radar's track

The 17th Squadron from Ubon, from the dry season of 1965–66. The Global Hawk orbit over the panhandle passes moving-target tracks of six vehicles or more by satellite link to the Airborne Battlefield Command and Control Centre and directly to the gunships in their armed reconnaissance areas³. The gunship flies to the track, finds the convoy on its infrared turret, and engages from outside the 37mm's reach with Griffins and SDBs for the first trucks and the 105mm from 12,000–15,000 feet for the rest².

Profile: launch from Ubon at 1900, 45–70 minutes' transit to the armed reconnaissance area in the panhandle, 3–4 hours on station, recovery by 0200; second wave 0100–0700. Eight to ten sorties a night in the dry season, five to six in the wet. The package is one gunship and one Global Hawk track feed, with an F-4 flak-suppression pair on call from 1967 when the 37mm and 57mm batteries thicken. Without the Global Hawk the gunship hunts on its own sensors as history's AC-130A did, and finds about half as many convoys a sortie².

#### EC015-4 · The night shift over the siege

At Plei Me, Con Thien, Khe Sanh, Kham Duc and in 1972 at An Loc and Kontum, two gunships over the besieged position all night, working between the B-52J cells under the direction of the fire-support coordinator inside the wire. The gunship takes the sappers at the wire, the digging parties, the mortars and the recoilless rifles at 50–400 metres from the defenders; the GBU-38s take the trenches and gun positions beyond 400 metres². In cloud, the Spooky II from 1968 fires on radar offsets from a beacon inside the perimeter; the Ghostrider waits.

#### EC015-5 · Over the night position

From 1965, a gunship assigned to each US or ARVN battalion or brigade that spends the night in contact or in a night defensive position in a contested area, on call to the battalion's forward air controller². The first case is the 1st Cavalry Division's battalions in the Ia Drang and the camps of the Special Forces on the border.

#### EC015-6 · Daylight close support (abandoned)

Tried in 1964 and early 1965, when the wing had more aircraft than nights to fill. A Ghostrider in daylight over a contact is a large, slow aircraft in a left-hand turn at 7,000–10,000 feet, visible from every point within ten kilometres, and the PAVN and VC learned in weeks to put every 12.7mm in range on it. One aircraft was damaged by 12.7mm fire over Binh Dinh in February 1965². The A-1 Skyraider did the same work faster, cheaper and with less risk. Daylight close support was abandoned by April 1965 except over sieges and in emergencies. The lesson was the dossier's: the gunship's advantage is the night.

#### EC015-7 · Under the cloud (abandoned)

Tried in the north-east monsoon of 1964–65 over Binh Dinh and Quang Ngai: a Ghostrider descending below a 1,500–2,000-foot cloud base to see a post under attack. It worked twice, and on the third night an aircraft took 12.7mm hits through the fuel tanks at 1,800 feet over Hoai Nhon and barely reached Qui Nhon. The wing forbade attacks below 3,500 feet in December 1964². From then on, on a monsoon night over the coast, the Ghostrider had nothing to give the posts of I and II Corps but the SDB on a coordinate the post could not give it. This is the constraint the dossier named (D14), and it is why the U matters most on the coast.

#### EC015-8 · The Vietnamese night net

The Spooky II's main work, from August 1967 in III and IV Corps and from 1968–69 everywhere. The concept mirrors EC015-1 on a smaller scale: one aircraft airborne over each corps from 1900 to 0100 and one from 0100 to 0600, called by the province or sector net directly in Vietnamese, cleared by the province chief, firing on visual or radar. Cycle at full strength (1970): about 25–30 sorties a night across the wing, each U about 18–22 sorties a month².

The concept's distinctive tool is the radar beacon. From 1968 every district town and the posts most often attacked carry a small American-supplied radar transponder, run on domestic dry cells from the Go Vap plant¹, which the U's APQ-180 uses as its offset reference through cloud². On a monsoon night on the coast, the U fires 40mm and 105mm on grid offsets from the beacon at a post it cannot see.

#### EC015-9 · Joint gunship crews (abandoned)

Tried in 1967: an American instructor pilot and fire-control officer flying with a Vietnamese crew on operational sorties. It sped training and it did not last. Vietnamese crews flew a different war from the Americans aboard — the language, the nets and the chiefs were theirs — and the instructors deferred or overruled at the wrong moments; by March 1968 operational sorties were all-Vietnamese and the instructors flew only training sorties².

#### EC015-10 · Over Cambodia (abandoned)

From May 1970, the 817th Squadron flew the roads of eastern Cambodia in support of the incursion and then of Lon Nol's army. The roads had 12.7mm and 37mm guns, the Cambodian army's calls came in French and Khmer, and in August 1970 the squadron lost an aircraft over Route 7 to a 37mm battery near Snuol². The JGS withdrew the squadron to the Delta in September 1970. The lesson was the dossier's again: the U is a weapon of the South's posts, and it dies over defended roads.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

The night attack on the post does not disappear. It moves in four directions, and each is a finding.

**F015-5** **The night attack moves to the cloud, to the uncovered post and into the hamlet.** First, into the weather: by the end of 1964 the Front's regional commands in the Delta and on the coast were timing assaults to rain squalls and the north-east monsoon's low cloud, and the share of post attacks made on nights of low cloud roughly doubled². Second, to the posts outside the net: the far ends of the Highlands' roads, the islands of the Delta's canals more than 50 nautical miles from Bien Hoa or Binh Thuy, the posts of Chuong Thien and An Xuyen where the orbits were thin. Third, to the hamlet itself: instead of assaulting the post from the paddy, the Front fought it from inside the hamlet and from its edges, where a gunship could fire only with the province chief's clearance and at the price of the people in it. Fourth, out of the night: assassination of hamlet officials by day, mines on the roads and dikes, and the mortar from 300 metres that is gone before a gunship comes. This is the Front's answer that POS004-1 and POSCP02-2 predicted for every provision that improved the post: it attacks what the provision does not reach. What the allies lose by it is the night attack as a signal; the Front's intentions in a district were read, in history, partly from where it attacked posts, and it attacks fewer.

**F015-6** **The trucks move under the trees, into the wet season and behind the guns.** Group 559 answered the Ghostrider as it had answered the B-52J: it moved truck parks under canopy, built more bypasses under trees, moved convoys at intervals of minutes rather than in columns, and from the dry season of 1966–67 escorted the main routes with 37mm and 57mm batteries a year earlier and about a third denser than history had them at the same date. The dry season's clear nights over Laos are the gunship's best — the Trail's season and the gunship's coincide — but the trucks that move under trees on roads the canopy closes over are invisible to it. I judge the net loss of tonnage at about two to four points more than history's in the dry seasons of 1966–71², less than the trucks killed suggest, because the Trail's capacity is several times its requirement.

### Bottleneck migration

**F015-7** **The post's constraint moves from the answer to the clearance and the weather.** Before this provision, the binding constraint on a post's defence at night, after memorandum 008, was the answer: no reaction force that could survive the road, and guns that reached a fraction of the posts. After it, on a clear night within the net, the binding constraint is the clearance. A gunship over the post in 15 minutes waits for the sector to reach the province chief, and the chief to approve fire beside a hamlet; in history the AC-47's clearance took 20–60 minutes, and the merit chiefs of P002b shorten it to perhaps 10–30. On a cloudy night the constraint is the weather, and nothing in the force but the U's radar answers it. A third constraint appears in the Highlands: the gunship's range from base and the thinness of the net. So the constraint on the territorial war migrates to the province chief's telephone and to the sky, in that order. The first is a matter of procedure that this headquarters fixed in July 1964 by delegating clearance for fire within 300 metres of a post under attack to the district chief, at the price of the civilian harm in F015-13².

**F015-8** **The VNAF's constraint moves from aircraft to technicians.** The VNAF of 1967 had aircraft enough and too few men who could keep a radar and a fire-control computer working in English (D9)⁷. The U makes that its binding constraint: about 150–250 radar, avionics and fire-control technicians who take a year or more each to train⁷. Every one the gunship wing takes is one the A-1 and A-37 squadrons do not get. It is the constraint that decides how many Us fly in 1974.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F015-9** **Ramp space, airfield defence and guided weapons.** Sixty-four $165-million aircraft at Bien Hoa, Nha Trang, Da Nang and Ubon need revetments and need the airfields defended. The VC's mortar attack on Bien Hoa of 1 November 1964 destroyed or damaged about 27 aircraft; in this war it was a gunship base, and two Ghostriders were among the aircraft hit, one destroyed. From 1965 each gunship base needed a battalion-size ground defence force — American, Korean or ARVN — and every one so assigned was a battalion not in the field². The guided weapons are rationed (D8); the Trail squadron's Griffins come out of a production line shared with every other American command in the world of 2026. The wing's 1,100–1,500 American spaces in country are against no ceiling, but they are Air Force maintenance and armament specialists of a kind the B-52J and the coordinate cell also need⁴.

**F015-10** **The VNAF's best men go to the gunships.** About 360 officers and 150–250 technicians of the VNAF's best are in the gunship wing by 1969 (D2, D9)⁷. The A-1 and A-37 squadrons, the transport squadrons and the helicopter wings are short what the gunship wing takes; I judge the VNAF's other aircraft about 0–4 points less operational on average through 1969–72². It is a real cost and the right trade: the U does at night what no other VNAF aircraft can do at all.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F015-11** **History's gunship force and its programme do not exist.** No Gunship I trials at Bien Hoa in December 1964; no 4th Air Commando Squadron's AC-47s; no AC-47s in Thailand in 1966, which lost aircraft over Laos for two interdiction sorties a night; no AC-119G or AC-119K; no AC-130A of the Gunship II programme in September 1967; no 71st, 17th, 18th or 16th Special Operations Squadron as history knew them. The C-47s and C-119s that history converted stay transports, about 100 aircraft that the Air Force and later the VNAF use for lift². History's gunship losses — 30 aircraft and about 160 men⁷ — are replaced by the Ghostriders' and the Us' own. History's gunship events change with them: the AC-47 that A1C John L. Levitow saved over Long Binh in February 1969 does not fly⁷; a Ghostrider is there instead, with a different crew and a different night. The attention history spent on the gunship programme, in a Pentagon that resisted the concept for two years (Ballard's account, M015·S6), is spent on nothing: the aircraft arrive finished.

### Perverse effect

**F015-12** **The post holds, and the platoon stays behind its wire.** POS008-4 found that the rule of fire first made the post cheaper to hold and kept the platoon inside, so that the post held and the hamlet did not. The gunship makes that rule stronger. A PF platoon that knows a Ghostrider will be over it in fifteen minutes has every reason to stay behind its wire and fire flares, and none to go out to the hamlet's edge in the dark where the gunship cannot tell it from the enemy. The safest place on a dark night in the Delta is now inside the post; the hamlet's people are outside it. That is the perverse effect: the provision that makes the post hold makes the hamlet a little more the Front's at night, wherever the district chief does not insist on the platoon's night patrols (RFPF.effectiveness.night_patrol). I judge it a modest offset to the night-control gain, enough to hold that gain to 0–2 points in the Delta by 1967 rather than the 2–5 the posts' survival alone would suggest².

**F015-13** **Fire beside the hamlet.** When the Front moves its attack into the hamlet, a gunship that fires on it fires into the hamlet. The 105mm's lethal radius is about 35 metres and its danger radius several times that⁸; a 30mm or 25mm burst into a hedge line at a hamlet's edge kills whoever sleeps behind it. With clearance delegated to the district chief within 300 metres of a post, the decision to fire is made fast and by a man whose post is under attack. I judge the gunships add 200–1,500 civilian dead and wounded a year by 1967², offset in part by the posts that do not fall and the reprisals and forced recruitment that do not follow. A single bad night — a gunship firing on a hamlet that the Front had left an hour before — is the kind of event the Front's propaganda section in every province waits for.

### Anticipation

**F015-14** **The assaults that are not made.** The Front does not assault a covered post on a clear night once it has learned what happens. By the second half of 1964 in the Delta and by 1965 elsewhere, the night assault on a post within the net, in clear weather, became rare². That is the gunship's largest effect, and it is invisible: the metrics show fewer posts lost and fewer attacks, and the gunship's kills at posts fall month by month after the first spring. The war in the hamlets does not improve with them. The Front's cadre still collect the tax, still recruit, still kill the hamlet chief by day; what the provision prevents is the fall of the post and the loss of the platoon's weapons and the village's files, not the Front's government. This headquarters will report the posts' survival as what it is — fewer posts lost, a real gain to the hamlet's defenders — and will not report it as pacification. It is the case A13 names: a provision that improves the war in one part by making the enemy decline, and shows it only as quiet.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F015-15** **Hanoi learns the gunship in a season and learns it wrong for a year.** The Ghostrider's first engagements over the Delta in January 1964 were detected by the provincial units at once; a post that should have fallen did not, and the assault company was destroyed in the paddy by a fire that came from an aircraft they could hear and could not see. By March 1964 COSVN's military affairs committee had a name for it — the aircraft that sees in the dark — and a directive to the regions: do not attack covered posts on clear nights; attack in rain; attack posts far from the airfields; close with the wall before the aircraft comes. That much was right, and it propagated from COSVN to the district units within two to three months².

What the Front got wrong was the radar. Its directive of 1964 assumed the aircraft saw through cloud as it saw in the dark, and for most of the north-east monsoon of 1964–65 the regions of the coast did not exploit the cloud as they could have. By the spring of 1965 the provincial units of Binh Dinh and Quang Ngai had learned from their own attacks that the aircraft did not fire through low cloud², and from the monsoon of 1965–66 the coast's night war ran on the weather. In 1968, when the Spooky II's radar began to fire through the same cloud, the Front had to learn the lesson again in reverse, and for some months did not.

What Hanoi got right that hurt was the bases. A Ghostrider on the ground at Bien Hoa is a $165 million target for a mortar crew with twenty rounds, and from 1965 the sapper and mortar attacks on the gunship bases were planned for the aircraft, not the runway.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F015-16** **Most of the enemy's answer is cheap; the guns are not.** Moving attacks into the weather and away from the net costs the Front tempo and choice — it can attack only on some nights and in some places — but no men and no tonnage. Fighting from inside the hamlets costs it the people's goodwill where the gunship fires, which it can turn back on the government. Those adaptations cost the enemy less than the provision cost the allies. The anti-aircraft guns cost more: 12.7mm machine guns sent south a year earlier to the main-force battalions, about two to four per battalion by 1966², each with its ammunition carried down the Trail, and in the panhandle 37mm and 57mm batteries a year earlier and a third denser than history at the same date. That is a few percent of the Trail's tonnage spent on guns to keep the gunships off, and the guns kill other aircraft too (AIR.losses). The exchange rate is mixed: in the South it favours the enemy's cheap answers, on the Trail it is closer to even.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F015-17** **A few hundred American lives, a larger secret war in Laos, and no change in the clock.** The loop runs: fewer posts and camps lost and fewer American units overrun at night → 80–400 fewer Americans killed in 1967⁷ and perhaps 500–1,500 over 1965–71, less the Ghostrider crews lost → no measurable change in American tolerance, as POSMT01-5 found for every sum of saved lives below a band. The second loop runs through Laos: a Ghostrider squadron at Ubon from 1965 is a larger American war in Laos, secret under the ambassador's control as history's was, and the Thai government's price for the base is paid in aid and in visibility at Udorn and Ubon². The third loop runs through Moscow and Beijing: more and earlier anti-aircraft guns to the Trail → a higher loss rate for every American aircraft over Laos from 1966–67 → no change in the negotiation, because Laos was never in it. None of the loops returns to the theatre with a change in tolerance, appropriation or ceiling. The one loop that does return is the U after 1973: an aircraft the Republic owns, bought with the 1974 appropriation, is an argument for the appropriation that the Republic can make to Congress with its own results⁶, and I would not put a number on it.

### Open channel

**F015-18** **The gunship's camera keeps an honest ledger.** The Ghostrider's turrets record every engagement in full-motion video. History's gunship claims on the Trail were discounted by three-quarters by the CIA and DIA⁷; in this war the video goes to the same cell at Tan Son Nhut that kept the radar's second ledger, and the truck claims are counted against what the video shows destroyed. At the posts, the body counts that the districts report for a gunship's night are counted against the video's figures, and the district's figure falls by half to two-thirds². It is a small gain to the honesty of the war's metrics and a real one: the gunship's effect at the posts is reported as attacks defeated and posts held, which the video shows, not as enemy killed, which it inflates. The same video, in a bad night beside a hamlet, is evidence the Inspector General can use, and it is not always welcome.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F015-19 · Counter-directional** **For nine years the night belongs to an American aircraft, and in 1973 only a third of it stays.** From the first night of 1964 the Republic's territorial forces learn that the answer to a night attack comes from the sky, and for five years it is American. The posts, the districts and the sectors build their night defence on it: the rule of fire first, the PF behind the wire, the reaction force that waits for the morning. The Spooky II takes over part of the answer from 1969, but at about a half of the Ghostrider's nightly sortie rate and covering perhaps half to two-thirds of the posts at its best; when the Ghostriders leave in 1972–73, the posts beyond the U's net and beyond its sorties find the night empty in a way history's posts, which never had the Ghostrider, did not. The habit outlasts the means. I judge this a moderate offset at its true weight: against history's Republic, which had three VNAF gunship squadrons without radar and lost the territorial war of 1973–74 slowly, this Republic is still better off at night after 1973, by the U's coverage; against the Republic of 1969–72 in this war, it is much worse off, and the fall from one to the other in 1973 is steeper than history's because there is more to fall from. The provinces beyond the U's net — the far Highlands, the islands of the Delta, the coastal districts on monsoon nights the beacons do not reach — lose their posts in 1973–74 at nearly history's rate, and their commanders, who for nine years had called the sky, are slower to rebuild the ground reaction forces that history's commanders never stopped relying on². The civilian harm of F015-13 runs the same way: it is real, it accrues with the gunships' success at the posts, and it is the Front's argument in the hamlets.

**F015-20 · Strongest positive** **The night attack on the post, the Front's main instrument in the village war, loses about a quarter to two-fifths of its effect from 1964 to 1972, and a Vietnamese gunship keeps a part of that after 1973.** The post at night was where the Front's local forces took weapons, files, posts and with them the hamlets around them. With the Ghostrider from 1964, attacks on covered posts in clear weather mostly stop, and those that are made fail more often: I judge 4–10 points more of the attacked RF and PF posts hold from 1964–72 than they would have without the gunships, on top of what the land, the chiefs, the rifles, the radios and the training had already given them. Each post that holds keeps a platoon's weapons out of the Front's hands, a police post and its files alive, and a village chief sleeping in the village. Over nine years that is perhaps 1,000–2,500 posts that do not fall⁹. From 1969 the Spooky II carries a part of the gain into the years after the Americans leave, when history's posts fell to the land-grabbing attacks of 1973–74 without any night fire from the air: I judge 3–8 points more of the attacked posts hold in 1973–74². That is the largest gain the record has yet given to the territorial war at night, and the first that survives the withdrawal. It does not hold the hamlets, it does not reach the infrastructure, and it does not touch the conventional offensives that decided 1972 and 1975. It is still the most important thing this memorandum changes.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

The subsector and district advisers become, from 1964, the link between a post and the American gunship net: the adviser's radio and his English are how a Popular Forces post reaches a Ghostrider before the combined net of P012a reaches the districts. That deepens the dependence POS006-3 named — the adviser's standing comes from the fire he can call — and the Selected Advisory Corps of P006c is told from 1967, as for artillery, to hand the call to Vietnamese observers and from 1969 to the VNAF's gunship net. MACV's J-3 gains a gunship operations cell of about 20 in the tactical air control system; J-2 gains the video ledger.

### US conventional forces

American battalions in night positions and in contact at night have a gunship on call from 1965. Their night defensive positions are harder to assault, and the PAVN and VC regiments that historically attacked American battalions at night — at Ia Drang, at the fire bases of 1966–69 — make fewer such attacks and pay more for those they make. The effect is a few hundred American dead over the war, and the battalions' night patrolling is unchanged.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

The border camps are the gunship's natural clients. Every camp under attack at night from 1964 — Nam Dong, Plei Me, Duc Co, A Shau, Lang Vei, Bu Prang and the rest — has a Ghostrider over it within 20–40 minutes from Nha Trang, Da Nang or Bien Hoa, and from 1968 the Spooky II for the camps handed to the ARVN Rangers². The camps in the canopy and the mountains benefit least, because the attacker is unseen until he is at the wire and because the north-east monsoon covers the camps of I Corps. A Shau in March 1966 was lost in cloud with gunship support that could not see; in this war the Ghostrider over A Shau could not see either (D14).

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

ARVN battalions in night positions call the gunship through their advisers until 1969 and the VNAF's net after. The JGS gains its first say in the air war's employment through the VNAF gunship wing, and its first stake in an air instrument it can request from Congress by name.

### RF/PF and territorial security

The largest change in the force. From 1964 the posts within the gunship net hold more often and are attacked less often on clear nights; from 1967 the rule of fire first is answered from the air; from 1969 the answer is Vietnamese. The PF stay behind their wire, and the district chief's delegated clearance makes him the man who decides whether a hamlet's edge is fired on.

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. The gunships do not fly over the North, and the opening strike and standing authorities of memorandum 013 are untouched by them.

### Trail interdiction

The Ghostrider squadron at Ubon flies over the panhandle from the dry season of 1965–66, two years before history's AC-130A, with Global Hawk tracks. Commando Hunt from November 1968 opens with a mature gunship force rather than a new one. Truck kills on the radar's honest count rise several-fold; tonnage arriving falls by a few points more than history's. The 37mm and 57mm defences thicken a year early.

### Arc Light

Over sieges the B-52J cells work by day and in intervals; the gunship works between them. Arc Light's sorties are otherwise unchanged.

### Airmobile formations

The 1st Cavalry Division and the airmobile brigades gain night cover over their landing zones and night positions. Helicopter gunships keep their work by day and at the landing zone; the fixed-wing gunship takes the night.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The Mobile Riverine Force and the Vietnamese river groups gain night cover on the Delta's canals from 1967; the Ghostrider's infrared sees sampans moving at night, and the canal ambushes the Front laid for the river boats become harder in clear weather². Market Time at sea is unchanged.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Indirectly: posts that hold keep the village police posts and the Special Branch files alive. The PIOCCs and DIOCCs and the task force of P012b gain nothing directly.

### Third-country forces

The Korean divisions in Binh Dinh and Phu Yen and the Australian task force in Phuoc Tuy call the gunship through the combined net of P012a⁵. The Korean posts on the coast face the north-east monsoon's cloud like everyone else's.
