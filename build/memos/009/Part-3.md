## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I now command is fought, from its first day to its last, by private soldiers who have been trained. That is the whole of this memorandum's change, and it is both smaller and larger than it sounds. It is smaller because no battle in this war was decided by a private's training: the enemy chose where and when to fight, the generals chose where to send the battalions, and Washington and Hanoi chose how long it would last. It is larger because the private is the man who dies, and in a war of ambushes, outposts and night attacks, how he fights for the first minute and whether his friend can stop his bleeding decide how many of him come home.

On 1 January 1964 the Republic's army already held seven years of trained men. Since 1957 every recruit to the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers and the Marines had passed ten weeks at Quang Trung or a division centre on the 2026 American programme, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit, the men who became the Regional and Popular Forces, six weeks of it at his province's centre. About 120,000–160,000 regulars and 60,000–100,000 territorials were trained men when this headquarters opened, and every American who arrived in the theatre had passed his own service's 2026 course¹. The rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was put on the muster roll as the number of the cycle a man had passed, and the Muster Service checked it at dawn. In September 1964 it caught a division training centre in Binh Dinh that had been sending men to the battalions after four weeks and drawing six weeks' rations for them, and the Commission charged the commandant. The course had a price, and the army paid it in the two worst years: two to three thousand of its best sergeants were standing in front of recruits at Hoc Mon while the battalions were being broken in Phuoc Long and Quang Ngai.

Phase I ran its historical course in Saigon and on the main-force battlefield. Khanh took power, the generals fought each other, Hanoi sent its regiments south, and the Americans came in the summer of 1965. In the hamlets of the held belt the trained Popular Forces platoon changed the first ten minutes of a night attack. At Long Huu in Kien Hoa on 17 November 1964 Corporal Pham Van Tho's platoon had kept a third of its ammunition for the second rush, as the territorial course taught, and its two wounded were alive with tourniquets on when the boats came from Ham Long at 0320. The posts with their platoon held about nineteen attacks in twenty rather than nine in ten, and the Front did what it does against every better post: it went for the constables, the militia and the camps the course did not reach. At Dong Xoai in June 1965 the CIDG companies broke first, as they always had; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment was landed on its division's plan beside the rubber and lost half its men regardless of its privates' training; and the 7th Airborne brought about 50 of its 60 wounded alive into Thuan Loi because men who had never been taught first aid in history's army had been taught it here. The army's battalions finished the phase a band steadier than history's, which is the first time in this exercise that the ARVN of 1964–65 has moved by a band on anything other than its weapons².

Phase II was the American war, and the American soldier in it was a little better and a good deal more likely to live. At the Ia Drang the troopers of the 1st Cavalry Division who lay in the grass at Albany through the night of 17 November 1965 put tourniquets on each other in the dark while the PAVN searched the field, and about eight to twelve of them lived who had died there historically. That is the rate on which this memorandum rests its whole American saving, about two to four percent of the American killed in action, and Albany is where it can be seen at work. It is not a large rate. Over the war it is about 900–1,900 American lives, and I sign for every one of them gladly².

The ARVN of Phase II was a trained army under board-chosen battalion commanders, carrying the carbines and squad guns of 1964, and the enemy treated it as he had treated every better unit: he declined to fight it. The 272nd Regiment that would not spring its ambush on Major Trieu's flanking companies on Route 13 in May 1966 ambushed the Regional Force convoy at Bau Bang instead, and the RF soldiers came off their trucks into the ditch and fought back in pairs, as their course had taught them; they lost about 32 men rather than 40, and they were still on their trucks when the fire came, because their sector commander had put them there. That is this provision in one morning: the private fought better, and the order above him was unchanged. Across 1966–67 I judge that the ARVN's battalions lost a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still laid, beyond what the rifles and the officers had saved, and that about one in eight fewer battalions was broken; no report shows it, because the fights it would have shown were not fought².

The enemy's answer is the clearest thing in this memorandum, and it cost the Americans as much as the course saved them in one district. At the end of 1965 the command of the Quang Da Special Zone concluded that its local companies could not win firefights against units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds, and ordered its village guerrillas to make mines and booby traps their principal work. The Marines of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines at Dien Ban, who did everything right in a firefight and were given none, lost about two-thirds of their dead to devices in the year from September 1966, and the farmers of Dien Ban and Dai Loc stepped on the same devices. What the course gave those Marines was the tourniquet on the shattered leg, and it kept a dozen of them alive without their legs. The enemy had moved his war to the weapon the course did not teach, as this headquarters had said he would.

In June 1966 the piaster was devalued and the private's rice cost half as much again, and here the war showed what training does not buy. The men who walked out of the 25th Division's battalions that autumn had all been through Quang Trung; most went home to their families' fields, some to the Regional Forces, and a few hundred to the guerrilla platoons of Cu Chi and Duc Hoa, where a captured notebook records a three-week course for the district's squad leaders taught by a deserter on firing in pairs and the tourniquet, with the remark that the puppets' course was good and their pay was not. Colonel Hoan cut his division's desertion by a quarter in 1967 by moving the families inside the perimeters and paying the ration in rice. That was not training. It was the family, the pay and the rice, and it is the reason this provision moves the desertion band by nothing.

Tet came on the historical night and turned on the historical decisions, in Saigon, at Hue, at Khe Sanh and in Washington. The basic course changed it where the rifles and the officers had already changed it, on the compound walls of the district towns, where the RF companies held their fire and their ammunition, and in one place they had not: the wounded. Through the night of 31 January and the weeks of Hue, men lying in the streets with B40 fragments in their legs were alive in the morning because the man beside them had put on a tourniquet. I put the ARVN's dead in the first wave at about 2,150 rather than 2,300, and the American dead at 30–60 fewer, the rate of Albany at work in a second battle. The decision of 31 March, which was made on the casualties of 1965–67 and on the shock of the offensive, was made as before.

In June 1968 the mobilization met a training base that would not send men to units untrained, and the cost was a month. The Joint General Staff ordered the course cut to six weeks; the training command ran its centres in two shifts and held it at ten, and the new battalions and territorial companies reached the field after the third offensive had begun rather than before it, trained. The drill sergeants the army had held at the centres in 1964–65 came back as platoon sergeants, and about four in five of the new Popular Forces platoons had a man who had held the rank, against two in three on the course memorandum 007 had left. The third offensive went, as every offensive had since 1964, for what the course had not reached: the People's Self-Defense Force, armed with the Garands of 1950 and three days' instruction. In Hau Nghia it overran two-thirds of the groups it attacked. At Rach Kien in Long An a PF platoon came across the paddies in sixteen minutes to a self-defence group's flare and broke the attack at the hamlet gate, and the district chief of Can Duoc sent his returned drill sergeants to teach the groups the first ten minutes on their own evenings; it was the only district in three provinces where the groups held through the autumn.

From 1969 the line holds regardless of this provision. Vietnamization, the Cambodian incursion and Lam Son 719 are decided by the American withdrawal, the palace and the corps, and the trained private changes none of their outcomes; Lam Son 719 still goes in on its corps commander's plan and comes out as it did. The Easter Offensive of 1972 is decided at Quang Tri by the orders of the corps and at An Loc and Kontum by the B-52J; the trained soldiers who hold at An Loc hold for longer with their wounded alive, and the city held anyway. In 1974 the RVNAF bleeds in the attrition the ration imposes, with tourniquets and with fewer rounds than its training assumed, and in March 1975 its corps dissolve toward their families on the palace's orders. The course did not teach a soldier to leave his family on the road at Cheo Reo, and nothing a basic course could teach would have kept him from going to them. The Ho Chi Minh Campaign takes Saigon on 30 April 1975, and the course's graduates, the best-trained private soldiers the Republic ever had, are among the men in the camps.

What this provision did, it did to men. About 5,000–14,000 American and South Vietnamese soldiers are alive at the end of the war who died on the historical course, most of them South Vietnamese, many of them without an arm or a leg; the Front's opening volley broke fewer units in the years the war was being lost; the territorial post held its first ten minutes; and the enemy moved his war to the mine and the self-defence hamlet, where the course did not go². It did not change who chose the battle, who chose the generals, what the soldier's family ate, or what Congress voted in 1974. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, and the Republic that falls is the same Republic with better privates.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +2..+4 · POP.gvn_legitimacy +2..+4 · VCI.strength -4..-2 · FIND.main_force +2..+4 · FIND.vci +5..+7 · ARVN.effectiveness +5..+7.*

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964, none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year³. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon; at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve and its regiments in set-piece battles, and at Song Be the government holds a province capital only narrowly and with American firepower. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

Five things in this phase are new, and all of them begin on its first day. Neither the coups in Saigon nor the summer offensive turns on any of them.

The first two belong to the Republic itself. On 1 January 1964 the junta decrees land to the tiller, confirming by name what the Viet Minh and the Front had already given, and an independent Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it; the same session gives the Republic a resident rural police with a Special Branch, and province and district chiefs chosen by examination from a ranked list. Khanh's coup tests both within a month and both survive it. By the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the musters have shown the RVNAF to be about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls⁴; about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the Front has killed about 410 constables, nearly all in posts settled ahead of night security⁵. The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

The third is what this command can see. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks fly from January 1964. The coastal picket closes the trawler route within a year, and Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months before it did historically. On the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats near the destroyers, Pierce Arrow is not flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the real attack of 2 August alone.

The fourth is in the hands of the men who fight: modern carbines for the whole RVNAF and the territorials, and a belt-fed gun and a marksman in every regular squad. In the held belt a post with its Popular Forces platoon now holds about nine attacks in ten. Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy still chooses where and when to fight, but they are lost at a smaller price and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out. The outposts that fall give the Front the same carbines.

The fifth waits on Guam: B-52Js carrying GPS-guided bombs and, from March 1964, a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut that measures the U-2S's imagery into targets for them. Washington refuses every request to use them over the North, and the first Arc Light strike of June 1965 flies without tankers and finds as little as history's did.

A sixth thing begins on the same first day and does not show until the second year. The decree of 1 January 1964 puts a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch and orders a coordinating centre in every province and district, built on the posts and files the police decree creates; the centres are staffed by the Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the sector S2, forbidden quotas, held to warrants under the police statute, and audited by the Commission. About 100 district centres are open by the end of 1964 and all of them by the end of 1965. In the same month the Army begins to select its field advisers by board, to train them for eight months in Vietnamese and in advising, and to keep them eighteen months; the first of them reach the subsectors and battalions in October. In March 1965 the centre at Hoc Mon breaks the district committee above the hamlets the police had already cleared, and the Front learns that leaving a village no longer puts its leaders out of reach.

A seventh thing began before the first day, and it is the only one that reaches back past it. Since 1957 every new officer of the ARVN, and every new American officer, has passed a branch course after commissioning and the courses of the 2026 continuum before each promotion, so that on 1 January 1964 seven such classes, about 10,000–14,000 ARVN officers, hold most of the platoons and companies of the army⁶. The session of 1 January gives that army an Officer Selection and Promotion Board with written criteria, a rated evaluation report, central command lists for battalions and regiments, relief for cause, a path to commission for proven sergeants, and a ranked list from which generals and division commanders must be chosen, behind an American aid condition. Khanh's coup tests it in its first month. Its effect in this phase is in the companies, where the educated officers already stand, and in the first battalions the board fills; Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy chose them and the generals planned the relief.

An eighth thing stood on the first day, and it was made of steel and copper. The Republic began the war with an industrial base a generation above its own: a steelworks, an arsenal and rebuild works at Bien Hoa, a radio plant, a battery works and a wire mill at Go Vap, placed under a Directorate of Defence Industry. For fourteen months it had more capacity than customers, until the Aid Compliance Board made the RVNAF buy a share of its ammunition bodies, batteries, wire, radios and steel from it from March 1965. Its first effects were small and real: the Popular Forces platoons beside the police posts carried their own radios from the spring of 1964, and the Front learned to attack the posts that could not call; the bridges the November floods took on the coast were back in six weeks rather than months. No battle of the phase turned on it.

A ninth thing stood in the ranks on the first day, and it was the plainest of them. Since 1957 every man who enlisted or was drafted into the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers and the Vietnamese Marines had passed a ten-week basic course on the 2026 American programme at Quang Trung or a division centre, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit a six-week abridgement of it; every American recruit since 1957 had passed his own service's 2026 course. On 1 January 1964 about 120,000–160,000 of the Republic's regular soldiers and about 60,000–100,000 of its territorials had been trained so, and the rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was put on the muster roll as a course number the Muster Service could check¹. It changed no battle's choice of ground in the phase. It changed how the posts' first ten minutes went at Long Huu, how many of the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment came back from Thuan Loi, and how many wounded men in both armies were alive in the morning.

#### The Land-to-the-Tiller Decree and the Commission Against Corruption
*1 Jan 1964 · Saigon · new · EV001-1*
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh, with Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho's cabinet, promulgated two decree-laws drafted with the US Mission over the preceding weeks. The first ended the collection of rent on tenanted rice land from the coming main-season harvest, made every present tiller the provisional owner of the land he worked up to 3 hectares in the Delta and 1 hectare in the Central Lowlands, set the landlord's retention of tenanted land at zero and his compensation at 2.5 times the land's annual paddy yield, 20% in cash and the rest in eight-year bonds indexed to the paddy price, and confirmed by name every distribution the Viet Minh or the Front had made to the man now tilling the land. The second created the Commission Against Corruption under a Commissioner appointed for six years, Judge Truong Vinh Khoa of the Saigon Court of Appeal, a Special Court for Corruption, a Pay and Muster Verification Service, and a Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board whose American co-chair, the Deputy Chief of Mission, could suspend aid to a named command, importer or province and whose concurrence was needed to remove the Commissioner⁷.

Minh read the land decree on Radio Saigon that evening; its first sentence was the confirmation of the Front's distributions. Within a week the Vietnamese Information Service's loudspeaker teams and leaflets had carried it to the district towns of the Delta and the coastal Centre. The landowners' associations of Saigon and Can Tho protested to the council within days, and the protest was carried by officers of landed families who sat on it. Two USAF-contracted survey aircraft began photographing the rice lands from Tan Son Nhut on 13 January, tied to ground control laid by an Army topographic detachment from the old French triangulation; they finished the Delta in early April and the coastal Centre in May⁴.

#### The Police and Provincial Administration decrees; the first list
*1 Jan – 27 Apr 1964 · Saigon · new · EV002-1*
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964, in the same session that promulgated the land and anti-corruption decrees, the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh promulgated two more decree-laws drafted with the US Mission and the aid mission's Public Safety Division. The first rebuilt the National Police around the village: a resident post of 8 to 12 constables in every village the government held, a Police Special Branch with sole charge of intelligence on the Viet Cong infrastructure, a Police Field Force to be raised at once rather than in 1966, a national identity card and family census, a pay and career statute, and magistrate review of every arrest within 72 hours, with detention without trial capped at two years and reviewed every six months. The Directorate General was given to Vo Dinh Cuong, a career inspector of the old Sûreté who had served in the police under the French and under Diem and had kept out of Nhu's political service, for a fixed term removable only for cause⁵. The second decree created the Provincial Administration Selection Board, seven members under Judge Dinh Quang Tuyen of the Court of Cassation, and an Inspectorate of Provincial Administration, and made unlawful the appointment of any province chief, district chief or deputy for administration who was not taken from the Board's ranked list⁸.

The two decrees were read on Radio Saigon on the evening of 1 January after the land decree, and drew less notice than it did; the landowners protested the land decree, and the officers who would lose their provinces said nothing yet, because there was nothing yet to lose. The British Advisory Mission under R. G. K. Thompson, in Saigon since 1961, lent four Special Branch officers from the Federation of Malaysia to write the Special Branch's first manuals; the first 2,500 recruits a month entered the police training centres at Vung Tau and Da Lat from February. The Board's written examination was sat on 2 March at the National Institute of Administration by about 1,640 officers and civil servants, of whom about 1,000 were judged credible after the three-day assessments held through March and April⁵.

On 27 April 1964 the Board published its first list in the official gazette: ranked candidates for 31 posts, the 6 province chiefs and 25 district chiefs whom the Inspectorate's first sweep had found worst, nine of them in Khanh's "acting" appointments of February. Khanh's government appointed 27 of the 31 from the first name on each list, 3 from the second and 1 from the third; it refused no list. By 31 December 1964 about 95 of the Republic's roughly 290 chiefs had been appointed through the list, a third of the whole, and the Board's register of candidates had become the first record the Republic had ever kept of what its officers could actually do⁵.

#### The Phung Hoang decree, the first centres and the first selected advisers
*1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Special Warfare School at Fort Bragg and the Defense Language Institute at Monterey; the district towns of the held belt in IV and III Corps · new · EV006-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The Phung Hoang decree was the third signed in the session of 1 January 1964, after the land decree and the police decree, and it was the shortest of the three. It created a national Phung Hoang Committee under the Minister of the Interior, a national coordinating centre of about 150 staff in the Directorate of National Police, and an intelligence and operations coordinating centre in every province and district as the Special Branch's sections opened there: a PIOCC of 25–40 in each province capital and a DIOCC of 10–15 in each district office. Each centre sat in the Special Branch's office, under the district or province chief, with a Special Branch officer as its head, an officer of the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and the census teams and the Chieu Hoi office feeding it. Three articles were this headquarters' and were fought for in the drafting: no centre would be given a monthly target or credited with a number; no person would be arrested on a centre's file except on a magistrate's warrant under the police statute, on the three-source rule; and the Commission Against Corruption would sample the centres' reports of persons neutralized, name by name, from the first quarter of 1965. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the same statute as a police arm, about 44 units of 60–100 men under the province chiefs, with a rule that a raid's object was a prisoner⁹.

The national centre's director was Lieutenant Colonel Tran Dinh Hieu, a Military Security officer who had served in the Sûreté's political section before 1954 and had been passed over twice under Diem for refusing to work on the Can Lao's lists. Beside him sat an American cell of about 60, and in Saigon an exploitation detachment of about 80 Vietnamese-speaking analysts and translators, drawn from the 500th Military Intelligence Group, the Agency's station and the language school, under Lieutenant Colonel Martin J. Ashby of the US Army¹⁰. The detachment's work was the method the 2026 practice calls F3EAD and the period had no name for: every arrest and every rallier was exploited within the week, every captured document translated, every name placed on a chart of the district committee, its sections, its couriers and its families, and the chart returned to the district centre with the next questions⁹. About 1,500 SEEK II kits enrolled every detainee and rallier by fingerprint, iris and face against an offline watchlist⁹; the kits were the one instrument of 2026 the centres kept whole, and the Muster Service's technicians maintained them with their own.

The centres opened behind the posts, not ahead of them. The first DIOCC opened at Phu Tan in An Giang on 16 March 1964, two weeks after the first village post; by the end of June there were about 40, all in districts of the Delta and around Saigon where the posts already stood, and by 31 December about 100 DIOCCs and 20 PIOCCs. In the contested districts of the coastal Centre the province chiefs asked for centres before they had posts, and Hieu refused them; that refusal, which Ashby supported and this headquarters endorsed, kept the method out of the villages where a file could only be built from a rallier's grudge (EC006-12 abandoned)¹⁰.

The advisers came by another road. In January 1964 the Department of the Army opened the Selected Advisory Corps to volunteers and convened its first boards in February: captains, majors and senior NCOs with a combat or command record, assessed over two weeks at Fort Bragg for temperament, judgment and aptitude for language, of whom about half passed. The first class of about 1,100 began the course on 3 February: six weeks of advising, about 24 weeks of Vietnamese at the Defense Language Institute, and four of weapons, combat medicine and the calling of fire and air. They reached the field from 1 October, replacing advisers on the old short tour as each left, and by the end of December about a third of the field posts below corps were held by men who had chosen to be there, could speak to their counterparts without an interpreter and would stay eighteen months⁹. Among them was Captain Samuel J. Whitcomb, a Korean War NCO commissioned in 1956, who took over the Can Giuoc subsector on 9 October, four months after the killings there, and on his first night asked the district chief, in Vietnamese, which families still held their titles at the district office and why. The Front noticed the new advisers before this headquarters measured them: a COSVN security bulletin of December 1964, captured in 1966, warned district committees that "the new American advisers speak our language and must be assumed to understand what is said in front of them"¹⁰.

#### The Officer Selection Board and the first board season
*1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Joint General Staff at Tan Son Nhut; the Thu Duc Reserve Officers School and the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · new · EV007-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The officer decree was the fourth signed in the session of 1 January 1964, and the generals who signed it understood it least. It made every promotion from first lieutenant to colonel, and every command of a battalion or regiment, the decision of an Officer Selection and Promotion Board under the Joint General Staff, sitting in panels of five senior officers on written criteria; it required that every officer be rated twice a year by his commander and by his commander's commander on a form that allowed no more than a third of any senior rater's officers in the top block; it gave the board the power to relieve a commander for cause after a thirty-day review, opened a commission to sergeants with a combat record and a recommendation, abolished the ninth-grade diploma as a bar to the rank of major, and required that the President choose each new general and each division commander from a ranked list of three. Article 14, the one this headquarters and the US Mission fought for, put the general-officer list under the Aid Compliance Board's condition: a division whose commander was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds for its operations⁶.

The board's first chairman was Brigadier General Phan Khac Minh, a French-trained infantry officer of fifty-one who had run the personnel directorate of the General Staff in 1955–57 and been sent to command a training camp by Diem for refusing to promote the Can Lao's men ahead of the list. Beside the board sat an American cell of about 40 under Colonel Robert E. Lindqvist, who had built the evaluation system's trainers' course at Fort Benning and had never served in Asia; his first report to this headquarters said that the hard part of the reform was not the form but persuading a colonel to give a nephew a middle rating, and that he had no idea how it would be done¹¹.

The board inherited the officers that seven years of the education had made. The classes of 1957–63 had passed the branch basic course after Thu Duc or Dalat, and the first four of them the career course as well, so that in January 1964 most of the ARVN's rifle companies and many of its artillery batteries were commanded by lieutenants and captains who had been taught to make a fire plan, to scout a route, to run a range and to write an order, in Vietnamese, to the American standard of 2026⁶. Above them the army was what it had been: about two-thirds of the battalion commanders had been appointed by a corps or division commander for reasons of his own, about a third of the battalions were commanded by captains because there were not enough majors, and the generals were the generals of the November coup. One of the captains was Bui Quang Trieu, commissioned from Thu Duc in 1957 in the first educated class, who had commanded a company of the 5th Division's 9th Regiment for three years and was rated in the top block by both his raters in the first season¹¹.

The first season ran from April to September. The panels read about 9,000 evaluation reports, promoted about 6,500 officers, nearly all of them on the old seniority with the new reports beside it, and published on 30 June the army's first central command list: 61 battalion commands and 9 regimental commands, about a third of those falling vacant in the year. The corps commanders were asked to nominate and did; about half of their nominees were selected, and the rest of the list came from officers no corps commander had put forward. The career-course gate held the majors' grade to the officers already through the course, and the shortage of majors that the historical army met with captains in command was met here with captains as well, for another year and a half. The schools took their price at the same time: the career course at Thu Duc and the staff course that opened at Dalat in September drew about one officer in fifteen out of the units and about 120 of the army's best captains and majors as instructors, in the year the summer offensive was being prepared¹¹.

The coups tested the board twice before the year ended. Khanh's coup came thirty days after the decree. In September, when Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc moved on Saigon, two of the battalions they brought were commanded by officers the board had refused for the June list, and none by an officer it had chosen. Minh told Lindqvist that he had not expected the list to show its worth so soon, and that he expected the generals to have noticed as well¹¹.

#### The Directorate of Defence Industry and the domestic share
*1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Bien Hoa industrial zone, Bien Hoa Province; the Go Vap and Thu Duc works, Gia Dinh Province; the Joint General Staff and the Aid Compliance Board, Saigon · new · EV008-1*
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Republic owned what no Southeast Asian state of its size owned: an electric-arc steelworks at Bien Hoa with two 30-tonne furnaces, a billet caster and a mill rolling bar, rod, light sections and light plate; a forge and heat-treatment shop beside it; an arsenal with four case-and-bullet lines and two forging lines for mortar and 105mm projectile bodies; engine, vehicle and weapons rebuild works; a steel boatyard at Thu Duc; fabrication shops for bridge spans and fortification steel; and, at Go Vap and in the Bien Hoa zone, a communications plant licensed for the AN/PRC-10, a dry-cell and storage-battery works, a wire and cable mill, a transistor assembly plant owned by an American firm, and a signal rebuild depot. About 35,000–50,000 people worked in the metal trades and 20,000–35,000 in electronics, with about 1,800–2,800 engineers between them¹². The same session of the junta that decreed land to the tiller and the rural police placed the state's share of all of it under a Directorate of Defence Industry in the Ministry of Defence, and named as Director Vuong Dinh Toan, a metallurgist trained at the École Centrale who had built the Bien Hoa melt shop¹³.

For its first fourteen months the base had more capacity than customers, and that was the whole of its problem. The RVNAF's Ordnance and Signal services drew their ammunition, radios, batteries, wire and spares from American stocks under the Military Assistance Program, free and delivered, and no Vietnamese quartermaster would pay piasters for a cartridge case the Americans gave him. The arsenal ran its case lines at about a third of capacity in 1964, selling to the National Police and the Combat Youth what the Army would not buy, and the steelworks ran at about half for want of scrap, rolling 100,000–150,000 tonnes a year, most of it reinforcing bar and the pickets and wire of the outpost programme¹². The agricultural machinery shops did better than the arsenal, because a farmer who owned his land had a reason to buy a pump: about 3,000 diesel pumps and small engines went to owner-cultivators in An Giang, Vinh Long and Phong Dinh in 1964–65, bought on credit from the Agricultural Credit Office against the new titles¹³.

The fix was a rule, and this headquarters wrote it. Colonel Howard B. Teague, the Ordnance Corps officer who headed the advisory element to the Directorate, proposed in November 1964 that a fixed share of the RVNAF's requirement in eight categories be bought from the Directorate with counterpart piasters before any American stock was drawn: small-arms cartridge cases and bullets, 60mm and 81mm mortar bodies, 105mm projectile bodies, field wire, radio dry cells, PF and hamlet radios, construction steel and bridge spans. I sent it to CINCPAC, and on 1 March 1965 the Aid Compliance Board made it a condition of the counterpart-fund release: a quarter of the requirement in 1965, a third in 1966 and half from 1968. The American ordnance staff at Long Binh objected that domestic cases cost about a fifth more than the American article and were unproven, and both objections were true in 1965¹³. The rule held because the Board held, as it held for the land titles and the province chiefs.

The base also had a price in men. About 31,000–45,000 of its workers were of military age, and every one needed a deferment that the mobilization decrees of 1964 did not provide¹². The Directorate's deferment list became a market within a year: in Bien Hoa in the spring of 1965 a place on it cost about two months of a skilled worker's wage, paid to a clerk in the Directorate's personnel office, and the Commission Against Corruption opened its first case in the factories in May. The plants also took, at better pay, the electricians, machinists and radio technicians the Signal and Ordnance Corps had expected to draft. By July 1965 the communications plant had delivered about 5,000 PRC-10s and 6,000 hamlet radios, the battery works about 600,000 dry cells, and the wire mill about 40,000 kilometres of field wire, most of it to the territorial forces of the held belt¹³.

The Front read the base wrongly and organised it rightly. A COSVN study captured in Bien Hoa in 1966 called the plants "American factories employing Vietnamese labour", which they were not; its proselytizing section had by then put cells into the arsenal's case lines and the battery works, and was collecting dues from workers whose brothers were in the ARVN¹³. It was the first time since 1954 that the Front had an industrial working class in the South to organise, and it set about it as the Party in the North had been taught to.

#### ◆ The ten-week cycle and the course number
*1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the national and division training centres; the RF/PF training centres of the provinces; the Muster Service and the Commission, Saigon · new · EV009-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The basic course was already seven years old when this headquarters opened, and the work of 1964 was to keep it honest under a war that wanted men faster than it could train them. At Quang Trung on 6 January 1964 the cycle that began was the fifty-sixth since 1957: about 1,200 recruits in four companies, each company in platoons of about 40 under two Vietnamese drill sergeants, ten weeks on the 2026 programme in Vietnamese, with the M4A1 and its CompM5 on the ranges from the fourth week, the M249 for the men the battalions would make gunners, the field exercise the Americans called the Forge in the ninth, and in the second week the block that every recruit remembered, which taught him to put a tourniquet high and tight on a friend's leg in the dark in under a minute. The senior drill sergeant of the academy that made those sergeants, Sergeant Major Tran Van Hai, had been in its first class in 1957; the advisory detachment of eleven Americans under Lieutenant Colonel Raymond F. Coyle watched, scored and did not teach, by the rule that a recruit would take his orders from a Vietnamese². In the provinces, the RF/PF centres ran the six-week territorial course in platoon intakes, so that a PF platoon raised in a village went to the centre together and came back together¹.

What the course could not make was a squad leader, and in 1964 it cost the army the sergeants who could. The academy and the centres held about 2,000–3,000 of the ARVN's best NCOs as drill sergeants when the battalions were being broken in Phuoc Long and Quang Ngai, and about 1,300–3,600 of them were men the battalions would otherwise have kept in 1964–66. The Joint General Staff asked twice, in June and November 1964, for a third of them back. The training command refused the first request with the advisory chief's support and met the second by moving 400 drill sergeants of the 1957–59 classes to the battalions on promotion and replacing them from the battalions' own corporals, which was the first turn of what became the drill sergeant's circle².

The rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was the course's hardest part, and it was enforced by a number. From April 1964 every man on a muster roll carried the number of the cycle he had passed, and the dawn musters of the Muster Service checked it against the centres' rolls. In September 1964 the muster of a 22nd Division battalion in Binh Dinh found 212 privates whose cycle numbers belonged to a cycle at the division's training centre that the centre's own ration accounts showed had been fed for four weeks, not ten; the centre's commandant had been sending men to the battalions after the rifle range and drawing the remaining six weeks' rations. The Commission charged him in October and the board relieved him in November. The 212 went back to the centre and finished the course. That was the first of eleven such cases in 1964–65, and the advisers estimated that a fifth to a third of the centres' qualification figures in those years were false in some degree². The figures were scored by the men they rated, as every figure in this war was; the course number was the one check that did not depend on them.

The Americans' course arrived in Vietnam in their recruits and asked nothing of this headquarters. The advisers of 1964 and the Marines and paratroopers of 1965 had taken the 2026 basic course of their service, the riflemen and also the clerks, cooks and truck drivers for whom it was all the combat training they would ever have¹. The first record of it in this theatre is an adviser's report from Kien Hoa in October 1964: a Signal Corps radio repairman, wounded in the thigh by a mine fragment on the road to Mo Cay, put his own tourniquet on before the medic reached him and was alive at the province hospital two hours later, and his adviser forwarded the report because the man had never been taught first aid anywhere but in basic². By the summer of 1965 the field hospitals of the build-up were receiving American wounded with tourniquets on as a matter of course, and the surgeons at Nha Trang were already complaining about how long some had been left on.

#### Operation TIDE TABLE
*6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · The II Corps coast from Qui Nhon to Cam Ranh Bay (Binh Dinh, Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa Provinces) and the Ca Mau coast of An Xuyen Province · new · EV003-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The first Global Hawk orbit went up from Clark Air Base on 6 January 1964 and took station off the II Corps coast, about 60 nautical miles out between Qui Nhon and Cam Ranh Bay, with its radar's maritime mode sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep¹⁴. The operation was named TIDE TABLE. Its quarry was the steel-hulled trawlers of about 100 tons run south from Haiphong and the Do Son peninsula by the PAVN's maritime transport group, Group 759, which became Group 125 of the PAVN Navy in the course of 1964. Its hunters were few: the Vietnamese Navy's Sea Force, with a handful of PC-461-class submarine chasers and PCE escorts out of Qui Nhon and Nha Trang, the motorised and sail junks of the Junk Force's coastal divisions, and, when the Seventh Fleet lent them, a US destroyer or two off the coast. Before January nobody had been looking for the trawlers at all; the coast is about 2,000 kilometres long and carried tens of thousands of fishing junks [M-TNA].

The first track came on the night of 18–19 February. A contact moving at a steady ten knots on a course that had begun in the Gulf of Tonkin two days earlier, running 80 nautical miles offshore past Quang Ngai, turned west at dusk toward the Phu Yen coast; it did not stop, did not fish and did not change speed. The exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut flagged it at 1940, and the Vietnamese Navy's coastal command at Nha Trang sent a PC-461 from her patrol line off Tuy Hoa. The radar held the trawler for seven hours, so the patrol craft did not have to search; she had only to steam. She came up with the trawler at 0310 about four miles off Vung Ro Bay, under a low overcast and in a moderate northeast swell. The trawler's crew ran her toward the beach and fired her demolition charges, as Group 759's standing orders required; the forward hold blew, the after hold did not. A boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of cargo before the fire reached it: Chinese Type 56 carbines and assault rifles, B-40 (RPG-2) launchers and their rockets, 82mm mortar rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm ammunition. Seven of the crew were taken from the water, two of them wounded; nine died in the explosion or drowned. The Vietnamese lost one sailor killed and four wounded to small-arms fire from the beach, where a VC reception party had waited with porters¹⁵.

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven contacts that the cell judged to be trawlers, on the II Corps coast and on the Ca Mau peninsula, where the landings went into the mangrove creeks of the Bo De and the Cua Lon. Four were intercepted: two scuttled or blew up, one was taken nearly intact off Binh Dinh in April by the destroyer USS *Edsall*'s boarding party with a Vietnamese liaison officer, and one ran into the Bo De ahead of a junk division and landed most of its cargo. Six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon, which the radar saw as a reversal of course at about the time the trawler's own radar would have detected the patrol. One landed unobserved in May, on a night when the orbit's aircraft had diverted to Clark with a generator fault and the relief was four hours late¹⁵. The casualty figures above are counts of bodies and prisoners and are reliable; the cargo tonnages are estimates from what was recovered and from the prisoners' statements of their loads, and carry a margin of about a third¹⁶.

The prisoners told the rest. Interrogated at Nha Trang, the crew of the April capture described the landing points, the signal lamps, the reception parties and about two years of earlier voyages, and the Vietnamese Navy found two of the landing beaches in Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa from their directions¹⁵. TIDE TABLE did not starve the Front; the tonnage it stopped in six months was a small fraction of what moved by sea and through Laos. What it ended was the cheapest and most direct route by which heavy weapons reached the coastal provinces of II Corps and the lower Delta, and it ended it by being seen, which is the subject of the next event in this chapter.

#### Operation IRON GATE
*6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Saigon port and Tan Son Nhut; the Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the division training centres and the province RF/PF training camps of IV and III Corps, then countrywide · new · EV004-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The first ship of the rearmament, MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez*, berthed at the Khanh Hoi wharves in Saigon on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines, their Aimpoint CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249 light machine guns and 320 M110A2 marksman rifles, and about 380 tonnes of 5.56mm and 7.62mm ammunition. MACV called the issue Operation IRON GATE, because the first rule of it was that no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate in exchange. The second rule was the zeroing range. Every company that drew carbines drew them by name against its muster roll, fired each one at 25 metres until the dot sat on the point of aim, and handed in its M1 Garands, M1 and M2 carbines, BARs and Thompsons before it left. Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn, chief of the small-arms new-equipment training team, set the course at five days for an infantry company and three for a Popular Forces platoon, and refused to shorten it when the corps commanders asked¹⁷.

The order of issue was the order of the war's danger. The ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions in the Delta and the Regional and Popular Forces of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac provinces drew first, in January and February; the Airborne Brigade, the Marine battalions and the Ranger battalions of the general reserve in February and March; I and II Corps from April. Khanh's coup of 30 January stopped the issue for nine days and cost more: three of the division commanders who had sent their companies to the ranges were replaced, and their successors recalled the companies to garrison the province towns against a counter-coup before the course was finished. Venn's teams counted about 2.4 million Vietnamese training man-days in the first half of 1964 and reckoned that about a fifth of them were lost to the coup and its aftermath, in units that drew carbines and went back to their posts without zeroing them¹⁷.

By 30 June about 310,000 carbines had been issued, about 180,000 of them to the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles to the regular infantry; the marksmen's course at Quang Trung, three weeks long, had graduated its first 1,100. The depots took back about 290,000 old weapons. About a third of them went, at the Directorate's insistence and over this headquarters' objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia, who were not covered by the rearmament and wanted something better than shotguns; the rest were crated at the Go Vap arsenal. On the ranges of the Delta the change was visible in an afternoon. A Popular Forces platoon at Can Duoc that had fired its Garands at a 100-metre target and hit it about once in four shots put most of its rounds into the same target by the third morning with carbines its smallest men could hold steady, and asked to be allowed to keep the spent cases as proof to show the village. The Front's province committee in Long An reported the new weapons to the region in February and called them a "trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty"; it ordered its local-force companies to capture one for study, and the first was taken on 22 March in an ambush at Rach Kien¹⁷.

#### Khanh's coup
*30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · changed from history · EV000-1*
*Initiative: neither*

Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh without firing a shot. He acted with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were put under house arrest on a charge of plotting a French-backed neutralist settlement, and Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho was detained. The one death was Major Nguyen Van Nhung, Minh's aide, executed after the takeover. Khanh kept Minh as a figurehead chief of state. The generals were tried in closed session on 28–29 May and found guilty only of "lax morality"¹⁸.

The coup was not caused by the decrees of 1 January; it was the product of the generals' rivalries and Khanh's ambition, and it came on the historical date. But the reforms shaped what followed. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay gave Khanh their quiet support in the hope of a government that would let the decrees lapse. In February Khanh asked Commissioner Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals; Khoa replied that the Commission had no evidence against them and would bring no case without it. Khanh moved to replace him, and the American co-chair of the Aid Compliance Board refused the concurrence the decree required⁴. The generals went before a military court instead, on the "lax morality" charge of the historical record.

Washington was surprised but quick to back the new man; President Johnson sent Khanh a personal note on 2 February¹⁸, which in this war carried the condition, at the urging of the US Mission and this headquarters, that the United States would support no government that repealed either decree⁴. Khanh, who needed American backing more than he needed the landlords, accepted it and kept both. The coup mattered less for what Khanh did than for the precedent it set: a second violent change of government within three months, carried out by officers against officers, and accepted by the United States. Khanh moved, as every coup-maker had, to place his men in the provinces, and the decree of 1 January now stood in his way. In March, Secretary of Defense McNamara visited and endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 of 17 March 1964 committed the United States to the survival of an independent, non-communist South Vietnam. In June 1964 General William C. Westmoreland, Harkins's deputy since January, took command of MACV, and General Maxwell D. Taylor replaced Henry Cabot Lodge as ambassador in July.

In this war Khanh's coup was the first test of the police and provincial decrees, and it came thirty days after them, before the Board had published a list. On the morning of 30 January Khanh's officers detained the new Director General of National Police, Vo Dinh Cuong, as they had detained the police chief on the historical course, and in the first week of February Khanh named "acting" chiefs in eleven provinces and districts. The US Mission and this headquarters treated the two decrees as part of the condition Johnson's note attached to the land and anti-corruption decrees: the Aid Compliance Board gave notice that a province whose chief was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds once the first list was published, and Cuong was released on 2 February and restored to his office. Khanh kept the Board, because he had no candidate for its chairmanship and saw a reputation for reform in it, and kept his acting chiefs until the list replaced nine of the eleven in April and May; the other two placed well enough to be appointed from it⁵. It was a partial failure of the reform's first test and a partial success, and it set the pattern that held until 1970: the Board survived each government because the government that inherited it found it cheaper to keep than to break.

The officer decree was tested in the same month, and in the same way. On 3 February Khanh's staff sent the Joint General Staff a list of 23 promotions and six regimental commands for officers who had served the coup; the board, which had existed for 34 days and had not yet read a single evaluation report, had no list of its own to set against it. The Mission added the officer decree to the condition of Johnson's note, and Minh offered Khanh a bargain that set the pattern for the next six years: the board confirmed the eleven promotions that met its written criteria and refused the twelve that did not, and Khanh's regimental commanders kept their commands until the first command list in June, which confirmed four of the six¹¹. The generals were the exception. Until the first ranked list in January 1965 Khanh made the generals he needed, and after it every junta learned to place its partners among the three names rather than outside them. The reform's first lesson to Saigon was that the board could be bargained with and could not be ignored; its first lesson to this headquarters was that the list for division command was exactly as strong as the aid condition behind it.

#### ◆ The first posts and the Front's answer
*1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · new · EV002-2*
*Initiative: both*

The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village. From February the platoon beside the post carried M4A1 carbines with red-dot sights, drawn under IRON GATE; the constables, whom the rearmament did not cover, kept the carbines of 1950¹⁷. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads⁸. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered⁵.

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected. A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required⁵.

Where the platoon was there, it now held. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about nine in ten held, against about four in five that the programme's design would have given them with the platoons' old weapons. The case that the Delta's advisers told for a year was the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140 against a post whose PF platoon had zeroed its carbines in March. The platoon's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and the company's first assault, which on the Front's own experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause; the second was not pressed, and the company withdrew before the Regional Forces boat company from Ham Long arrived at 0320, leaving eleven dead on the bund and taking its wounded. The Front learned that lesson faster than this headquarters expected. From December its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in, and, against the posts that held, the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts¹⁷.

The weapons the Front took changed with its targets. Of the roughly 300 weapons lost with the police posts, nearly all were the constables' old carbines; but across the countryside the outposts that did fall, the Popular Forces deserters who walked away with their arms, and the ARVN battalions ambushed in the summer of 1965 gave the Front between about 10,000 and 30,000 M4A1 carbines by the end of 1965, with their sights¹⁷. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and each one needed the Republic's own 5.56mm to fire.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed⁵. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

From the summer of 1964 the Special Branch's harvest had a second layer. The district centres that opened behind the posts took each arrest and each rallier and asked a different question of them: not who in this hamlet belongs to the Front, but who sends the hamlet's cadres their orders, who collects the tax above the village, and where the district committee's couriers sleep. Of the roughly 1,900 people the Branch arrested in the held belt by July 1965, about 260 were members of district or village party committees or their security and finance sections, against perhaps 100 that the Branch's own folders would have reached without the centres' charts. The biometric enrolment caught its first case in December 1964 at Cho Moi in An Giang, a finance cadre released by a magistrate in August on a bribe paid through a cousin, who was rearrested in November under a new name with a new identity card and found by the kit in forty seconds¹⁰. The Commission prosecuted the magistrate's clerk in 1965. The Front's answer to the centres came, as its answer to the posts had, within a season: from January 1965 its district committees in the held belt began moving their offices out of the villages to the contested edge, and the Dinh Tuong clerk who had copied informant cards in 1964 was the first of the penetrations the Branch would find in the centres rather than the posts.

In this war the post's radio was not alone. From April 1964 the PF platoon beside each post carried its own set, a PRC-10 from the Go Vap plant, and about half the villages of the held belt had a hamlet radio on the district net, with dry cells from the battery works exchanged at the district every week. At Long Huu on 17 November it was the platoon's PRC-10 that called the Ham Long boats at 0143, three minutes after the first assault; on the old course the call would have been a flare and a runner, and the boats would have come at dawn. The Front learned the radio in a season, as it learned the carbine. From early 1965 the reconnaissance cells of its district companies reported which posts had a set on the air and which did not, and by the summer of 1965 posts in the held belt that could call were being attacked about a third less often than posts that could not. The sets and batteries also leaked: by August 1965 Go Vap dry cells were being found in the firing circuits of the Front's mines in Long An and Dinh Tuong, bought in the markets from PF soldiers and from the warehouses that issued them¹³.

The platoons beside the posts were trained men, and that was what made them platoons. Nearly every PF platoon of the held belt in 1964 had been raised since 1957 and had gone through the six-week territorial course together, and the course's first lesson after the rifle was the first ten minutes: hold fire until the target is in the light, fire in pairs so that one man is always loaded, report on the radio what you see rather than that you are attacked, and keep the ammunition for the second assault¹. At Long Huu on 17 November it was the second assault that the platoon's corporal, Pham Van Tho, trained at the Ben Tre centre in 1962, had kept a third of his ammunition for; and the two PF soldiers wounded on the bund were alive at 0320 when the boats came, both with tourniquets on, where on the old course the advisers would have expected one of them to have bled out before dawn. Across the held belt I estimate that the posts with their platoon held about nineteen attacks in twenty in 1964–65 rather than about nine in ten, and that the PF platoons lost about a fifth fewer of their wounded. The constables, whom the course did not reach, died as before; the posts without platoons, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia remained what the Front attacked, and the gap between the posts inside both grants and the posts outside them widened. And from 1965 some of the men the Front's district companies sent against the posts had been trained on the same course: PF deserters who had walked home with their training if not their carbines, whom the companies made their squad leaders².

#### The coordinate cell and the first folders
*3 Mar 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · Tan Son Nhut air base, Saigon; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; the Laotian panhandle and North Vietnam below the 20th parallel · new · EV005-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The first thing the new bombers produced in this war was not a strike but a room. On 3 March 1964 a detachment of eleven Air Force and Army officers and twenty-six enlisted analysts opened the coordinate cell in a Quonset hut beside Harold Kessling's radar and imagery exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut, under Major Edwin T. Sorrell, a geodesist from the Aeronautical Chart and Information Center who had spent the 1950s tying the Pacific islands to a single survey. Its task was to turn the U-2S's GPS-true imagery into coordinates a GBU-31 could fly to: a bridge abutment, a ferry slip, a fuel tank farm, the revetment of an anti-aircraft gun, each measured to within a few metres and filed with its photographs and its repair history. Nothing in the American inventory of 1964 had needed coordinates of that kind before, and nobody in the theater knew how to make them; Sorrell trained his first twenty analysts himself, on imagery of Tan Son Nhut and the Saigon bridges, against points his survey teams had fixed with receivers on the ground¹⁹.

The cell's first discovery was an error in every map in the country. The Army Map Service's 1:50,000 sheets of Indochina were drawn on the Indian 1960 datum, and the U-2S's coordinates were true to the satellites; at Tan Son Nhut the two differed by several hundred metres, and in the mountains of the panhandle, where the sheets themselves were drawn from French surveys of the 1930s and air photographs, the map placed some ridges and river crossings 300 metres or more from where they stood²⁰. Sorrell's memorandum of 14 May 1964 to the MACV J-2 and the Seventh Air Force's predecessor, the 2nd Air Division, said in one sentence what the cell would spend a decade enforcing: "No coordinate taken from a map is to be given to a guided weapon, and no coordinate given to a guided weapon is to be plotted on a map without conversion." The Army's artillery in the South took a second lesson from the same memorandum: by the end of 1965 the cell had given the II Field Force and the 1st Cavalry Division survey control points at every major base, and its first artillery batteries were laid on true coordinates¹⁹.

By June 1965 the cell held about 1,400 folders: every road and rail bridge between the 20th parallel and the DMZ, the ferries on the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the passes at Mu Gia and Ban Karai, the fuel and ammunition depots around Vinh and Dong Hoi, and the first truck parks the U-2S found in the Laotian panhandle. About a quarter to a third of the U-2S's hours went to re-imaging known targets for the folders rather than searching for new ones, which is a cost this command paid knowingly²⁰. On Guam the 3960th Strategic Wing received its first B-52Js with GBU-31 and GBU-38 kits in storage from the spring of 1964, and the crews trained on ranges in the Marianas against coordinates the cell sent them¹⁹. Through all of 1964 and the first half of 1965 no folder was struck. The aircraft were ready; the authority was not. That interval, sixteen months of a precision bombing force with a target list and nothing to do, is the whole of the provision's history in this phase, and its consequence is in the next.

#### The first titling teams
*16 Mar – 31 Dec 1964 · An Giang, Phong Dinh, Vinh Long, Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · new · EV001-2*
*Initiative: allied*

The first 40 mobile titling teams of about 25 men each took the field on 16 March 1964, working outward from province capitals and main roads into villages the district could reinforce by day, each escorted by a Regional Forces company armed with M1 and M2 carbines and M1918A2 automatic rifles. The first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen in An Giang on 21 April, in the Hoa Hao country where the Front was weakest, and the first in a contested district at Phung Hiep in Phong Dinh on 11 May. By July 70 teams were in the field, the most the provinces could escort without stripping their daytime reaction forces. They moved by road, in the LCM-6 and LCVP craft of the River Assault Groups and by hired sampan, and to villages beyond both by CH-21C and UH-1B helicopters when the operations staff would release them⁴.

By 31 December 1964 titles had been issued for about 210,000 hectares to about 170,000 families, about a fifth of the target, almost all of it in hamlets held by the government at night or contested only lightly: An Giang, the riverine districts of Phong Dinh and Vinh Long along the Bassac, the inner districts of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and the coastal plain around Hoi An and Tam Ky in Quang Nam. The rent stop held at the main-season harvest of December 1964 in most districts the government controlled; landlords' agents collected under Regional Forces escort in parts of Vinh Long, Vinh Binh and Dinh Tuong, which led to the first suspension a few months later. Twenty-three team members were killed by mines and ambush during the year⁴. The figure the Mission reported to Washington each month was titles issued; this headquarters reported it with the warning that it measured the government's paperwork, not the peasants' allegiance.

#### The dawn musters
*7 Apr – 31 Dec 1964 · ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions and the Regional and Popular Forces of the Delta, then countrywide · new · EV001-4*
*Initiative: allied*

The Pay and Muster Verification Service's first 20 teams began unannounced dawn musters of the ARVN 21st Division's 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments around Bac Lieu and Soc Trang on 7 April 1964, and of the 7th and 9th Divisions in Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long in May; by the autumn 60 teams were mustering about 480 units a month across all four corps. Each man was enrolled on an identity card with his photograph and fingerprints, and from August the Service's paymasters paid the enrolled units of IV Corps at the parade rather than through their commanders. By 31 December about 41,000 names had been struck from the rolls of a force carried at about 514,000, about 8%; the rate ran from about 4% in the Airborne and Marine battalions to about 15% in some Regional Forces companies of the Delta⁴. These are counts of men present against names on the roll, not estimates, and they are the best figures this headquarters had ever had for the army it advised.

Washington read them as a smaller army. Secretary McNamara, visiting in May, was briefed on the first returns and told the President that the RVNAF's real strength was below its reported strength by a margin that would grow as the musters spread. In the units mustered twice, the second muster found few ghosts, because commanders had struck them before the teams came. The Commission opened 412 cases by the end of the year, almost all against company and battalion commanders and province finance officers, and brought 61 to trial⁴.

#### The Can Giuoc killings
*9 Jun – 14 Jul 1964 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · new · EV001-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

In May 1964 titling teams from Tan An began work in the villages of Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, the rice districts of Long An between Saigon and the sea, where the survey of December 1963 had found most of the strategic hamlets destroyed and the Front's infrastructure intact. The province chief, under pressure from Saigon for a showcase close to the capital, sent the teams into hamlets the government held by day only and posted the claimants' names in the village offices, as the procedure then required⁴. Each team worked under the escort of a Long An Regional Forces company that withdrew to its post at dusk.

On the night of 9 June armed propaganda teams of the Can Giuoc district committee, with the Long An provincial security section and screened by a company of the provincial 506th Local Force Battalion, entered three hamlets of Phuoc Lai village, read out names from the posted list, and shot four men who had claimed titles and the village land committee's secretary. Over the following five weeks the same pattern was repeated in eleven hamlets of the two districts: by 14 July 37 title holders and 9 members of village land committees had been killed, and several hundred families had brought their titles back to the district office at Can Giuoc rather than keep them in the house. The Regional Forces companies, posted in mud forts along Route 5 and the canal, did not move at night, and no reaction force came. The figures are this headquarters' count from district and province reports, cross-checked by the American province adviser, and are a floor⁴.

The killings were the land programme's first lesson in what it did not change. Teams stopped posting names in hamlets not held at night, and titles in contested hamlets were thereafter held at the district office until the hamlet was secured or the owner asked for his. A COSVN directive captured near Duc Hoa in August told district committees not to obstruct the titling, to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper", to keep collecting the Front's agricultural tax, and to make examples only where a hamlet's people began to inform⁴. In Can Giuoc the titling was suspended until the following spring. It was the first evidence that the reform's value in any district was exactly the value of the security behind it.

#### The camps, the bases and the towns: Nam Dong, Bien Hoa and Song Be
*6 Jul 1964 – 12 May 1965 · Nam Dong CIDG camp west of Da Nang; Bien Hoa air base; Song Be and Phuoc Binh, Phuoc Long Province · changed from history · EVCP01-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

Three attacks are told together because they showed one thing three ways: the enemy chose the night and the target, and what held was whatever was already inside the wire.

At 02:30 on 6 July 1964 about two battalions of VC and PAVN, some 800–900 men, attacked the CIDG camp at Nam Dong near the Laotian border, held by Special Forces Detachment A-726 under Captain Roger H. C. Donlon with an Australian warrant officer, 311 Montagnard strikers, 50 Nungs and seven Vietnamese Special Forces. The outer perimeter was penetrated and many strikers deserted or went over; the inner perimeter, held by the Americans and the Nungs, survived five hours of close fighting until the attackers withdrew at dawn. Two Americans and the Australian were killed, 58 strikers and Nungs died, more than 100 strikers defected, and 55–62 attackers' bodies lay inside the wire; Donlon became the first American awarded the Medal of Honor in Vietnam²¹. The strikers carried the weapons they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach the CIDG.

On the night of 31 October–1 November, two days before the American election, a VC mortar team fired on Bien Hoa air base for about thirty minutes, destroying five of the B-57Bs parked wingtip to wingtip there since the Tonkin crisis and destroying or damaging as many as 30 aircraft in all; four Americans were killed and 72 people wounded, and the attackers escaped²². In this war an 81mm round burst on the revetment of the U-2S that was in maintenance that night and holed its left wing, fuel cells and forward equipment bay. It was out of service from 1 November to 20 December, and the daily deep look over the panhandle, where the dry season's first trucks were moving, lapsed to twice a week¹⁴. I asked for and got a covered shelter for each U-2S, built by the Seabees by February 1965, and a Regional Forces company for the hamlets on the base's approaches¹⁵. Johnson declined a reprisal on the eve of the election, and the question of who would guard the bases brought the first American combat troops ashore four months later²³.

On 10–11 May 1965 B-2 Front forces, the 763rd Regiment with the 840th Battalion or elements of the 271st–273rd Regiments, attacked Song Be, the capital of Phuoc Long, and the district town of Phuoc Binh. The 36th Ranger Battalion, about 150 territorials and about three dozen American advisers of Detachment B-34 held the town with the gunships of the 197th Aviation Company, and a relieving Ranger battalion arrived by midday on 11 May. The ARVN lost 58 killed, 96 wounded and 83 missing, five Americans were killed and a B-57 was lost; MACV's estimate of up to 1,000 enemy killed and wounded is of the kind A13 warns against²⁴. The Rangers had carried the M4A1 and the M249 since February 1964. I do not adjust the losses, most of which were taken in the first hour to mortars, recoilless rifles and sappers inside the wire, where the squad weapons counted for little²⁵.

#### The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution
*2–11 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · changed from history · EV000-3*
*Initiative: both*

On 30–31 July 1964 South Vietnamese commando boats under the covert OPLAN 34A programme, run by MACV's Studies and Observations Group, raided the North Vietnamese islands of Hon Me and Hon Ngu. On 2 August the destroyer USS *Maddox*, on a DESOTO signals intelligence patrol under Captain John J. Herrick, was attacked in daylight by three P-4 torpedo boats of the 135th Torpedo Squadron. The *Maddox* fired, four F-8 Crusaders from USS *Ticonderoga* attacked the retiring boats, and the North Vietnamese lost four killed and six wounded with three boats damaged; the *Maddox* was struck by one machine-gun round and suffered no casualties. Johnson, as historically, chose a warning rather than retaliation, and ordered the patrol resumed with USS *Turner Joy* in company²⁶.

At Admiral Sharp's request I moved the Global Hawk orbit from the II Corps coast to the Gulf on 3 August. There were no SA-2s in the North and nothing that could reach 60,000 feet, and the aircraft took station about 40 nautical miles east of Hon Me, with the destroyers, the coast from Hon Matt to the mouth of the Song Ma and the open Gulf to the east in its radar's maritime mode¹⁴. On the night of 4 August the *Maddox* and *Turner Joy*, in rough weather with thunderstorms, fired for about two hours at radar contacts they took to be a second attack. The watch in the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut that night was Major Harold Kessling's. His radar log showed the two destroyers and their manoeuvres, a cluster of fishing junks about 30 nautical miles to the west, rain cells moving across the Gulf, and no fast small craft within 40 nautical miles of the ships at any time between 2000 and 2330 local. P-4 boats making attack runs at 40 knots are good radar targets even in a sea state of three and four, and the radar had tracked the three boats of 2 August from their base on the same set. Herrick cabled that the "entire action leaves many doubts"²⁶. Kessling's report reached MACV at about midnight Saigon time and CINCPAC an hour later, which in Washington was the late morning of 4 August, while McNamara and Sharp were still trying to establish what had happened¹⁵.

What followed was Washington's decision, and it went the other way from history. Historically Johnson had decided on retaliation within thirty minutes of the first reports²⁶. Here McNamara told him in the early afternoon that the ships' reports were contradicted by a radar that had watched the whole engagement, that Herrick himself doubted them, and that the Navy could not confirm an attack. Johnson held the strike order and, at about 1900, cancelled it. Operation Pierce Arrow was not flown. The 64 sorties against the torpedo-boat bases and the Vinh oil storage did not happen; Lieutenant (jg) Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Lieutenant (jg) Everett Alvarez was not shot down and taken prisoner that day¹⁵.

The resolution was sent anyway. Johnson had carried a draft since May, an election was three months away, and the attack of 2 August was real. On 5 August he asked Congress for authority to repel armed attack against US forces and to prevent further aggression, citing the 2 August attack on a US destroyer in international waters and Hanoi's support of the war in the South, and he said nothing of the night of the 4th. The debate ran three days longer than history's: Senator Wayne Morse, tipped about the 34A raids, asked why the destroyer had been near Hon Me at all, and Senator Gaylord Nelson's amendment against a land war was again declined by Senator Fulbright on the administration's assurance that no such war was intended. Congress passed the Southeast Asia Resolution on 10 August, 414–0 in the House and 85–3 in the Senate, in the words of the historical text¹⁵. Johnson signed it on 11 August. It was again the only legislative authority for the war that followed, but it rested on one attack that had happened rather than on two of which one had not, and the radar log of 4 August sat in the files at Tan Son Nhut and Honolulu as a record of a false alarm, not as the concealed proof of a pretext. B-57B bombers were moved to Bien Hoa in the aftermath²², within mortar range of the countryside around it.

#### The Vung Tau Charter crisis and the September coup attempt
*16 Aug – 14 Sep 1964 · Saigon, Hue and Da Nang · changed from history · EV000-4*
*Initiative: neither*

Khanh tried to use the Tonkin crisis to make himself president under a new constitution, the Vung Tau Charter of 16 August 1964. Students and the Buddhist movement led by Thich Tri Quang answered with demonstrations and riots in Saigon, Hue and Da Nang, and Khanh conceded the repeal of the charter. At a Military Revolutionary Council meeting on 26–27 August he promised to resign, but the generals could agree on no successor, and a triumvirate of Khanh, Minh and Khiem was formed to rule for two months; Khanh dominated it. Khiem was sent abroad as ambassador to Washington and Minh on a "goodwill tour"²⁷.

Before dawn on 13 September Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc, with about ten battalions recruited among them and four moving up from the Mekong Delta, seized key points in Saigon and announced a new government on the radio while Khanh took refuge in Da Lat. The coup collapsed within a day. US officials publicly backed Khanh; Air Commodore Nguyen Cao Ky, commanding the Vietnamese Air Force, flew jets low over the city and brought loyal marines and paratroopers into Tan Son Nhut, and General Nguyen Chanh Thi supported him; Phat and Duc backed down on the morning of 14 September. No one was killed²⁷. In this war the coup-makers had a programme as well as grievances. General Duc, the IV Corps commander, had learned in August that the Commission had opened a case on the ghost payrolls the musters had found in his corps, and Phat's broadcast of 13 September promised to "suspend the land decree pending review" and to dissolve the "foreign-controlled" Aid Compliance Board. It was the first time a coup had announced a policy toward the countryside, and the policy was the landlords'; the same broadcast promised to "restore the authority of the corps commanders over the provinces", which meant the abolition of the Selection Board⁵. Its failure within a day attached the reforms to the side that won: the Young Turks who had saved Khanh took up the land decree as their own cause, and Ky spoke of it in his first public statements as an argument for their claim to govern⁴.

The lasting result was a new power centre: the younger officers, the "Young Turks" around Ky and Thi, who had saved Khanh and now knew they could unmake him²⁷. The war in the countryside was left to commanders whose first concern was the next coup.

The police played a part in this coup that it had never played before. The Special Branch, which watched the generals under its statute as well as the Front, reported to Cuong on 11 September that battalions of the 7th Division were being readied at My Tho without orders, and Cuong passed the report to Khanh and to the US Mission the same night. The warning did not stop the coup, which came on schedule, but on 13 September the Saigon police stayed in their stations and the Field Force companies in their barracks, on Cuong's order, rather than joining either side, and the Mission's knowledge of the movement two days in advance let Ambassador Taylor tell the coup-makers within hours that the United States would not recognise them⁵. A police that was not one faction's instrument had raised the cost of a coup by a little, and the plotters' programme of abolishing the Board had made the Young Turks who defeated them its defenders, as it had made them the land decree's.

#### The Hop Tac programme
*Sep 1964 – mid-1965 · Saigon and the surrounding provinces · changed from history · EV000-5*
*Initiative: allied*

In September 1964, at the urging of the US Mission and Westmoreland, the government launched Hop Tac ("cooperation"), a plan to pacify a ring of territory around Saigon outward in phases, the "oil spot" concept applied to the capital region. The priority was explicit: concentrate resources on the Saigon area and accept little progress elsewhere for the time being. By then the trend elsewhere was plain. Ambassador Taylor's mission reported on 6 October that the provinces rated unsatisfactory for pacification had risen from 7 in July to 14 in August, and named the diversion of resources to Hop Tac as one possible cause. The mission nonetheless recommended unanimously that Hop Tac be given full support²⁸.

The programme never reached its schedule. Its ARVN units were repeatedly pulled away by coups and coup alerts, its Vietnamese chain of command changed with each government, and the Viet Cong infrastructure in Gia Dinh, Long An and the provinces north of the capital was left largely intact. Hop Tac carried the titling teams into the inner ring of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and by mid-1965 about 38,000 hectares there had been titled; in the hamlets of the inner ring the Front's tax collectors found fewer families willing to pay without coercion, and the province advisers' reports of volunteered information rose from a trickle to a few dozen reports a month⁴. The provincial reform reached the ring at the same time: Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An received province chiefs from the list between May and July 1964, and about 160 police posts were settled in the inner districts by mid-1965, with a Special Branch section in each district town. In the inner ring of Gia Dinh the Special Branch broke two of the Front's village committees and the courier line that ran from the Hoc Mon rice fields into the city's northern wards, and the hamlets around the posts were held at night through the coups of 1964–65 even when the ARVN battalions assigned to Hop Tac were pulled away⁵. That did not save the programme. By mid-1965 Hop Tac was effectively moribund. Its failure foreshadowed the problem of the whole war: pacification required a stable government and a security force that would stay in the hamlets at night, and in 1964 the Republic of Vietnam had neither.

#### Hanoi commits the regulars; the Trail and the first air war in Laos
*Sep 1964 – Apr 1965 · North Vietnam, the Laotian panhandle and the Central Highlands · changed from history · EV000-6*
*Initiative: enemy*

The Ninth Plenum of the Lao Dong Party Central Committee, in December 1963, had resolved to seek a decisive military victory in the South before the United States could intervene in strength. Through 1964 the Politburo under Le Duan acted on it. Infiltration down the Trail, managed since 1959 by the PAVN's Group 559, shifted from regrouped southerners to northern-born conscripts, and in the autumn of 1964 the first complete PAVN regular regiments were ordered south. The 95th Regiment of the 325th Division arrived in Kontum Province in December 1964; the 101st Regiment followed in February 1965 with the division commander, Colonel Nguyen Huu An; the 18th Regiment came a few months later. The division was then broken up among the B3 (Central Highlands) Front and Military Region 5²⁹. These were the first regular North Vietnamese formations committed to the war, and by the US estimate of 1965 about 4,500 PAVN troops and 300 tons of materiel moved through Laos each month³⁰. Both figures are intelligence estimates of their time; the movement was disciplined, at night, under canopy and through a country that US ground forces could not enter.

The decision to commit the regulars had been taken before the land decree existed, and it was not caused by it. The decree gave it a second argument. Party reporting from the Delta through 1964 described the "land trick" as the most dangerous of the enemy's measures, because it threatened the movement's base in exactly the provinces where it was strongest, and Le Duan's line that victory must come before the Americans and before the political base eroded drew strength from it. The schedule of the 325th Division's regiments was set by their training and by the Trail, not by the reform, and the regiments came when they would have come⁴.

The American answer in Laos began on 14 December 1964 with Operation Barrel Roll, a small programme of armed reconnaissance by US aircraft over northern and eastern Laos. On 3 April 1965 the 2nd Air Division opened Operation Steel Tiger against the Trail in the southeastern panhandle³⁰. Both were bound by the fiction of Laotian neutrality under the Geneva Accords of 1962, by the ambassador in Vientiane's control of targets, and by the bar on ground operations across the border. The Trail at this stage was still largely foot and bicycle traffic with truck routes being built; its capacity was growing faster than anything flying over it could reduce.

#### The trawlers turn back; the Sihanoukville route opens
*Sep 1964 – Jun 1965 · Haiphong and the Gulf of Tonkin; the II Corps coast; Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Cambodia · new · EV003-2*
*Initiative: enemy*

Hanoi did not give up the sea at once. After TIDE TABLE's first half-year Group 125 paused through the summer of 1964, and in September, while the Global Hawk orbit was still held in the Gulf of Tonkin after the August crisis, it sent two trawlers south. One landed its cargo at Vung Ro on the night of 21–22 September in a gap the Gulf watch had opened on the II Corps coast; the other was tracked off Binh Dinh after the orbit returned, and was intercepted on 9 November by the Vietnamese Navy escort *Chi Lang* and a Junk Force division off the Tam Quan coast, where her crew beached and burned her. Sixteen of her crew were killed or drowned and four captured; the junk sailors lost two killed. Those are counts, and reliable. In December a third trawler reversed course off Quang Ngai at dusk when a Seventh Fleet destroyer closed her, and went home¹⁵.

The decision to change routes was taken in Hanoi in December 1964 and January 1965. Party records captured later in this war describe the Central Military Commission's conclusion that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night" and that the route was no longer worth the ships and the men. The alternative was already prepared. Sihanouk, who had renounced American aid in November 1963 and was moving toward China, would break diplomatic relations with Washington on 3 May 1965. Through the winter Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators in Phnom Penh arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville, the port the French had built at Kompong Som, with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and Cambodian trucking contractors to carry the cargo inland along Route 4 to Kompong Speu and thence to the border base areas. Historically this arrangement took shape in late 1966. Here the first arms cargo for the Front came ashore at Sihanoukville under a Chinese flag in late June 1965, about eighteen months early, and was trucked to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak by night¹⁵.

Two things followed from it that did not happen historically. The trawler that an Army helicopter pilot, Lieutenant James Bowers, found by chance unloading at Vung Ro Bay on 16 February 1965, with about 100 tons of arms aboard, was not there to be found; there was no trawler on the II Corps coast that month. And Market Time, which the Navy opened on 11 March 1965 historically because of Vung Ro, opened on the same date here because of TIDE TABLE's record, with a picture of what it was looking for and, increasingly, nothing to find¹⁵. The weapons the sea route would have landed in 1965 still reached the Front, more slowly and by a longer road, and from mid-1965 the enemy's principal line of supply to the southern half of the country ran through a neutral port that no American could stop or seize.

#### Operation LONG SPAN: the floods of November 1964
*6 Nov 1964 – 15 Feb 1965 · Route 1 and the coastal railway from Da Nang to Quang Ngai; Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai Provinces · new · EV008-2*
*Initiative: neither*

In the first week of November 1964 two typhoons and the rains behind them put the coastal plain of Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai under water, in the worst flood in central Vietnam in living memory; the dead were counted in thousands, by some Vietnamese accounts about 7,000, and Route 1 and the coastal railway were cut at dozens of points between the Hai Van pass and Quang Ngai. The ARVN 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and the province garrisons of the coast were cut off from Da Nang by road, and so were the rice and the relief.

In this war the bridges came back from Bien Hoa, in an operation the Directorate called LONG SPAN. The fabrication shops held a standard 30-metre steel truss span and a 12-metre girder span, drawn in 1963 for the outpost and road programme, and the Directorate had about forty of them in stock when the water fell. Between 14 and 30 November the shops cut and drilled about ninety more, and two Vietnamese Navy LSTs and three chartered coasters carried them from the Thu Duc yard to Da Nang and to the beach at Sa Huynh, where the 2nd Division's engineers and two Vietnamese contractor crews met them. Route 1 was open to the 2nd Division's trucks from Da Nang to Quang Ngai on 21 December, about six weeks after the flood and, by the Directorate's own estimate, two months sooner than spans ordered from Okinawa would have reopened it¹³. The railway took longer, as it would have in any case, and its last bridge south of Tam Ky was not back until 15 February.

The ninety-day road became the Directorate's term for its standard: any bridge the Front or the weather took on a national route was to be back in ninety days, and on Route 1 it usually was. It changed no battle in Phase I. Ba Gia was lost in May on ground the road did not decide. What it changed was the winter of 1964–65 on the coast: the rice of the Quang Ngai lowlands went north by truck in January instead of rotting in the district towns, the relief reached the flooded districts of Dien Ban and Duy Xuyen in December instead of February, and the Front's Region 5 committee, which had planned its winter around a coast cut off until the dry season, found the 2nd Division supplied by road¹³. On the historical course the flood was one of the reasons the coastal Centre was so nearly lost in the spring of 1965. Here it was a smaller one.

#### Saigon's governments and the war in the city
*19 Dec 1964 – 19 Jun 1965 · Saigon · changed from history · EVCP01-1*
*Initiative: both*

Six months of Saigon's politics are told here as one event, because they were one process: the generals unmaking every government they made, while the Front's city teams showed they could strike anywhere in the capital. In the week before Christmas 1964 the Young Turks, with Khanh's acquiescence, dissolved the High National Council and arrested several of its members; Taylor dressed the officers down, Khanh attacked him publicly, and the Mission's relations with the junta reached their lowest point²⁷. At 17:45 on 24 December a car bomb of about 90 kg under the Brink Bachelor Officers' Quarters killed two Americans and wounded about 60–100 people; Johnson refused a reprisal, saying he had "never felt that this war will be won from the air", and changed his mind within six weeks³¹.

On 27 January 1965 Khanh and the Armed Forces Council removed Huong. On 16 February the council appointed Dr Phan Huy Quat prime minister, and on 19–20 February, in the confusion of a failed coup by General Lam Van Phat and Colonel Pham Ngoc Thao, who was a communist agent, it voted Khanh out; he left the country on 25 February²⁷. On 30 March a car carrying about 300 pounds of plastic explosive blew up outside the US embassy; 22 people were killed, among them two Americans, and 183 wounded. Rolling Thunder was already running, and there was no reprisal³². On 12 June Quat, deadlocked with the chief of state Phan Khac Suu, handed power back to the generals; on 14 June Major General Nguyen Van Thieu became chief of state, and on 19 June Air Vice Marshal Nguyen Cao Ky prime minister³³.

In this war the sequence ran on its historical dates and for its historical reasons. One thing in it was different: each government that inherited the decrees of 1 January 1964 kept them, because Johnson's condition of February 1964 and the Aid Compliance Board had made repeal dearer than survival, and the September plotters' promise to repeal them had attached the reforms to the Young Turks who defeated them⁴.

#### The battle of Binh Gia
*28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · changed from history · EV000-9*
*Initiative: enemy*

Binh Gia, a Catholic village of northern refugees in Phuoc Tuy Province east of Saigon, was the site of the Viet Cong's first large set-piece battle. The attackers were the 271st and 272nd Regiments (then called the 1st and 2nd), the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, with the 80th Artillery Detachment and local battalions of the B-2 Front, an estimated 1,800 men. They took the village on 28 December and then waited for the relief. The ARVN committed the 30th, 33rd, 35th and 38th Ranger Battalions and the 4th Marine Battalion of the general reserve, with two artillery platoons and a section of M24 tanks, about 4,300 men, lifted and supported by the US 118th Aviation Company and the gunships of the 68th Assault Helicopter Company³⁴. Every one of those battalions had carried the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 since the spring; the attackers carried Type 56 and SKS rifles, RPD guns and recoilless rifles¹⁷.

The relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal, the artillery badly positioned and air support thin, as they were historically. The rifles did not change the generals' plan, and the plan was the problem. On 30 December a 68th Company gunship was shot down near Quang Giao and its four crewmen killed. On 31 December the 4th Marine Battalion, going to recover the bodies, was ambushed in the rubber by the 271st at a range of 20 to 40 metres, as it was historically, and its commander, Major Nguyen Van Nho, was killed in the first minutes. What followed was different. The battalion's squad guns, three to a company platoon, put a base of fire into the trees that the Marines' BARs could not have sustained, and the companies, instead of being cut apart in the order they had marched, closed into two perimeters within about a quarter of an hour and fought out of the rubber at dusk carrying most of their wounded. The battalion lost about 60–70 killed and 90 wounded, against 122 killed and 71 wounded on the historical course¹⁷. Captain Donald Cook, the Marine adviser, was wounded and captured going back for a wounded Marine, as he was historically, and died in captivity. MACV counted allied losses from 28 December to 6 January at about 140 killed, 220 wounded and 45 missing³⁵. MACV reported four helicopters shot down³⁴. The VC claim of 1,783 killed and captured is no more credible here than it was historically; their own dead, of whom at least 32 were confirmed historically, I put at about 120–200, because the Marines' marksmen and squad guns killed men in the rubber whom the 271st could not carry away³⁶.

The significance was still the form. A Viet Cong formation had held ground for four days against armour, artillery and helicopters, chosen the field, ambushed the general reserve on the road it had to use, and withdrawn intact. The 271st took the honorific "Binh Gia Regiment"³⁴. US observers concluded, as they did historically, that the ARVN could not defeat main-force units of this kind without American firepower; what they could not say, as they said historically, was that a Marine battalion of the general reserve had been destroyed. The 4th Marines were back in the line in February.

#### The American escalation: Flaming Dart, Rolling Thunder, the Marines and the July decision
*7 Feb – 28 Jul 1965 · Pleiku and Qui Nhon; North Vietnam; Da Nang; Washington · changed from history · EVCP01-2*
*Initiative: both*

The six months in which the United States went from an advisory war to a war of its own are told here as one event, because each step made the next one. At 01:50 on 7 February 1965, while McGeorge Bundy was in Saigon and Kosygin in Hanoi, about 300 men of the 30th Company of the VC 409th Sapper Battalion attacked Camp Holloway and the advisers' compound at Pleiku, killing seven Americans and wounding 104 and destroying 10 aircraft. About twelve hours later 49 aircraft from the *Coral Sea* and the *Hancock* struck the barracks at Dong Hoi, the first strike of Flaming Dart; after the VC blew up the enlisted billet at Qui Nhon on 10 February and killed 23 Americans, about 160 aircraft struck Chanh Hoa and Chap Le³⁷. Bundy's report of 7 February argued for "sustained reprisal", and the administration accepted it.

On 2 March Rolling Thunder opened with 104 USAF aircraft against the ammunition depot near Xom Bang while 19 VNAF A-1s struck the naval base at Quang Khe. It was graduated by design: targets chosen at the President's Tuesday meetings, no strikes within 30 nautical miles of Hanoi, 10 of Haiphong or 30 of the Chinese frontier, and the North divided into route packages. Through 24 December 1965 the two services flew 54,139 sorties and dropped 43,207 tons for 180 aircraft lost³⁸.

On 8 March Battalion Landing Team 3/9 came ashore at Da Nang and BLT 1/3 flew in from Okinawa, the nearly 5,000 men of the 9th Marine Expeditionary Brigade under Brigadier General Frederick J. Karch, to guard the air base that Rolling Thunder needed and the Bien Hoa attack had shown to be vulnerable. Their orders, that they "will not, repeat will not, engage in day-to-day actions against the Viet Cong", lasted three weeks: on 1 April the President approved their use in active combat and 18,000–20,000 more support troops, and on 6 May the brigade became the III Marine Amphibious Force³⁹⁴⁰.

On 7 June Westmoreland asked for a major reinforcement and by 22 July the President had authorized 44 battalions⁴¹. On 28 July, at a midday press conference rather than before Congress, Johnson announced that US strength would rise at once from 75,000 to 125,000, with more as requested, that monthly draft calls would rise from 17,000 to 35,000, and that the Airmobile Division would deploy; the reserves were not called and no new taxes were sought⁴². Westmoreland's concept behind it, to halt the losing trend in 1965, take the offensive in 1966 and then destroy the enemy's main forces and base areas, committed the United States to attrition against an enemy who controlled his losses by deciding when to fight.

In this war every step came on its historical date for its historical reason. The provisions changed what Washington knew while it decided, and nothing that it decided²⁵.

#### Operation STILL WATER
*8 Mar – 30 Jun 1965 · Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province, inside the Hop Tac ring northwest of Saigon · new · EV006-2*
*Initiative: allied*

STILL WATER was the first operation in the war aimed at a district committee rather than at the hamlets beneath it, and it was the case for the method. By February 1965 Hoc Mon had 11 village posts settled under the police decree, a Popular Forces platoon in each, about 70 percent of its families on the census, and a district chief from the first list, Major Pham Van Truc; the Front's hamlet cells in the posted villages had mostly been arrested or had left. What remained was the Hoc Mon district committee of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region, about 14 members with a security section, a finance section and about 40 couriers, which had moved in January into the hamlets along the Saigon River at the district's northern edge, toward the Ben Cat and Cu Chi base areas, and governed the posted villages from there by messenger. The DIOCC at Hoc Mon, opened in July 1964 under Captain Ngo Van Tam of the Special Branch with 12 staff and two American advisers, had by February a chart of that committee with 31 names, 19 of them confirmed on three sources, built from 44 arrests, 23 ralliers and a cache of finance-section receipts found by a PF patrol in December¹⁰.

The operation was Tam's plan, approved by Truc and by the Gia Dinh province chief on 1 March. It used no regular troops. Two Police Field Force companies and the Gia Dinh PRU of about 80 men made the arrests; the Regional Forces companies of Hoc Mon and Go Vap, with M4A1 carbines and their M249s, held the cordons at night; a reaction platoon of the Gia Dinh sector and two UH-1Bs of the 120th Aviation Company stood by at Tan Son Nhut for the reaction; and every arrest went first to the biometric kit and then to a magistrate within 72 hours. Captain Whitcomb's counterpart in the next province was not involved; the American subsector adviser at Hoc Mon, Captain Luis Arredondo, was, and by the rule of the decree he did not go on the raids (EC006-13 abandoned)¹⁰.

The turn came on the night of 26–27 March. A finance-section courier arrested on 19 March, a woman of 19 who carried tax receipts in the hem of a conical hat, rallied on the 24th after the Chieu Hoi officer offered her brother's release from a prison on Con Son, to which he had been sent in 1962 under Diem without trial, and named the house in Tan Thoi Nhi where the committee's security section met twice a month. The PRU went in at 0300 under a moon nearly full with two Police Field Force platoons in the cordon. Four men were taken alive in the house, including the section's deputy chief, with a steel box of informant reports on the posts of four villages; a fifth was killed in the paddy behind it after firing on the cordon with a K-50 sub-machine gun; one PRU member died. The box named nine of the committee's own informants inside the posted villages, three of them PF soldiers¹⁰.

By 30 June the operation had arrested 63 people on warrant, of whom the magistrates released 17; 9 of the committee's 14 members were dead, in prison or had rallied, 2 of them killed resisting arrest; the remaining five had moved north into Cu Chi. The Commission's first audit sample, drawn in July from STILL WATER's files, found 4 of the 63 arrests unsupported and the release of one man already paid for, and sent both cases to the Special Court. The Front did not rebuild the Hoc Mon committee inside the district; it governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi until 1968, and the tax receipts captured in 1966 showed collections in the district's posted villages at about a fifth of 1964's¹⁰. In this war STILL WATER was a small operation, 63 arrests and three deaths in four months, and it changed the war in one particular: it showed the Front that the government could follow its leaders out of the villages it had cleared, three years before the historical Phoenix tried to.

#### The Thanh Hoa Bridge and the northern air defences
*3 Apr – 27 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong), Thanh Hoa Province, and the Hanoi region · changed from history · EV000-15*
*Initiative: allied*

On 3 April 1965 a 79-aircraft package led by Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner, 46 F-105s with F-100 escorts, RF-101Cs and KC-135 tankers, attacked the Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa; 16 F-105s carried AGM-12 Bullpup missiles and the rest 750 lb bombs. The bridge was hit repeatedly and stood; traffic stopped for a few hours; an F-100 and an RF-101 were lost. On 4 April 48 F-105s returned with 750 lb bombs. Eight MiG-17s of the VPAF 921st Fighter Regiment, half acting as decoys, attacked the F-105s and shot down two, killing Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson; a third F-105, Captain Carlyle Harris's, was lost and its pilot captured. The VPAF lost three MiG-17 pilots that day; how each was lost is disputed. The bridge, struck by more than 300 bombs, still stood; it would stand until laser-guided bombs dropped its span in 1972⁴³. In this war the U-2S's radar pass over Ham Rong at dawn on 5 April told CINCPAC what the RF-101s had been lost trying to see: both spans standing, the approaches cratered, and rail traffic moving again within the day¹⁵.

In this war the folder for the Ham Rong bridge had been complete since the previous August, with the coordinates of both abutments and the central pier measured to within a few metres. On 6 April Admiral U. S. Grant Sharp at CINCPAC forwarded to the Joint Chiefs a request drafted in Saigon: four B-52Js from Guam, flying at 40,000 feet in any weather and releasing GBU-31(V)3/B penetrators about twelve miles from the bridge, outside the guns and the MiGs' patrol line, with forty weapons on the abutments and the pier. The staff's estimate was that the bridge would be down in one night with no aircraft lost. The request reached the Secretary of Defense on 9 April and was refused on 13 April. The reasons given were the ones that governed every B-52 decision of 1965: the bombers were a strategic force whose use over the North would be read in Moscow and Peking as a step up the ladder, Rolling Thunder was a graduated signal whose pace was set in the White House, and a bridge could be struck by fighter-bombers. A second request, for the Dragon's Jaw alone on any night of the commanders' choosing, was refused in June¹⁹. The F-105s went back to Ham Rong through the summer, and the bridge stood.

The missiles were seen coming. On 22 March the U-2S's radar change detection showed a new clearing southeast of Hanoi with the six-pointed pattern of revetments and access roads that every Soviet SA-2 site follows, and the ASIP signals payload heard the first emissions of the SNR-75 Fan Song fire-control radar on 18 April. By 24 July the U-2S had mapped eleven prepared sites around Hanoi and Haiphong and judged, from emissions and vehicle movements, that four of them were occupied on any given day. The Joint Chiefs asked to strike the sites under construction; McNamara and his deputies declined, as they had historically, for fear of killing Soviet technicians. Here the request carried coordinates for every launcher revetment, and the proposal to strike them from 40,000 feet in cloud with GBU-31s was refused with the rest¹⁹. On 24 July an SA-2 Guideline missile shot down an F-4C of the 47th Tactical Fighter Squadron near the munitions depot at Dien Bien Phu, the first US aircraft lost to a surface-to-air missile; Captain Roscoe Fobair was killed and Captain Richard Keirn captured³⁸. The flight had been warned that morning that a site west of Hanoi was occupied; the missile came from a battery that had moved overnight¹⁵.

The retaliation, Operation Spring High on 27 July, went differently. Historically the F-105s were sent against two sites, at least one of them empty, through guns massed around them, and lost aircraft for nothing. Here the U-2S's pass of 26 July showed the eastern site with dummy launchers of bamboo and its missiles gone, and the guns doubled around it; the strike was re-targeted on the one occupied site, went in low from the west, destroyed two launchers and the Fan Song van, and lost two F-105Ds, one pilot killed and one recovered¹⁵. Within five months the North's air defence had become an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles, and the restricted zone around Hanoi had become its sanctuary; what had changed was that every part of it was on a map in Saigon before it fired, and that a weapon able to strike every part of it in any weather sat on Guam with its coordinates loaded, waiting for a decision Washington would not take for seven years¹⁹.

#### The Vinh Long suspension
*6 Apr – 11 May 1965 · Vinh Long Province · new · EV001-5*
*Initiative: allied*

At the December 1964 harvest landlords' agents collected rent in four villages of Vinh Long's Tam Binh and Vung Liem districts under the escort of Regional Forces platoons the province chief had lent them, and tenants who refused were taken to the district headquarters. The Commission documented 26 cases from the tenants' complaints to the land court on circuit and from the American province adviser's reports, and on 2 February 1965 sent them to the Ministry of the Interior with 30 days to act. Nothing was done; Saigon was occupied with the fall of Khanh. On 6 April the Aid Compliance Board suspended the release of counterpart piasters to Vinh Long Province. On 28 April the Quat government replaced the province chief, and on 11 May, after the Commission certified that the escorts had ended and the collected rents had been returned, the Board restored the funds⁴.

It was the only suspension the Board made against a province in the war's first two years, and it worked as designed: the next province chief stopped the escorts, and in the rest of the Delta the threat was enough. It also cost what this headquarters had warned it would cost. Liberation Radio broadcast for a month that the "American masters" had dismissed a "puppet" who displeased them, and in Saigon the officers who resented the Board pointed to the same fact⁴.

#### The battle of Ba Gia
*28–31 May 1965 · Ba Gia, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · changed from history · EV000-17*
*Initiative: enemy*

On 29 May 1965 the VC 1st Regiment of the forming 2nd Division (40th, 60th and 90th Battalions, with the 45th Independent Battalion and local force battalions), directed by PAVN Major General Chu Huy Man, overran the outpost at Loc Tho near Ba Gia, about 10 km from Quang Ngai, and ambushed the ARVN 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry Regiment as it came to help. On the historical course the battalion was destroyed. On this one it was cut in two on the road from Ba Gia and lost its headquarters company and most of one rifle company in the first half hour; the other three companies, each with a squad gun in every squad and a marksman in most, formed a perimeter on the low ridge north of the road and held it until dark, and about 280 of the battalion's men came back to Quang Ngai over the next two days. Major General Nguyen Chanh Thi, commanding I Corps, sent a task force under the 51st Regiment: its 2nd Battalion, the 3rd Marine Battalion, the 37th and 39th Ranger Battalions, two 105mm artillery battalions and an M113 squadron. On 30 May the 39th Rangers fought the VC 45th Battalion on Mount Chop Non, and the 2nd Battalion, 51st and the Marines were hit near Phuoc Loc; US fighter-bombers and UH-1 gunships, delayed because forward air controllers could not find the fight, prevented a rout⁴¹. On the night of 30–31 May the 3rd Marines held a moat line through repeated assaults, with nine M249s across the line firing under illumination from the 105s, and their marksmen picking off the 40th Battalion's machine-gun teams setting up in the paddy dikes beyond the moat at 300–400 metres. The 39th Rangers were reduced to about two companies, against one historically¹⁷. The VC withdrew at dawn on 31 May.

I put ARVN losses at about 75 killed, 110 wounded and 190 captured or missing, against the US Army's historical figures of 107 killed, 123 wounded and 367 captured or missing; the individual weapons lost, 384 historically, I put at about 230, most of them M4A1 carbines with their sights⁴⁴. About 150 VC bodies were found, against 84 historically, and MACV's estimate of up to 826 VC casualties is no more reliable than it was. Ba Gia was still a defeat. The VC had overrun the outpost, broken a battalion and held the field, and by 3 June they controlled much of northern Quang Ngai, because the ARVN pulled its battered task force back to the province capital. On 5 July they overran Ba Gia again, and the ARVN abandoned the post⁴¹. In Washington it was read as evidence that the ARVN was being worn down faster than it could be rebuilt, not, as historically, as proof that a regimental task force could be wiped out; the distinction mattered to the 51st Regiment and not to the July decision. The 230 carbines went to the 2nd Division's local battalions, who were firing 5.56mm taken from the dead within the month¹⁷.

The officer reforms changed Ba Gia at its edges and left its centre alone. The 1st Battalion, 51st Regiment was commanded by a major whom the corps had appointed in 1963, and its companies by lieutenants and captains of the classes of 1959–62; it was those company commanders, with the battalion's headquarters gone in the first half hour, who brought three companies onto the ridge north of the road, set the squad guns and called the battalion's two mortars onto the paddy below them. Part of what memorandum 004 credited to the squad gun at Ba Gia belongs to the officers who placed it, and I do not count it twice: the losses above stand. The 2nd Battalion, 51st had been given in March to Major Le Van Tri, one of the board's first selections, and on 30 May it moved to Phuoc Loc with a company on the high ground on each side of the road, which is why it was hit in the flank rather than in column¹¹. The decisions that lost Ba Gia were taken above both battalions: the corps sent the task force forward piecemeal into ground the VC 1st Regiment had chosen, and pulled it back to Quang Ngai afterwards, and none of the officers who made those decisions had been through a course or before a board.

#### ◆ The battle of Dong Xoai
*9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · changed from history · EV000-18*
*Initiative: enemy*

Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers. The CIDG companies and the militia carried the carbines and Garands they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach them; the 111th RF Company and the Americans carried M4A1s. The camp was largely overrun during the night, as it was historically, from the CIDG sectors inward, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters, where the 111th Company had fallen back with its carbines, until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overrun, losing about half its men against nearly all of them historically¹⁷; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it in the rubber⁴⁵.

The Airborne fight on 12 June is the clearest measure in this war of what the squad weapons could and could not do. Historically about 150 of the 470 came back. On this course the battalion's lead company was lost in the opening fire as it was historically, but the three companies behind it each turned their squad guns into the trees and held, and about 270 men fought their way to the landing zone at Thuan Loi over the afternoon, bringing about 60 wounded. The 271st had chosen the place, the hour and the range, and it won; the Airborne battalion was broken, though not wiped out¹⁷. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had⁴⁵.

I put the allied losses at about 300 South Vietnamese killed, 210 wounded and 150 missing, and 18 Americans killed as historically, against 416 killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing on the historical course; about 300 civilians were killed and 2,500 made refugees, as historically. More than 300 VC bodies were found historically; I put the figure here at about 400, with the same scepticism about the allied estimate of 625 more. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact⁴⁵. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had about two reserve battalions left, against one historically¹⁷. That was not enough to change Westmoreland's request of 7 June, which rested on the PAVN regiments entering the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and it did not change the answer.

At Dong Xoai the board had not yet reached the 5th Division. The 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment was landed at Thuan Loi on 10 June on a division plan that put it on an open landing zone beside the rubber where the 271st was known to be waiting, under a battalion commander appointed by the division in 1962. The board reviewed the landing in July on its own motion, under the thirty-day rule, and in August relieved the battalion commander and the division operations officer who had written the plan, the first reliefs for cause in the ARVN's history that were not punishments for political disloyalty¹¹. The 5th Division's commander was untouched, and remained so, because he had been appointed before the ranked list existed. The night at the camp and the 7th Airborne's fight on 12 June went as told above⁴⁵.

The basic course changed Dong Xoai at its edges and not at its centre. The CIDG companies, which the course did not reach, broke first, as they had historically and in this war before it. The 111th RF Company, which had taken the territorial course as a company in 1961, fell back to the district headquarters in order and carried its wounded with it; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment, whose privates had all been through Quang Trung, was still landed on the plan its division wrote, on the open zone beside the rubber, and still lost about half its men, because no private's training reaches a landing zone chosen for him. The difference was in the evening of 12 June, when the 7th Airborne's survivors came into Thuan Loi: of about 60 wounded they brought out, about 50 were alive the next morning, with tourniquets put on in the rubber by men who had never been taught first aid on the historical course and were taught it in basic here. I put the allied killed at about 270 rather than about 300, a judgment on the wounded who lived and the companies that held, and I do not adjust the enemy's losses².

#### The first Arc Light strike
*18 Jun 1965 · Near Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · changed from history · EV000-20*
*Initiative: allied*

On 18 June 1965 Strategic Air Command flew its first B-52 mission of the war. Thirty B-52Js of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings were launched from Andersen Air Force Base, Guam, in place of the historical B-52Fs. They flew to the South and back without tankers, on the F130's endurance, and there was no rendezvous over the sea; the collision of two B-52Fs while circling to meet their KC-135s, which killed eight crewmen on the historical first mission, did not happen⁴⁶. The Soviet trawler that kept station off Guam still reported the bombers' departure, and Hanoi's warning net still had about three hours' notice of their arrival; what was gone was the second warning, the tanker track over the Philippine Sea, which had told an observer the bombers' heading²⁰¹⁹.

Twenty-eight aircraft struck a suspected Viet Cong base area near Ben Cat, about 40 miles north of Saigon, with 750 lb and 500 lb bombs, about 51 bombs each where the historical F carried 51 and the later Big Belly D would carry 108⁴⁶²⁰. Two carried twenty GBU-31s each, aimed at the coordinates of four structures the U-2S had imaged in the forest edge, believed to be a regimental headquarters; all forty weapons fell within a few metres of their aimpoints, and the ground follow-up found four collapsed huts that had been empty for days. The rest of the box found what it found historically: little, because the enemy had left or had not been there in strength. The mission was ridiculed as a sledgehammer swung at flies, and in this war the press added that the sledgehammer was now accurate to a few metres and still swung at flies¹⁹.

What its first strike showed is the find-and-fix problem in its purest form, and the J changed none of it: unlimited and now precise striking power, aimed at a target located by intelligence that was hours or days old. Within five months B-52s would be striking within a few kilometres of US troops in the Ia Drang; Arc Light would continue until August 1973 and fly about 126,615 sorties⁴⁷. In this war it flew them with about 10–20 percent fewer maintenance men, without the tanker bridge that historically tied a KC-135 to every bomber, and with a carpet that was half as dense whenever it carried unguided bombs²⁰¹⁹.

#### How the phase ends
By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though none of the general reserve has been wiped out. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and outside the held belt the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security it needs. Inside it the summer is not the historical one: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's⁴. Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

About 1,100 village posts are settled, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure has begun to leave the villages around the posts⁵. The sea route to the coast is closed and the Cambodian route open, and the enemy's rear is on the U-2S's maps while his regiments under the canopy and his cadres in the hamlets are as hidden as before. The general reserve enters Phase II weakened but whole, and the Front enters it with between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines¹⁷. On Guam the B-52Js hold about 1,400 measured targets in the cell's folders and authority to strike none of them in the North.

Phung Hoang ends the phase with about 140 district centres open, every one behind a settled post, and a method that has reached above the hamlet in one district on the edge of Saigon and in a dozen in the Delta¹⁰. About 1,100 selected advisers are in the subsectors and battalions, speaking to their counterparts without interpreters; the rest of the field posts are still held on the old tour. The Front has begun to move its district committees out of the held belt, which costs it the villages' daily obedience and gains it the safety of the contested edge.

By the end of July 1965 the board has filled about 70 of the army's roughly 190 manoeuvre battalion commands and relieved its first nine commanders; the rest are still held by men the generals put there, and about a third of all battalions are still commanded by captains, because the army is short of majors and the career-course gate makes none faster¹¹. The companies are led almost everywhere by the classes of 1957–63. The generals are the generals of 1963, and every one of them has learned that the board can be bargained with and cannot be ignored.

The Directorate ends the phase with its plants at about half their capacity and the domestic share four months old; about 5,000 PF radios, 6,000 hamlet radios and 600,000 dry cells have gone to the held belt, and the Front's mines in Long An have Go Vap batteries in them¹³. Route 1 on the coast has been reopened after the worst flood in memory, and the Front has an industrial working class in Bien Hoa to organise.

The army that enters Phase II has trained every private it has enlisted for eight years, and every territorial; it has kept about 2,000–3,000 of its best sergeants at the training centres to do it, in the two years it could least spare them, and the Muster Service has caught the centres that sold the course short. The general reserve was broken at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and came back from both with more of its men than on any course before this one; the battalions are steadier than history's by a band, though the enemy still chooses where they fight².

### Mechanism

The phase is changed in three places, and each is a mechanism the second part of this memorandum named. The first is the post's first ten minutes. A PF platoon that has been through the territorial course together, on the M4A1 it carries and with the PRC-10 the Go Vap plant gave it, fights the opening of a night attack as a unit: it holds its fire for the light, fires in pairs, reports what it sees, and keeps ammunition for the second assault. At Long Huu on 17 November 1964 that is the difference between a post the Front's 516th Battalion company withdraws from and one it overruns on its second try, and the advisers' count of posts held across the held belt moves from about nine in ten to about nineteen in twenty. That is a large change in a small number. Of perhaps 400 attacks on posts with their platoons in 1964–65, it is the difference between about 40 posts lost and about 20, with their platoons, their radios and their carbines².

The second is the wounded. In both armies the course teaches every soldier the tourniquet, and the first place this war shows it is the rubber at Thuan Loi on 12 June 1965, where the 7th Airborne brings out about 60 wounded and about 50 live. The ARVN's evacuation was slower than the American and its surgical beds farther away; the tourniquet buys the wounded man the hours the helicopter takes, and in the ARVN those hours were longer. This is why the provision saves more Vietnamese than American lives by a factor of four or more².

The third is the army itself, and it is the reason for the one band this memorandum moves. The ARVN of 1964–65 had been broken in battle after battle by an enemy who chose the ground, and the historical cause the record gives, beside its commanders, is the untrained draftee sent straight to his unit and the private who could not shoot or move under fire¹. With the course, the carbine of memorandum 004 and the lieutenants of memorandum 007 forming one standard from private to captain, the ARVN's battalions in this phase are steadier than history's by enough to move its effectiveness from one band above history to two². They still lose Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, because the enemy chose those fights and the generals planned the reliefs, and the band records the battalions that came back from them, not the battles.

The phase also shows the course's cost and its corruption. The 2,000–3,000 drill sergeants it held at the centres were the sergeants the battalions most needed in the two years they were being broken, and the training command's compromise of November 1964, sending the 1957–59 drill sergeants to the battalions on promotion and replacing them with corporals, began the circle that would repay the debt from 1966. The Binh Dinh centre that sold six weeks of rations in September 1964 was one of eleven such cases; the course number on the muster roll was the only check on a figure the centres scored for themselves.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy did not detect the course, because it had always been there. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee wrote its doctrine of 1963–64 against an army whose privates had been trained since 1957, and the lessons it drew in 1964 were the ones it was already drawing from the rifles: attack what is not trained or rearmed, ambush the column the general sends, avoid the wall of a post that holds². Its district companies in the Delta moved from the posts with platoons to the constables, the militia and the camps from December 1964, as the chapter records, and the course widened the gap they were exploiting.

Two things in its response were new. The Front's military proselytizing sections read the conduct block within a season and called it American indoctrination of the puppet soldier; in the held belt it took a little of the force out of their propaganda among the soldiers' families, and outside it none². And from 1965 the district companies made PF deserters who had taken the territorial course their squad leaders, so that the course began, in a few hundred men, to train its own enemy. That is the counter-directional finding of this memorandum, and the first phase is where it starts.

### Causal chains

The phase's chains begin here and land later: C009-2 (the trained soldier's first seconds to the mines of Dien Ban) and C009-1 (the drill sergeants held in 1964 to the self-defence groups of Can Duoc) are placed in Phases II and III, where their last links fall.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits an ARVN one band steadier than it would otherwise have been, though it is already at the cap there, so the gain appears as battalions not lost rather than in the band. It inherits a drill-sergeant corps about to begin repaying the battalions, a Front that has learned to attack what the course did not reach, and the first trained deserters in the Front's district companies. Phase III inherits the posts' first ten minutes in the towns, and the mobilization of 1968 inherits a training command that has already refused once to shorten the course.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +2..+4 · POP.gvn_legitimacy +2..+4 · VCI.strength -7..-5 · FIND.main_force +2..+4 · FIND.vci +8..+10 · ARVN.effectiveness +8..+10.*

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967³. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed most. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 titled to about 640,000 families⁴. GOLDEN SHEAF, its first clearing operation in the western Delta, shows what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of what the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark. The Commission survives the Buddhist crisis and the election behind the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 convicts its first general. With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case. The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its cadres into the towns, and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before. None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

The war of the enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch counts the Trail's trucks from December 1965; the U-2S photographs the dispersed fuel drums before the POL strikes fly; and by March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree, from the radar's count of convoys out of Kompong Speu, that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps. The counts show the bombing raising the toll on the transit without lowering what arrives, and change no rule in Washington.

Every American and South Vietnamese infantryman carries a modern carbine, and every squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman. The Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle. The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by the corps, and the districts they leave pay for it. None of it changes who chooses the battle.

Above the Trail the B-52J is allowed against fixed crossings in the panhandle from April 1966 and cuts every bridge it can measure; the enemy answers with fords, causeways and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before. At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is measured every night and lifts three weeks early at a heavy price to the PAVN. It is the first siege the enemy loses badly, and Westmoreland draws a lesson from it that will cost the coastal hamlets.

Three changes of 1964 run through the phase without altering its battles. Phung Hoang's centres cover every district by the end of 1965 and break the Front's district administration in the held belt before Tet rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN takes the Can Giuoc committee itself, not only its security chief. The method spreads by its own success into the contested belt, where it becomes the historical Phoenix, and a PRU raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 makes "the American Phoenix" a press story three years early. The American battalions arrive and leave as units for twelve-month tours, trained together and commanded for the whole of them; they fight better in the contacts the enemy grants and get no more of them, and the enemy learns to aim at the week each one changes over, as it did at Dien Ban in August 1966. And the field advisers, now chosen and trained and on eighteen-month tours, are the men who carry what the American battalions forget each year.

The officer reforms change the ARVN in this phase more than any provision before them. From the board's second season in 1965 the battalions are filled from its command list, their commanders serve eighteen months and are relieved when they fail, and by the end of 1966 about three in four of the army's battalion commanders are men a board chose, against about one in two competent commanders on the historical course¹¹. The enemy notices within a season and does what he does against every better unit: he declines to fight it, and lays his ambush for the battalion beside it, as the 272nd Regiment does on Route 13 in May 1966. The Buddhist uprising finds the board's commanders in I Corps on neither side, and in 1967 the ranked list gives the 25th Division its first commander chosen for competence, under the aid condition, while an agent in the board's secretariat gives COSVN every list it publishes. Above the battalions nothing moves: the corps are commanded by the same men, Westmoreland's strategy turns the ARVN to pacification as it did historically, and the generals still decide where the battalions go.

A fifth war runs under the fourth, in the factories. The steelworks at Bien Hoa rolls the pickets, wire and bridge spans of the countryside, at a fifth to a tenth below its rating because the Front has learned to drop the towers of the Da Nhim line. The radio plant puts a PRC-25 in every post of the held belt from 1966, and the Front answers the radio as it answered the carbine: it attacks the posts that cannot call, and at My Phuoc Tay in March 1967 it attacks a post in order to ambush the company that answers the call. The district chiefs answer with guns instead of trucks, and their platoons stay inside their wire. In Chuong Thien the posts call and nobody comes, because the corps has borrowed the companies. And at Phu Cat in August 1967 a bad lot of shell bodies teaches the army what an arsenal is worth without honest acceptance. None of it changes who chooses the main-force battle.

Under all of it, the private soldier of both armies is trained. Every ARVN recruit of the phase passes ten weeks at Quang Trung or a division centre before he joins his battalion, every territorial six weeks at his province's centre, and every American his service's 2026 course; from 1966 the drill-sergeant corps begins to return its sergeants to the battalions as platoon sergeants¹. The effect is the one the officer reforms found: the better unit is attacked less, and the attack that is not made leaves nothing to count. The 272nd Regiment's ambush of the Bau Bang convoy breaks on trained Regional Force soldiers who cannot be trained out of riding their trucks. At the Ia Drang the troopers keep more of their wounded alive through the night at Albany. And the enemy answers the trained soldier as he answered the rifle, by moving his war to the weapon the course does not teach: in the lowlands south of Da Nang the mine and the booby trap become the principal killers of the Marines. The devaluation of June 1966 then shows what training does not buy: a trained private whose pay no longer feeds his family goes home with his training, and some of it reaches the Front.

#### The war on the district chiefs
*Aug 1965 – Jun 1966 · The Delta, the provinces around Saigon and the coastal Centre · new · EV002-3*
For its first year the Front read the Provincial Administration Reform as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, and it took until the summer of 1965, when the list-appointed chiefs had stayed through the fall of Khanh, the Quat government and the arrival of Thieu and Ky, for the provincial committees to report that the new chiefs were not changing. The conclusion COSVN drew in August 1965 was the one that hurt: the chiefs who kept their Popular Forces in the hamlets at night and directed the police were the enemy's most dangerous officials, and the district committees were to kill them. Between August 1965 and June 1966 nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs were killed by mines, ambush and assassination, sixteen of them appointed from the list. On 14 November 1965 Major Tran Van Sang, district chief of Mo Cay in Kien Hoa since June 1964, was killed with his driver and two bodyguards by a command-detonated mine on the road from Mo Cay to Ben Tre; the provincial committee's own report, captured in 1966, called him "the most harmful of the puppet district chiefs in the province" because the hamlets of his district no longer sheltered its cadres at night⁵.

The killings did not empty the districts, and that was the reform's quiet gain. A dead chief's deputy for administration acted at once, and the Board sent a ranked list for the vacancy within an average of about three weeks; Mo Cay had a new chief, a captain who had served as the district's deputy for security, on 6 December. Under the patronage system a vacant district had waited months for a patron's choice, and the Popular Forces had drifted back to the district town while it waited⁸. Each killing cost the Front a security cell's best men, because the chiefs now travelled with Field Force escorts and varied their roads, and five of the twenty-one attacks were traced and their teams taken by the Special Branch within the year⁵. The Front went on killing chiefs for the rest of the war. It never again did so at the rate of 1965–66, because the chiefs it could kill easily had been killed and the rest had learned.

#### The big-unit war: Starlite, A Shau, Hastings, Long Tan, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City
*18 Aug 1965 – 14 May 1967 · The Van Tuong peninsula and the DMZ in I Corps; the A Shau valley; Phuoc Tuy; War Zone C and the Iron Triangle in III Corps · changed from history · EVCP01-4*
*Initiative: both*

The big-unit battles of 1965–67 are told here together, because together they are the shape of the phase: each won on the ground, each followed by the enemy's return, none changing the war. On 18–24 August 1965, on a deserter's information, the 7th Marines under Colonel Oscar F. Peatross attacked the VC 1st Regiment on the Van Tuong peninsula south of Chu Lai by sea and helicopter in Operation Starlite, the first regimental-sized American battle of the war. The Marines lost 45 killed and 203 wounded and reported 614 VC killed but only 109 weapons taken, the first clear instance of a count inflated by its counters; the regiment was back in the field within months⁴⁸. On 9–10 March 1966, under cloud that kept most air support away, four battalions of the PAVN 325th Division's 95B Regiment overran the Special Forces camp in the A Shau valley; Major Bernard F. Fisher landed his A-1E on the strip under fire to rescue a downed pilot and received the Medal of Honor, and the valley became a PAVN base area and a terminus of the Trail for the rest of the war⁴⁹.

In July 1966 the PAVN 324B Division crossed the DMZ, and Task Force Delta of the 3rd Marine Division, with the ARVN 1st Division under Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong, drove it back in Operation Hastings for 126 American and 21 South Vietnamese killed against about 700 PAVN claimed. Its lasting effect was to draw the Marines north to the DMZ, away from the coastal hamlets III MAF had wanted to secure⁵⁰. On 18 August, in monsoon rain in the Long Tan rubber, D Company, 6th Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment, about 108 men under Major Harry Smith, held for three hours against the VC 275th Regiment and the D445 Battalion with the guns of Nui Dat behind it, losing 18 killed and counting 245 enemy dead on the field⁵¹. From September to November Operation Attleboro grew from a brigade's shakedown northwest of Dau Tieng into a corps battle against the VC 9th Division and the PAVN 101st Regiment, costing 155 American dead against a claimed 1,016 enemy, of whom about 500 is the better estimate⁵².

In 1967 the sweeps reached their largest size. In Cedar Falls, from 8 January, the 1st and 25th Divisions with brigades and armoured cavalry sealed the Iron Triangle, moved about 6,000 people from Ben Suc to a camp at Phu Loi and destroyed the village; the VC were back within days, and a year later the Triangle was a staging area for Tet⁵³. In Junction City, from 22 February to 14 May, II Field Force sent two divisions and three brigades into War Zone C to destroy the 9th Division and find COSVN, with the war's only major American parachute assault at Katum. The enemy attacked American firebases five times at night or at dawn, at Prek Klok, Ap Bau Bang, Suoi Tre and Ap Gu, and lost heavily each time; the Americans lost 282 killed and claimed 2,728 enemy dead, COSVN moved, and the 9th Division withdrew into Cambodia⁵⁴.

The figures above are the historical returns. In this war the American battalions in these fights, and the ARVN 1st Division at Hastings, carried the M4A1 and the M249; the Australians at Long Tan did not, because the provision did not cover them. I do not adjust these returns. In each of these battles who attacked whom, where and when decided the losses far more than the squad weapons did, and the enemy's own losses were never counted closely enough for a difference in rifles to show in them²⁵. What the phase's big-unit battles proved here is what they proved historically: that the enemy lost heavily when he attacked an American position, and that he decided for himself whether to do so.

#### The command lists and the thirty-day reviews
*1 Sep 1965 – 30 Jun 1966 · The Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters; the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · new · EV007-2*
*Initiative: allied*

The board's second season was the first in which it had two years of evaluation reports to read, and it changed the army's battalions faster than anything since 1955. Between September 1965 and June 1966 the panels selected about 230 battalion and regimental commanders, most of the army's manoeuvre commands, and by the end of June 1966 about three-quarters of the army's battalions were commanded by an officer the board had chosen, about two-thirds of them majors and lieutenant colonels of the classes of 1957–59 who had passed the career course and the first Dalat staff year. Every command was for eighteen months, against a median of under a year on the historical course⁶. The captain-commanded battalion, about a third of the army in 1965, fell to about one in ten by the end of 1966¹¹.

The thirty-day reviews were the part the army felt. In the season the board relieved 27 battalion commanders and three regimental commanders for cause, each after a review that heard the officer, his raters and his American adviser's written statement, and each review's finding was sent to every battalion commander in the army in a monthly digest the Central Training Command called Lessons of Command. Most of the reliefs were for what the courses taught against: a convoy moved without route security, a night defensive position without listening posts, a fire plan never made, a company sent forward without the battalion knowing where the others were. Two were for the sale of rice rations, and the Commission Against Corruption prosecuted both¹¹. The digest was read, as such things are read, mostly by officers who wished to know what the board punished; that was the point of it.

The board's own sale of commands ended and patronage moved to where the board did not reach. In the corps headquarters the price of a command fell to nothing, and the price of an assignment rose: the generals could no longer give a man a battalion, but they could still give him a division staff post, a province's sector headquarters, a seat on the career course ahead of his turn, or a battalion in the Delta rather than one on the Cambodian border. Lindqvist estimated in June 1966 that about half of what had been sold in 1964 was no longer for sale, and that the other half had changed its name¹¹.

The field commissions began in the same season. About 600 sergeants with a combat record and a commander's recommendation were commissioned in 1966 after a twelve-week course at Thu Duc and the branch basic course, most of them sons of farmers from the Delta and the coastal Centre, men who would never have passed the diploma bar the decree had abolished¹¹. They went to the platoons of their own regiments, and in the PF and RF training camps it was noticed within the year that the new lieutenants spoke to the recruits in the recruits' own accent. Major Bui Quang Trieu took command of the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, 5th Division from the September list.

#### The Da Nhim line
*3 Oct 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The 230 kV line from the Da Nhim station at Don Duong, Tuyen Duc Province, through Di Linh in Lam Dong and Long Khanh to the Thu Duc and Bien Hoa substations · new · EV008-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

The steelworks ran on electricity it did not make, and the Front found that out in its second year. The melt shop drew 70–100 MW at peak, about half from the line that brought the Da Nhim hydro station's power 250 kilometres down from the Lang Biang plateau to Thu Duc and the rest from the 66 MW thermal station at Thu Duc¹². On the night of 3 October 1965 a sapper team of the Front's Region 6 dropped two towers of the line in the hills east of Di Linh with charges of TNT in Bien Hoa-made ammunition boxes, and by the end of the year it had dropped eleven more. The Region 6 committee, which commanded the Front's war in the southern Highlands and the provinces east of Saigon, had chosen the line for its own reasons: it was the one target in its region whose loss was felt in Saigon, and it could be reached from the forest without fighting anybody¹³.

The line was harder to cut than to mend, and the Directorate made it so. The fabrication shops at Bien Hoa built replacement towers to the Japanese drawing, a set of four in a week, and the line crews had a dropped tower back in four to ten days, against the six to eight weeks that a tower shipped from Japan would have taken. The power company and the Directorate paid the Koho villages along the line, between Don Duong and Di Linh, a monthly wage to watch it, and gave each line-watch village a hamlet radio on the Tuyen Duc sector's net¹³. They were the first Montagnard hamlets in the province with either a government wage or a radio. The watch reported the sappers more often than it stopped them, because the villagers were not armed and were not asked to be.

The cost to the works was real and bounded. In 1966 the Front dropped about forty towers and the steelworks lost about a fifth of its hours; in 1967, with the watch, about twenty-five and a tenth. Bien Hoa rolled about 180,000 tonnes in 1966 and 220,000 in 1967 against a rated 200,000–300,000, and the shortfall fell on construction steel, not on the arsenal, whose lines ran on the Thu Duc station. The FULRO risings of these years were in Darlac, Quang Duc and Phu Bon, among the Rhade and Jarai, outside the country the line crossed; the base did nothing for them and they did nothing to it¹³. The Front got from the line a cheap and lasting target, and the Republic got from it, without meaning to, thirty Koho villages that took its wage, carried its radios, and told the sector when the Front came through.

#### ◆ Plei Me and the Ia Drang
*19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · changed from history · EV000-23*
*Initiative: enemy*

On 19 October 1965 the PAVN B3 Front under Major General Chu Huy Man attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, south of Pleiku, and the next day ambushed the ARVN relief column; the relief reached the camp at dusk on 25 October and the PAVN withdrew west towards the Chu Pong massif on the Cambodian border. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), newly arrived, was turned loose in pursuit. On 14 November the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray at the foot of the Chu Pong, into the assembly area of the 66th Regiment. For three days the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th, with elements of the 33rd, assaulted a perimeter held with the help of the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert Tully, massed tube artillery, tactical air and, from 15 November, B-52s striking about 7 km to the west, the first tactical use of the bombers, in five missions and 96 sorties over the campaign. The troopers carried M4A1s, with two M249s in each squad and an M110A2 marksman in most, where historically they carried the M16 and had the M60 at platoon; the perimeter held as it held historically, and I put American losses at X-Ray at about 70 killed and 115 wounded against 79 and 121¹⁷. Moore's count of PAVN dead, 634 after his own reduction, and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record and the same caution applies to them⁵⁵.

On 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade, marching overland to Landing Zone Albany, was ambushed in column in tall grass by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd Regiment. The PAVN, following Colonel Nguyen Huu An's order to "grab them by the belt", fought inside the column where American artillery and air could not reach them, for about sixteen hours. This is the kind of fight the squad gun was built for, and it helped less than it should have. Two belt-fed guns in every squad meant that the platoons which kept their cohesion, Alpha Company's and the battalion command group's at the head of the column, formed a perimeter in the clump of trees at Albany within minutes and held it all afternoon; but the companies strung out behind in the grass were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, and a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends. The battalion lost about 125 killed, 130 wounded and 4 missing, against 155 killed, 124 wounded and 4 missing on the historical course, still the worst American loss of a single day so far. Over the campaign the United States counted about 205 dead, against 237 historically, and claimed a kill ratio of about ten to one; a PAVN report of 1966 gives 559 killed and 669 wounded¹⁷. Both sides inflated the other's losses⁵⁵.

Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success, and each drew the lesson that confirmed its strategy: Westmoreland that airmobile forces with heavy fire support could destroy PAVN regulars at a favourable rate; the PAVN that by closing to close quarters and choosing when to fight they could neutralize American firepower and withdraw across the Cambodian border to rebuild⁵⁵. The B3 Front's after-action study added one sentence that the historical one did not need: that the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and that the belt-grabbing assault must therefore be made from closer still, at night, or not at all¹⁷. McNamara, visiting after the battle, advised the President at the end of November that even a doubling of US forces in 1966 might produce only a military stalemate at a higher level.

The officer education changed Albany in the companies and not in the column's order. Moore and McDade had been commissioned long before 1957 and the decision to march overland in column through the grass was McDade's; but the company commanders of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry were captains of the classes of 1959–62, and they had been through the branch course and the career course, which taught the movement to contact in close country as a drill. Two of them put flank security out into the grass on their own authority when the column halted at about midday, and their companies were the ones whose men were found in perimeters rather than in knots; two did not, because the column was moving and the battalion had given no order. I put the battalion's loss at about 105–115 killed rather than about 125, a judgment on two companies' conduct that I hold loosely, and the campaign's American dead at about 190¹¹. It remains the worst American loss of a single day so far.

The basic course changed Albany in the night after the ambush, which is where the battalion lost men it need not have lost. The troopers of the 1st Cavalry Division had all passed the Army's ten-week course of the 2026 programme, and every one of them had been taught in its second week to put a tourniquet on an arm or a leg and to put it on himself if no one else could¹. Historically, many of the wounded of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry lay in the grass through the afternoon and night of 17 November, where the medics could not reach them, and a share of those found dead in the morning had died of wounds that a tourniquet would have stopped⁵⁵⁵⁶. Here the men in the grass put them on each other and on themselves, in the dark, while the PAVN searched the field. I put the lives this saved at Albany at about eight to twelve, and at X-Ray at two or three, and the campaign's American dead at about 180 rather than about 190. That is the rate this headquarters uses for the whole war, about two to four percent of American killed in action, shown on one night in one battle; it is not larger, and on that night it was not smaller. The PAVN wounded who reached the Chu Pong had no such drill, and the B3 Front's medical returns, captured in 1966, put its dead of wounds after the battle at about one in four of its wounded².

#### Operation NIGHT LANTERN
*15 Nov 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · new · EV002-4*
*Initiative: allied*
NIGHT LANTERN went back to the districts where the land programme had learned its first lesson. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings, the titles of about 400 families were still held in the district office, because no one could protect the families who kept them. Long An had been given a province chief from the first list in June 1964, Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, an engineer officer who had placed third in the country in the examination, and Can Giuoc a district chief from the second, Major Le Cong Phuong, who had grown up in Can Duoc. In September 1965 they proposed to the American province adviser an operation built not around a regiment but around the settled post, the census and night check and the target folder, in the 14 villages of the two districts between Route 5 and the Vam Co⁵.

The forces were Long An's own: eight Regional Forces companies, 26 Popular Forces platoons raised in the villages themselves, two Police Field Force companies of about 150 men each with M1 and M2 carbines, M1918A2 automatic rifles and 60mm mortars, 140 rural constables for the 14 posts, the province Special Branch section of about 45, and four census teams. Fire support was two M101 105mm howitzers at Can Giuoc and an AC-47 of the USAF 4th Air Commando Squadron on call at night; lift was the RF's sampans, two LCVPs of the River Assault Group at Nha Be, and UH-1B helicopters when III Corps would release them. The rule was Lan's: no post settled until its PF platoon slept in the same hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour, and no arrest without three sources⁵.

The posts went in from 15 November, one village at a time, working out from Can Giuoc town along the canals. The turn came in two steps. On the night of 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th Local Force Battalion attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, the village where the killings of June 1964 had begun. The PF platoon and the ten constables held the post and the hamlet behind it for three hours; an RF company came down the canal in sampans and reached the post at 01:40, and the AC-47 kept the attackers off the paddy until it arrived. The 506th left 38 dead and 14 weapons; the defenders lost 9 killed, 3 of them constables. On 4 February a night check in Long Huu found a young man whose card had been made for someone two years older; his booklet did not match the house, and within a week the courier route he served had led the Special Branch to the chief of the Can Giuoc district committee's security section, arrested on 11 February with his files⁵. The Can Giuoc DIOCC, open since August 1964 in the Special Branch's office beside the district chief's, took the files apart in nine days: the section chief's notebook listed the committee's courier stations by the names of the families who kept them, and the census matched 17 of the 22 to households and three to households that had been registered under false names in 1965. On the night of 24–25 February the Long An PRU and a Police Field Force platoon, in sampans with an RF company holding the canal behind them, took the district committee's deputy secretary and its finance chief in a hamlet of Phuoc Vinh Dong at the edge of the Rung Sat, where the committee had moved in January believing itself beyond the posts¹⁰. Neither man fired. The deputy secretary rallied in April and spent the rest of the year with the province centre, drawing the chart of the Long An province committee above him.

By 30 April all 14 posts were settled and held. The Special Branch had arrested 212 people on three-source evidence, of whom the magistrates released 71, and 64 cadres and guerrillas had rallied to the police posts, which they found easier to approach than an army camp. What was left of the district committee, five of its eleven members, moved deeper into the Rung Sat and visited its villages instead of living in them, and the Can Giuoc tax ledger captured in June showed collections for the first quarter of 1966 at about a third of the same quarter of 1965. The about 400 titles held since 1964 were handed to their families in April, in their hamlets⁵. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 RF, PF and police dead. It was the first operation in the war in which the province chief, the police and the land programme acted as one district government, and it did in six months what no battalion sweep of Long An had done in six years.

The American who saw the whole of it was Captain Whitcomb, the Can Giuoc subsector adviser, whose eighteen months ran from October 1964 to April 1966 and so spanned the killings' aftermath, the settling of the posts and the operation itself; his successor arrived on 1 March and they worked the district together for six weeks. Whitcomb's report of 30 April said three things this headquarters quoted to Washington: that the committee's arrest, not the battalion's sweep, was what broke the tax; that the Commission's sample of NIGHT LANTERN's 212 arrests had found 9 unsupported, all made in the first fortnight before the centre's charts were complete; and that the families who received their titles in April had asked him, not the district chief, whether the arrests would last¹⁰.

#### Operation STEADY COUNT
*6 Dec 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes; Routes 23, 911 and 912 south to Tchepone; the Laotian panhandle · new · EV003-3*
STEADY COUNT was the first dry season of the panhandle watch, and it was named for what it did best. On 6 December 1965, the day Tiger Hound opened its strikes in the southern panhandle, the Global Hawk orbit moved from the II Corps coast to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the Laotian panhandle west of Tchepone, outside the guns and beyond the reach of anything the North could fly. From 60,000 feet its radar watched the roads that Group 559 had cut through the Annamite Chain that year: Route 23 south from the Mu Gia pass, Routes 911 and 912 from Ban Karai, and the fords and truck parks along the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Banghiang down to Tchepone and Route 9¹⁴. The northeast monsoon gave Laos its dry season, and low cloud lay on the passes most mornings in January; the radar saw through it.

The engagement ran through three headquarters, and its weakness was in the joints. A track of six or more vehicles moving south at night went from the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut to the 2nd Air Division's control centre, from 1 April the Seventh Air Force's, and to the airborne command post, which passed it to whatever was in the air: A-1E Skyraiders of the 602nd Air Commando Squadron, B-57Bs, F-100Ds and F-105Ds on armed reconnaissance, and, from February, AC-47 gunships. Strikes in Laos needed the rules approved by Ambassador William Sullivan in Vientiane, which allowed armed reconnaissance on the motorable roads. Of about 1,900 tracks passed in the five months, aircraft reached about one in six within forty minutes, found and attacked about one in ten, and destroyed or damaged trucks in about one in twenty. The pilots claimed about 560 trucks destroyed; the cell's own count, from tracks that stopped and did not move again and from the U-2S's radar imagery the next day, was about 210. Nine US aircraft were lost over the panhandle in STEADY COUNT's sectors, most of them to 37mm guns that Group 559 moved to the passes from January; seven crewmen were killed or missing¹⁵. The drivers, porters and road troops killed are unknown, and any figure for them would be invented.

What the counts showed was the war's arithmetic. Southbound truck movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 a night at the peak in March, and the radar measured what came out at the southern end, on the roads toward Route 9 and the A Shau. On 12 April 1966 B-52s struck the Mu Gia pass for the first time; the orbit watched the first trucks move through the craters about twenty hours later. By February the drivers had learned the orbit's habits: traffic fell by half on the nights when the aircraft was late on station and rose in the hours of its changeover, and the road troops began to trellis the open stretches with bamboo and living trees. MACV J-2 estimated, from the tracks, that about 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle at the dry season's peak, and that this was more than the forces in the South required from the North¹⁵. It was the first time any headquarters had counted the Trail rather than estimating it, and the count said that the bombing raised the toll on the transit without lowering what arrived.

#### Operation GOLDEN SHEAF
*10 Jan – 30 Jun 1966 · Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien Provinces, along the Xa No canal from Can Tho to Vi Thanh · new · EV001-6*
GOLDEN SHEAF was the first operation of the war planned around the land programme rather than around an enemy unit. Its object was to carry the titling line west from the Bassac along the Xa No canal, the main waterway from Can Tho to Vi Thanh, into about 60 villages of Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien that the government held by day only or not at all, and to leave behind hamlets held at night by their own titled families. IV Corps gave it to the ARVN 21st Division, with its 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments, two Ranger battalions, about 40 Regional Forces companies of the two provinces, 24 titling teams and 6 muster teams; the 21st's advisory team and the American province senior advisers at Can Tho and Vi Thanh planned it with the division staff in November and December 1965. Fire support was the division's M101 105mm howitzers moved by LCM-6 along the canal, VNAF A-1H Skyraiders on call, and AC-47 gunships at night over posts under attack; lift was the US Army helicopter companies in IV Corps, UH-1B and UH-1D, about 30 aircraft on the operation's busiest days⁴. The dry season set the timing: from January to April the paddies were dry or stubble, the canal and the main arroyos were the only water lines, and troops could move off the dikes; the rains from May slowed every movement after that⁵⁷.

The method was the land oil-spot at battalion scale. A regiment cleared a block of four to six villages by day in a sweep of about a week, with Rangers on the far side of the block as a stop line; RF companies, one to each two or three hamlets, stayed in the hamlets overnight rather than withdrawing to posts; the titling teams followed about ten days behind, posting parcel numbers rather than names, and the muster teams recruited a Popular Forces platoon in each titled hamlet from the families who had just received their land, issued M1 carbines and AN/PRC-25 radios through the district, and paid them at the parade. A regiment moved to the next block only when the American province adviser and the district chief rated the last one able to hold at night with its RF company and its new platoon⁴.

The enemy declined battle. The Front's Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien provincial battalions and the regional main-force battalion this headquarters' order of battle placed west of the Xa No withdrew south-west towards the U Minh forest as the first blocks were entered, leaving local guerrillas, mines on the dikes and snipers at the canal bends⁵⁸. The one serious engagement came on the night of 22–23 March, when about two companies attacked an RF company and a new PF platoon in a hamlet at kilometre 28 on the canal; the post held for four hours under an AC-47 and illumination from the howitzers at Phung Hiep, and the attackers withdrew before dawn leaving 31 bodies and 9 weapons; the defenders lost 14 killed. Across the operation the ARVN, RF and PF lost about 214 killed and 640 wounded, more than half to mines and booby traps. The units reported about 690 enemy killed; the muster teams' audit of the reports, the first ever made of a division's body count, accepted 268 by body and weapon and estimated the true figure at 250–350, and recovered 140 weapons in all⁴. Of the reported figure, I am confident only that it is too high.

By 30 June about 46,000 hectares had been titled to about 38,000 families in 112 hamlets, and 74 of those hamlets were rated by the district and the province adviser as held at night by their own forces. The other 38 were held by day, and the titles for them stayed in the district office at Phung Hiep and Long My. The operation stopped at the line the Regional and Popular Forces could hold, about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh, not at the limit of the titling teams, who were idle for most of June: the bottleneck had moved from the offer to its delivery. In the 74 held hamlets the Front's tax collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn the province recorded the first rice sales through the government's market at Phung Hiep from villages that had never sold there⁴. It is the first place in this war where the government's arrival was what F001-17 said it would be: a guarantee rather than a threat, delivered by the government's own troops to people who had something of their own to defend.

#### Operation Masher/White Wing
*24 Jan – 6 Mar 1966 · Bong Son plain and the Kim Son and An Lao valleys, Binh Dinh Province · changed from history · EV000-24*
In the first large-unit operation of 1966 the 1st Cavalry Division, with the ARVN 22nd Division, an ARVN airborne brigade blocking along Highway 1 and the ROK Capital Division, swept the Bong Son plain and the valleys behind it in northern Binh Dinh, the heartland of the PAVN 3rd (Sao Vang) Division: the 22nd and 12th Regiments and the VC 2nd Regiment, about 6,000 men. The Marines ran Operation Double Eagle in Quang Ngai to the north, and the Koreans Operation Flying Tiger to the south. President Johnson had the name changed from Masher to White Wing because the original sounded too brutal⁵⁹.

The division lost 228 killed and 834 wounded, and 46 more men died in a separate aircraft crash; Double Eagle cost the Marines 24 killed and 156 wounded. The United States claimed 1,342 PAVN and VC killed and the ARVN and Koreans another 808; 254 weapons were recovered. The disproportion between the two figures drew criticism at the time. US figures counted more than 27,000 civilians displaced, and of about 8,000 people in the An Lao valley, 4,500 left; a mid-1966 Army study found US and South Vietnamese bombing and artillery to be the main cause of refugee movement in the province. Civilian deaths are largely unknown; Korean troops of the Capital Division are reported to have killed more than 1,000 civilians at Binh An/Tay Vinh, a figure from Vietnamese sources that has never been tested in any proceeding⁵⁹.

The 3rd Division slipped away, much of it before the operation reached it, and intelligence reported its return to the area about a week after the cavalry withdrew. The 1st Cavalry came back in Operation Crazy Horse two months later and again in Operation Thayer in October⁵⁹. Masher/White Wing is the template of the phase: a tactical success measured by body count, a large refugee population, and an enemy whose infrastructure in the hamlets survived intact.

In this war the operation and its displacement run as they did historically; the bombing and artillery that emptied the valleys were not touched by the provisions. What changed is what the refugees left behind. The coastal plain of northern Binh Dinh had been surveyed in May 1964, and by the end of 1965 the Binh Dinh land service had issued provisional titles for about 9,000 hectares on the Bong Son plain, at the Centre's ceiling of 1 hectare a family, including several thousand parcels the Viet Minh had distributed and the decree confirmed⁷. Under an instruction of the Ministry of Land Reform of February 1966, drafted with this headquarters after the first refugee returns reached Qui Nhon, the parcels of families who fled were recorded in the land court's register as held for the absent tiller and could not be reallotted, sold or reclaimed by a former landlord while the family lived. When the camps at Bong Son and Phu My began to empty in 1967, families who returned to the plain found their parcels recorded in their own names. The Front's cadres, who had told the camps that the Americans had driven the peasants off the land so that the landlords could have it back, lost the sentence that had served them best in Binh Dinh since 1955. The An Lao valley itself, a Front base area since the French war and never surveyed, gained nothing: its 4,500 refugees had no titles to keep⁴.

#### ◆ The mines of Dien Ban
*1 Mar 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Dien Ban, Dai Loc and Duy Xuyen Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon; Son Tinh and Binh Son Districts, Quang Ngai Province · new · EV009-2*
*Initiative: enemy*

The enemy's answer to the trained soldier was not to fight him; it was to kill him without fighting, and nowhere did that answer cost more than in the hamlets south of Da Nang. In late 1965 the command of the Quang Da Special Zone, under the Military Region 5 Party Committee, reviewed a year of contacts with ARVN battalions and American Marines in Dien Ban and Dai Loc, and concluded that its local-force companies were losing more than they gained in firefights with units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds and did not bunch up when they were hit. Its directive for 1966, captured near Dien Ban in February 1967, ordered every village guerrilla platoon in the lowland districts to make mines and booby traps its principal work, set each platoon a quota of devices a month, and told the district companies to fight only where a mine had already broken a column². The materials were everywhere: unexploded American bombs and shells, the Republic's own grenades bought or stolen, and the Go Vap dry cells that had been turning up in the Front's firing circuits since 1965¹³.

The Marines of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, who had come to Dien Ban as a unit in August 1966, met the result every day for a year. Their patrols moved as they had been taught in basic and at the School of Infantry, dispersed, with a point man, at intervals, and the hedgerows and paddy dikes of the Thu Bon lowlands were laid with M16 bounding mines taken from American minefields, 105mm shells rigged as command-detonated mines, and grenades in tin cans on tripwires across the paths between hamlets. Between September 1966 and August 1967 the battalion lost, by its own returns, about 38 killed and about 290 wounded, and about two-thirds of the dead and four-fifths of the wounded were to mines and booby traps rather than to fire; in the same districts in 1965, before the directive, the proportion had been about two-fifths. Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey wrote in his report of August 1967 that his Marines "do everything right in a firefight and are given none", and that the battalion's training, from basic onward, had taught it nothing about the thing that was killing it. What the course did give the battalion was the tourniquet: the mine's wound is a shattered leg, and the battalion's surgeon reckoned that about a dozen men who reached the aid station at Hoi An alive in that year would have died on the dike on the historical course, most of them without the leg².

The cost fell also on the hamlets. A mine does not choose who steps on it, and the province hospital at Hoi An recorded about 300 civilian mine casualties in Dien Ban and Dai Loc in 1966–67, farmers and children on the paths to the fields, about a third more than the year before. The Marines, who were losing men to devices in hamlets whose people knew where they were, came to treat the people as the enemy's accomplices; the conduct the course had taught them held better in the firefight than in the twelfth month of the mines, and the battalion's relations with the PF platoons and the villages, which Major Carl E. Haney had built since 1965, did not survive the year intact. In Quang Ngai the same directive ran under the Front's command in Son Tinh and Binh Son, against the ARVN 2nd Division's battalions and the Korean Marines, with the same result: fewer ambushes, more mines, and the same men dead by another means². The enemy had moved his war to the weapon the basic course did not teach, as this headquarters had said he would, and it cost him almost nothing to do it.

#### The Buddhist uprising
*10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · changed from history · EV000-26*
*Initiative: neither*

At the Honolulu conference in February 1966 Johnson embraced Ky publicly, and Ky came away believing he had American backing to move against his principal rival, Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps. On 10 March the junta dismissed Thi. When Ky let him return to Da Nang and Hue on 16–17 March he was met by crowds, and the "Struggle Movement" of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers, led by Thich Tri Quang, took control of the two cities and called for civilian government. Ky's first attempt to reassert control, landing marines at Da Nang on 3–5 April, was blocked by I Corps troops and withdrawn; on 9 April US Marines interposed on a Da Nang bridge to stop a rebel convoy⁶⁰.

On 15 May Ky airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang and took the city centre; the I Corps commander, General Ton That Dinh, fled to the US Marine headquarters, and III MAF found itself between two factions of its ally, preventing a bridge and an ammunition dump being blown and readying jets to intercept Vietnamese aircraft if Ky's air force struck the rebels. Thi agreed to go into exile at the end of May; on 26 May a funeral riot burned the US Information Service library. In June the Struggle supporters blocked Hue's streets with family altars until police removed them; Tri Quang was arrested and placed under house arrest in Saigon, and government control of Hue was restored by 19 June. About 150 people were killed and more than 700 wounded on both sides, including 23 Americans wounded⁶⁰.

The uprising ended the Buddhist movement as an organised political force and confirmed Thieu and Ky in power⁶⁰. It also suspended operations in I Corps for three months at the height of the American buildup, and was answered with a political concession: elections for a Constituent Assembly, held on 11 September 1966.

In this war the uprising runs on the historical dates and ends as it did, and the police that Ky used against it was the one the decree of 1964 had built. On 12 March 1966, two days after Thi's dismissal, the Directory removed Director General Cuong by a decree "reorganising" the Directorate, which his statute did not allow, and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, already director of the Military Security Service. The US Mission protested and did not pull the lever; the Directory was the government the Americans had just embraced at Honolulu, and Washington would not quarrel with it over a policeman in the month the Struggle Movement began. In May and June Loan took Field Force companies and Saigon police into Da Nang and Hue, and in Hue the Special Branch's provincial section gave him the family census of the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university, compiled in 1965 for another purpose. About 1,300 people were detained in Thua Thien and Quang Nam by the end of July. The magistrates' 72-hour review was the one part of the statute Loan could not suspend without a further decree, and the Mission made that decree the price of the Public Safety programme; by October about 1,000 of the detained had been released by the courts⁵.

The repression was more efficient than the historical one and it cost more. The first American newspaper stories about "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, three years earlier than the Phoenix stories of the historical course. Several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders in Hue, knowing that the census had found them, went to the Front's Hue city committee in the summer of 1966, as some of their number did historically. They took with them what they knew of how the census was kept and where its copies were held. The rural sections of the Special Branch were not used against the pagodas, because the Mission's line held in the provinces where it had failed in Saigon, and in the villages the police went on as before⁵.

The officer reforms put a third party into the uprising, and the palace noticed it. Of the 1st Division's battalions in Thua Thien and Quang Tri, about two-thirds were commanded in March 1966 by officers from the board's list, most of whom owed nothing to Thi; when the division headquarters declared for the Struggle in April, four of those battalions stayed on operations in the hills west of Hue and refused orders from both Hue and Saigon to march on the cities, while the battalions whose commanders Thi had appointed went into the streets as they did historically. The Struggle's armed support in Hue was therefore thinner than on the historical course, and Ky's operation of May took Hue no faster, because the Struggle's strength had always been in the crowds. After the uprising the board's ranked list for the 1st Division was headed by Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong, and Ky, who needed a commander the Americans would trust in Hue, took the list's first name, as the historical junta took the same man. The corps went to Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam, a loyalist the Directory chose for loyalty, because no list governed the corps. In Saigon the Directory drew the lesson that the merit commanders had disobeyed its orders as well as Thi's, and the first report of the Military Security Service on "the board's officers as a political factor" is dated July 1966¹¹.

#### The Regional Force companies go to the corps
*4 Apr – 30 Oct 1966 · Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu Provinces, IV Corps, and the edge of the U Minh forest · new · EV004-2*
*Initiative: allied*

The rearmed Regional Forces were good enough by 1966 that the corps wanted them, and that is the whole of this event. On 4 April 1966 the IV Corps headquarters at Can Tho ordered 34 Regional Force companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu, the companies that had fought best in 1965 by the advisers' reports, formed into six "mobile groups" under corps control for a season of operations against the fringes of the U Minh forest. The order made military sense on its face. The companies carried M4A1s with sights and had been trained on them for two years; the ARVN 21st Division was stretched between GOLDEN SHEAF on the Xa No canal and the U Minh, and RF companies cost the corps nothing in its own establishment. Lieutenant Colonel Lam Van Thien, the province chief of Chuong Thien, protested in writing that the companies were the reaction force for forty-one PF outposts and nine police posts in his province, and was told that the outposts had their own carbines and their own radios now¹⁷¹³.

The mobile groups fought eleven operations between May and September along the Cai Lon and the U Minh's eastern edge, and they fought them well: in the largest, on 18–21 July near Thoi Binh, two groups and a battalion of the 21st Division caught a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion crossing a canal at dawn, and the RF marksmen and squad fire killed about 40 of it for nine of their own dead. The corps counted about 610 enemy killed for the season, a figure inflated in the usual way, and the province chiefs counted what the season cost them. Between May and August, in the three provinces whose companies had gone, 23 PF outposts and six police posts were overrun or abandoned, about 140 Popular Forces soldiers and 31 constables were killed, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s; in the same months of 1965, with the companies at home, the same districts had lost seven outposts. The Front's U Minh command had not needed to plan anything. It had watched the boats carry the companies away and attacked the posts whose calls no longer had anyone to answer them¹⁷.

The companies went back to their provinces in October, after the province chiefs of Chuong Thien and Ba Xuyen took their returns to the American deputy for pacification in IV Corps, and the case went from Can Tho to Saigon. It was the evidence this headquarters used in the spring of 1967, when CORDS was being formed, for the rule that Regional Force companies belonged to the province chief and could be taken for corps operations only with the province chief's consent and for no more than thirty days¹⁷. On the historical course the RF were not good enough in 1966 to be worth borrowing. Here they were, and the hamlets paid for it until the rule was written.

In this war every one of the forty-one PF outposts in Chuong Thien had a set on the district net, and the returns that went to Saigon were the better for it. Of the 23 outposts lost between May and August, 19 had called the district within ten minutes of the first round, and the district's logs showed each call, the time, and the answer: no company to send, and a 105mm battery at Vi Thanh whose guns could reach only nine of the forty-one posts. Lam Van Thien put the logs in front of the American deputy for pacification beside his returns. The radio had not saved the posts; it had moved their weakness from the call to the answer, and it had written down, minute by minute, where the answer failed. That record made the case for the rule of 1967 harder to refuse, and it made a second case beside it, for guns that the districts could call without the corps: the territorial artillery platoons that the districts of the Delta received from 1970 began as Thien's request of October 1966¹³.

#### Operation HARD CROSSING
*12 Apr – 31 Oct 1966 · Mu Gia pass and Route 15; Ban Karai pass and Route 912; the Xe Bang Fai crossings and the Ban Laboy ford; the Laotian panhandle south to Tchepone · new · EV005-2*
*Initiative: allied*

Operation HARD CROSSING was the first campaign in which the B-52J was allowed to do what it was built for, and it is narrated at length because what it proved was not its primary result. On 12 April 1966, the day Washington historically first sent B-52s into North Vietnam, against the Mu Gia pass, it authorized the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the Laotian panhandle and in Route Package 1 below the 18th parallel. The bombers had struck in Laos since December 1965 with unguided bombs on truck parks the panhandle watch had found; now they were given the folders. On the first night eight Js of the 4133rd Bombardment Wing (Provisional) put 160 GBU-31s on eleven targets: the two road bridges and the bypass causeway at the foot of the Mu Gia pass on Route 15, the bridges on Route 12 below the pass, and the fords and cable ferries where Route 912 and Route 911 crossed the Xe Bang Fai. The weather was low cloud over the Annamite chain and made no difference. Every target was down or cratered by dawn, and the U-2S's radar pass on 13 April confirmed it through the cloud¹⁹.

For six weeks the crossings stayed down. Each time the engineers of Group 559 repaired a bridge or reopened a ford, the panhandle watch saw the trucks begin to move across it, the cell re-measured the new structure, and the next night's Js cut it again; on 28 April the Mu Gia bypass was cut for the fourth time in sixteen days. The counts at the panhandle watch's entry gates fell through May to about a third of the March level, and MACV J-2's daily estimate of tonnage moving south of Tchepone fell to about half¹⁹. In Saigon and in Honolulu that was read, for a few weeks, as the Trail closing. It was not.

What the radar watched through the summer was the enemy rebuilding the panhandle on a principle the bombs could not touch. Group 559, under Phan Trong Tue and his engineers, gave up building bridges where the J could find them. At every crossing the bombers cut, the engineer battalions laid two, three and then five alternatives: fords paved with stone a foot under the water, which the U-2S's radar could not see and its camera saw only at low water; bamboo and log causeways floated into place at dusk and sunk or dismantled at dawn; pontoon sections of Soviet manufacture, which arrived through Haiphong from June, hidden by day under the bank. At Ban Laboy, where Route 912 crossed the Xe Bang Fai, the cell counted one ford in April, four in July and nine by the end of October, linked by a braid of bypass tracks that the U-2S mapped faster than the folders could hold them¹⁹. Each new crossing cost the enemy a few hundred labourers' nights; each cost this command a measurement, a coordinate, a sortie and a GBU-31 kit. The arithmetic ran in his favour from the first month, and his engineers knew it before we did.

By September the trucks moving south of Tchepone were back to the March level, and by the end of October about 15 percent above it, as the dry season approached. HARD CROSSING had destroyed about 210 bridges, ferries and causeways at a cost of about 1,900 GBU-31 kits and no bombers lost. It had delayed the enemy's flow for about six weeks in the spring and taxed it for the rest of the year, and the radar's ledger showed the tax clearly: about 10–15 percent of what would otherwise have moved, at its peak in May, and about 5 percent by October. What it had built for him, without his asking, was a road net with no single point of failure. The panhandle of the autumn of 1966 was harder to cut than the panhandle of the spring had been, in a way that the panhandle of history never became, because history never cut its bridges so cleanly¹⁹. The lesson the enemy drew was the right one and was drawn in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so he would own nothing it could measure. The lesson this headquarters drew took a year longer. Bridges that die in a night are not the same thing as tonnage that does not arrive.

#### ◆ Operation OPEN ROAD
*3–21 May 1966 · Route 13 from Chon Thanh to An Loc, the Xa Cam rubber and the Tau O crossing, Binh Long Province; Bau Bang, Binh Duong Province · new · EV007-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

The ambush that was never sprung on Route 13 is the clearest case in this war of what the officer reforms did and why no report showed it. In April 1966 the VC 9th Division ordered its 272nd Regiment, three battalions of about 1,500 men with 82mm mortars, 75mm recoilless rifles and RPD guns, to lay an ambush on Route 13 between Chon Thanh and An Loc for the next ARVN convoy to the Binh Long garrison, on the road where the regiment had broken relief columns in 1965. The regiment's commander, Lieutenant Colonel Dang Huu Thoi, chose the ground that every regiment on that road had chosen: the Xa Cam rubber south of An Loc, where the trees came to the edge of the laterite on both sides, the Tau O stream crossing to stop the head of the column, and an L of two battalions along the eastern side with the third astride the road behind¹¹. The dry season had not yet broken, and the rubber's floor was open for 300 metres under the canopy.

The convoy left Chon Thanh at 0600 on 4 May: 62 trucks of rice, ammunition and fuel for the Binh Long sector, escorted by the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, with a troop of M113s from the 1st Armored Cavalry Squadron. Its commander, Major Bui Quang Trieu, had been given the battalion from the board's list in September and had written the operation order himself on the pattern the career course taught for a route through ground the enemy could choose. Two rifle companies moved on foot through the rubber 300 to 400 metres on each side of the road, a kilometre ahead of the first truck, with PF guides from the hamlets at Tan Khai; the battalion's 81mm mortars and a battery of 105mm M101A1 howitzers at Chon Thanh were registered on the Tau O crossing and four points in the Xa Cam rubber; a US Army O-1 Bird Dog flew over the column with the battalion's adviser on its net; and the trucks did not move until the flanking companies reported each kilometre clear¹¹. It was slow. The convoy covered 27 kilometres in eleven hours, about half the speed the sector had demanded, and Trieu's division commander told him so by radio twice.

The 272nd's reconnaissance saw the flanking companies at about 0830, coming through the rubber towards the eastern arm of the L at a walk, and Thoi had to decide. The Front's rule for its regiments was to fight only where victory was certain, and an ambush whose killing zone would be entered from the side by an infantry company with its fire already registered was not that. His political officer argued that the regiment had been in position for nine days and that its men expected a fight. Thoi withdrew the regiment east into the rubber at 0910, without firing, and the convoy reached An Loc at 1700¹¹. The 5th Division's report of the day recorded Operation OPEN ROAD as completed without contact. MACV's weekly summary did not mention it.

On 21 May the 272nd's 1st and 3rd Battalions ambushed an RF convoy of the Binh Duong sector, two companies escorting fourteen trucks on Route 13 near Bau Bang, which moved in column with its escort on the trucks and no one in the trees. The RF lost about 40 killed, 30 wounded and nine trucks burned; about 20 bodies were found and the sector's report of 85 enemy killed is no better than such reports usually were¹¹. The regiment had not stopped fighting. It had moved its fight to the unit that did not scout its route.

What happened on 4 May was learned only in January 1967, when a notebook of the 272nd's operations officer was taken near Lai Khe. It recorded the decision of 4 May in a sentence: that "the puppet battalion now walks beside the road and not on it", and that the regiment should "seek its targets among the units that still ride". That is the reform's largest effect in the field, and the one no indicator records: the battalions the board chose lost fewer men because they were attacked less, and an attack that is not made leaves nothing to count. I estimate that the ARVN lost about a third fewer battalions rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, a judgment that rests on cases like this one and cannot be measured¹¹.

The Bau Bang convoy is also the clearest case of what the basic course could not do. The two Regional Force companies of the Binh Duong sector had each been through the six-week territorial course, and when the 272nd's fire hit the trucks at 0940 their men did what the course had taught them: they got off the trucks on the side away from the fire, found cover in the ditch, returned fire in pairs and reported the enemy's positions on the sector net, and the second company, at the rear, put its two M249s into the treeline within the first two minutes¹. It made the ambush cost the regiment more and the RF less: I put the RF dead at about 32 rather than about 40, with about 30 wounded of whom more lived, and the 272nd's dead at about 25 found rather than 20. It did not make the convoy walk its flanks. The companies rode because their sector commander ordered them to ride, and a private's training does not reach the order that puts him on a truck in the Xa Cam rubber. The 272nd's notebook of January 1967 drew the right conclusion in its last line: that the puppet territorials "now fight from the ditch", and that the regiment's ambushes must kill in the first volley or not be laid².

#### ◆ The devaluation and the trained deserters
*18 Jun – 31 Dec 1966 · Saigon; Gia Dinh, Hau Nghia and Long An Provinces; the Quang Trung Training Center and the 25th Division's districts around Duc Hoa and Cu Chi · new · EV009-3*
*Initiative: neither*

On 18 June 1966 the government devalued the piaster from 60 to 118 to the dollar for most transactions, under American pressure to absorb the money the build-up was pouring into Saigon. The soldier paid for it. Prices in Saigon rose by about half over 1966, the Republic that had exported rice ten years earlier imported it that year, and the pay of an ARVN private, raised by far less than his family's rice, bought about a third less by December than it had in May⁶¹. The families of the 25th Division's soldiers lived in the division's own districts of Hau Nghia and Long An, in the dependents' quarters at Duc Hoa and in hamlets where the Front collected its own rice tax, and the wives who had fed a household on a private's pay in May could not do it in the autumn².

Desertion rose as it rose historically, and the basic course did nothing to stop it, because men did not desert for want of training. The course did change what a deserter took home. The men who walked out of the 25th Division's battalions in the second half of 1966, about 2,000 of them by the division's count, had all been through Quang Trung; most went back to their families' fields, some to the Regional Forces nearer home, and a few hundred, by the Front's own documents, to the guerrilla platoons of Cu Chi and Duc Hoa districts. A notebook taken from a Cu Chi district unit in March 1967 recorded a three-week course for the district's guerrilla squad leaders taught by "a brother who was trained by the puppets at Quang Trung", on firing in pairs, moving by bounds and the tourniquet, with the observation that the puppets' course was good and that their pay was not. Across the provinces around Saigon I estimate that a few thousand trained deserters a year in 1966–67 reached the Front's local forces and guerrillas, and that their training made the Front's village platoons there somewhat better at the first minute of a fight than history's; it did not change their numbers, which the Front's recruitment and taxation decided².

Colonel Vu Ngoc Hoan, who took the 25th Division in March 1967, answered with what lay within his reach: he moved the dependents' quarters inside the battalions' perimeters, paid the soldiers' rice ration in rice rather than in piasters wherever the division's quartermaster could buy it, and cut the division's desertion by about a quarter in the year². None of that was training. It was the family, the pay and the rice, which the course did not touch and the division commander did.

#### The POL strikes
*29 Jun 1966 · Hanoi and Haiphong petroleum storage areas · changed from history · EV000-27*
On 28 June 1966, after weeks of postponement for weather and leaks, McNamara asked the President for authority to strike the two principal petroleum, oil and lubricant storage installations in the Hanoi–Haiphong area; Johnson, worried among other things about hitting a Soviet tanker in Haiphong harbour, assented, and the strikes went in on 29 June. McNamara's argument was that the North's ability to support the war in the South depended on its fuel⁶². The strikes destroyed most of the bulk storage the North then had.

In this war the argument against them was on the table before they flew, and it was the U-2S's. Its imagery of May and June showed 55-gallon drums stacked in their thousands under trees along the roads south of Hanoi, small buried tanks at the river crossings, and tank barges moored in the creeks of the delta: the dispersal had begun before the decision was taken. MACV J-2 sent the photographs to CINCPAC on 14 June with the judgment that the bulk farms were no longer the system. The decision was the President's and it did not change; the target was in the Rolling Thunder programme, and a better photograph of it did not alter the theory that had chosen it. The U-2S's radar and camera pass on 30 June showed the Duc Giang farm at Hanoi and the Thuong Ly farm at Haiphong burning. Over the next four weeks the Gulf watch counted the trucks on Route 1A and the coastal road south of Thanh Hoa, and found no fall in their number. On 1 August J-2 reported to CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs that the POL campaign had not measurably reduced movement to the South, about three months before the intelligence community reached the same judgment historically¹⁵.

Imports through Haiphong continued. It was the most important target system Rolling Thunder was allowed to attack before 1967, and the clearest demonstration that an agrarian economy supplied by its allies could absorb strategic bombing directed at a modern economy's vulnerabilities. What changed was when Washington knew it. McNamara had the measured result before the Jason summer study of August 1966 reported to him, and his doubts about the bombing, which historically hardened through the autumn, hardened in August instead¹⁵. The bombing went on; it was not this headquarters' to stop, and the President did not stop it.

#### The Sihanoukville count
*Jul 1966 – Nov 1967 · Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Route 4 and Kompong Speu, and the roads to the base areas of the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak, Cambodia; Tan Son Nhut and Washington · new · EV003-4*
By the summer of 1966 the sea route that TIDE TABLE had closed had been a year in Cambodia, and the argument over it was the oldest in the intelligence community. Historically MACV J-2 held that most of the arms for the war in III and IV Corps came through Sihanoukville, and the CIA held that the port's traffic was mostly rice and contraband, until the records of the Hak Ly Trucking Company, seized after Lon Nol's coup in 1970, showed about 21,600 tons of arms and ammunition moved through the port between December 1966 and April 1969. Here the argument was settled by counting, in the first half of 1967.

The count had three sources. The U-2S photographed the port obliquely from the Gulf of Siam, from outside Cambodian airspace, about twice a month: ships alongside, their flags, cargo on the quays and trucks in the marshalling area. The Global Hawk, on the nights the orbit could be spared from the coast, stood off over Tay Ninh and Binh Long inside South Vietnamese airspace and watched the road traffic up to 150–200 kilometres into Cambodia: convoys of ten to thirty trucks moving at night from Kompong Speu and Kompong Cham toward Snoul, Mimot and the Fishhook, and toward Svay Rieng and the Parrot's Beak, stopping at depots that did not appear on any Cambodian map¹⁴. And from May 1967 the reconnaissance teams of MACV's Studies and Observations Group, crossing the border under Operation Daniel Boone, were sent to look at the points where the convoys stopped. The radar did not see what the trucks carried. The teams, twice, found ammunition crates with Chinese markings in the depots the tracks had led them to; one team of three Americans and nine Montagnards was lost near Mimot in September 1967¹⁵.

On 9 March 1967 MACV J-2 and the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence agreed a joint estimate: that between July 1965 and February 1967 about 9,000–13,000 tons of military cargo had moved from Sihanoukville to the border base areas, and that the route supplied most of the arms and ammunition of the PAVN and VC forces in III and IV Corps. Lieutenant Colonel Harold Kessling's cell had built the count night by night for twenty months. The Joint Chiefs proposed in April a naval quarantine of Sihanoukville and air strikes on the depots; the President declined both, and Sihanouk's neutrality, and the bar on operations into Cambodia, stood¹⁵. The estimate changed no rule. It changed the argument: from 1967 the war's central sanctuary was a measured fact, and the case for and against relief of the Cambodian constraint was made from evidence, three years before history made it from captured ledgers.

#### The lot numbers
*12 Jul – 19 Dec 1966 · Cao Lanh and the Plain of Reeds, Kien Phong Province; the 9th Division ammunition supply point at Sa Dec; Cholon; and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · new · EV004-3*
On 12 July 1966 a company of the ARVN 9th Division overran a VC firing position on the edge of the Plain of Reeds north of Cao Lanh and picked up about 900 spent 5.56mm cases from the ground behind it. The Front's local forces in Kien Phong had been firing captured M4A1s for a year; what the company's American adviser, Captain Robert Halloran, noticed was that the cases were bright and all bore the same headstamp lot, and that the lot was one his own battalion had drawn in May. Every case of M855A1 delivered under IRON GATE carried its lot on the headstamp and on the crate, and since 1964 the Pay and Muster Verification Service had recorded the lots issued to each unit against its muster roll alongside the serials of its carbines¹⁷. Halloran sent the cases to Can Tho; the Commission's investigators had them in Saigon within the week.

Commissioner Truong Vinh Khoa gave the case to the same documentary team that was building the ration case against the IV Corps quartermaster. Over four months they matched lots recovered from eleven VC firing positions and two caches in Kien Phong and Kien Tuong against the issue ledgers of the 9th Division's ammunition supply point at Sa Dec, and found that about 1.4 million rounds of 5.56mm had left the point in 1965–66 on issue vouchers for units whose muster rolls showed no such expenditure. The rounds had gone by sampan to two dealers in Cholon and from them, by way of a Front purchasing agent in Hong Ngu, to the Plain of Reeds, at about 18 piasters a round. On 19 December the Commission's judicial police arrested the supply point's commander, a major, his chief clerk and three sergeants, and the two Cholon dealers; the Special Court convicted all seven in March 1967¹⁷.

The case did not stop the leak of ammunition to the Front; it moved it. After December the dealers bought whole crates from units in the field rather than loose lots from the depot, and the Front's purchasing agents paid more for unmarked Chinese 7.62mm than for the Republic's 5.56mm, which they had learned could be traced¹⁷. What it did was establish, three years into the rearmament, that a cartridge case found on a battlefield could put an officer in prison, and that the trail from a firefight to the man who sold the rounds now ran through a register the Commission kept. Khoa told the Board in January 1967 that it was the first case in which the war itself had supplied the evidence.

#### The relief at Dien Ban
*8–31 Aug 1966 · Dien Ban and Dai Loc Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon · new · EV006-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

The relief at Dien Ban was the first time the enemy attacked an American battalion in the week it changed over, and it showed both halves of what unit rotation had done. The 1st Battalion, 9th Marines had come ashore at Da Nang in July 1965 and had spent a year in the hamlets of Dien Ban south of the airfield, the same companies under the same commander throughout. Under the rotation it was to be relieved in place by the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, which had formed at Camp Pendleton in October 1965, trained together for nine months and sailed from Okinawa in late July under Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey; the two battalions were to overlap from 8 to 26 August, company by company¹⁰. The relief dates had been fixed in March and were known, as all such dates were, to every Vietnamese who sold the Marines ice or washed their clothes.

The 1st Battalion, 26th Marines arrived knowing nothing of Dien Ban. Its men had trained on the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 for six months and fired them as squads that had not changed since January; its officers had studied Ellison's area from the outgoing commander's reports and an overlay; none of them had walked the paddies at Phong Thu or Ky Lam, where the R-20 Doc Lap Battalion and the Front's Dien Ban district company had laid mines along the bunds for a year and knew every one of the old battalion's habits. What Dien Ban did have was continuity in the other places this headquarters had put it. The ARVN 51st Regiment's battalion advisers and the Dien Ban subsector adviser, Major Carl E. Haney, were all men of the Selected Advisory Corps on eighteen-month tours, two of them in their thirteenth month, and the district chief and the DIOCC had been in place since 1965. Haney walked each of Dorsey's company commanders over his ground with the Popular Forces platoon leaders of the hamlets it held, and gave each a list, in English and Vietnamese, of the PF corporals who would tell a Marine what the hamlet knew¹⁰.

The Front had read the calendar too. On the night of 18–19 August, with two companies of the old battalion already embarked at Da Nang and two of the new on the ground for less than a week, the R-20 Battalion and the district company attacked Company B, 1st Battalion, 26th Marines in its new position at the hamlet of La Huan, about 4 km west of Route 1, at 0130 under a thin moon, with 82mm mortars from across the Song Ky Lam and a sapper assault from the south through a tree line the outgoing company had kept clear and the new one had not yet learned to watch. The first assault put a section of sappers inside the wire. What followed was the half of the event the rotation had bought: the company's platoons, which had trained together for nine months, closed on the breach without waiting for orders, the squad guns took the paddy south of the wire under grazing fire, and by 0230 the sappers inside were dead and the second wave had not crossed the bund. A PF platoon in the next hamlet, which Haney had introduced to the company's commander on the 14th, reported the R-20's withdrawal route by AN/PRC-25 at 0310, and two F-4Bs of Marine Aircraft Group 11 and the battalion's 81mm mortars hit it at the Song Ky Lam crossing before dawn¹⁰.

The relief window had cost the Marines 14 killed and 37 wounded, about four times the battalion's average night that summer, and the enemy about 40–60 dead, of whom 23 were found⁶³. The outgoing battalion would not have been hit that way; it knew the tree line. The incoming one, under the old individual system, would have been a battalion of strangers at the breach, and I believe the sappers would have held the breach long enough to reach the company's ammunition point⁶⁴. MACV drew two orders from Dien Ban: from October 1966 reliefs were staggered across six weeks by company and kept off the dark of the moon, and no incoming company took over a position without a night with the outgoing one beside it. Both orders added men in theater at every relief. The R-20 Battalion drew its own lesson in a document captured in November: the new Marines "fight like the old ones in their third month and know the ground like the old ones in their first"¹⁰.

#### Operation Bolo
*2 Jan 1967 · Over the Hanoi area, North Vietnam · historical · EV000-31*
By late 1966 the MiG-21s of the VPAF were attacking F-105 strike formations from bases the rules of engagement placed off limits. On 2 January 1967 the 8th Tactical Fighter Wing under Colonel Robin Olds flew F-4C Phantoms along the routes and at the altitudes and speeds of an F-105 strike, using F-105 call signs and electronic countermeasures pods. The MiG-21s rose to meet what they took for bomb-laden Thunderchiefs, and seven were shot down in twelve minutes with no US loss. It was the most successful single air battle of Rolling Thunder for the United States, and it bought a few months' relief; the VPAF stood down to retrain and came back with better tactics, and its strength grew from about 100 interceptors in 1967 to 180 MiGs by 1968³⁸.

#### The arrest count falls
*Feb – Nov 1967 · Saigon; Phong Dinh, Long An and Vinh Long Provinces; and the towns of the Delta · new · EV002-5*
*Initiative: allied*
In the first quarter of 1967 the Special Branch's arrests of infrastructure in the held belt of the Delta fell to about 40 percent of their peak of the second quarter of 1966, and the Phung Hoang centres' neutralizations of district-level cadres fell with them, from about 140 a quarter in 1966 to about 50. The Office of the Secretary of Defense's systems analysts read the decline as a programme losing its grip, and in February Robert Komer, then the President's special assistant for pacification, proposed monthly arrest targets by province as the measure of the attack on the infrastructure. This headquarters answered in writing on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working: in the districts with posts three years old there was almost no one left in the villages to arrest, and the measures the enemy could not fake, captured tax ledgers, the count of functioning village committees in captured documents, and the age of captured guerrillas, all showed the infrastructure shrinking there. The Director General of National Police, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, wanted the targets; Commissioner Khoa opposed them on the record, and so did Hieu, whose decree of 1964 forbade them in terms⁵¹⁰.

The decree held, and the half-victory of the historical-course compromise was a whole one on paper: no centre and no province was given a target in 1967, and the held belt was measured by captured documents and the contested belt by the Commission's audit. It was less than a whole one on the ground. The pressure that would have gone into targets went instead into extending the method to the contested belt, where it produced what the abandoned target list had predicted, raids on thin files, and after Duc Thanh the extension was halted village by village. In the second half of 1967 the Commission received about 190 complaints of false arrest from the contested districts with centres and about 40 from the held belt, against the 430 that targets had produced on the earlier course¹⁰. And the infrastructure that had left the held villages had not disappeared. The Special Branch's provincial sections reported through 1967 that legal cadres and their families were settling in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the outskirts of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000⁵. The Branch built urban sections to follow them, slowly. The war against the infrastructure had moved toward the towns, and so, as the Branch's own reports warned in November, had the Front's means of acting there.

#### The Mo Duc raid and the Phoenix story
*11 Feb – 30 Jun 1967 · Duc Thanh village, Mo Duc District, Quang Ngai Province; Saigon; Hanoi; Washington · new · EV006-4*
*Initiative: both*

The raid at Duc Thanh was what the method became where the night was not held, and it reached the American press within a month. By the end of 1966 the held belt had little left for the district centres to find, and the corps commanders and the CORDS planners wanted the same results from the contested provinces of I and II Corps, where the Front still ran most villages after dark. Quang Ngai's province chief opened DIOCCs in four contested districts in the autumn of 1966 against Hieu's advice, and the Quang Ngai PRU, which had been raised under the statute in 1964 and had operated by its rules in the coastal towns, began to raid on files built mostly from ralliers and from the S2's informants. On the night of 11 February 1967 a PRU team of 24 men, acting on a file that named a hamlet schoolteacher in Duc Thanh as the Mo Duc district committee's propaganda chief on two sources, both ralliers from the same hamlet, went into his house at 0200; a man in the house fired a pistol, the team fired back with M4A1s and an M249, and the teacher, his wife, his father and two of his children died. The district committee's propaganda chief was a different man, arrested in May; the schoolteacher had been named by a rallier whose family had a quarrel with his over a well¹⁰.

The Front's answer was ready because Hanoi had decided, for its own reasons, to fight on a new front. The Thirteenth Plenum of January 1967 had adopted the line of "fighting while negotiating", and on 28 January Foreign Minister Nguyen Duy Trinh had said that talks could follow an unconditional halt in the bombing; the decision came from Hanoi's reading of Soviet pressure, the American election calendar and the world's opinion of the bombing, and from nothing this headquarters did. Its consequence for the Front's propaganda apparatus was a demand for material that would carry abroad. Liberation Radio broadcast the Duc Thanh deaths on 19 February as "a massacre by the American Phoenix murder teams", and the story reached an American wire service in Saigon through a Quang Ngai stringer on 2 March; by 10 March two national newspapers had run it, with the programme's American name, the CIA's role in its design and the PRUs' American pay¹⁰. On the historical course the Phoenix stories began in 1970.

Two things were different from what the historical stories would have found, and both mattered. The Commission's inspectors had the Duc Thanh file within a week and published their finding on 6 April: the file had met the three-source rule only by counting two ralliers from one feud as independent, the warrant had been signed by a district judge who had not read it, and the PRU had entered without a capture plan. The PRU team leader was tried before the Special Court in June and convicted of manslaughter; the judge was removed; Hieu withdrew the centres' authority to issue raid files in the four contested districts of Quang Ngai and in eleven others in I and II Corps until each had a post and a platoon in the village concerned. And the national committee published its first audited count on 30 June: since January 1965, about 14,500 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 9,800 were tried or held under review, about 3,900 had rallied and about 800 had been killed, about 300 of them in the programme's own raids, with the Commission's sample finding about one claim in ten unsupported in the held belt and one in four in the contested belt¹⁰. The count was small, and it was the first figure on the attack on the infrastructure that the newspapers did not later have to correct. It did not undo Duc Thanh. The word Phoenix was in the American vocabulary three years early, and it meant what the Front had said it meant.

#### The estuary field
*26 Feb – 31 Dec 1967 · The mouths of the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the lighterage anchorages at Quang Khe, Dong Hoi and Cua Viet north, and the coastal waters of Route Package 1, North Vietnam · new · EV005-3*
*Initiative: allied*

On 26 February 1967 Washington authorized, for the first time, the aerial mining of the North's rivers south of the 20th parallel. Historically Navy A-6As from the *Enterprise* laid the first Mk 50-series mines in the mouths of the Song Ca and Song Giang that night. In this war the first field was laid by four B-52Js from U-Tapao, which put 80 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes into the Song Ca estuary below Vinh and the Song Giang at Quang Khe from about 1,000 feet in a single pass each, at night, outside the guns that later covered both mouths¹⁹. The target detecting devices were set for the steel-hulled lighters and coastal freighters that carried supplies down from Thanh Hoa and Vinh; the wooden junks were left to the pressure settings, and the sampans were not mined at all, because no setting would catch them²⁰.

The panhandle watch and the Gulf watch counted the effect. In the first fortnight two steel lighters sank at the Quang Khe bar and a third in the Song Ca, and the steel traffic on the coastal route south of Vinh fell within a month to about a fifth of what it had been. Through the spring the Js reseeded each field every three to four weeks, on the cue of the Global Hawk's imagery when the North's sweepers came out, and lost no aircraft doing it. By June the steel craft had stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa¹⁹.

The cargo did not stop. It moved to wooden junks, to sampans under tow and to trucks on Route 1A and Route 15, and by the autumn the radar's count of southbound truck movements below Vinh was about 25–30 percent higher than in 1966 while the tonnage counted into the panhandle was about the same. The coastal lighterage had carried perhaps a fifth of what moved south of Vinh, and the road took nearly all of it in four months. Its value was in time and labour: the North spent perhaps 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers on Route Package 1 in 1967 than it otherwise would, and its trucks spent more nights in the open where the radar could count them. Hanoi asked Moscow for wooden-hulled craft and for minesweeping help; it got trucks, which was what it needed¹⁹. The field stayed in place until the bombing halt of November 1968, and nobody in Washington asked, because nobody could answer, how much less arrived in the South because of it. My own answer is very little.

#### The list and the 25th Division
*4 Mar – 31 Oct 1967 · Duc Hoa, Hau Nghia Province, and the 25th Division's districts in Hau Nghia and Long An; the Joint General Staff and the Officer Selection Board, Saigon · new · EV007-4*
*Initiative: allied*

The 25th Division was the ARVN's worst, and in 1967 it became the test of whether the ranked list could reach a division. Its battalions, in Hau Nghia and western Long An between Saigon and the Parrot's Beak, had the highest desertion rate and the lowest rate of contact in III Corps, its regiments had been commanded by the division commander's appointees until the board's lists of 1965–66, and its commander, appointed in 1965 before the list existed, had refused to move his headquarters out of Duc Hoa at night. When he was due for reassignment in March 1967 the board's ranked list put three colonels before the Directory. Ky wanted a fourth man, an Air Force patron's cousin who was not on it; Thieu, as Chief of State, took the second name, Colonel Vu Ngoc Hoan, a Dalat graduate of 1952 who had commanded a regiment of the 7th Division well under the board's list, after the US Mission told both men through the Aid Compliance Board that the division's counterpart funds depended on the list (Article 14). It was one of two division commands in ten filled from the list against a palace preference by the end of 1967¹¹.

Hoan relieved three battalion commanders in his first ninety days, all through the board's thirty-day reviews and all for what the reviews called failure to close with the enemy, and put the division's battalions into the districts at night beside the Popular Forces, which the corps had never asked it to do. By October the division's desertion rate had fallen by about a quarter, its battalions were in contact about twice as often as in 1966, and the Front's district companies in Duc Hoa and Duc Hue had moved their bases toward the Cambodian border¹¹. None of this changed who held most of Hau Nghia's hamlets after dark, which the division could visit and could not hold.

In July the board extended its lists to the commanders of the Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, at the request of the III Corps commander after the losses of the RF convoys on Route 13. About 280 RF company commanders were reviewed by the end of the year and about 60 replaced, most of them by field-commissioned lieutenants of their own provinces¹¹.

On 11 October the Military Security Service arrested Lieutenant Truong Cong Hy, a clerk of the board's secretariat since 1965, as an agent of the B2 Front's military intelligence. He had copied every command list the board had published, with the panels' ratings of each selected officer, and passed them by courier to the Cu Chi district committee. The COSVN target notes taken with his courier in November showed what the lists had been used for: the B2 Front's regiments had been told which battalions in III Corps were under new commanders and which were not, and had planned their ambushes of 1967 against the second¹¹. The board's best evidence of its own effect came from the man who had betrayed it.

#### The Special Court convicts the IV Corps quartermaster
*6 Mar – 18 Apr 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province, and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · new · EV001-7*
On 6 March 1967 the Commission Against Corruption arrested Brigadier General Phan Duy Khiem, IV Corps deputy commander for logistics since 1965, with eleven officers of the corps quartermaster and finance staffs at Can Tho. The case was documentary and had been built for fourteen months. The Pay and Muster Verification Service's parade payments had removed ghost pay from the corps' units in 1964–65, and the graft had moved, as this headquarters had expected, to rations and fuel. The Service's auditors matched the corps' monthly ration draws against the strength the musters had established and found the corps drawing rice, canned fish and salt for about 9,000 more men than it had; a parallel audit by the Joint Aid Compliance Board traced about 2.1 million litres of motor fuel issued to RF companies with no vehicles and sold through two Cholon dealers. The total over two years came to about 310 million piasters, about $2.6 million at the official rate⁴.

The arrest was made by the Commission's own judicial police, Vietnamese officers under Commissioner Khoa, with no American present. General Khiem was the first general officer the Commission had charged, three years after it was founded; it had refused the junta's invitations to begin at the top in 1964 and had worked upward from battalion and district. His patron in the Directory tried to have the case moved to a military court in March; the Deputy Chief of Mission, as American co-chair of the Board, told the Directory's chairman, General Thieu, that the US Mission would suspend counterpart funds to IV Corps under the Board's authority if the case was moved, and Thieu, then preparing his presidential candidacy and wanting no quarrel with the Embassy, let it stand. The Special Court tried the case in public in Saigon over nine sitting days and on 18 April 1967 sentenced Khiem to eight years and confiscation of property, and nine of the eleven officers to terms of two to six years⁴.

The conviction mattered more in the officer corps than in the countryside. It established that the Commission's reach now ran to the rank below the Directory and no higher; that it reached that rank only on documentary evidence and with the American lever behind it; and that a general who stole from his troops' rations could go to prison. The American press reported it as a sign that the Saigon government was cleaning itself up, and for a few weeks the corruption stories of the spring were about a conviction rather than a scandal. The officers of the IV Corps staff who replaced the convicted were chosen in part from the Commission's own list of officers with clean records, and there were not enough of them⁴.

#### The night of the relief at My Phuoc Tay
*9–10 Mar 1967 · The PF post at Ap My Loi and the canal road to Cai Lay district town, My Phuoc Tay village, Cai Lay District, Dinh Tuong Province · new · EV008-4*
*Initiative: enemy*

This is the night the radio's second-order effect became visible, and the night that shows it best. By 1967 every Popular Forces post in the held belt of Dinh Tuong had a set on the district net, a PRC-25 from the Go Vap plant since the previous autumn, and a fresh battery every Thursday from the exchange at the district¹³. The Front had stopped assaulting those posts in 1965, as it had stopped assaulting the rearmed ones. In the first months of 1967 the Dinh Tuong provincial unit decided to use the radio against the men it called.

At 2235 on 9 March a mortar section of the 514th Local Force Battalion put twelve 60mm rounds into the post at Ap My Loi, a mud-walled fort on the canal six kilometres north of Cai Lay, from about 300 metres across the paddy. Sergeant Le Van Muoi, who commanded its PF platoon, called the district at 2237, reported the mortar and an assault forming on the dike to the west, and asked for the reaction force. The assault did not come. Two companies of the 514th were not on the dike at all; they were lying in the sugar cane on both sides of the canal road, three kilometres south of the post, where the road crossed a concrete culvert that the district's trucks had to slow to cross¹³.

The district chief, Major Nguyen Thanh Liem, did what the rule of 1964 told him, that a post that called would have its reaction within the hour, and sent the 247th Regional Force Company north in four trucks at 2250. At 2304 a command-detonated mine under the culvert lifted the lead truck off the road. The firing circuit, the district's engineers found in the morning, was two kilometres of WD-1 field wire from the Republic's own mill and a pack of Go Vap dry cells of the kind the post had been issued the week before. The two companies fired into the other three trucks from both sides for about eight minutes, took the weapons from the first, and were gone into the cane before the 7th Division's battery at Cai Lay had been given a target. The 247th lost 31 dead and 18 wounded and about 40 weapons, most of them M4A1 carbines and two M249s. The post itself lost one man, wounded by the mortar. The district reported 46 of the enemy killed; eight bodies were found at the culvert in the morning, and I believe the 514th lost between ten and fifteen killed¹³.

Liem wrote new rules the following week, and they are the reason the night belongs in the history. A post's first call would be for fire, not men: every post in the district had its concentrations plotted for the 105mm battery at Cai Lay and the 7th Division's guns at Cai Be, and the call was answered by the guns within four minutes. A relief would move by water or at first light, never by road at night on the one road the Front could predict. And a post that could hold behind its wire would be expected to. The rules spread through IV Corps in the spring, carried by the province advisers and by the 7th Division's artillery officers. In the months that followed, attacks on posts within range of the guns in Dinh Tuong fell by about a third, and the Front's district companies turned to the posts beyond the guns' 11 kilometres and to the posts whose batteries were run down at the end of the exchange week¹³.

The rules had a cost that the returns did not show and Liem's adviser did. A platoon that could call down the guns had less reason to leave its wire after dark, and in 1967 the night patrols of the PF platoons in Cai Lay fell by about a third; the post held itself, and the hamlet outside it was walked at night, more often than before, by the Front's tax collectors¹³. Ap My Loi held every attack made on it for the rest of the war. Whether the hamlet that bore its name was the government's after dark is a question the post's radio log cannot answer.

#### The Hill Fights at Khe Sanh
*24 Apr – 11 May 1967 · Hills 861, 881 South and 881 North, northwest of Khe Sanh, Quang Tri Province · changed from history · EV000-34*
On 24 April 1967 a Marine patrol from Khe Sanh ran into PAVN troops on Hill 861, setting off three weeks of fighting for the hills that dominate the Khe Sanh plateau. The PAVN 325C Division had six battalions on the hills, preparing the ground for an attack on the Khe Sanh base. The 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 3rd Marines, with companies of the 9th Marines, took the hills one at a time against bunkers built to survive bombing: 861 on 28 April after the PAVN withdrew, 881 South on 2 May, 881 North on 5 May, with B-52 strikes and heavy artillery fire⁶⁵.

Historically the new M16 rifles carried by the Marines jammed repeatedly in this fighting, Marines were found dead beside rifles they had been trying to clear, and the failures led to a congressional inquiry and changes in ammunition and maintenance. In this war the Marines had carried the M4A1 since 1965 and none of that happened. The carbines fouled in the red mud of 881 South and were cleaned at night like every other weapon on the hill, and the battalion armourers' reports for the three weeks record stoppages at about the rate of the M14 they had replaced, not the rate of the 1967 M16. The rifles did not take the hills. The bunkers of the 325C Division, log-and-earth with firing slits a hand's breadth high, were broken by 2,000-pound bombs, 8-inch howitzers and the M72 LAW and the flamethrower at the aperture; what the squad weapons did was keep the PAVN gunners' heads down while the assault teams closed, and the marksmen, firing from the saddle between 881 South and 881 North at 400–500 metres, killed machine gunners in the apertures who would otherwise have had to be killed by a man with a satchel charge¹⁷.

I put the Marines' losses at about 125 killed and 400 wounded, against 155 killed and 425 wounded historically; the difference is the men who died with a jammed rifle in their hands, and an estimate of how many fewer died in the assaults on the bunker lines because the fire covering them did not stop. The Marines reported 940 PAVN killed historically, and I do not adjust the figure, which is the usual mixture of count and guess. About 1,500 Marines had fought about 2,000 PAVN⁶⁵. The attack on Khe Sanh was forestalled for the year, and the hills were garrisoned. No subcommittee of the House Armed Services Committee investigated the Marines' rifle, and no letters home about a rifle that jammed reached the newspapers in the summer of 1967¹⁷. The fighting confirmed the PAVN's interest in the northwest corner of Quang Tri, and fixed Westmoreland's attention on it for the next nine months.

#### CORDS is established
*9 May 1967 · Saigon · changed from history · EV000-35*
*Initiative: allied*
On 9 May 1967 the President placed the American pacification effort, civil and military, under MACV as Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support, with Robert W. Komer as one of Westmoreland's deputies, holding ambassadorial rank and authority over military personnel and resources, the first civilian to serve in such a role inside a US military command. It followed the failure of the Office of Civil Operations, set up in November 1966 to coordinate the civil agencies under civilian leadership. CORDS was about 85 percent military. Each corps had a CORDS deputy, usually civilian, under the corps senior adviser, and each province an American province senior adviser with civilian and military staffs alongside the Vietnamese province chief, usually an ARVN colonel⁶⁶.

Komer's priorities were the territorial forces and the attack on the VC infrastructure. The Regional and Popular Forces numbered about 300,000 in 1967; their advisory presence was later multiplied through Mobile Advisory Teams⁶⁶. In mid-1967 CORDS created the Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme, ICEX, to target the VCI, which became the Phoenix programme at the end of the year. The Hamlet Evaluation System, introduced in January 1967, began rating every hamlet monthly on a scale reported by the American district advisers; its figures were at once an indispensable record and a measure produced by officers rated on the result. CORDS solved the American management problem of pacification. It did not solve the Vietnamese one, that security in the hamlet at night depended on Vietnamese forces and officials who had to stay there.

In this war CORDS is established on the historical date and in the historical form; the provisions did not create a single manager. What CORDS inherits is different. The American land advisers, about 260 in 1964 and about 340 by 1967, pass into it as a land reform division under the CORDS deputy, and the provincial land services' register of titled parcels, which by May 1967 lists about 740,000 hectares and the households that hold them, becomes the first hamlet-by-hamlet household record the American pacification effort has ever had⁷. Komer adds titled hectares as a factor in the Hamlet Evaluation System's economic rating from its first revision. This headquarters objected in writing on 22 June 1967 that a title measured the government's paperwork and not the peasant's allegiance, that the Front had told its cadres to let peasants take titles and keep paying the Front's tax, and that titles issued would rise fastest where they meant least. The objection was recorded and the factor kept, at half the weight Komer had proposed. The Phung Hoang centres, which had used the land register since 1964 to locate the families of known cadres, passed into CORDS's reporting with it; the Commission had been receiving complaints about that use since 1965⁴¹⁰.

In this war CORDS inherits a police as well as a land register. The aid mission's Public Safety Division, about 300 advisers in 1966, passes into CORDS as its Public Safety Directorate, and with it a National Police of about 70,000, about 1,500 village posts, and a Special Branch with district sections in about 200 districts and files on the infrastructure kept since 1964⁸. There is no ICEX in this war, because there is nothing for it to create: the Phung Hoang centres have sat in those district sections since 1964, under the district chief, with the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and every district has one by the end of 1965. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the police statute in 1964 and answer to the province chiefs, not to the Agency; their methods are inside the statute's safeguards in law, and Duc Thanh in February showed how far that law reached in a village the government did not hold at night. Komer's contribution was the money to put a centre's American adviser in every contested district, which Hieu accepted, and a proposal for monthly targets, which the decree forbade¹⁰. What the American pacification effort created in 1967 on the historical course, here it found already running, and the province senior advisers found a counterpart chief who had been in his post for two or three years⁵.

#### Operation Buffalo and the siege of Con Thien
*2 Jul – 31 Oct 1967 · Con Thien and "the Marketplace", Quang Tri Province, south of the DMZ · changed from history · EV000-36*
*Initiative: enemy*

Con Thien, a low hill 2 miles south of the DMZ and the western anchor of the planned strongpoint barrier, was the most exposed Marine position in the country, within range of PAVN artillery north of the Ben Hai. On 2 July 1967, at a place the Marines called the Marketplace, PAVN forces ambushed and nearly destroyed Company B, 1st Battalion, 9th Marines; it was the single worst day for the Marines in Vietnam, with 86 killed. The resulting Operation Buffalo, 2–14 July, cost 159 Marines killed and 345 wounded; US sources claimed 1,290 PAVN killed. The ambush at the Marketplace was a fight in hedgerows at a few dozen metres, and nothing in the B-52J changed it¹⁹. In August there were 60 Arc Light strikes around the position⁶⁷.

From September the PAVN laid Con Thien under siege by fire, and here the siege went differently, because in this war it was the first siege to be measured. Historically the shelling ran at 200 rounds a day and more, rising to about 1,200 rounds on 25 September, and between 19 and 27 September more than 3,000 rounds hit the base⁶⁷. The guns were 130mm M-46s and 152mm howitzers dug in north of the Ben Hai and in the northern half of the DMZ, with 122mm rockets and mortars closer in, and the PAVN 90th and 812th Regiments of the 324B Division dug trenches and bunkers in the scrub south of the river to cover them. From 4 September the Global Hawk's second orbit stood over the DMZ from dusk to dawn, and its synthetic aperture radar in spot mode imaged the ground around the hill every night: each new gun pit, each new trench, the paths worn by ammunition carriers. Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut measured the positions before dawn, and from 11 September, the day Operation Neutralize began, a cell of three Js came over Con Thien every two to three hours carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s each, every weapon aimed at a measured position and the nearest of them about 400 metres from the Marines' wire²⁰¹⁹.

The shelling did not stop, but it changed shape. A gun that fired on the hill was a gun whose flash the Marines' counter-battery radar and the Global Hawk both located, and in the DMZ, where Washington had let the B-52s strike since 1966, it was dead by the next cell's arrival. The PAVN gunners learned within ten days to fire a few rounds and move, which halved their rate of fire; the great bombardment of 25 September came at about 600 rounds rather than 1,200, and the guns that fired it were struck that night. The infantry learned the same lesson at a higher price. On the night of 13–14 September the 812th Regiment's trenches had reached within about 900 metres of the hill on the southeast, and by morning about thirty GBU-38s had fallen along them. Prisoners taken in October said the regiment had stopped digging in the open and had moved its companies back across the river, because a trench that was there at dusk was found before midnight¹⁹.

Operation Neutralize brought B-52s, tactical air and naval gunfire onto the PAVN artillery and assembly areas, with 12,577 Marine artillery rounds, 6,148 naval rounds and more than 5,200 Marine and USAF sorties⁶⁷; here about a quarter of the B-52 sorties carried GBU-38s and the rest the half-density unguided carpet. The siege lifted in the first week of October, about three weeks earlier than historically. I estimate that the Marines on and around the hill lost about a third fewer killed in September and October than the historical garrison, and that the PAVN lost perhaps half again as many men to the bombs, figures that are judgments built on the historical losses and the change in the shelling, not counts. Con Thien was held. The Marines along the DMZ remained a static garrison under artillery they could not reach on the ground, but for the first time the artillery could be reached from the air, every night, in any weather; and the lesson Westmoreland took from the hill, that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle, would shape where he put his battalions in the winter¹⁹.

#### Hanoi decides on a general offensive
*6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · changed from history · EV000-37*
In April 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive in the South, and in May sent the plan to Hanoi. Thanh briefed the Politburo in July and died there on 6 July, by the most common account of a heart attack after a party, by another of wounds from a US bombing raid. The plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho; General Vo Nguyen Giap, who had favoured protracted war, did not originate it and modified it reluctantly. Beginning on 27 July, hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals were arrested in the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair", with a second wave in November and December, which removed opposition to the offensive and much of Giap's staff⁶⁸.

The premise was that the ARVN was no longer effective and the Saigon government unpopular, and that a general offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising. In October the Politburo chose the Tet holiday of 1968 as the date; it reaffirmed the decision in December and formalized it as Resolution 14 in January 1968⁶⁸. The border battles of the autumn, at Con Thien, Loc Ninh, Song Be and Dak To, were part of the preparation, drawing US forces away from the cities⁶⁹. This was a decision taken in Hanoi for Hanoi's reasons: the stalemate in the South, the factional struggle in the Party and a reading of the South that proved wrong.

In this war the decision is taken on the same dates by the same men, and for the same reasons; the provisions did not make Le Duan a militant or remove Giap's staff. They added an argument to the militants' case and subtracted one from its premise. COSVN's reports of 1966–67 recorded that the Front's agricultural tax in the Delta was falling, that recruitment in the titled districts along the Bassac was harder each season, and that the "land trick" had not been exposed as the cadres had promised; Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait. At the same time the premise that the Saigon government was unpopular in the countryside was weaker in the Delta than anywhere else, and COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 in the autumn said so; it was overruled⁴. The urgency rose and the forecast of a general uprising was carried forward unchanged, so that in this war the offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than the historical one.

The premise about the ARVN was wrong in a second way here, and the purge made sure nobody in Hanoi said so. In the spring of 1967 a study group of the General Staff's combat research department, working for Giap from the B2 and B3 Fronts' after-action reports since 1964, had concluded that the puppet infantry and its territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat as they had in 1963 and that the local forces could not expect to overrun district towns defended by them without main-force support. The group's three senior officers were among those arrested in the second wave in December, for reasons that had nothing to do with their study and everything to do with whose staff they were on, and the study went into the files with them¹⁷. The offensive was planned, as historically, on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve on contact. In this war it had been better armed than its attackers for four years.

#### The Phu Cat lot
*14 Aug – 20 Dec 1967 · A firing position of the 22nd Division's artillery on Route 1 south of Phu Cat, Binh Dinh Province; the Bien Hoa arsenal; the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · new · EV008-5*
*Initiative: neither*

On the morning of 14 August 1967 an M101A1 105mm howitzer of a 22nd Division battery firing in support of a sweep of the Phu Cat hills burst its tube on the eleventh round of a mission. Four of the crew were killed and three wounded. The round was high explosive from a lot the Bien Hoa arsenal had forged and loaded in May, and within a week the 22nd Division's artillery had found two more rounds of the same lot with cracks at the base of the body that a gauge would have caught¹³.

The investigation was the Commission's, and it was quick because the paper was good. Truong Vinh Khoa's inspectors found that the lot had been accepted by a Directorate inspector on the strength of the forging line's own records, without the proof-firing of a sample that the specification required; the inspector had accepted four lots that way since March, and had been paid for the time he saved the line. He was convicted by the Special Court in December and sentenced to six years. The arsenal recalled about 40,000 rounds from five lots and destroyed about 9,000 of them¹³.

The gunners' verdict took longer to reverse than the court's. Through the autumn of 1967 and into 1968 the artillery of II Corps, and much of the rest of the army's, refused domestic 105mm rounds where it could draw American, and the share of 105mm bodies bought under the Aid Compliance Board's rule fell from about a third to about a tenth for most of a year. What restored it was the officers the 1964 courses had made: the Ordnance Directorate's acceptance branch, under captains and majors who could read a metallurgical report, rewrote the procedure so that every lot was proof-fired at the Long Thanh range with an officer's signature on the result, and the 22nd Division's own artillery commander was invited to witness the first ten¹³. Binh Dinh's gunners went back to Bien Hoa's rounds in the summer of 1968. It was a small event, and the reason it is in the record is that it showed, three years before the palace took the Directorate, what an arsenal is worth without honest acceptance: less than nothing, because a soldier who does not trust his round will not fire it.

#### The constitution and the presidential election
*3 Sep 1967 · Republic of Vietnam · historical · EV000-38*
The Constituent Assembly elected in September 1966 produced a constitution, promulgated on 1 April 1967. In the presidential election of 3 September 1967, held with the Senate election, Thieu ran for president with Ky as his running mate, after the generals forced Ky to stand aside. Eleven tickets ran. Thieu and Ky won 34.83 percent, 1,649,561 votes; the runner-up, Truong Dinh Dzu, a lawyer campaigning on negotiations with the NLF, won 17.26 percent, 817,120 votes. The reported turnout was 83.17 percent of 5,853,251 registered voters. There were credible reports of soldiers showing voters how to mark their ballots, and of a secret military committee that would govern behind the elected government; on 10 August 57 members of the US House had condemned the conduct of the campaign. Dzu was later imprisoned. Thieu was inaugurated on 31 October and the lower house was elected on 22 October⁷⁰.

The election gave the Republic of Vietnam an elected government for the first time since 1963 and, under Thieu, the stability that the previous four years had lacked. It did not give it legitimacy in the countryside: two-thirds of the voters chose someone else, and a civilian peace candidate had come second.

#### The barrier and Muscle Shoals
*8 Sep – Dec 1967 · The DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand · changed from history · EV000-39*
In 1966 the Jason Division of the Institute for Defense Analyses, at McNamara's request, studied an anti-infiltration barrier of mines and sensors, and on 15 September 1966 McNamara placed Lieutenant General Alfred D. Starbird at the head of a Defense Communications Planning Group to build it. In this war the Jason study began from a count: the track data of STEADY COUNT and its second dry season, which showed where the trucks moved in the open, where they vanished under canopy, and how fast traffic recovered after a strike. Its conclusion was historical, that bombing alone could not stop the flow and that sensors were needed to find what the radar could not see¹⁵. Johnson authorized construction on 13 January 1967. Its eastern, conventional part, a cleared strip with strongpoints at Gio Linh, Con Thien and the bases west of them, was built under fire by the Marines in 1967; its western, electronic part across the Laotian panhandle was named Muscle Shoals on 8 September 1967⁷¹⁶⁷.

Air-delivered seismic and acoustic sensors, the ADSID and the ACOUSID among them, developed in part from the Navy's sonobuoys, would be dropped by OP-2E Neptunes of the Navy's VO-67 squadron and relayed by EC-121R aircraft to an Infiltration Surveillance Center, Task Force Alpha, at Nakhon Phanom in Thailand, where IBM 360 computers analysed the signals. The sensor strings were laid where the radar's tracks ended: at the edges of the trellised stretches on Routes 911 and 912, where the trucks went under the trees, and at the fords where they came out again. Task Force Alpha took the Global Hawk's tracks by teletype from the cell at Tan Son Nhut from its first day, so that its plotters saw a convoy in the open on the radar, lost it under canopy, and picked it up again on the sensor strings¹⁵. Field testing began in southeastern Laos on 25 November 1967. Construction cost $1–1.7 billion and operation about $1 billion a year⁷¹. The system, renamed Igloo White in 1968, would find trucks on the Trail at night as nothing before had, under the trees where the radar could not; whether it reduced what arrived in the South is a question for later phases.

#### The battle of Dak To
*3–23 Nov 1967 · Dak To and Hills 1338, 724 and 875, Kontum Province · historical · EV000-40*
In late October and early November 1967 the PAVN 1st Division, with its 24th, 32nd, 66th and 174th Regiments and the 40th Artillery Regiment, moved into the hills around Dak To near the tri-border with Laos and Cambodia, and a defector gave away much of its plan. The 4th Infantry Division and the 173rd Airborne Brigade, with the 1st, 2nd and 4th Battalions, 503rd Infantry, the 3rd Battalion, 12th Infantry, the 3rd Battalion, 8th Infantry and two battalions of the 1st Cavalry Division, and the ARVN 42nd Regiment and 2nd, 3rd and 9th Airborne Battalions, fought for the ridges in three weeks of bunker fighting in double- and triple-canopy jungle. The 3rd Battalion, 12th Infantry took Hill 1338 through a bunker complex linked by field telephone; on Hill 724 the PAVN struck the laager of the 3rd Battalion, 8th Infantry, killing 18 and wounding 118. On 15 November mortar fire set off the ammunition dump at Dak To, destroying more than 1,100 tons of ordnance and two C-130s⁶⁹.

From 19 to 23 November the 2nd and 4th Battalions, 503rd Infantry fought for Hill 875; on the first evening a misdirected US bomb fell on the 2nd Battalion's perimeter, killing many of its men. By the time the paratroopers took the crest on 23 November the PAVN had withdrawn. The Americans lost 361 killed, 15 missing and 1,441 wounded, and about 40 helicopters; the ARVN 73 killed. US figures for PAVN dead run from about 1,000 to 1,644, with allegations of inflation; a PAVN report gives about 450 killed and 1,089 wounded. The battle consumed 151,000 artillery rounds, 2,096 tactical air sorties, 257 B-52 strikes and 2,101 Army helicopter sorties. Westmoreland called it a defeat for the enemy. It was also exactly what Hanoi intended: one of the border battles that, with Loc Ninh, Song Be and Con Thien, drew US forces to the frontiers, so that by January 1968 about half the US manoeuvre battalions in South Vietnam were in the border regions⁶⁹.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year³. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December³⁸, and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000⁶⁸, and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view"⁶⁸. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside⁴.

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest⁵. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

The enemy's external supply is measured: the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count, the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what the South's forces need from the North¹⁵. That knowledge is honest from 1965 rather than from 1970, and it has not changed the rules, the bombing or the strategy. Its main effect on Washington has been to deliver bad news early.

The RVNAF enters 1968 with the rifles it has carried for four years and a Regional Force that the corps commanders want for their own operations, bound since 1967 by the rule that keeps it in its provinces. The American army has had no rifle crisis and no inquiry, and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights; it is the same army in every other respect, fighting the same battles on the enemy's terms.

The bombers have learned two things in two years: that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot, and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle. The second lesson is the one the headquarters remembers going into 1968.

Phung Hoang has a centre in every district and an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, about one in five of them members of district or village committees, and the Front's district administration in the held belt now governs from the contested edge by courier. The word Phoenix has been a press story since March 1967. About 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps; the American battalions have changed over once or twice each as units, have fought better in the contacts they were granted, and have learned at Dien Ban to keep their reliefs off the dark of the moon¹⁰. None of it has changed who chooses the battle.

The ARVN ends 1967 with about four in five of its battalions commanded by officers the board chose, on eighteen-month commands, with the captain-led battalion nearly gone and the thirty-day review feared and read; about a third fewer of its battalions have been rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, which no report shows because the enemy declined the fights that would have shown it¹¹. The 25th Division has a commander from the list and the corps have the commanders they had. The Military Security Service has its first file on the board's officers as a political factor, and COSVN has had every list the board published.

The factories end 1967 producing what the domestic share buys, with the steelworks short of power and the arsenal short of its gunners' trust after Phu Cat. Every post of the held belt has a radio, the Front attacks those that can call about a third less often than those that cannot, and where the guns can reach the posts the first call is for fire¹³. The posts hold themselves better than the hamlets around them.

The ARVN ends 1967 trained to the man and as short of squad leaders as history's; its battalions have lost about a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still lays, beyond what the rifles and the officers saved, and about one in eight fewer battalions has been broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms alone would have left. The drill-sergeant corps has begun to return about 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year to the battalions². In the lowlands of Quang Nam and Quang Ngai the mine has replaced the firefight as the enemy's way of killing Americans, and in the districts around Saigon the Front's guerrilla squads are drilled by men the Republic trained.

### Mechanism

Phase II is where the course's two effects separate. Its effect on the fighting is real and invisible, for the reason that memorandum 007 found: the enemy declines the better unit. Its effect on the dead is real and visible, because a man who lives through a wound appears in the returns whether or not the enemy chose the fight. Of the two, the second is the one this headquarters can show, and the Ia Drang is where it shows it: about eight to twelve men alive at Albany on the morning of 18 November 1965 who died in the grass historically, about 180 American dead in the campaign rather than 190². That is the rate, two to four percent of the American killed in action, and it holds whatever the enemy does, because no adaptation of his takes the tourniquet out of a trained man's pocket.

The fighting gain is shown at Bau Bang on 21 May 1966, where the RF companies did everything the course taught them and nothing the course could not teach them. They came off their trucks, found the ditch, fired in pairs and put their squad guns into the treeline in two minutes, and they lost a fifth fewer men than on the course before this one; they were on the trucks in the first place because their sector commander rode them there. That is the bottleneck this memorandum predicted, moved from the private to the squad leader, the platoon and the officer who writes the order. Across the ARVN I judge the ambush losses of 1966–67 down a tenth to a sixth beyond the rifles and the officers, about one in eight fewer battalions broken, and the strongest positive effect of this memorandum, the first government army that the Front cannot reliably break with its opening volley, at work in every fight the enemy accepts².

The phase also shows the limit DIRMT01-9 and DIRMT01-11 ask about, and it is a hard one. The devaluation of 18 June 1966 cut the private's rice by about a third by December, and the families of the 25th Division in Hau Nghia and Long An could not eat on his pay. Desertion rose as it had historically. The course touches none of its causes, and it adds one cost: the deserter carries his training home, and a few hundred a year in the Saigon belt carry it to the Front. Colonel Hoan's answer, families inside the perimeter and the ration paid in rice, cut his division's desertion by a quarter, which is more than this provision did for the whole army; that is the measure of where desertion lived.

By region, as the standing directive asks: in the Delta, the trained territorial post and the casualty drill carry on what Phase I began, and the posts of Chuong Thien and Dinh Tuong hold their first ten minutes as Long Huu did. In the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps, the effect is the mine: Dien Ban and Dai Loc in Quang Nam and Son Tinh and Binh Son in Quang Ngai become the places where the enemy's answer to the trained soldier is written. In the Highlands, the course reaches the Montagnard recruits of the RF companies of Darlac, Pleiku and Kontum and taught them in Vietnamese, which told them that the army was Kinh, and it does not reach the CIDG camps, which stay what the enemy attacks; FULRO's grievances are untouched by it. In the Saigon belt, the effect runs both ways: the board's battalions are trained and steadier, and the Cu Chi guerrillas are taught the tourniquet by a deserter.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response is the mine, and it is a reasoned one. The Quang Da Special Zone's command and the Military Region 5 Party Committee concluded at the end of 1965 that firefights with trained units cost them more than they gained, and made the mine and the booby trap the village guerrilla's principal work. It cost them almost nothing: the explosive came from American duds and the Republic's grenades, the batteries from Go Vap, and the labour from guerrillas who were in the hamlets anyway. It cost the Marines of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines about two-thirds of their dead in a year, and it cost the farmers of Dien Ban a third more mine casualties. It cost the Marines' relations with the hamlets, which the conduct block could not protect through a twelfth month of mines.

The enemy's own decision in this phase was taken in Hanoi, for reasons that had nothing to do with anything this headquarters did. On 19 March 1967 the Ministry of Defence established the Sapper Command as a separate branch of the People's Army, under the General Staff of Senior General Van Tien Dung, gathering the sapper battalions that had grown up in the regions since 1965 into a branch with its own schools, doctrine and commanders. It was driven by the PAVN's own doctrine and its institutional history: the sapper had been the revolutionary army's answer to fortified positions since the war against the French, the regions' sapper units had been formed piecemeal and the General Staff wanted them under one doctrine, and the branch's advocates in the General Staff argued that the war against American bases, airfields and depots needed specialists the infantry regiments could not provide². This headquarters did not provoke it, and nothing in this memorandum makes it more or less likely. What it does is meet the trained soldier from the other side: the sapper's method, to reach the wire unseen and attack the sleeping post rather than the alert one, is the method that a soldier trained for the first ten minutes is least able to answer, because it is designed to give him no first ten minutes. From 1968 the sapper battalions are the units that attack the posts and the district compounds that the trained RF and PF hold against infantry, and the course has nothing to say to them.

The B-2 Front and COSVN drew a narrower lesson from Bau Bang and Route 13. The 272nd's notebook of January 1967 says it: the puppet territorials now fight from the ditch, and the regiment's ambushes must kill in the first volley or not be laid. That is a command that lays fewer ambushes and makes them heavier, which is consistent with the fighting gain being real and with its appearing as fights not fought.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits an army trained to the man and short of squad leaders, a drill-sergeant corps repaying the battalions with 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year, a Front that has moved to mines and sappers in the lowlands and to the untrained target everywhere, and a Sapper Command that will reach the district compounds after Tet. It inherits about a dozen Marines from Dien Ban alive and without legs, and the first of the amputees in the RVNAF's hospitals who would have died on the historical course. The 1968 mobilization inherits a training base that has the capacity, and the habit, to refuse a short course.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +2..+4 · POP.gvn_legitimacy +2..+4 · VCI.strength -7..-5 · ENEMY.engagement_control -4..-2 · FIND.main_force +2..+4 · FIND.vci +8..+10 · ARVN.effectiveness +8..+10.*

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war, and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end³.

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone⁶⁸.

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way; Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington. The difference is in the Delta and in the hamlets. The Front enters the offensive with a smaller base in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre, recruits fewer men there in 1968, and loses the towns of the western Delta faster because the hamlets around them warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers. When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled, and the Commission audits the new Phoenix programme against the land register⁴. The American political calendar is not changed by any of it.

The police changes Tet in the same places and in the same way: in the held belt of the Delta the Front's urban cadres are known and its guides do not arrive, and in Hue its lists are better because the Republic's own census fed them. Phoenix, here, is only the name Washington learned for a programme whose district centres had run since 1964 without quotas and under an audit.

Above all this the radar watches the border, and at Khe Sanh it gives the headquarters and the White House the most complete picture of an enemy concentration either has ever held, while the forces attacking the cities move among the Tet crowds where no radar can tell them from anyone else. After the halt it measures exactly how the North uses it, and Commando Hunt opens with a second ledger beside the Air Force's claims.

The ARVN and the territorials go into Tet with the weapons the historical ARVN received only after it. In the district towns, which the first wave attacked with local forces, about a third of the attacks never get inside the compound walls; in the cities, where the PAVN and the main force fight block by block, the rifles lower the cost and do not shorten the battles. The mobilization of June arms its new territorials from stock within the month.

In the hills of Quang Tri the siege of Khe Sanh is measured every night and its trenches destroyed before they reach the wire; the PAVN pulls back about two weeks early, at a much heavier price, and the garrison loses about a third fewer men. It is the most complete American victory of the war on the ground and changes nothing in Washington; the brigade pulled from Thua Thien's hamlets to win it is missing when the Front returns to them. Abrams refuses to make the victory a doctrine of border bases and closes Khe Sanh on the historical date, and Commando Hunt opens against a panhandle net built since 1966 to be cut.

The district centres change Tet in the held belt in one more way: the leaders who came into the Delta towns to direct the uprising had been charted for three years, and a third of them were taken in February. In Hue the province centre's informant cards burn in the first hour and the district centres' do not. The American battalions that fight the offensive have been together for months, and the advisers beside the ARVN have been in their posts for a year; the battles are shorter at the edges and the same at their centres.

The officer reforms meet Tet at the battalions and the companies, which is where they had worked. The ARVN units that fought in the towns had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and in IV and III Corps they recalled their men from leave faster and fought the first day closer to their strength¹¹. The mobilization of June finds its platoon leaders without the historical crash course, because the field commissions of 1966–68 had already made about 3,000 lieutenants from the army's sergeants, and the new territorials pay for it in sergeants. COSVN, reading its failure in the towns, opens a proselytizing campaign aimed at the officers the board had passed over. At Hue and Khe Sanh the battles are the historical ones, and the decision of 31 March is unchanged.

The private soldier in Tet is trained, and the mobilization of June finds a training base that will not send men to units untrained. In the district towns the RF companies that hold their compound walls are firing as the territorial course taught them, and in the towns and the cities the wounded of both allied armies are alive in larger numbers in the morning. In June the Joint General Staff wants the ten-week course cut to six to put the mobilization's men into the field by August; the training command holds it at ten by running the centres in two shifts, and the new battalions and territorial companies reach the field about a month later than history's and trained, with a man who has held the rank of sergeant in about four new PF platoons in five rather than two in three. The Front's third offensive goes, as its earlier offensives had, for what the course did not reach: the People's Self-Defense Force.

#### The siege of Khe Sanh
*21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province; Route 9 in Laos to Ban Houei Sane · changed from history · EV000-41*
*Initiative: enemy*

In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion⁷².

In this war the concentration was watched from the day it began. From mid-December the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos and the trucks of Group 559 turning east from Tchepone toward Ban Houei Sane, about 20–25 a night rising to about 80 by mid-January. The U-2S's signals payload fixed the headquarters of the 325C Division northwest of Hill 881 North on 2 January and that of the 304th near Co Roc, across the border, on 11 January, and from the volume and pattern of their traffic the J-2 analysts identified a front headquarters forming above them. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration this command had ever held. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", read it as confirmation, and after Con Thien he read it as an opportunity: here, he told his staff on 13 January, the enemy would give us the battle we could win⁷². The radar plots went every morning to Saigon and every evening to the White House, where the President kept a terrain model of Khe Sanh in the Situation Room¹⁵. The 1st Cavalry Division moved north to I Corps in January, as it did historically, and in this war a brigade of the 101st Airborne Division moved with it, drawn from the coastal districts of Thua Thien where it had been working with the Regional Forces¹⁹.

The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that would attack the cities on 30–31 January moved into them in small groups, on foot and by bus and sampan among the millions travelling home for Tet, under the eyes of their own people; nothing in the radar picture warned of Saigon, Hue or the provincial towns. General Frederick Weyand's decision of 10 January to pull fifteen battalions back toward Saigon rested, as it did historically, on signals and ground intelligence about the enemy's units in III Corps¹⁵. The radar deepened the American fixation on Khe Sanh, which history had without it; the siege box, by promising a victory there, deepened it again.

The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump. The answer was Operation Niagara, and in this war Niagara had two halves. The first was the historical one: about 60 B-52s on an average day, sensors and reconnaissance, more than 158,000 artillery rounds in the first three months, and boxes on the valleys, the parks and Route 9 in Laos that the radar had seen fill during the night⁷²¹⁵. It fell as a carpet half as dense as the Big Belly's, because the J carries about half the D's unguided bombs, and in tonnage of unguided bombs Niagara delivered about two-thirds of its historical weight²⁰. The second half was new. From 23 January a Global Hawk stood over the Khe Sanh plateau every night, and its radar imaged the ground within three kilometres of the perimeter each hour; Sorrell had moved a detachment of the coordinate cell to Da Nang, and from the night of 26 January cells of three Js arrived every ninety minutes, each carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s aimed at positions measured that night¹⁹.

What the PAVN had come to do at Khe Sanh, it did by digging. Historically the trenches of the 304th and 325C Divisions crept toward the perimeter from mid-February and reached within a few dozen metres of the wire on the east, where the 37th Rangers held⁷². Here the trench lines were imaged as they were dug. A trench opened at dusk was on the cell's board before midnight and under a GBU-38 before dawn, and from 15 February the cells were cleared to bomb as close as 400 metres to the Marines and Rangers, inside the kilometre that history's carpets kept²⁰. The 304th Division dug three times toward the eastern perimeter, on 17, 22 and 27 February, and each time its trenches were destroyed at 500–800 metres. On the night of 29 February, when a regiment of the 304th formed to assault the Rangers' sector, it formed in the open beyond the trenches it had not been able to keep, and the GBU-38 cells and the Marine artillery broke it before it reached the wire, as they did historically, at a higher cost to the attackers. The PAVN's guns at Co Roc across the border, 130mm and 152mm pieces rolled out of caves to fire, were struck at their firing positions by GBU-31(V)3/Bs within hours of each mission, and by mid-March they fired from Co Roc only at night and only a few rounds a gun¹⁹.

On the night of 6–7 February the panhandle watch tracked a column of eleven or twelve slow vehicles leaving the Ban Houei Sane area eastward on Route 9, and the warning reached the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei about forty minutes before twelve PT-76s of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, attacked it at about 0030 on 7 February. The defenders knocked out seven tanks; the camp still fell, its last fight a matter of metres in which no bomb could help, and of about 500 CIDG some 160 were killed or missing and of 24 Americans eight were killed¹⁵. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, and the air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters⁷². Here the flak around the approaches, measured each night, was struck each night, and I estimate the air bridge lost about a third fewer helicopters¹⁹.

The front command under Major General Tran Quy Hai pulled the 325C Division back from the hills in mid-March and the 304th from the eastern trenches before the end of the month, about two weeks before its historical withdrawal. Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July⁷². I estimate that the garrison lost about a third fewer killed than the historical one, about 180, and that the PAVN's dead were about half again its acknowledged figure, perhaps 3,500–4,000 to July; both are judgments built on the historical figures and the change in what the bombs could reach, not counts. In this war Khe Sanh was the worst battle the PAVN fought in 1968, and the most complete American victory of the war on the ground. It was won at the place Hanoi had chosen, on the border, while the war was decided in the cities and in Washington; and the brigade taken from Thua Thien's hamlets to help win it was not in Thua Thien when the Front's local forces returned to them in February¹⁹.

#### ◆ The Tet Offensive: the first wave
*30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-42*
*Initiative: enemy*
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital⁶⁸.

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed⁶⁸.

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces. In IV Corps it goes differently in two of them. At Can Tho, the corps headquarters on the Bassac, the battalions that attacked the town and the Binh Thuy air base moved up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh that GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left in the hands of their own Popular Forces; PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, two of them fought, and the 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters, and Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, with far less of the town destroyed than on the historical course. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days. At Ben Tre, in Kien Hoa, the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta, where the government had titled little outside the town because it held little outside the town, the battle and the destruction were as they were historically. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964. Allied and enemy losses for the country as a whole are not materially changed by it, and I do not adjust them⁴.

The police and the chiefs changed the first wave in the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon, and changed it less than they might have, because the infrastructure they had driven from the villages had gone to the towns. In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced on 31 January to lead the uprising, and in the first two weeks of February arrested about 1,700 of them on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs. Where the Front's guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later: the battalions that attacked Can Tho waited on the night of 30–31 January for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal who never arrived, because both had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier. The ARVN 21st Division, whose desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its strength on the muster roll, and the half of the ARVN that was on Tet leave elsewhere was a smaller half in IV Corps. In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands, where the police counted for little against PAVN regiments and VC main-force battalions, the battle went as it did historically⁵.

The rifles changed the first wave everywhere the ARVN and the territorials fought, and most where the fight was small. Historically most of the ARVN went into Tet with the M1 Garand and the carbine, the M16 having reached only the Airborne, the Marines and a few regiments in 1967, and the Regional and Popular Forces with weapons of the Korean War; American officers watched ARVN soldiers in Saigon and Hue fight with eight-round rifles against attackers with AK-47s and B40s, and the M16 airlift to the ARVN was one of the first decisions after the offensive. In this war every ARVN soldier and every territorial had carried a modern carbine for four years. The difference is clearest in the district towns, where the first wave was made by VC local-force battalions and companies against garrisons of an RF company or two, a PF platoon and the district police. Of the 64–72 district towns attacked historically, I estimate that the attackers penetrated about two-thirds on this course against nearly all historically, and that the ones they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, as at Long Huu in 1964. At Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st with one squad gun lent by the 7th Division and its own carbines, and the battalion, which had planned to be through the town by 0400, was still in the market when the 7th Division's 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived at 0730. In the provincial capitals and the cities, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments and the fighting was block by block, the rifles changed casualties and not outcomes. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,500, against 2,788 historically, and do not adjust the enemy's losses, which were never measured to a precision the change would show in¹⁷.

Phung Hoang added a layer to that harvest. The province committees of Phong Dinh, Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long had sent their senior cadres into the towns to direct the uprising, the men who were to form the "revolutionary committees" of each city, and the PIOCCs at Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long had carried charts of those committees since 1965 with photographs from the biometric enrolment of their arrested relatives and ralliers. Of about 140 province- and district-committee cadres who came into the three towns, about 50 were taken by 15 February, most of them on the identifications of ward police who had their photographs, and the Can Tho "revolutionary committee" never held its first meeting. The ARVN battalions that cleared the three towns had American advisers who had been with them for about a year, and in Can Tho the 21st Division's 1st Battalion, 31st Regiment had an adviser, Captain Jerome Pike, who took the call of a PF corporal on the Xa No canal in Vietnamese at 2340 on 30 January and moved the battalion's reaction company to the university before the attackers reached it. The American units committed to Tet in IV Corps, the 9th Division's 2nd Brigade and the Mobile Riverine Force, had been together since their arrival in 1967 and fought the streets of My Tho and Vinh Long as the units they had trained as¹⁰. None of this touched Saigon, Hue or the Highlands, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments that no district centre had ever charted.

The officer reforms changed the first wave in the ARVN's battalions, and changed it most where the battalions were in the towns. By January 1968 about four in five ARVN battalion commanders had been chosen by the board and had held their battalions for more than a year; the order that sent half the army on Tet leave was the Joint General Staff's, and the board's commanders obeyed it, but in IV and III Corps they had recall plans written to the career course's pattern, and by the evening of 31 January the battalions of the 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions were at about 70 percent of their strength in the towns against about half historically. The Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, whose commanders the board had reviewed since July 1967, fought the district compounds under lieutenants many of whom had been sergeants in the same companies two years before. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,300 rather than about 2,500, a judgment on faster recall and steadier companies that I hold loosely, and I do not adjust the enemy's losses¹¹. At Hue the 1st Division under Truong fought as it fought historically, and its commander had been the list's first name since 1966.

The basic course changed the first wave in the same places the rifles did, and in one place they did not: the wounded. Every ARVN soldier and territorial who fought on 31 January had passed the ten-week or the six-week course, and the RF companies on the compound walls of the district towns fired as the territorial course taught, in pairs, with ammunition kept for the second rush; at Cai Lay the 311th RF Company still had about a third of its basic load at 0600, which is why it was still firing at 0730 when the 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived. I estimate that the attackers were held out of perhaps a few more district towns than the rifles and the officers had already kept them from, and I do not put a number on it. The larger difference was in the night after the assaults, in the towns and in Saigon, where the wounded lay in the streets for hours before anyone could reach them. Historically the ARVN's wounded in the offensive were about three times its dead⁶⁸; in this war more of those wounded with an arm or leg torn by a B40 fragment or a burst of AK-47 fire were alive at dawn because the man beside them had put on a tourniquet. I put the ARVN's killed in the first phase at about 2,150 rather than about 2,300, and the American killed in the first phase at about 30–60 fewer, at the rate shown at the Ia Drang. In Hue, where the wounded of the 1st Division and the Marines lay in the Citadel's streets for a month of fighting, the course saved men every day of it, and the surgeons at Phu Bai complained, as Nha Trang's had in 1965, about the tourniquets left on too long². The enemy's losses I do not adjust.

#### The battle for Saigon and the attack on the US embassy
*31 Jan – 7 Mar 1968 · Saigon, Cholon and Tan Son Nhut · historical · EV000-43*
At 02:45 on 31 January a 19-man team of the VC C-10 Sapper Battalion blew a hole in the wall of the new US embassy compound in Saigon and fought inside the grounds for more than six hours without entering the chancery; the compound was secured by 09:20, and five Americans were killed. The sappers were all killed or captured. Other VC units attacked Tan Son Nhut air base with three battalions, the Joint General Staff compound, the Presidential Palace and the naval headquarters, and held the national radio station for about six hours, though they could not broadcast⁶⁸. The image of the embassy under attack, broadcast in the United States hours after Westmoreland had declared the enemy's offensive capacity broken, did more political damage than any battle of the war.

The fighting in Saigon lasted weeks in Cholon, the Chinese quarter, where VC battalions held out around the Phu Tho racetrack. On 4 February residents were ordered out and the area declared a free-fire zone; the last battle in Cholon was fought on 7 March⁶⁸. On 1 February the national police chief, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shot a captured VC officer in the head in a Saigon street before a photographer and a film camera. The image became, with the embassy, the American public's picture of Tet.

#### The battle of Hue
*31 Jan – 2 Mar 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · changed from history · EV000-44*
*Initiative: enemy*
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division⁷³.

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west and did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February⁷³. The ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel; the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March.

The squad weapons made Hue somewhat less costly to retake and no shorter. On the first night the Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound with M4A1s and four squad guns against the 800th and 802nd Battalions' assault teams, where historically it held with M16s it had only recently received; the difference was small, because the Hac Bao was among the few ARVN units historically re-armed by then. Across the river the Marines fought the street battle with two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman with an M110A2 who, in a city where the PAVN held the upper floors and the far side of every street, could put rounds into a window at 200 metres from cover that a squad's rifles could not reach. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines' after-action report credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and the public health building between 4 and 6 February. What took the blocks was what took them historically: the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, tear gas and men going through walls. The weather, the Citadel's walls and the PAVN's reinforcement from the west set the length of the battle, and the rifles touched none of them. I put the ARVN dead at about 410 and the American at about 215, against 458 and 250 historically¹⁷.

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded, on the historical course. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800⁷³. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war.

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966, and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor⁵.

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order that this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred: every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a rehearsal every quarter⁵. In the provinces the plan was rehearsed. Whether it would be carried out on the day it was needed was a question for another year.

The Thua Thien province centre was in the province headquarters south of the river, which the attackers held from 31 January until the 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines retook it on 6 February⁷³. Its informant cards were not in the attackers' hands. The PIOCC's chief, Major Ton That Dinh Lang, had kept the cards of the province's 340 informants in a single safe, against Hieu's rule, because he did not trust his district centres' security; when the attack reached the compound wall at about 0330 on 31 January he burned the cards in the safe with a can of gasoline and was killed an hour later on the stairs. The district centres of Huong Thuy and Phu Vang, outside the city, kept their copies and lost them when their offices were overrun on 31 January and 1 February; the Hue city security section's lists for the southern suburbs included about 60 informants and centre staff named from those files, of whom about 40 were among the dead found in the graves at Phu Thu and Da Mai Creek¹⁰. The national file in Saigon, which held every one of those names, was never in danger in 1968. It was the first time the record showed what a fused file was worth to the enemy, and the destruction order of 9 March was written with the Huong Thuy file in front of it.

#### The reinforcement request and the decision of 31 March
*20 Feb – 31 Mar 1968 · Saigon and Washington · historical · EV000-45*
On 12 February Westmoreland asked for 10,500 more troops; on 13 February a brigade of the 82nd Airborne Division and a Marine regimental landing team, already authorized, were sent. On 20 February the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Earle Wheeler, went to Saigon, and returned with a request for 206,000 more men, framed by the Chiefs to force a mobilization of the reserves. On 28 February McNamara left office and on 1 March Clark Clifford succeeded him, charged with reviewing the request⁶⁸. On 10 March the *New York Times* reported it. On 12 March Senator Eugene McCarthy, running against the war, took 42 percent of the vote in the New Hampshire primary; on 16 March Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race. On 25–26 March the senior outside advisers, the "Wise Men", told the President that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.

On 31 March Johnson announced in a televised address that he was stopping the bombing of North Vietnam except in the area just north of the DMZ, that he would send only about 13,500 more troops, that the United States was ready to talk, and that he would not seek or accept his party's nomination for another term³⁸. The speech capped the American commitment. Westmoreland's strategy of attrition by American forces had been judged by the President's own advisers to have no end that the country would accept, and from this point the questions were how to get out, and how to leave the South able to stand.

#### The My Lai massacre
*16 Mar 1968 · Son My village (My Lai 4 and My Khe), Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · historical · EV000-46*
On the morning of 16 March 1968 Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Brigade, Americal Division, operating under Task Force Barker, was landed by helicopter at the hamlet of My Lai 4 in Son My village, in an area of Quang Ngai long held by the VC 48th Local Force Battalion. It met no resistance. Its men, led among others by Second Lieutenant William L. Calley, killed the unarmed villagers they found, mostly old men, women and children, in their homes, on the paths and in an irrigation ditch; Company B, 4th Battalion, 3rd Infantry killed civilians at My Khe nearby. The dead number from 347, the US Army figure, to 504, the Vietnamese figure. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson, flying an OH-23 scout helicopter overhead, landed between the soldiers and a group of villagers, threatened to fire on his own side, and evacuated survivors; his crewman Glenn Andreotta pulled a wounded child from the ditch⁷⁴.

The Army reported the operation as a victory over 128 Viet Cong, and the brigade's investigation conceded about 20 accidental civilian deaths. The cover-up held for a year and a half, until Ronald Ridenhour's letters to Congress in March 1969 and Seymour Hersh's report of 13 November 1969⁷⁴. My Lai belongs to this phase as an event of the war in the countryside: a company of a division assembled hastily, under a command climate that measured success by bodies, in a province where the population was treated as the enemy's. Its effect on the war came later, when it became known.

#### The battle of Dai Do
*30 Apr – 3 May 1968 · Dai Do and the hamlets north of Dong Ha on the Cua Viet, Quang Tri Province · historical · EV000-47*
At the end of April, as the second wave of the offensive began, battalions of the PAVN 320th Division, two of them identified as belonging to the 48th and 52nd Regiments, moved into the hamlets of Dai Do, Dinh To and their neighbours north of Dong Ha, threatening the Marine base and the Cua Viet supply route. The 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines under Lieutenant Colonel William Weise attacked across open paddies from amphibian tractors and boats, with the 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines, ARVN units of the 2nd Regiment and a mechanized battalion, and from 1 May the US 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry. The Marines lost 81 killed and the ARVN 5; the Americans claimed more than 600 PAVN killed, and the PAVN has not released its losses. By 3 May the PAVN had gone. Whether Dai Do was a main thrust against Dong Ha or a diversion is not settled; the frontal assaults of 1 and 2 May were costly enough to be criticized by later historians⁷⁵.

#### The Paris talks open
*May 1968 · Paris · historical · EV000-48*
After weeks of argument over a site, talks between the United States and North Vietnam opened in Paris in May 1968, with W. Averell Harriman leading for the United States and Xuan Thuy for Hanoi. They stalled at once: Hanoi demanded a complete and unconditional end to the bombing of the North before substantive talks, and refused to recognise the Saigon government; Saigon refused to sit with the Front⁷⁶. Through the summer the talks were a forum for statements, while both sides fought for position on the ground. Their opening nonetheless changed the war's logic. From May 1968 both governments were fighting to shape a settlement as well as to win, and every offensive that followed had a negotiating purpose.

#### The May offensive in Saigon
*5 May – 15 Jun 1968 · Saigon and its outskirts, and towns throughout the South · historical · EV000-49*
On 5 May the PAVN and VC launched the second phase of the general offensive, which the Americans called "mini-Tet", with attacks by fire on more than 100 towns and installations and a renewed assault on Saigon. VC battalions fought their way into the southern and western districts of the capital and into Cholon, where they were destroyed or driven out by the ARVN and US forces over several weeks, with air strikes and artillery used inside the city; much of District 8 was destroyed and tens of thousands were made homeless. The phase lasted until 15 June⁶⁸. Its losses fell heavily on the same local forces and cadres who had survived February, and many of the units that fought in Saigon in May were filled out with northern replacements. American deaths in May 1968 were the highest of any month of the war.

#### The evacuation of Kham Duc
*10–12 May 1968 · Kham Duc Special Forces camp and Ngok Tavak, Quang Tin Province · historical · EV000-50*
On 10 May the 40th Battalion of the PAVN 2nd Division's 1st Regiment overran the outpost of Ngok Tavak, held by an Australian-led company of the 11th MIKE Force and a battery of the 2nd Battalion, 13th Marines. The PAVN 1st and 21st Regiments then closed on the camp at Kham Duc, held by Special Forces Detachment A-105, CIDG and a SOG detachment, reinforced by the 2nd Battalion, 1st Infantry of the Americal Division. On 11 May Westmoreland approved evacuation, and on 12 May, under about 350 air sorties, roughly 1,500 soldiers and civilians were flown out. A C-130 flown by Major Bernard L. Bucher was shot down just after take-off, killing all 183 Vietnamese civilians aboard, a Special Forces officer and the five crewmen; a second C-130 was wrecked on the runway. Lieutenant Colonel Joe M. Jackson landed a C-123 on the abandoned strip to recover a three-man combat control team left behind, and received the Medal of Honor. The Americans lost about 42–46 killed and 31–33 missing and twelve aircraft; about 220 CIDG were killed or missing; the US claimed about 345 PAVN killed, and a later study estimates 1,500–2,000. The abandonment of the camp removed one of the few allied positions between the Laotian border and the lowlands of Quang Tin and Quang Nam, and its loss was one of the few clear PAVN victories over a US-defended position in 1968⁷⁷.

#### ◆ Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned
*10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · changed from history · EV000-51*
*Initiative: allied*

On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy⁷⁸.

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969⁷⁹. Historically the expansion outran the rifles: the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better. In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment, about 260,000 carbines already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June against the year's expected growth, and were on the ranges within a month of induction. The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but sergeants: the training centres could zero a carbine in an afternoon and could not make a squad leader in less than a year, and Venn's successor reported in September that about a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before. The old weapons the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law¹⁷.

On Khe Sanh the decision was harder than it had been historically, because in this war the base had been a victory rather than an ordeal. Westmoreland, in his last weeks, argued for keeping it as the anchor of a line of border bases under the siege box, and the III Marine Amphibious Force's staff drew up a plan for three more such bases along Route 9 and the Laotian border, to be held by battalions drawn from the coastal districts. Abrams refused it on 15 June. His reason, in a message to Wheeler that his staff kept, was that a base the enemy would not attack was a base that held nothing, and that after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July⁷². The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ. Here it was read in Hanoi correctly, as a decision by an American commander that the siege the PAVN had lost was not worth inviting again, and in the American press, less correctly, as a retreat from a battle won¹⁹.

The officers for the mobilization came differently. Historically the Thu Duc candidate course was cut from 37 weeks to 24 in June 1968 to commission the platoon leaders the expansion needed⁶. In this war the cut was to 32 weeks, and the gap was filled by the field commissions: about 3,000 sergeants had been commissioned since 1966 and about 2,500 more were commissioned in the second half of 1968, after the branch basic course that the decree did not allow to be waived. The cost was in the ranks they came from. The sergeant shortage that Venn's successor reported in September, a third of the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank, was in part the field commission's price: the army had made lieutenants of the sergeants who would have led those platoons¹¹. The board's lists governed the new RF companies from their first month, and the new PF platoons not at all.

The mobilization met a training base that would not send men to units untrained, and the cost was a month. Historically the RVNAF in 1968 sent some draftees straight to units without basic training, and the ARVN basic course was cut from twelve weeks to nine to put men through faster. In this war, on 21 June, the Joint General Staff ordered the course cut to six weeks for the duration of the mobilization; the Central Training Command, with Coyle's successor and the Muster Service's chief behind it, answered that six weeks would leave out the Forge and the field week, the part the new soldier most needed, and proposed instead to run the 33 national and division centres in two daily shifts, which raised their capacity from about 65,000 to about 105,000 in cycle¹. The Joint General Staff accepted on 28 June. About 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended; about 40,000 of them waited three to six weeks at the centres for a cycle to open, and the new battalions and territorial companies reached the field about a month later than they did historically, after the third offensive had begun rather than before it. They reached it trained. The new RF companies and PF platoons of the summer went through the six-week territorial course as platoons, on the M4A1 they had drawn from the depots, and came back to their districts in September and October rather than August².

The sergeants came differently too, and the result was not the one memorandum 007 predicted. The field commissions had made lieutenants of about 5,500 of the army's best sergeants by the end of 1968, and on the historical training base that would have left the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank. In this war the drill-sergeant corps had been returning sergeants to the battalions since 1966, about 650–1,800 a year, and in July 1968 the training command released about 1,100 more from the centres' second shift to the new territorial platoons, each a man who had trained a platoon of recruits for two years. Venn's successor reported in September that about a fifth of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank of sergeant, rather than about a third². The People's Self-Defense Force, which the law raised in the same summer and which the course did not reach, was given its Garands and carbines and a few days of instruction by the district chiefs, as historically.

#### The third offensive
*17 Aug – 23 Sep 1968 · Provincial towns and bases, especially in III Corps and the Central Highlands · changed from history · EV000-52*
*Initiative: enemy*

The third phase of the general offensive began in mid-August with attacks by fire and ground assaults on towns and bases, including attacks around Tay Ninh in III Corps. It was weaker than its predecessors: the local forces that had carried the attacks of February and May were largely gone, and the main forces would not commit themselves against American firepower in the same way again. It ended in late September without lasting gains. Taken together, the three offensives of 1968 cost the VC about 50,000 dead by one estimate against about 4,000 US and 6,000 ARVN. Their recruitment in the South collapsed: in Region 5, 1,700 recruits in 1969 against 8,000 in 1968, in the Mekong lowlands 100 against 16,000. By the end of the year about 70 percent of the main-force troops in the South are reported to have been northerners. MACV estimated total PAVN and VC strength at 287,000 in January 1968 and 251,000 at its end⁶⁸. The losses forced a reassessment in Hanoi that would carry into 1969.

In this war the third offensive runs as it did historically, in a part of the country the provisions barely touched. What changes is the arithmetic behind it. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968, 16,000 on the historical course, is about 11,000 here, because about a third of the tenant families who would have supplied the recruits had been titled by the government in districts it held at night, and the Front's own conscription in those districts had become coercion that the PF platoons could resist. The 1969 collapse comes from a lower base and is no less complete. The consequence is that COSVN begins filling its Delta main-force battalions with northern replacements in the summer of 1968, several months earlier than on the historical course, and the northerners who arrive in Chuong Thien and Kien Giang are strangers in hamlets where the Front's strength had always been that its soldiers were local. The proportion of northerners in the main forces at the year's end, about 70 percent historically⁶⁸, is a few points higher here⁴.

COSVN read the year's failure in the towns, among other things, as a failure of its proselytizing among the ARVN's officers, and it chose a new target. A military proselytizing directive of August 1968, captured in Tay Ninh in December, ordered the province committees to work among "the officers whom the puppet promotion board has humiliated": the commanders relieved for cause, the majors passed over three times, the colonels kept from the list. It attributed the board, wrongly, to American selection of the ARVN's officers, and its propaganda leaflets of the autumn called the board's commanders "officers chosen by the Americans". About 30 relieved or passed-over officers were identified by the Military Security Service as contacted in 1968–69, and two were arrested as agents; the directive's yield was small and its cost to the Front was smaller. In the third offensive itself the attacks on Tay Ninh met the 25th Division's battalions in positions they had held since 1967, and the town was not entered¹¹.

#### ◆ The self-defence hamlets
*17 Aug – 31 Oct 1968 · Duc Hue and Trang Bang Districts, Hau Nghia Province; Can Duoc and Rach Kien, Long An Province; Cai Be and Cai Lay Districts, Dinh Tuong Province · new · EV009-4*
*Initiative: enemy*

The third offensive went for what the course did not reach, and in the provinces around Saigon that was the People's Self-Defense Force. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee, planning the offensive of August after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, ordered its subregions to avoid the RF and PF posts that had held in February and May and to strike the new self-defence groups of the mobilization law, whose members were townsmen and farmers with a Garand and three days' instruction. The Front's subregion commands around Saigon had a second reason that the Military Affairs Committee did not need to state: every self-defence group destroyed told the hamlet that the government's new law armed it without protecting it².

The attacks began on the night of 17–18 August and ran through September. In Duc Hue and Trang Bang the Front's local-force companies attacked about 30 self-defence groups in their hamlets, usually at night and from two sides, and overran about two-thirds of them; in Long An, around Can Duoc and Rach Kien, where NIGHT LANTERN had left the posts strong and the infrastructure weak, it attacked fewer and lost more, because the PF platoons beside the hamlets came out to the self-defence groups' calls. In Cai Be and Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong it went for the self-defence groups in the hamlets between the posts, where no platoon was near enough to come. Across the three provinces, by the end of October, I estimate that about 600–900 members of the self-defence groups were killed and about 1,500–2,000 of their weapons taken, almost all of them Garands and carbines of 1950 that the RVNAF had handed in since 1964. In the same weeks the RF and PF posts of the three provinces were attacked about a third less often than on the historical course, and held about as often as they had in May².

The trained PF platoon could not give the self-defence group its own training, but at Rach Kien it showed what it could do for it. On the night of 2–3 September a company of the Front's 506th Battalion attacked the self-defence group of a hamlet a kilometre from the PF post at the Rach Kien canal bridge; the group's leader, a schoolteacher, fired a flare as the PF corporal had shown him, and the platoon came across the paddies in sixteen minutes, in pairs, firing and moving as the territorial course had taught it, and broke the attack at the hamlet gate. Two self-defence members died and four were wounded, all four with tourniquets the PF soldiers put on. In the next month the district chief of Can Duoc sent his PF platoon sergeants, the men the drill-sergeant corps had released in July, to give the self-defence groups of the district three evenings each of the first ten minutes, which no order required². It was the only place in the three provinces where anyone did, and the only one where the groups held through the autumn.

#### The bombing halt and the American election
*31 Oct – 5 Nov 1968 · Washington, Paris and Saigon · historical · EV000-53*
In October Hanoi signalled that it would accept the Saigon government at the table if the bombing stopped. On 31 October Johnson announced the end of all bombing of North Vietnam from 1 November, ending Rolling Thunder³⁸. Thieu refused to join the talks on the terms agreed. Through Anna Chennault the Nixon campaign had encouraged him to wait for a better deal, and on 2 November she told the South Vietnamese ambassador to "hold on a while longer"; Johnson learned of it through intercepts and ordered the campaign watched, but did not make it public. Historians disagree on whether the channel changed anything, since Thieu had his own reasons to refuse. On 5 November Richard M. Nixon was elected president by a narrow popular margin. Talks in Paris did not resume in substance until the new year, after a dispute over the shape of the table was settled with a round table for the governments⁷⁶.

Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder had flown 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine attack sorties, and lost 506 USAF, 397 Navy and 19 Marine aircraft over or near the North³⁸. It ended without having achieved its purposes of breaking Hanoi's will or stopping the flow of men and supplies south.

#### The Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix
*1 Nov 1968 – Jan 1969 · The countryside of South Vietnam · changed from history · EV000-54*
*Initiative: allied*
On the historical course Phung Hoang, the Vietnamese programme to coordinate the attack on the VC infrastructure, had been established by decree in December 1967 out of the American ICEX programme of June 1967, and Thieu put his own decree behind it in July 1968. Its instruments were district intelligence centres, the CIA-run Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the police; its method was to identify, capture, turn or kill the cadres of the parallel administration⁸⁰. On 1 November 1968, at the urging of Komer and his successor William E. Colby, the government launched the Accelerated Pacification Campaign, a three-month drive to raise about 1,000 contested hamlets to relative security before any ceasefire could freeze the map, using RF and PF, Revolutionary Development cadre and the new People's Self-Defence Force, with regular forces screening.

The campaign met its targets by the Hamlet Evaluation System, a scale filled in by American district advisers under pressure to show progress, and in many places those hamlets did become more secure after dark, because the VC local forces that had protected the infrastructure had died in the offensives. Phoenix's own figures, 81,740 people "neutralized" between 1968 and 1972, 26,369 of them killed, were produced against monthly quotas that encouraged false arrests and padding, and about 87 percent of the killings credited to it were by ordinary military operations. Communist cadres later described 1968–72 as their hardest period, and some American advisers concluded that Phoenix had been a mortal threat in some provinces and a paper programme in others⁸⁰.

In this war the campaign is launched on the historical date under the same men, and Phoenix is not launched at all, because Phung Hoang has run in every district since 1965 without quotas. Thieu's decree of July 1968 is issued, as a reaffirmation that adds his name to Khanh's. Three things are different. The district intelligence centres have worked from the land register since 1964, which by November 1968 records about 900,000 titled hectares and the households that hold them; for the first time a district knew who lived in each titled hamlet, and who had left it for the Front, from a record compiled for another purpose and not for the quota. And the Commission Against Corruption's inspectors, under the decree of 1964 rather than an agreement Colby made, had sampled the neutralization reports of 40 districts in IV and III Corps each quarter since 1965 and checked each name against the register, the province prison rolls and the PRU and police reports. The sample reported in February 1969, the sixteenth, found about one claim in seven unsupported across the 40 districts: fewer than on the earlier course, because there were no quotas to pad and the districts had learned since 1965 that a name would be checked. False arrests for ransom were still cases before the Special Court, about 30 in the quarter¹⁰. The register had been made public in 1964 only as parcel numbers, after Can Giuoc; in 1968 it was used, for the first time, as a list of persons, and the Front's propaganda said so⁴.

The third is that Phung Hoang was not a new instrument here but an old one with a new urgency. The centres had sat in the Special Branch's district offices since 1964, and the Special Branch had been running target folders in the held belt since then⁵. In the held belt the unsupported share in the February sample was under a tenth; in the districts recovered after Tet, where the Accelerated Pacification Campaign pushed the centres into villages a few weeks old, it was about a quarter, and in the eleven districts where the province chiefs let the PRUs and the sector S2 build the files, about two-fifths. Without quotas the contested belt did not become a paper programme, but where the files were thin it became the raid on a rallier's grudge, as at Duc Thanh, and the Commission's cases came mostly from there. The audited count for 1968 was about 9,600 neutralized, with about 1,400 killed, of whom about 500 in the programme's own operations; on the historical course the claims for the year were larger and were never audited¹⁰. In the held belt it was a refinement of what the police had done for four years, and the Front's cadres there had left before it began.

#### The halt measured
*1 Nov – 31 Dec 1968 · The coastal road and railway from Thanh Hoa to Vinh and Dong Hoi, Route 15 to the Mu Gia pass, and the ferries at Quang Khe, North Vietnam · new · EV003-5*
The bombing of the North stopped on 1 November 1968. The Gulf watch did not. Its orbit, 40–60 nautical miles off the coast and outside the SA-2 belt, was a reconnaissance flight over international waters, and it went on counting the traffic on Route 1A, the coastal railway and Route 15 below the 19th parallel through November and December¹⁴. What it saw changed within a week. Trucks that had moved only at night and in rain, in convoys of five or six, moved by day in columns of forty and fifty; the ferries at Quang Khe ran in daylight; the railway, repaired south of Thanh Hoa within three weeks, carried trains to Vinh. Southbound vehicle movements on Route 15 toward the Mu Gia pass in December were about three times the October level¹⁵.

The count went to Washington twice a week, as the bombing halt's terms required this command to report the North's use of it. It did not change the halt, which the President had traded for the opening of the expanded talks in Paris and which the President-elect did not reverse. It changed the next campaign. When Commando Hunt opened on 15 November, its planners knew from the radar how much more was coming toward the passes, and when and where, and the U-2S found the new truck parks and the new fuel dumps north of Mu Gia that the surge required¹⁵. The halt was Hanoi's to use, and it used it exactly as expected; the difference was that the use was measured as it happened, and the headquarters that would fight the dry season of 1968–69 knew the size of it on its first night.

#### Commando Hunt begins
*15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · changed from history · EV000-55*
*Initiative: allied*

On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night⁸¹. In this war the Global Hawk's panhandle watch was the campaign's third sensor from its first night, and the AC-130s of the 16th Special Operations Squadron were sent to radar tracks as well as to sensor activations¹⁵.

The B-52Js and the coordinate cell were its fourth instrument. Historically Commando Hunt's interdiction points, the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes and the Ban Laboy ford, were struck by B-52 boxes and fighter-bombers night after night to crater them⁸¹. Here they were cut with GBU-31s on measured coordinates, and the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Kong below them were sown with Quickstrikes set for the steel pontoons the North had brought in since 1966. The tankers freed by the J's range refuelled the F-4s working the passes, which flew about a quarter more sorties a night over the panhandle than the historical force could keep in the air²⁰. The interdiction points did not close. The enemy had spent two and a half years building a net with no single point of failure; at Ban Laboy the cell now counted fourteen fords and two underwater causeways, and on any night the J could cut the ones it had measured and the trucks crossed at the ones it had not¹⁹.

Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000⁸¹. Here they were not believed by this headquarters either. The radar counted the trucks entering the panhandle at Mu Gia and Ban Karai and leaving it toward the South on Routes 92 and 96, and by that measure Phase I destroyed or disabled about 2,600–3,200 trucks and reduced the tonnage reaching the South by about a sixth, not the third the claims implied. The J's crossings added little to that sixth, perhaps a fiftieth part of the flow, because the net they cut had been built to be cut¹⁹. MACV J-2 sent the figure to Washington in May 1969 beside the Air Force's, and from then on the two numbers travelled together¹⁵. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it; this time the growth was counted.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent³.

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that. About 900,000 hectares have been titled, the Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier⁴. The American decision of 1968 is unchanged, and so is its timing.

The National Police numbers about 95,000 and the Special Branch has buried its first constables of the Tet battles; the infrastructure in the held belt is weaker than anywhere since 1959, and every file it keeps is now held under a destruction plan⁵.

The enemy's rear enters Phase IV counted: the surge after the halt is measured, the first dry season of Commando Hunt is scored against the radar as well as the claims¹⁵, and the American decision of 1968 is unchanged by any of it.

The RVNAF ends 1968 larger and armed throughout with weapons the historical one was still waiting for; its new territorials have rifles and too few sergeants¹⁷. The gap the rearmament opened over history is now closing from the historical side, as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969.

The PAVN leaves 1968 having learned that it does not lay siege to a position the Americans can measure; that is the one way of choosing its battles it has lost¹⁹. It chooses all the others. The panhandle is harder to cut than history's, because the bombers taught its engineers how.

Phung Hoang enters 1969 with five years of files, an audited count the press has learned to quote, and no quotas; the Front's Delta committees have lost a third of the leaders they sent into the towns¹⁰. The Hue district files have shown what the national file would be worth to the enemy. The selected advisers are in every field post, the first of them on their second tours, and the American battalions have fought the year as units.

The ARVN's battalions come out of 1968 with commanders who have held them through Tet and a board that has not stopped working; the mobilization's platoons have lieutenants who were sergeants and squads that have no sergeants¹¹. COSVN has opened a campaign among the officers the board passed over, and the Military Security Service has a second file on the board's officers, this time as targets.

The RVNAF ends 1968 with the mobilization's soldiers trained to the same standard as the regulars of 1957, and about a month late in the field for it; the People's Self-Defense Force, which no course reached, has paid for the difference in Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong. About 2,150 rather than 2,300 ARVN soldiers died in the first wave, and the hospitals of both armies are full of men who would have died on the historical course and are alive without a leg².

### Mechanism

Tet turns on the decisions it turned on historically, and the basic course changes it in the margins that the rifles and the officers had already opened, and in one they had not. On the compound walls of the district towns the RF companies fire as the territorial course taught, in pairs and with ammunition kept; at Cai Lay the 311th RF Company still has a third of its basic load at 0600. In the night after the first assaults, and through the month of Hue, the wounded of both allied armies are alive in the morning in larger numbers. That is the second dated battle in which this memorandum's rate is shown at work, as the standing directive requires: about 150 fewer ARVN dead in the first wave and 30–60 fewer American, against about eight to twelve at Albany, the same rate in a battle of a different kind². The strongest positive effect is in those numbers.

The mobilization is the phase's real change, and it has a cost this memorandum must state at its weight. On the historical course the RVNAF sent some of its 1968 draftees to units untrained and cut its course from twelve weeks to nine. In this war the Joint General Staff ordered six; the training command held ten by running two shifts, and about 40,000 men waited at the centres for a cycle. The new battalions and territorial companies reached the field about a month after history's, in September and October rather than August, after the third offensive had begun. For those weeks the districts around Saigon had fewer new territorials than history's and better ones afterwards. I judge the trade right, because an untrained PF platoon in a hamlet is a supply of rifles for the Front, but it was paid in the self-defence hamlets of Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong in August and September.

The sergeants are the other half of the mobilization, and they show this memorandum's integration finding at work. The drill sergeants the army could not spare in 1964–65 had been coming back to it since 1966, and in July 1968 about 1,100 more went from the centres' second shift to the new territorial platoons. The gap memorandum 007 left, a third of the new PF platoons without a man who had held the rank, closed to a fifth².

### Enemy response and initiative

COSVN's Military Affairs Committee planned the August offensive after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, and it aimed it at the People's Self-Defense Force, the one armed body in the hamlets that no course had reached. That was a reaction, and a correct one: in Duc Hue and Trang Bang it overran about two-thirds of the groups it attacked and took 1,500–2,000 old rifles across three provinces, while the RF and PF posts were attacked about a third less often than historically. Its subregion commands around Saigon read a second purpose into it, that every group destroyed told the hamlet the government's law armed it without protecting it. Where a trained PF platoon was near enough to answer the group's flare, as at Rach Kien on 2–3 September, the attack failed, and the Front's 506th Battalion learned to strike groups more than a kilometre from a post.

The sapper battalions of the new branch began to appear against the district compounds in the May and August offensives, attacking at the hours before dawn when the trained garrison was asleep, and the course had no answer to them; that is the cost of Hanoi's decision of March 1967, met on its own timeline.

**Chain review.** Memorandum 007's C007-3 predicted that the field commissions of 1966–68 would empty the pool of sergeants, so that a third of the mobilization's new PF platoons would have no one who had held the rank. In the war as now written it resolves differently. The commissions still take their 5,500 sergeants, but the drill-sergeant corps this memorandum adds has been refilling the pool from 1966, and the gap is a fifth rather than a third. The chain's first three links hold; its terminal link is changed by a provision it could not have foreseen, which is what a chain review should sometimes find.

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits an RVNAF of about 900,000 trained men, its new territorials trained and a month late, and a People's Self-Defense Force that the course never reached and the third offensive bled. It inherits, in the hospitals at Cong Hoa and in the provinces, tens of thousands of RVNAF soldiers who would have died on the historical course and live, a third to a half of them amputees, whose grievance will reach the streets of Saigon with the other veterans' grievances of 1970². It inherits a Front whose village platoons around Saigon have been taught some of the course by deserters, and a Sapper Command that the course cannot answer.

### Clock

The clock in 1968 is spent by Tet and by the 14,592 American dead of the year, and this memorandum moves it by a sum it cannot show. It saves perhaps 300–600 American lives in 1968, at its rate, and about 900–1,900 over the war. That is fewer funerals in American towns, one family at a time, and it does not touch the shock of 31 January, the request for 206,000 men or the decision of 31 March, which were made on the scale of the casualties and the gap between the reports and the offensive. Against it, the 2026 course failed about 12–18 percent of the Project 100,000 intake, and the draft calls rose by perhaps 5,000–15,000 men a year to replace them, a small addition to the domestic engine of the clock². The two roughly cancel in the politics of 1968. The two weeks the course added to the Army's pipeline slowed the build-up of 1965–66 by two to three months, which was invisible to the public and visible to Westmoreland. The clock is unchanged by a band.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

The basic course does not bite on Phase IV because the phase was decided by the American withdrawal, the palace's capture of the institutions in 1970–71, and the corps commanders' plans for Cambodia and Laos, and no private's training reaches any of them. Lam Son 719 failed for reasons decided at corps and in Saigon. The course's effects run on in the casualty returns and the posts, within the band, and the historical army's training improved from 1969 under the Central Training Command, narrowing the gap. Phase III's end vector is unchanged by this memorandum, so nothing arrives to answer.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The Easter Offensive was decided at Quang Tri by the contradictory orders of the corps and the division, and at An Loc and Kontum by the B-52J and the coordinate cell. Trained soldiers hold in both places with more of their wounded alive, and change neither outcome nor the PAVN's losses, which the measured sieges decided. The course does not reach the commanders who lost Quang Tri, and the countryside of northern Binh Dinh fell to a PAVN division, not to the territorials' first ten minutes. No incoming band has moved.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The collapse of 1975 was decided by the palace's orders of 13–14 March, the corps dissolving toward their families, and Hanoi's calendar against the rains; the basic course reaches none of the three. In 1974 trained soldiers with tourniquets lose fewer of their wounded in the attrition the ration imposes, perhaps 1,000–2,000 lives over the year at this memorandum's rate, and the battles go as written, because the PAVN chose them. The course teaches no soldier to leave his family on Route 7. No incoming band has moved.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the war as now written differs in men more than in events. About 5,000–14,000 American and South Vietnamese soldiers live who died on the historical course: about 900–1,900 Americans over 1965–72, shown at work at Albany and in the first wave of Tet, and about 4,000–12,000 South Vietnamese over 1964–75, shown at Thuan Loi and in the streets of the Delta towns². They are a few percent of each army's dead, and that is honest; they are also several thousand men, which is equally honest. Many of the South Vietnamese live without a limb.

The ARVN of 1964–65 is a band steadier than history's on this memorandum and the two before it, and from 1966 its battalions lose a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still lays, with about one in eight fewer broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms alone left. No battle of the war changes its outcome for it. The territorial posts of the held belt hold their first ten minutes about twice as often as they fell before (nineteen in twenty against nine in ten), which in the Delta and around Saigon means perhaps a few hundred posts and their platoons kept over the war, and in the coastal lowlands and the Highlands much less, because the enemy there fought with mines and with divisions rather than at the walls².

The enemy is not weaker for any of it. He moved to the mine in Quang Nam and Quang Ngai, to the self-defence hamlet around Saigon, and to the sapper everywhere, and a few hundred trained deserters a year gave his village platoons around Saigon some of the course. The mobilization of 1968 was a month later and better; the self-defence groups paid for the month. The Republic falls on the historical date.

By region: in the Delta the effect is the post (Long Huu, Cai Lay); in the coastal lowlands it is the mine and what it did to the Marines' relations with the hamlets (Dien Ban); in the Highlands it is small, the RF companies' Montagnards taught in a language that told them whose army it was, and the CIDG camps untouched; and in the Saigon belt it is the self-defence hamlets of 1968 and the deserters of Cu Chi.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The rung stays at (a), collapse, and this memorandum gives it no reason to move. The ladder's next rung needs a Republic whose state survives the withdrawal of American combat forces for longer than history's two years, and the basic course does not reach any of the mechanisms that decided that survival. It does not give the Republic an air arm it owns, it does not survive the 1974 appropriation any better than the force did, and it does not choose the corps commanders. A better private in an army with the historical generals and the historical aid vote is still in that army.

**The date of the fall, tested.** I estimate the fall at 30 April 1975 within a window of about 27 April to the first days of May, as before. The mechanisms in the cumulative force that pull it later are the ones meta-checkpoint 01 named, the better battalions at Duc Duc, Xuan Loc, Phan Rang and Cu Chi, the PAVN's greener divisions, the stronger held belt and, from memorandum 008, the domestic ammunition that stretched the last aid dollar; this memorandum adds trained privates who live through their wounds and hold their first ten minutes, which act in exactly the fights the PAVN bypassed in 1975. The mechanisms that pull it earlier are a President more certain his army could not hold alone and an army more dependent on fire it no longer had; this memorandum adds nothing to either, except that the families the soldiers went to in March were no better fed. The palace's orders and Hanoi's calendar dominate, as before.

**The critical path.** Tested against the palace's orders of 13–14 March, the 1st Corps' march against the rains and Minh's surrender, the basic course reaches none: the orders were taken in the palace, the march was the PAVN's, and the surrender was the President's. The collapse of I and II Corps came from families on the roads and contradictory orders, and a trained private goes to his family as surely as an untrained one. The delay it could buy is hours at Xuan Loc or Phan Rang, not the three weeks that would have put the assault into the rains.

**Running sums.** American lives saved by all provisions: about 3,400–6,900, the meta-checkpoint's 2,500–5,000 plus this memorandum's 900–1,900, a judgment resting on rates (two to four percent of killed in action for this memorandum's tourniquet), shown now at Albany and in the first wave of Tet; it is six to twelve percent of the historical dead and does not cross a band, because tolerance was spent by casualties of a scale and by Tet. RVNAF lives saved by this memorandum: about 4,000–12,000, two to five percent of the RVNAF's historical dead, below a band, shown at Thuan Loi and in the Delta towns at Tet. ARVN battalions not lost: memorandum 007's third fewer broken in 1966–67, and this memorandum's further one in eight, within a band that is already at its cap¹¹². The 1974 appropriation: nothing in this memorandum changes what the Republic asked Congress to pay for or what Congress voted; a better-trained army needs the same ammunition, and the vote stands as history's.

**State of the Republic at the end of 1974.** Phase VI is not revised, but for the record: at the end of 1974 the RVNAF is trained to the man, its posts in the held belt hold better and its wounded live more often, and it is short of ammunition, fuel and pay exactly as before; the course has made the attrition of 1974 somewhat cheaper in lives and has not changed one position the PAVN chose to take.

No phase redraw is proposed. The war's shape has not changed.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The wounded live.** About 5,000–14,000 American and South Vietnamese soldiers who died on the historical course are alive, because every soldier of both armies was taught the tourniquet in basic training; shown at Thuan Loi in June 1965, at Albany in November 1965 and in the first wave of Tet². It is the largest single effect of the provision and no adaptation of the enemy's touches it.
- **The first minute.** The ARVN and the territorial forces are the first government army in this war that the Front cannot reliably break with its opening volley: the ARVN a band steadier in 1964–65, its ambush losses down a tenth to a sixth in 1966–67, and the territorial post holding its first ten minutes nineteen times in twenty.
- **Nobody joins untrained.** The untrained draftee sent straight to his unit is gone from the war, enforced by a number on the muster roll, and the mobilization of 1968 is trained rather than rushed.
- **The sergeants come back.** The drill-sergeant corps the army could not spare in 1964–65 repays it from 1966, and the sergeant gap memorandum 007's field commissions opened in 1968 is a fifth rather than a third.
- **The enemy's war moved.** In the coastal lowlands the mine became the principal killer of American Marines and of farmers; around Saigon the third offensive went for the self-defence groups; and a few hundred trained deserters a year taught the Front's village platoons some of the course.

### What has not changed

- **Who chooses the battle.** The enemy declined the trained unit and attacked the one beside it, as he had declined the rearmed and the well-led ones; the fighting gain appears as fights not fought.
- **The order above the private.** The RF convoy at Bau Bang rode its trucks because its sector commander put it there; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment landed where its division told it; the corps commanders of 1972 and 1975 were chosen as before.
- **Desertion and the family.** Men deserted for pay, rice and family, which the course does not touch; the devaluation of 1966 and the roads of March 1975 show it.
- **Unit training and the squad leader.** The battalion's collective training, historically almost nonexistent, is untouched; the binding constraint has moved from the private to the squad leader and the platoon.
- **The clock and the end.** American tolerance, the 1974 appropriation, the critical path of 1975 and the date of the fall are as before. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, at rung (a).
