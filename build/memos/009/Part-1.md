# Memorandum 009 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


Basic training is the one thing every man in this war has in common. The general and the Popular Forces private both began on a drill field, and for most of the men I command it is the only formal training they will ever have before someone shoots at them. This grant replaces that beginning, for every American and every South Vietnamese soldier who has entered service since 1957, with the beginning the United States Army gives its recruits in 2026. It gives nothing after it: no advanced individual training, no infantry school, no NCO course. I read the grant as three provisions, because the three courses are taught to three different kinds of men in three different places: the American services' entry-level training, the RVNAF regular army's basic course, and the abridged course for the Regional and Popular Forces.

Two readings govern everything below, and I state them once here. First, "the same date as officer education" is 1957: the officer education of memorandum 007 reaches every officer commissioned from 1957 and its 1957–63 classes stand as a condition of the world on 1 January 1964¹. I read this grant the same way. Every enlisted man who entered US or RVNAF service from 1957, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit from 1957, took the 2026 course; the programmes themselves arrive with the campaign². I have recorded that as a standing condition in CONFIG, as memorandum 007's was. Second, "2026 BCT" is a curriculum, a standard, a method and a cadre, not a set of equipment. Where the 2026 programme of instruction teaches a task on equipment that does not exist in 1964, the task is taught on the equipment that does, and I say in each dossier which lessons survive that substitution and which do not.

## §1.1 US entry-level training to the 2026 standard, from 1957 — P009a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** Every man who enlists or is inducted into the United States armed forces from 1957 passes through his service's entry-level training as it is run in 2026, and only that: the grant excludes all further training². For the Army, which fights most of this war on the ground and takes most of the draftees, that is Basic Combat Training: ten weeks in four phases. The Yellow phase, weeks one and two, teaches Army values and begins physical and tactical training. The Red phase, weeks three and four, issues the weapon, teaches hand-to-hand combat and lifesaving, and ends in a field exercise called the Hammer. The White phase, weeks five to seven, is basic rifle marksmanship and target engagement and ends in the Anvil, two days and two nights in the field. The Blue phase, weeks eight to ten, adds machine guns and hand grenades and movement under fire, and ends in the Forge³.

**The 2026 design.** The course is the product of two redesigns. The 2018 redesign put back time for discipline, drill and ceremony, Army history and barracks inspection after the Army found its new soldiers undisciplined, raised the fitness bar, expanded first aid to the Medical Command's standard for shock, open wounds and fractures, and made an 81-hour Forge a graduation requirement⁴. The 2024 redesign, Forge 2.5, turned the course from a round-robin of individual tasks into a sequence of collective events: trainees work and lead in small teams from reception onwards, drill sergeants serve as squad leaders in the field, and the Forge becomes a four-day patrol-base exercise in which trainees evaluate casualties, send reports, plan missions and issue orders⁵. The fitness standard is the 2025–26 one, set by a department-wide directive of September 2025⁶. A 2025 review of training-school standards against those of 1990 was ordered but had not changed the basic course's length or structure by 2026⁷⁸.

**The other services.** I read "all US forces" as all four services. The Marine Corps' recruit training runs thirteen weeks at Parris Island and San Diego and ends in the Crucible; the Navy's runs ten weeks at Great Lakes; the Air Force's Basic Military Training runs seven and a half weeks at Lackland⁹. The Marine course matters almost as much as the Army's here, because the III Marine Amphibious Force fights the northern war; the Navy and Air Force courses matter little in combat and I do not develop them beyond noting that their 2026 versions include the same casualty-care instruction¹⁰.

**What it is not.** It is not advanced individual training, the infantry one-station unit training, the Marine School of Infantry or Marine Combat Training². A rifleman's trade beyond the individual soldier's skills is still taught by the historical eight-week infantry AIT¹¹. That boundary governs every judgment below.

**In-service date.** A 2026 configuration of a course redesigned in 2018 and 2024⁵⁴. The men who entered service in 1957–63 completed it before the campaign opened².

**Quantity.** Every US enlisted entrant from 1957². Selective Service inducted between 82,060 and 142,246 men a year in 1957–63. Volunteers, many of them draft-motivated, outnumbered inductees by about two or three to one, so the four services took in about 450,000–550,000 enlisted men a year before the war¹². In the build-up inductions rose to 230,991 in 1965 and 382,010 in 1966¹³, and total enlisted accessions reached about 800,000–1,000,000 a year in 1966–68¹⁴. The seven classes of 1957–63 amount to about 3.2–3.8 million men, of whom perhaps 1.1–1.4 million remain on active duty in January 1964¹⁵.

**Organisation in theater.** None. The provision is taught at Fort Jackson, Fort Benning, Fort Polk, Fort Dix, Fort Leonard Wood, Fort Knox, Fort Gordon, Fort Ord, Fort Bliss and Fort Lewis, at the two Marine recruit depots, at Great Lakes and at Lackland¹⁶. Its product reaches the Republic inside every squad, crew, section and advisory team.

**Who it reaches in 1964.** The career men who enlisted in 1957–63 are, in January 1964, specialists four and five, corporals, sergeants and some staff sergeants: the fire-team and squad leaders of the brigades that land in 1965¹⁷. The platoon sergeants and first sergeants entered before 1957 and are untouched. NCO education is a separate matter and not part of this grant².

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Cadre.** The 2026 Army trains in platoons of about 40–60 recruits under three drill sergeants, or about one to fifteen or twenty¹⁸. The 1960s Army ran larger platoons with fewer cadre, and only began its Drill Sergeant programme in 1964¹⁹. At the 1966–68 accession peak the Army and Marine training bases would need about 3,000–5,000 more drill sergeants, drill instructors and support cadre than they historically had²⁰.

**The two weeks.** The Army's course is two weeks longer than the eight-week course of the period¹¹³. At peak, every Army recruit spends two more weeks as a trainee, which holds about 20,000–25,000 more men in the pipeline at any time [D: 550,000–650,000 Army accessions a year times 2/52]. The Marine Corps' course is longer than its 1960s course for most of the war as well²¹.

**Spaces in the Republic.** None. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling; even if it did, nothing here is in theater²².

**The real cost.** It is the drill sergeant. The men who would be drill sergeants are the experienced sergeants and staff sergeants the Army was also sending to Vietnam and to the new divisions in 1965–66. Three to five thousand more of them at Fort Jackson and Fort Polk are three to five thousand fewer squad leaders and platoon sergeants in the 1st Cavalry and 25th Infantry Divisions²³. The 1957–63 entrants, by then experienced sergeants themselves, are the natural source and soften that cost.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The provision's instruments are the programme of instruction, the method, the standard and the drill sergeant.

**The programme.** Ten weeks of instruction in the soldier's individual skills: Army values and the law of land warfare, drill and ceremony, physical training on the 2026 holistic-health model, combatives to Level 1, basic rifle marksmanship and qualification, hand grenades, machine-gun familiarisation, movement under fire by individual and team, land navigation, the protective mask, and casualty care³⁴²⁴. The 2024 Forge 2.5 adds the collective tasks: the patrol base, the casualty evaluation, the report, the short order⁵.

**The weapon.** In this exercise the recruit trains on the weapon he will carry. Memorandum 004 gave every US and RVNAF rifleman the M4A1 carbine with the URG-I upper and the Aimpoint CompM5 red-dot sight firing M855A1, and put the M249 in every squad. The 2026 marksmanship programme is written for that carbine, that sight and that machine gun; it transfers whole. The Blue phase's machine-gun familiarisation uses the M249²⁵ and the M60, which is the period's general-purpose gun. The hand grenade is the M26 or M61 in place of the M67, functionally the same lesson²⁶. The rest of the Blue phase is taught on what the 1964 Army issues: the M79 grenade launcher and the M72 LAW in place of the M320 and the M136 AT4, the M18A1 Claymore, which is the same mine in both eras, and the M17 protective mask in place of the M50²⁷. The Forge's reports go over the AN/PRC-10 and, from 1965, the AN/PRC-25 rather than the 2026 squad radio²⁸. Under the historical course the recruit qualified on the M14 and met the M16A1 only in Vietnam or in AIT from 1966–67²⁹; here he never fires the M14 at all.

**Casualty care.** The 2026 course teaches every recruit tactical combat casualty care to the all-service-members standard: stop the massive bleed first, with a tourniquet high and tight on the limb; then airway, respiration, circulation and hypothermia; then the drag, the carry and the call for help³⁰. The 1960s Army taught first aid as the field dressing and pressure, and taught that a tourniquet was a last resort that cost the limb; that teaching survived in US doctrine until about 2001. Of 2,600 Americans killed in action in the Maughon study of Vietnam, 193, or 7.9%, died of isolated extremity hemorrhage, the leading cause of preventable death in the war³¹.

**The tourniquet itself.** The grant gives the training, not the device. The CAT tourniquet of 2026 does not exist in 1964³². What survives is the method: a recruit taught to apply a windlass tourniquet in under a minute can apply one improvised from a cravat bandage and a stick, or the issue strap tourniquet of the period, and the 2026 course teaches the improvised windlass as well³³. Whether the Army then issues a purpose-made windlass tourniquet to every soldier is a decision for the Army, not something this grant supplies.

**The authority.** The course's power is the graduation standard. A recruit who cannot qualify with his weapon, pass the fitness test or complete the Forge is recycled or discharged⁴. The 2026 course is a standard, not an attendance requirement.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.* The course learns about the recruit, not about the enemy.

**What it learns.** Ten weeks of observed performance tell the drill sergeant who can shoot, who can lead a buddy team, who freezes, and who cannot keep up. The Forge is a deliberate test under fatigue⁵. The 2026 course uses that information to recycle or separate the man who cannot meet the standard; about one recruit in ten does not finish³.

**What it teaches about the war.** Nothing specific to Vietnam. The 2026 course is built for a soldier who may fight anywhere; its scenarios since 2024 are large-scale combat against a peer⁵. It teaches the soldier to move at night and to keep noise and light discipline in a patrol base, which transfers to the canopy; it teaches nothing about paddies, booby traps on trails, villages or the Viet Cong. The Vietnam orientation of the period lived in AIT and in the in-country replacement courses³⁴, and those remain historical.

**Night.** The 2026 course trains with the AN/PVS-14 monocular and the AN/PEQ-15 aiming laser; neither exists in 1964³⁵. The night lessons survive only as discipline and movement without the device.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Throughput.** About 450,000–550,000 enlisted entrants a year in 1957–64 and about 800,000–1,000,000 at the 1966–68 peak³⁶. Each Army recruit spends ten weeks rather than eight³¹¹; each Marine recruit thirteen rather than about eight to twelve⁹³⁷.

**The rate limit.** Cadre, not ranges or barracks. The Army's training centres of 1965–66 doubled their throughput by running more cycles with fewer cadre³⁸. At the 2026 cadre ratio the same doubling needs drill sergeants in numbers the Army does not have in 1965 without stripping the units, and the honest expectation is that the 1965–66 surge takes about two to three months longer to train than it did³⁹.

**Time to the theater.** Historically a soldier reached Vietnam about six months after enlisting¹¹. Under this grant an Army infantryman takes about six and a half months [D: two added weeks].

**What graduates.** About 90% of starters in 2026³. Against the 1966 Project 100,000 intake, the men accepted below the old mental-category floors, the 2026 standard will fail more: I estimate 12–18% of starters recycled or discharged at the peak⁴⁰. The graduation standard does not change the Army's entrance standard, and the grant does not.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** Continental Army Command's training centres and the Marine recruit depots report to their services; the course has no link to MACV beyond the replacements it sends⁴¹.

**Interface with the RVNAF.** Indirect but real. The American adviser who enlisted after 1957 learned the same basic course that the Vietnamese recruit now learns under P009b, and the same casualty-care drill. A common basic vocabulary between the American sergeant adviser and the ARVN squad is a small thing that makes combined operations easier.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Every enlisted American. The provision needs no security in the Republic; it is taught at home.

**Preconditions.** It needs drill sergeants, ranges and time. All three exist in the United States, at a cost in sergeants and in about two weeks of each soldier's service (D2).

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Ammunition.** The 2026 marksmanship programme fires more rounds per recruit than the 1960s course, about 500–800 rounds of 5.56mm against a few hundred of 7.62mm⁴². At peak, about 300–500 million rounds a year for training [D: Army and Marine accessions times the round count]. That competes with the theater's M855A1 supply, which memorandum 004 already strained²⁵.

**Ranges and posts.** The training centres need more range lanes and Forge training areas to run collective events for larger platoons; the 1965–66 expansion reopened and built posts anyway⁴³.

**Cadre.** About 3,000–5,000 added drill sergeants and drill instructors at the peak (D2).

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Nothing is asked of the RVNAF.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The course itself transfers; that is P009b. What does not transfer is the drill sergeant. The 2026 American drill sergeant is a staff sergeant or sergeant first class with six to twelve years of service who has passed his own ten-week academy⁴⁴. The ARVN NCO corps of 1964 cannot supply drill sergeants of that experience in the numbers P009b needs without help, and the help is American advisers, not American drill sergeants. The transfer is therefore of the curriculum and the method, at once, and of the cadre over three to five years.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** for the individual soldier's skills that are the same in every war: shooting the carbine accurately under stress, moving with a buddy under fire, putting a tourniquet on a wounded friend, keeping silent in a patrol base, keeping going when exhausted.

**It degrades** where the war needs skills the course does not teach. A soldier who has learned to move with his fire team on a range in Georgia has not learned to walk a trail in Quang Ngai looking for tripwires. That is taught in AIT, in the unit and in theater, and is historical.

**It collapses** where the unit that receives the soldier undoes the course. A replacement who joins a company whose sergeants do things their own way will do things their way within weeks⁴⁵. Memorandum 006's unit rotation keeps a soldier with the men he trained beside and the sergeants who received him, and helps here⁴⁶.

**Over weeks rather than once.** A basic course decays into habit or is lost; marksmanship and casualty care are perishable skills and decline without refreshment within months⁴⁷. The grant gives no further training, so their survival depends on the unit.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The course is cut in the surge.** The most likely failure. In 1965–66 the Army, short of men and drill sergeants, shortens the course to eight weeks or drops the Forge "for the duration"⁴⁸. The grant sets the course², and I plan on it holding; Washington would have had every incentive to break it. A partial failure looks like the Forge cut to a day; a total failure looks like the 1960s course with a 2026 name.

**The standard is waived.** The recycle and discharge rate rises with Project 100,000 and the Army waives the standard to keep the pipeline moving. A partial failure passes men who cannot qualify; a total failure makes the course an attendance requirement.

**The drill sergeant is absent.** If the Army cannot find 3,000–5,000 more drill sergeants, the course is run with 1960s ratios and loses much of the individual attention that makes it work (D2).

**During the window it fails.** The soldier still arrives with the historical training, so nothing in the war is worse than history; the gain is lost, not reversed.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** For the force as a whole the curve rises, not falls: in January 1964 the 1957–63 entrants still serving are already trained, and every new man adds to them. By the end of 1966 almost every enlisted American in Vietnam has had the course⁴⁹.

**What declines first.** In the individual soldier, marksmanship and casualty care, both perishable within months without refreshment (D10). In the institution, the cadre ratio and the Forge, both expensive and both the first things a surge cuts (D11).

**What lasts.** The tourniquet reflex and the habit of working in a team, which are taught to the point of reflex and are refreshed by the war itself.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the 1960s Army basic course.** Eight weeks, in some cases nine¹¹, against ten³; a round-robin of individual tasks against a sequence of collective events with an 81- to 96-hour field exercise at its end⁵⁴; first aid with the field dressing against tactical combat casualty care with the tourniquet³¹; a marksmanship course on the M14 at known-distance and pop-up targets against the 2026 programme on the M4A1 and CompM5 that the soldier will carry²⁵. The margin is about 25% more time and a different method in the time.

**The size of the margin, honestly.** The basic course is about a sixth of an infantryman's formal training before Vietnam, the rest being AIT and the unit⁵⁰. Even a large improvement in a sixth is a modest improvement in the whole, except in the two places where the basic course is the whole: the tourniquet, which no other course of the period taught, and the soldier who is not infantry. The cook, the truck driver and the clerk had basic training and nothing else in combat skills, and in Vietnam they were shot at on every convoy and in every base attack⁵¹. For them the 2026 course is most of their combat training.

**The margin for the tourniquet.** I estimate that the reflex taught to every soldier, with improvised or period tourniquets, saves a quarter to a half of the Americans who would have died of extremity hemorrhage before reaching a surgeon: about 900–1,900 American lives over 1965–72⁵². The basis is the 7.9% share in S14 applied to about 47,000 American hostile deaths, with improvised tourniquets less effective than the CAT.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It is basic training only.** It does not touch advanced individual training, infantry school, NCO education or unit training; the rifleman's trade beyond the individual soldier's skills is historical.
- **It teaches nothing about Vietnam.** No booby traps, villages, paddies or Viet Cong; its scenarios are large-scale combat against a peer.
- **It does not issue a tourniquet.** It teaches the drill; the 2026 CAT does not exist, and an improvised or period tourniquet is less reliable.
- **It does not change who is drafted.** Project 100,000 and the entrance standards are untouched; the course fails more of the weak intake rather than improving it.
- **It costs sergeants and time.** About 3,000–5,000 more drill sergeants at the peak, and two more weeks for each Army recruit, which slows the 1965–66 build-up by two to three months.
- **It does not reach the senior NCOs.** Platoon sergeants and first sergeants of 1964–68 entered before 1957.
- **It decays without refreshment.** Marksmanship and casualty care are perishable; the grant gives no further training to keep them.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the wounded American who bleeds to death from a leg wound in the ten minutes before the medic reaches him, and some of the new soldier's fatal mistakes: the man who cannot hit what he aims at, who bunches up, who talks in the ambush position. It denies him nothing at the operational level.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a modest provision for the American war with one real saving inside it. The ten-week course makes the American private and the young sergeant of 1964–68 somewhat better at the individual soldier's work, and much better for the cook and the truck driver whose basic course was all the combat training they had. Its largest single effect is the tourniquet: every American taught to stop an extremity bleed, in a war where that was the leading preventable cause of death. I estimate that at about 900–1,900 American lives over the war, which I sign for gladly. It does not change how the war is fought, it teaches nothing about the hamlet, and it costs drill sergeants and two weeks of each soldier's time during the build-up.

**Key figures — P009a**

- Courses: Army BCT 10 weeks in four phases with the Hammer, the Anvil and the Forge; Marine recruit training 13 weeks, Navy 10, Air Force BMT 7.5⁵³
- Historical Army basic course: 8 weeks, in some cases 9⁵³
- Forge: 81 hours as a graduation requirement from 2018⁵³, a four-day patrol base in Forge 2.5 from 2024⁵³
- Accessions: about 450,000–550,000 enlisted a year in 1957–64 and 800,000–1,000,000 in 1966–68, all services⁵⁴
- 1957–63 entrants still serving on 1 Jan 1964: about 1.1–1.4 million, the junior NCOs of 1964–66³⁶
- Cost: about 3,000–5,000 added drill sergeants and instructors at the peak; 20,000–25,000 more Army trainees in the pipeline at any time; build-up slowed about 2–3 months⁵⁵
- Extremity hemorrhage: 7.9% of US killed in action in Vietnam, the leading preventable cause of death⁵³
- Lives saved by the tourniquet reflex: about 900–1,900 Americans over 1965–72⁵²
- Completion: about 90% in 2026⁵³; 82–88% against the Project 100,000 intake⁵⁶

## §1.2 RVNAF regular basic training to the 2026 US Army standard, from 1957 — P009b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** Every enlisted man who enters the regular armed forces of the Republic from 1957 takes the 2026 US Army's ten-week Basic Combat Training, in Vietnamese, before he joins a unit². I read "ARVN" as the grant uses it, for the regular RVNAF: the Army with its Airborne and Ranger units, the Vietnamese Marine Corps, and the enlisted recruits of the VNAF and VNN, who historically took an Army-model basic course as well⁵⁷. The territorial forces are P009c.

**The course.** The four phases, the field exercises and the collective-event method of the American course (§1.1 D1)³⁵, translated and taught by Vietnamese drill sergeants with American advisers. It is the same course, not a reduced one, and the same ten weeks². The programme's American institutional blocks (Army history, the American Army's values as written) are replaced with their Vietnamese equivalents: the history of the Vietnamese army and the obligations of the soldier to the people, written by the Central Training Command with its advisers⁵⁸.

**The schools.** Quang Trung National Training Center at Hoc Mon, 16 kilometres north-west of Saigon, founded in 1953, and the other national and division training centres. By 1968–70 the RVNAF ran 33 training centres, including the Regional and Popular Forces centres, the Ranger centre and ten division centres, with a normal capacity of about 65,000 trainees and an emergency capacity of about 105,655⁵⁹. In May 1964 MACV recommended extending recruit training from Quang Trung to four other national centres and to the Ranger centre at Duc My⁶⁰; I take that expansion as happening on the historical schedule, now for a ten-week course.

**What it displaces.** The historical basic course was eight weeks in the mid-1950s⁶⁰, twelve weeks by the 1960s and cut to nine in the 1968 mobilisation⁵⁹; the 1964 Regional Forces recruit course was nine weeks "matching the Vietnamese Army training centres"⁶¹. The sources differ, and I take the historical ARVN basic course of 1964–67 as nine to twelve weeks⁶². The ten-week course is therefore not longer than the historical one. Its gain is in what is taught and how, and in the rule that nobody skips it.

**The rule that nobody skips it.** Historically units were often filled with hastily drafted men who had no formal training at all⁵⁹. Under this grant no man joins a unit without the course². I read that as the grant's force, as the gate was the force of memorandum 007.

**In-service date.** The 2026 US programme of instruction adapted; the 1957–63 entrants completed it before the campaign opened².

**Quantity.** Every regular enlisted entrant from 1957². Quang Trung trained about 24,000 a year in the mid-1950s, and the 1964 plan was 30,000 there⁶⁰; I estimate 30,000–45,000 regular entrants a year in 1957–63 and 60,000–100,000 a year in 1964–67⁶³. In 1968 the training centres took 168,335 Army and Regional Forces recruits⁵⁹, of whom I estimate 130,000–170,000 a year were regulars at the 1968–69 peak⁶⁴. The seven classes of 1957–63 are about 250,000 men, of whom perhaps 120,000–160,000 are still serving on 1 January 1964⁶⁵.

**Who it reaches in 1964.** A large share of the regular army. ARVN conscripts served two years and were then often retained, and volunteers served longer⁶⁶. On 1 January 1964 something like half to two-thirds of the regular enlisted ranks entered after 1957 and hold the course⁶⁷]. Its corporals and young sergeants are almost all 2026-trained.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Cadre.** At the 2026 ratio of about one drill sergeant to fifteen or twenty recruits (§1.1 D2)¹⁸, a training load of about 15,000–25,000 regular recruits at any time in 1964–67 needs about 1,000–1,600 Vietnamese drill sergeants, and at the 1968–69 peak about 2,000–3,000 [D: the annual intake in D1 times ten weeks over 52, divided by the ratio]. With officers, range staff and instructors, the Vietnamese training cadre is about 2,500–5,000 men¹⁴.

**The drill sergeants come from somewhere.** They are corporals and sergeants pulled from the battalions. Historically training-centre posts were given to officers relieved of combat command⁵⁹, and the instructors were not the army's best. To run this course the RVNAF must send good NCOs to the centres and keep them there for two years, which costs the battalions their best squad leaders.

**US spaces.** About 250–400 US personnel in the Republic, of whom about 220–360 are military: advisory detachments of six to twelve at each of about 15–25 national and division centres, a team at a Vietnamese drill-sergeant academy, and a curriculum cell at the Central Training Command⁶⁸. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.*

**The programme.** The same as §1.1 D3: values and the law of war, drill, physical training, combatives, rifle marksmanship and qualification, hand grenades, machine guns, movement under fire, land navigation, the protective mask, and casualty care, ending in the Forge's patrol base³⁵⁴²⁴. The Vietnamese recruit fires the M4A1 with the CompM5 and the M249 that memorandum 004 issued to the whole RVNAF²⁵. Historically the ARVN recruit of 1964 trained on the M1 Garand, the M1 and M2 carbines and the M1918A2 BAR, with French MAT-49 submachine guns still in some units, and received the M16A1 only from 1967–68⁶⁹. That is the strongest single point in the dossier: the 2026 marksmanship course and the weapon it was written for are both here, for an army whose 1959 advisers judged its marksmanship training insufficient⁶⁰.

**Casualty care.** Tactical combat casualty care for every recruit, with the tourniquet first. For the ARVN this matters more than for the Americans, because its wounded waited longer: evacuation by American UH-1D and UH-1H ambulance helicopters was available to the ARVN, but less reliably and less quickly than to American units, and ARVN battalions had fewer medics⁷⁰. A man who stops his friend's bleeding has more time to buy.

**The conduct block.** The 2026 course teaches the law of war and the soldier's obligations toward civilians in the values phase⁷¹. In Vietnamese, rewritten as the soldier's duty to the people, it is the first time every ARVN recruit hears from his own army that the villager is not his to rob.

**The authority.** The graduation standard and the rule that nobody joins a unit without it². That rule is a JGS order, and its enforcement is an advisory matter (D6).

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.*

**What it learns.** Which recruits can shoot, lead a buddy team, keep going and keep silent; ten weeks of observation by a drill sergeant who knows each recruit's name⁵. For the RVNAF that is new information: historically a recruit passed through a crowded centre unknown to anyone⁷².

**What it teaches about the war.** As §1.1 D4, nothing specific to Vietnam. But for the Vietnamese recruit the war is at home. The patrol-base and night-discipline lessons are directly the lessons of his next two years.

**Night.** The 2026 night-vision lessons do not survive (§1.1 D4); the discipline does.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Throughput.** About 30,000–45,000 a year in 1957–63, 60,000–100,000 a year in 1964–67, and 130,000–170,000 at the 1968–69 peak³⁶.

**The rate limit.** Drill sergeants first, then centre capacity. The 1968–70 centres had a normal capacity of about 65,000 at a time, expandable to about 105,655⁵⁹, which carries the peak load. Drill sergeants are the binding constraint: about 1,000–3,000 Vietnamese NCOs of real quality, kept out of the battalions (D2).

**The pressure to cut.** Historically the RVNAF cut the basic course from twelve weeks to nine in the 1968 mobilisation⁵⁹. The grant holds the course at ten weeks², which means in 1968 it is a week longer than history. That is the moment when the rule will be tested.

**What graduates.** I estimate 85–90% of starters, below the American rate because of illness, family emergencies and desertion during the course⁷³.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** The centres report to the JGS Central Training Command; the advisory detachments report to MACV's training directorate⁷⁴.

**Leverage.** The rule that nobody joins a unit untrained depends on the corps and division commanders, who historically pulled recruits from the centres early when their units were short⁵⁹. The adviser can report it; only the JGS can stop it. Memorandum 007's officer selection reform gives the JGS commanders who answer for it¹.

**Common language.** The Vietnamese private and the American adviser have learned the same basic course. A combined patrol of a US adviser and an ARVN squad shares the same casualty drill and the same buddy-team movement.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Every regular enlisted man, at centres that are secure: Quang Trung on Highway 1 north-west of Saigon⁶⁰, and division centres inside division base areas. The centres need no hamlet security.

**Preconditions.** The centres must be kept from becoming what they historically were, posts for relieved officers and sources of graft: in 1967 Quang Trung's commandant was relieved for building an ice plant with trainee labour⁶⁰. Memorandum 001's anti-corruption commission and memorandum 007's selection reform are the protections⁷⁵¹.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Ammunition.** About 500–800 rounds of 5.56mm M855A1 per recruit⁷⁶, or about 30–130 million rounds a year at the regular intake [D: D1 times the round count]. Memorandum 008's machining industry may produce small-arms ammunition⁷⁷; if it does not, this is American supply.

**Ranges and barracks.** The centres need more range lanes and patrol-base areas; the historical expansion of 1964–68 built most of them⁵⁹⁶⁰.

**Rations and pay.** Recruits on a ten-week course are fed and paid for ten weeks; that is not more than history's nine to twelve.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** In 1964, enough NCOs fit to be drill sergeants. That is what the American advisers are for, and what the 1957–63 entrants, now experienced corporals and sergeants, can begin to supply by 1965–66.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

It is already Vietnamese: taught in Vietnamese by Vietnamese drill sergeants. The American advisers at the centres can be withdrawn about three to five years after the drill-sergeant academy opens, as Vietnamese cadre who have themselves graduated from the course become drill sergeants⁷⁸. Under Vietnamization it is among the most transferable things in this exercise.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** where the recruit joins a battalion that uses what he learned: an officer of the 1957 classes who knows the same doctrine (memorandum 007)¹, a squad leader who took the same course, and a platoon that keeps the M4A1 zeroed.

**It degrades** where the battalion undoes it. ARVN battalions were chronically under strength, short of NCOs and shuffled their men⁵⁹. A trained recruit in a battalion that does not patrol, does not shoot and does not keep its men together loses most of the course within months.

**It collapses** where the recruit deserts. Desertion in the RVNAF was driven by pay, family separation and posting far from home⁷⁹, and a ten-week course does not cure any of those.

**Over weeks rather than once.** As §1.1 D10: marksmanship and casualty care are perishable, and the grant gives no further training.

### D11 · Failure modes

**Recruits pulled early.** The most likely failure. Short battalions take recruits from the centres before the course ends; a partial failure is a five-week course, a total failure is the historical untrained draftee⁵⁹.

**The cadre is poor.** If the drill sergeants are the men the battalions did not want, the course is taught badly and the Forge becomes a camping trip. That is the historical training-centre problem⁵⁹.

**Graft.** The commandant sells the rations or uses the recruits as labour⁶⁰.

**During the window it fails.** The soldier arrives as historically trained, so nothing is worse than history.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** In January 1964 the regular army already holds about 120,000–160,000 men from the 1957–63 classes³⁶. The force-level curve rises as the remaining pre-1957 men leave and every new recruit has the course.

**What declines first.** In the soldier, marksmanship and casualty care, within months without refreshment. In the institution, the cadre, as the battalions pull their good NCOs back under the pressure of 1965 and 1968.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical ARVN basic course.** About the same length: ten weeks against nine to twelve⁶¹. A different course in the time: the 2026 marksmanship programme on the carbine the soldier carries against an instruction programme whose marksmanship advisers judged insufficient⁶⁰; a drill sergeant for every fifteen or twenty against crowded classes³⁷; tactical combat casualty care against the field dressing³¹; a collective field exercise at the end against none⁵. And, most important, a course everyone takes against a course many skipped⁵⁹.

**The margin.** I judge that the 2026-trained ARVN recruit is about as competent at the individual soldier's skills on his first day in a battalion as the historical recruit after four to six months in one⁵², judged on the method and cadre ratio against the historical course, and the fraction of historical recruits who joined untrained. The gain is largest in marksmanship and casualty care and smallest in fieldcraft, which neither course teaches.

**The tourniquet.** Applying the American share of 7.9% of killed in action dying of extremity hemorrhage³¹ to RVNAF combat deaths several times the American, and saving a quarter to a half with improvised tourniquets and slower evacuation, gives several thousand Vietnamese soldiers' lives over 1964–75⁵². The basis is the same as §1.1 D13, with RVNAF combat deaths of roughly 200,000–250,000. I give it as about 4,000–12,000 lives and mark it as an estimate built on an American ratio.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It is basic training only.** It does not train the battalion, the squad leader or the specialist; unit training, which historically was "virtually nonexistent", is untouched.
- **It is not longer than history.** Ten weeks against a historical nine to twelve; the gain is in method, cadre, the weapon and the rule that nobody skips it.
- **It does not cure desertion.** Pay, family and posting far from home drove desertion, and the course touches none of them.
- **It does not choose the officer.** A trained private under a political commander is still sent down the road into the ambush; that is memoranda 007's and 002's work.
- **It costs NCOs.** About 1,000–3,000 Vietnamese drill sergeants kept out of the battalions.
- **It teaches nothing about Vietnam.** Its scenarios are large-scale combat; the trail, the village and the mine are learned in the unit.
- **It does not issue tourniquets.** It teaches the drill.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the ARVN private who cannot hit what he shoots at, who joins his battalion untrained, and who bleeds to death from a leg wound. It denies him some of the ease with which his ambushes broke ARVN units whose men had never been taught to move under fire. It denies him nothing at the operational level.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a real provision for the ARVN, larger than P009a is for the Americans, because the gap it closes is wider. Its best effects are three: every regular soldier trained before he joins a unit, the 2026 marksmanship course on the carbine he will carry, and every Vietnamese soldier taught to stop a bleed. Its cost is the NCOs who teach it. It does not touch the officer who leads the soldier, the desertion that takes him away, or the battalion training that would make a unit of him. A better private in an army with a bad colonel is still in an army with a bad colonel, and memoranda 007 and 002 are what change the colonel.

**Key figures — P009b**

- Historical ARVN basic course: 8 weeks in the mid-1950s; 12 weeks cut to 9 in 1968; RF 9 weeks in 1964 matching the ARVN centres⁵³
- Historical untrained draftees sent straight to units 
- Centres: 33 in 1968–70, normal capacity about 65,000, emergency about 105,655⁵³; Quang Trung 24,000 a year in the mid-1950s, 30,000 planned in 1964⁵³
- Intake: about 30,000–45,000 regulars a year in 1957–63, 60,000–100,000 in 1964–67, 130,000–170,000 at the 1968–69 peak 
- 1957–63 entrants still serving on 1 Jan 1964: about 120,000–160,000, half to two-thirds of the regular enlisted ranks³⁶
- Cadre: about 1,000–3,000 Vietnamese drill sergeants; 220–360 US military spaces in RVN³⁷
- Margin: the 2026-trained recruit is about as competent on his first day as a historical one after 4–6 months in a battalion 
- RVNAF lives saved by the tourniquet reflex: about 4,000–12,000 over 1964–75⁵²

## §1.3 Abridged 2026 basic training for the Regional and Popular Forces, from 1957 — P009c

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** Every Regional Forces and Popular Forces recruit, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit from 1957, takes an abridged version of the 2026 US Army basic course before he joins his company or platoon. The grant says "abridged" and gives no length². I set it at six weeks⁸⁰.

**Why six weeks, and what is cut.** I keep every individual soldier's skill of the 2026 course and cut what serves the regular army rather than the territorial soldier: most drill and ceremony, the institutional history and inspection blocks, the large-scale-combat collective scenarios, and part of the physical conditioning time⁸¹. What remains is rifle marksmanship and qualification on the M4A1 and CompM5²⁵, hand grenades, the light machine gun, movement under fire by buddy team and fire team, the patrol base and night discipline, the protective mask, the law of war and conduct toward civilians, and tactical combat casualty care. Six weeks is the length of the historical Popular Forces course of 1960–64⁸² and three weeks shorter than the 1964 Regional Forces course⁶¹. I chose it so that the abridged course costs the PF nothing in time and the RF three weeks, and I say plainly that for the RF the change is a better course in less time, not more training.

**What it is not.** It is not a course in hamlet defence, village intelligence or territorial operations [U: basic training only]. The 2026 US Army has no such course to abridge.

**The schools.** The Regional and Popular Forces training centres, which numbered 37 and were cut to 19 under the Central Training Command⁸³; in 1960–64 the Self-Defense Corps trained at centres in 26 provinces⁸².

**In-service date.** An abridgement of the 2026 programme with no real-world equivalent; the 1957–63 recruits completed it before the campaign opened².

**Quantity.** Every territorial recruit from 1957². The Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps were about 46,000 and 40,000 in 1959⁶¹, and the Popular Forces lost 11,957 men to desertion in 1962, 18,540 in 1963, 36,608 in 1964 and 49,224 in 1965⁸³, each of whom had to be replaced. I estimate 20,000–35,000 recruits a year in 1957–63, 40,000–70,000 a year in 1964–67 and 60,000–90,000 a year in the 1968–69 expansion⁸⁴.

**Who it reaches in 1964.** The seven classes of 1957–63 are about 150,000–250,000 men, of whom perhaps 60,000–100,000 are still serving in January 1964⁸⁵. The authorised Popular Forces structure in 1964 was 110,000⁸², and the territorial forces as a whole were somewhat over 200,000⁸⁶, so perhaps a third to a half of the territorial soldiers of January 1964 hold the abridged course.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Cadre.** At one drill sergeant to fifteen or twenty recruits, a load of about 5,000–10,000 territorial recruits at any time needs about 300–600 Vietnamese drill sergeants [D: the annual intake in D1 times six weeks over 52, divided by the ratio]. With range staff and instructors the territorial training cadre is about 800–1,500 men¹⁴.

**US spaces.** About 200–350 US personnel in the Republic, of whom about 180–320 military: advisory teams of three to six at the 19–37 RF/PF centres and a share of the drill-sergeant academy team⁸⁷. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling.

**What it competes for.** The same Vietnamese drill sergeants as P009b; a single drill-sergeant academy serves both.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.*

**The programme.** Six weeks of the 2026 course's individual skills (D1), on the weapon the territorial soldier carries: memorandum 004 gave every RF and PF soldier the M4A1 carbine with the CompM5 sight in 1964²⁵. Historically the PF carried M1 carbines and old M1918A2 BARs as late as 1967, and Westmoreland was still asking for 268,000 M16s and 11,200 M79 launchers for the territorial forces in 1968⁸³. Here the PF private in 1964 has the 2026 carbine and the 2026 course for it.

**Casualty care.** Tactical combat casualty care for every territorial soldier⁸⁸. The territorial soldier had the slowest evacuation of anyone in the war; a PF platoon in a hamlet at night often had no medic and no helicopter until morning⁸⁹. A tourniquet in the first minute is the only treatment many of them will get.

**The conduct block.** The law of war and the soldier's obligations toward civilians, rewritten in Vietnamese as the territorial soldier's duty to his neighbours⁵⁸. The PF soldier was the GVN's face in the hamlet, and his conduct toward the villager was the government's conduct⁹⁰.

**The authority.** The rule that no territorial soldier joins his unit without the course². Historically in 1964 the territorial training programme ran up to 60% behind its target and its leadership quotas were never filled⁸².

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.*

**What it learns.** Which recruits can shoot and which can lead a buddy team, observed over six weeks.

**What it teaches about the war.** Nothing specific to the hamlet. But the patrol base, the night discipline and the casualty drill are precisely the territorial soldier's work. The night ambush outside a hamlet is the patrol base in miniature, and the six-week course teaches its elements: silence, security, the sector of fire and the casualty drill.

**Which half of find-and-fix.** Neither directly. A trained PF platoon that holds its hamlet at night does not find the VCI; it denies the VCI the hamlet after dark, which makes the Police Special Branch's work of memoranda 002 and 006 possible⁹¹⁴⁶.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Throughput.** About 20,000–35,000 a year in 1957–63, 40,000–70,000 a year in 1964–67 and 60,000–90,000 at the 1968–69 expansion³⁶.

**The rate limit.** Drill sergeants, and the district chief's willingness to release men to a centre for six weeks. Historically in 1964 RF recruiting met about 2,000 trained against a goal of 14,000⁶¹, and in 1964 the territorial training programme ran up to 60% behind⁸². The grant fixes the content and the rule, not the recruiting.

**What graduates.** I estimate 80–90% of starters⁹².

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** The territorial centres report to the Central Training Command; the recruit's unit reports to the province chief (RF) or the district and village chief (PF)⁸³.

**The gap.** Between the centre and the unit stands the district chief, who controls recruiting and release. Memorandum 002's merit-selected district chief is more likely to send his recruits to the course and less likely to keep them as his bodyguards⁹¹.

**Common language.** The PF platoon and the ARVN battalion that reinforces it have learned the same basic movement and the same casualty drill; that makes a combined night defence somewhat less of a muddle.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Every territorial recruit, at centres in secure province towns. The course needs security only at the centre.

**Preconditions.** That the recruit is released to the centre and returns to his hamlet. A PF soldier recruited in his own village and trained six weeks away from it is a soldier whose family is unprotected for six weeks; the historical course was the same length⁸², so this costs nothing new.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Ammunition.** About 300–500 rounds of 5.56mm M855A1 per recruit⁹³, about 15–45 million rounds a year [D: D1 times the count].

**Centres.** The historical territorial centres suffice⁸³.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** In 1964, enough drill sergeants (D2).

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

Already Vietnamese. As P009b, the American advisers can withdraw in three to five years.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** where the territorial soldier serves in a platoon that stays in its hamlet at night, under a leader who uses what he learned, with support that comes when called.

**It degrades** where the platoon is left alone without support. A trained PF platoon of about 35 men⁸² in a mud fort, attacked at night by a VC local force company, still loses if no artillery and no reaction force come.

**It collapses** where the district chief uses his territorials as guards for himself and his roads instead of putting them in the hamlets.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The recruit is not released.** The district chief keeps his men; the course is taught to whoever is sent.

**The course is not taught.** The territorial centres are the lowest priority for good cadre; if their drill sergeants are the men nobody wanted, the course is the historical one with a new name.

**The course is cut.** In the 1968 expansion, under the pressure of 588 new PF platoons in a year⁵⁹, the course is cut to three weeks.

**During the window it fails.** The soldier arrives as historically trained.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** The force-level curve rises as every new territorial recruit has the course; by the end of 1966 most territorial soldiers have it, because desertion and losses turned the territorial forces over quickly⁸³.

**What declines first.** Marksmanship without range time in the hamlet, and casualty care without refreshment.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical territorial courses.** Six weeks against the PF's historical six weeks and the RF's nine⁶¹: the PF gains a better course in the same time, the RF a better course in less time. In 1964 the historical territorial training ran up to 60% behind programme, so in practice many territorial soldiers had no course at all; that is the largest margin⁸².

**The margin.** I judge that the abridged course makes the territorial soldier about as competent at the individual skills as the historical ARVN regular recruit, in two-thirds of the time⁵², judged on the 2026 method and weapon against the historical regular course. It does not make a PF platoon an ARVN platoon.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It is not a hamlet-defence course.** It abridges the 2026 basic course; the 2026 Army has no territorial-defence course to abridge, and the grant gives no further training.
- **It is shorter than the historical RF course.** Six weeks against nine for the RF; the RF gains method and loses three weeks.
- **It does not hold the hamlet.** A trained platoon without artillery, a reaction force or a leader still loses its fort at night.
- **It does not recruit.** The district chief's control of recruiting and release is untouched.
- **It does not pay the territorial soldier.** RF/PF pay and the PF's family's safety drive desertion as before.
- **It does not touch the leader.** The PF platoon leader and the RF company commander are what the 1964 leadership training, which historically never filled its quotas, made them.
- **It does not issue tourniquets.** It teaches the drill.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the local force some of its easiest victories: the untrained PF platoon that fires all its ammunition into the dark in the first ten minutes, cannot hit the sapper at the wire, and bleeds out its wounded before dawn. It denies the VCI nothing by itself; it makes the hamlet slightly harder to enter at night.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a modest provision on paper and a meaningful one in the hamlets. Six weeks of the 2026 course is not much training, and it is not more training than the territorial forces historically had. Its value is that every territorial soldier gets it, on the carbine memorandum 004 gave him, with the casualty drill and the conduct block that matter most to a soldier who fights among his neighbours with no helicopter coming. The territorial forces took the heaviest losses in this war and were credited with a large share of the fighting; a better-trained militia in the hamlet is the cheapest improvement in night security I can buy. It does not hold the hamlet by itself, and it does not touch the platoon leader.

**Key figures — P009c**

- Course: six weeks abridged from the 2026 BCT [U: abridged; E: length chosen in D1]
- Historical: PF six weeks at centres in 26 provinces, 1960–64; RF nine weeks in 1964, refresher four weeks⁵³; 1964 territorial training up to 60% behind programme⁵³
- Centres: 37 cut to 19 under the Central Training Command 
- PF desertion: 11,957 (1962), 18,540 (1963), 36,608 (1964), 49,224 (1965)⁵³
- Intake: about 20,000–35,000 a year in 1957–63, 40,000–70,000 in 1964–67, 60,000–90,000 in 1968–69 
- 1957–63 recruits still serving on 1 Jan 1964: about 60,000–100,000, a third to a half of the territorial forces³⁶
- Cadre: about 300–600 Vietnamese drill sergeants; 180–320 US military spaces in RVN³⁷
