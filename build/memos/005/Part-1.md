# Memorandum 005 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


The order is that every B-52 in the inventory becomes a B-52J, and that the bombers come with three families of weapon: the 2,000 lb and 500 lb Joint Direct Attack Munitions, and the Quickstrike mines. I treat them as four provisions, because they are four different instruments and they will be used against four different parts of this war. The bomber is the truck. The GBU-31 is the weapon for the bridge, the ferry and the bunker. The GBU-38 is the weapon for the perimeter at night. The mine is the weapon for the river.

One judgment runs under all four dossiers, and I state it before the specifications so that nothing below inflates it. Each of these weapons turns a coordinate into a crater with an accuracy this theater has never seen. None of them produces the coordinate. The war's acute problem is finding the enemy, and on that problem this provision does nothing by itself. Where the coordinate is good, it changes what a bomber can do more than anything since the bomber was built. Where the coordinate is a guess, it places a very precise bomb on the guess.

## §1.1 Boeing B-52J Stratofortress (re-engined and re-radared B-52H, programme-of-record configuration) — P005a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**The B-52J does not yet exist as a fielded aircraft, and I write this dossier knowing that.** It is the Air Force's designation for the B-52H after two programmes, and both are still in development in the provision's 2026 era. The first programme is the Commercial Engine Replacement Program (CERP). It replaces the eight Pratt & Whitney TF33-P-3/103 turbofans with eight Rolls-Royce F130s, and it passed Milestone B into engineering and manufacturing development on 1 June 2026. The second is the Radar Modernization Program (RMP). It replaces the 1980s AN/APQ-166 with the Raytheon AN/APQ-188 active electronically scanned array, and it passed Milestone C into low-rate production on 21 August 2026. The Government Accountability Office puts the re-engined bomber's initial operational capability in the middle of fiscal year 2033, about three years later than earlier estimates¹. Two test aircraft are being modified at Boeing's San Antonio facility². The airframe beneath both programmes is the B-52H, built by Boeing at Wichita and first delivered in 1961³.

What I command, then, is a programme of record and not a measured aircraft. Every figure below for the J is one of three things: a published B-52H figure, which the J inherits unless a programme changes it; a published programme claim, such as the F130's fuel efficiency; or my estimate, tagged as such. No one has flown a B-52J on operations, and I will not write as if anyone had.

The configuration I assume is the full programme of record: F130 engines in new Boeing nacelles and inlets, redesigned after wind-tunnel tests found inlet distortion; the AN/APQ-188 radar⁴; upgraded hydraulics, generators, electrical power and digital cockpit displays; the 1760 Internal Weapons Bay Upgrade, which lets the bomb bay talk to GPS weapons⁵; extremely high frequency and hybrid satellite communications²; the H's electro-optical viewing system with forward-looking infrared and low-light television; and the H's defensive avionics, flown by an electronic warfare officer⁶. I also assume the targeting pod the H fleet carries in 2026, an AN/AAQ-28 Litening or AN/AAQ-33 Sniper on a wing pylon⁷.

**Quantity.** The provision is one for one⁸. In 1964 that means every B-52 in Strategic Air Command, about 600 airframes⁹. Most of that force stays where it was, on nuclear alert in the continental United States under the SIOP, and it touches this war only as the reserve from which Arc Light draws. The force committed to Southeast Asia follows the historical deployment unless a later part of this memorandum argues otherwise:

- 30 aircraft on Guam for the first strike of 18 June 1965;
- about 50–70 through 1966 and 1967¹⁰;
- about 90–100 from early 1968¹¹;
- about 40 at U-Tapao at the end of 1971, and nearly 50 more on Guam¹²;
- 210 in East Asia at the 1972 peak under Bullet Shot, 155 of them at Andersen¹³.

**Organisation.** The historical organisation stands. The 3rd Air Division on Guam controls Arc Light, the 4133rd Bombardment Wing (Provisional) and later the 43rd and 72nd Strategic Wings fly from Andersen, and the 4258th and later the 307th Strategic Wing fly from U-Tapao in Thailand from 1967, with Kadena on Okinawa used from 1968. The basic tactical unit remains the three-ship cell¹³. What changes inside that organisation is the weapons-planning cell, because a GPS weapon needs a coordinate, and the coordinate needs a cell in Saigon that can produce it. I account for that cell under the GBU-31 (§1.2 D2).

**Arrival.** The type replaces the inventory on 1 January 1964¹⁴. Its first employment in this theater follows the historical decision to commit B-52s to conventional bombing in the South, taken in the first half of 1965, unless a later part of this memorandum argues that the J changes the decision. A bomber in the inventory on 1 January 1964 is information. It does not bomb anything until Washington says it may.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Crew of five.** The B-52H carries an aircraft commander, a pilot, a radar navigator, a navigator and an electronic warfare officer, on six ejection seats⁶. The B-52D of Arc Light carried six, the sixth being the tail gunner with his four.50 calibre guns. The J has no gunner, because the H's tail turret was removed from the fleet long before 2026¹⁵. Every J sortie therefore puts one fewer man at risk than a D sortie. Across the roughly 126,000 Arc Light sorties of the historical war¹³, that is about 126,000 fewer crew-sorties exposed.

**Ground complement.** A deployed B-52 wing historically carried several thousand men for maintenance, munitions, fuel, security and support at Andersen and U-Tapao¹⁶. The J changes the engine shop most of all. The TF33 was a maintenance burden with regular off-wing overhaul. The F130 is expected to need far fewer maintenance hours and no off-wing overhaul before the bomber retires¹⁷. I estimate the J force's maintenance manpower at about 10–20% below the historical B-52 force for the same number of aircraft¹⁸. The J adds a smaller need for avionics and software technicians who did not exist in 1964, and for contractor field engineers on the F130, the AN/APQ-188 and the mission-planning systems¹⁹. These are 2026 skills in a 1964 Air Force, and they must come with the provision under A2's modern-maintenance rule.

**Personnel spaces against the ceiling.** CONFIG sets no troop ceiling. Even so, the bomber force consumes almost no spaces inside the Republic of Vietnam, because Arc Light flies from Guam, Thailand and Okinawa¹³. In-country the J needs an enlarged Arc Light liaison and coordinate cell at MACV and Seventh Air Force of about 40²⁰, counted in the manifest against this provision. The spaces that matter are outside the ceiling: they are in Thailand, where every American at U-Tapao is a political fact in Bangkok, and on Guam, where they cost nothing politically and only money.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**The J carries about 70,000 lb of mixed ordnance, including bombs, mines and missiles⁶.** What matters in this war is how that weight is divided into weapons, because the D it replaces was built to divide it into as many bombs as possible.

The B-52D after the "Big Belly" modification carried 84 bombs in the bomb bay and 24 more on the wing pylons. With 500 lb Mk 82s inside and 750 lb M117s outside, that is about 108 bombs and about 60,000 lb. A cell of three Ds laid about 324 bombs across a target box about 1.2 miles long and 0.6 miles wide¹³. That was Arc Light: a carpet, aimed at an area.

The J cannot be a Big Belly. Its bay was never modified that way, and its stations are built for fewer, heavier and smarter weapons. Its loads, by the weapons of this provision and of the historical stocks, are:

- **GBU-31 JDAM (2,000 lb):** 20 per aircraft, 8 internal on the 1760 Internal Weapons Bay Upgrade rotary launcher and 12 external on the two heavy stores adapter beams⁵. That is about 40,700 lb of guided bombs, each aimed independently at its own coordinate.
- **GBU-38 JDAM (500 lb):** I estimate up to 30–40 per aircraft²¹.
- **Mk 82 and M117 unguided bombs:** about 51 Mk 82s, 27 internal and 24 external²². That is less than half the Big Belly's count.
- **Quickstrike mines:** Mk 62s on the same stations as the Mk 82²³; Mk 64 and Mk 65 on the 2,000 lb stations²⁴; Quickstrike-ER with 12 stores per aircraft once certification is complete²⁵.
- **Not part of this provision:** the 2026 B-52H also carries the AGM-158 JASSM and LRASM and the AGM-86 cruise missile²⁶. No order supplies them, so they are absent from theater.

**What this means for Arc Light.** A cell of three Js on a box with unguided bombs puts down about 150 bombs where a cell of Ds put down 324. A cell of three Js with GBU-31s puts down 60 bombs, each on its own coordinate within a few metres²⁶. The J is worse at the carpet and incomparably better at the point. Which of those two the war needs is the question for §2, and I do not prejudge it here.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**The J's sensors aim its own weapons. They do not find the enemy.** The bomber carries four sensors that matter here, and each must be judged against canopy, night and the monsoon.

**AN/APQ-188 AESA radar.** The radar is derived directly from the AN/APG-79 of the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet. An AESA of that family should give the J high-resolution synthetic-aperture mapping and probably a secondary ground moving-target indication mode, but the published record presents these as possibilities, not confirmed capabilities. It is installed angled downward, which limits its ability to look up⁴. Against this war's targets it maps through cloud and rain without degradation of any consequence²⁷. It can see a bridge, a ferry landing, a river junction, a truck park in the open and a fortified hilltop, and it can measure their positions relative to the aircraft's GPS-known position. It cannot see through triple canopy. A truck park under the forest, a bunker complex in the Boi Loi woods, a regiment in its assembly area: none of these is a radar target²⁸.

**Electro-optical viewing system and targeting pod.** The H's EVS combines a platinum silicide forward-looking infrared with high-resolution low-light television, for targeting, damage assessment and flight safety⁶. The targeting pod I assume adds a stabilised high-magnification FLIR and daylight camera with a laser rangefinder²⁹. At night the pod sees a truck on an open road from bombing altitude, and it can generate a coordinate for that truck³⁰. It cannot see through cloud, and the monsoon over the panhandle and Laos is cloud for months³¹. It cannot see through canopy at all.

**Navigation.** The J knows where it is to within a few metres, at every moment, from GPS-aided inertial navigation⁶³². The D of 1965 knew where it was from dead reckoning, astro-navigation and its radar, and in the South it was usually directed by a ground radar site under Combat Skyspot rather than navigating to the target itself¹³³³. This one change, the bomber knowing exactly where it is, is the foundation of everything the J does differently.

**Defensive sensors.** The EWO's suite warns of radar-guided threats, the SA-2's Fan Song tracking radar and the fire-control radars of radar-laid AAA, and drives the jammers³⁴.

**Which half of find-and-fix.** Neither. The J's sensors serve its own weapons. It is a striker, not a finder. Its radar may add something to the search for vehicles on open roads at night, but the Global Hawks and the U-2S already do that work from above³⁵, and they do it continuously, which a bomber on a fixed mission does not.

### D5 · Performance

**Speed and altitude.** The H's cruise speed is about 650 mph, Mach 0.86, and its service ceiling is 50,000 ft⁶. The F130 changes neither thrust nor speed¹⁷. Arc Light bombed from about 30,000–35,000 ft³⁶, above the reach of 37mm and 57mm AAA, and the J will bomb from there or higher.

**Range.** The H's unrefuelled range is 8,800 miles. The F130 is about 30% more fuel-efficient than the TF33¹⁷. No figure for the J's range has been published. I estimate its unrefuelled range at about 10,500–12,000 miles³⁷. A figure for the H or the J with a full 70,000 lb load would be lower in both cases⁶.

**What the range buys from Guam.** A round trip from Andersen to the III Corps area and back is about 5,000–5,500 statute miles³⁸. The historical D flew this as a mission of about 12 hours with air refuelling from KC-135s³⁹. The J should fly it unrefuelled with a full conventional load and a safe reserve⁴⁰. That frees the KC-135s that Arc Light consumed. From U-Tapao, 400–700 miles from most targets in the South and the panhandle⁴¹, the J flies short missions of 2–4 hours, as the D did from 1967⁴².

**Protection.** None, in the armoured sense. The J survives by altitude, by its electronic warfare suite, and by releasing outside the envelope of the threat. Its GPS weapons glide up to 15 miles²⁶, which lets it bomb a target without overflying its point defences.

**Weights.** Empty weight is about 185,000 lb and maximum take-off weight 488,000 lb⁶, to which the J adds the weight of its new engines and nacelles, offset in part by the new engines' fuel economy⁴³.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**The J talks to satellites, to SAC, and to other aircraft of its own era. It does not talk to the force of 1965**. Its communications include extremely high frequency terminals and hybrid satellite communications², and in its 2026 fleet configuration a datalink and the cryptographic systems that go with it⁴⁴. CONFIG makes the satellites present [CONFIG conditions], so the J can receive retargeting in flight from MACV, from Seventh Air Force or from Guam.

That is a real change. A historical Arc Light cell flew to coordinates fixed before take-off, and changing its target in flight meant a voice message through a chain of headquarters⁴⁵. The J can take a new set of coordinates by satellite and load them into twenty GPS weapons in flight⁴⁶.

The J cannot talk to the 1965 force on any modern network. The historical ground commander's FM radio, the forward air controller's UHF and VHF, and the MSQ-77 Skyspot radar sites all use the era's voice and radar links⁴⁷. The J has UHF and VHF voice and can talk to a forward air controller in the clear or on the era's secure voice⁴⁸. Nothing in the 1965 force can pass the J a digital coordinate. An American or ARVN battalion in contact has a map, a compass, a radio and, in its best units, an artillery forward observer who reads a six-digit grid reference to 100 metres⁴⁹.

**Talking to ARVN.** Through American channels only. No ARVN commander in this war calls a B-52 directly. Historically ARVN requests for Arc Light went through the American adviser, the corps senior adviser and MACV, and that remains true⁵⁰. The language barrier sits at the bottom of that chain, at the adviser's radio, not at the bomber.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Basing.** The J needs a runway of about 10,000 ft or more at maximum weight⁵¹. Andersen on Guam, U-Tapao in Thailand from 1966–67, and Kadena on Okinawa all qualify¹³. Inside the Republic of Vietnam, Bien Hoa and Da Nang have long runways⁵², and I will not base B-52s there. The VC demonstrated at Bien Hoa on 1 November 1964 what a few mortars do to parked bombers, and a B-52J costs many times what a B-57 did. The bomber lives where the VC cannot reach it.

**Revetment and hardening.** Andersen needs none against the VC. U-Tapao needs perimeter security against sapper attack, which it historically received⁵³.

**Survivability over the South.** Small arms and 12.7mm machine guns reach about 3,000–6,000 ft⁵⁴. They cannot touch a bomber at 30,000 ft. Over the South the J is as invulnerable to enemy fire as the D was.

**Survivability over the Trail.** The Trail's 37mm and 57mm AAA reaches about 15,000–20,000 ft at most⁵⁵. At bombing altitude the J is above it. The threat to Arc Light over Laos came from SA-2s moved into the panhandle, and the first was fired at B-52s over the Trail in 1967 without reaching them¹². The J's warning receivers, its jammers and the 15-mile glide of its GPS weapons²⁶ let it stay outside a known SA-2 site's engagement zone for most targets⁵⁶.

**Survivability over the North.** Over the North the J faces the SA-2 and the MiG-21, and here its survivability is improved but not assured. In Linebacker II the historical force lost 15 B-52s in 729 sorties to 266 SA-2s, six of them on the third night when the routes and timings were fixed¹³. The J's defensive suite was designed against threats far more capable than the SA-2, and its GPS weapons release from standoff⁵⁷. But SA-2 sites around Hanoi engaged at slant ranges of about 25 miles⁵⁸, greater than the JDAM's 15-mile glide²⁶. A J bombing a target in the defended zone of Hanoi still flies inside the SA-2 envelope. I estimate its loss rate there at a third to a half of the historical rate⁵⁹. That is a judgment, not a measurement, and it will not be tested until Washington sends bombers north.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Fuel.** The J burns about 30% less fuel than the B-52 it replaces¹⁷. Arc Light at 1,800 sorties a month¹³ was one of the largest consumers of jet fuel in the Pacific, counting the tankers that served it⁶⁰. The J reduces both the bombers' fuel and the tankers' sorties, and the tanker sorties it frees can go to the fighter-bombers over the North. The F130 is a commercial-derived engine burning standard jet fuel⁶¹. Whether it is certified on 1964's JP-4 is not in my sources; I assume the provision supplies or certifies a usable fuel⁶².

**Spares.** The F130, the AN/APQ-188, the digital cockpit and the 1760 weapons bay have no supply chain in 1964. Rolls-Royce, Raytheon and Boeing exist, but their 2026 production lines do not. Under A2 the provision brings its own spares and maintenance. I treat that as a stipulation, and I record the dependence: the J's availability rests on a supply chain that exists only because this exercise says it does.

**Specialists.** Avionics, software, GPS-weapons and engine technicians trained to 2026 standards, about 150–250 contractor and military specialists across the deployed bases⁶³. These men do not exist in 1964's Air Force. The 1964 airmen who work beside them can learn to load bombs and change wheels in weeks, but need one to two years to learn the systems⁶⁴.

**Munitions.** The bombers' consumption is the war's largest munitions demand. Historical Arc Light dropped hundreds of thousands of tons a year at its peak⁶⁵. JDAM production cannot match that tonnage in guided kits; I come to the arithmetic under the GBU-31 (§1.2 D8).

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** All of it. Nothing about the J is supplied, maintained or understood by the RVNAF.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**None.** The B-52 was never offered to the VNAF and could not have been. A strategic bomber with a five-man crew, a 10,000 ft runway, a 2026 supply chain and a mission-planning cell is beyond any foreseeable South Vietnamese air force⁶⁶. Under Vietnamization the J leaves with the Americans. What the J does for the Republic is what the historical B-52 did: it stands behind the ARVN while the Americans are willing to send it. At An Loc and Kontum in 1972 the B-52s were the difference between holding and falling. The J does not change the fact that the Republic's survival depended on an American decision to keep the bombers coming. It may make that decision cheaper for Washington, because fewer bombers are lost and fewer crews die. That is a point about the political clock, not about the RVNAF.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**The J performs to specification when four conditions hold.** The first is a good coordinate. The second is a target that will still be at that coordinate when the bombs arrive. The third is a political authority to strike there. The fourth is a threat environment it can stay above or outside.

**Weather.** Weather barely matters. The radar sees through cloud, GPS weapons fall through it⁶⁷, and the bomber's navigation needs no ground reference⁶⁸. The monsoon that grounded the fighter-bombers over the panhandle for months does not ground the J⁶⁹. That is its first great advantage over the force it joins.

**Canopy.** Canopy decides almost everything. A target under canopy has no coordinate unless someone on the ground or a sensor below the canopy provides one. Under canopy the J is as blind as the D.

**Night.** Night is neutral for GPS weapons and the radar. It is favourable for the targeting pod's FLIR against vehicles in the open⁷⁰.

**Electronic warfare.** Against North Vietnam's 1960s electronic warfare, the J's environment is benign⁷¹. Nothing in the enemy's inventory jams GPS in this war, because GPS jamming requires equipment that did not exist and a threat that did not occur to anyone⁷².

**Radius.** Radius from base is effectively unlimited for any target in the theater⁷³.

**Sustained tempo.** Over weeks, tempo rests on maintenance, which the F130 improves¹⁷. It also rests on GPS kit supply, which is finite (§1.2 D8), and on coordinate production, which is the binding limit. Historical Arc Light flew a sustained 1,800 sorties a month from 1968¹³. The J force can fly that rate with unguided bombs. It cannot fly it with a full load of guided kits on every aircraft. Nor can MACV produce 36,000 good coordinates a month, which is 1,800 sorties at 20 weapons each.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The commonest failure will be a perfect strike on the wrong place.** The bomber will rarely fail mechanically. A GPS weapon dropped on a coordinate that was wrong, stale or mislabelled hits the wrong place with the same precision as the right one. The historical Arc Light's failures were largely of this kind. The enemy left before the bombs arrived, or had never been there in strength, as at Ben Cat on 18 June 1965. The J inherits that failure entirely.

**A partial failure.** A partial failure is a degraded weapons bus or a failed station. The aircraft can still drop unguided bombs, or GPS weapons from its working stations, and the cell continues⁷⁴.

**Loss of GPS.** A GPS failure is not a threat in this war. If it occurred, the weapons would fall back on inertial guidance with errors of about 30 m for 100 seconds of flight, with a good hand-off from the aircraft²⁶. That is still better than any bomb the historical force dropped.

**A total failure.** A total failure means a lost aircraft, to an SA-2 over the North or Laos, to an accident, or to the collision that killed eight men on the first Arc Light mission. During the window an aircraft is unavailable, the cell flies short or a spare launches⁷⁵. The force is large enough that the loss of a single aircraft is a tragedy, not a gap.

**Loss of the supply chain.** The failure mode particular to this provision is the loss of its 2026 supply chain. If F130 spares, radar modules or 1760 parts stop flowing, the J becomes a grounded aircraft, not a degraded B-52. There is no fallback to 1964 parts. I do not expect this, because A2 stipulates the supply, but I note it as the single point of failure the historical force did not have.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** The aircraft are new or newly modified, the contractor teams are present, and the crews are SAC crews trained for nuclear war at low level, now learning a digital cockpit and a conventional GPS-weapons mission⁷⁶. Mission-capable rates should be high: I estimate 75–85%⁷⁷. Crew proficiency with the new systems will be low for the first months.

**Month twelve.** Mission-capable rates hold at about 70–80%⁷⁸. The F130 removes the TF33's overhaul cycle, which historically drove the bomber's availability down under sustained tempo¹⁷⁷⁹. Crew proficiency rises steeply: by month twelve, crews have flown dozens of conventional sorties and the weapons cell has learned what makes a good coordinate.

**What declines first.** The JDAM kit stock declines first, if tempo outruns production. Then, if Arc Light is flown at historical rates, the airframes' fatigue life under heavy loads and many sorties⁸⁰. The H airframe has fatigue life into the 2050s at peacetime rates. A war rate of about 12–15 sorties per aircraft a month⁸¹ consumes that life several times faster. Over a ten-year war that matters less than it sounds, because the Air Force's own assessment gives the airframes decades².

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the B-52D "Big Belly" of 1966–72, the aircraft it chiefly replaces in Arc Light:**

| Measure | B-52D Big Belly | B-52J | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bombs a sortie, unguided | 108 | about 51⁸² | J carries about half |
| Guided 2,000 lb weapons a sortie | 0 | 20⁵ | from nothing |
| Accuracy of a weapon on a known coordinate | a few hundred metres⁸³ | 5 m or less²⁶ | about 50 times better |
| Weather | radar bombing through cloud by Skyspot¹³ | GPS weapons through cloud⁶⁷ | both all-weather; J needs no ground radar site |
| Guam to the South | about 12 hours with tankers⁸⁴ | unrefuelled⁸⁵ | frees the tankers |
| Fuel | TF33 baseline | about 30% less¹⁷ | about 30% |
| Crew at risk | 6 | 5⁶ | one fewer a sortie |

**Against the F-4 with laser-guided bombs from 1968–72**, the historical force's precision striker: the F-4 needs clear weather and a laser spot on the target, and carries two to four guided bombs⁸⁶. The J carries 20 GPS bombs⁵ in any weather. Against fixed targets the J is the better precision striker by an order of magnitude in volume. The F-4 retains one advantage: a laser spot can follow a moving target and a GPS coordinate cannot⁸⁷.

### D14 · Negative specification
The B-52J finds nothing under canopy: its AN/APQ-188 radar and targeting pod see bridges, ferries, roads and open ground, not regiments under triple canopy, and it addresses neither half of the find-and-fix problem. It is not a better carpet-bomber: it carries about 51 unguided 500 lb bombs⁸⁸ against the Big Belly B-52D's 108¹³, so an Arc Light box flown by Js with unguided bombs is half as dense. Its precision lives entirely in its GPS weapons and in the coordinates fed to them; no 1965 ground unit can pass it a digital coordinate or read a grid reference finer than about 100 m⁸⁹. It does not escape the SA-2 over Hanoi, whose engagement range of about 25 miles⁵⁸ exceeds the JDAM's 15-mile glide²⁶. It is not in service in 2026: its performance figures are the B-52H's plus programme claims²¹. It does not transfer to the VNAF. It does not lift any political constraint: where it may bomb is still decided in Washington.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the protection of weather and of distance from base, since GPS weapons fall through the monsoon and the bomber reaches any target unrefuelled from Guam. Against fixed targets, it denies the enemy safety in point defences, since GPS weapons can be released up to 15 miles outside them²⁶. It denies him the habit of reading Arc Light's intentions from the tanker rendezvous⁹⁰.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The B-52J is a better aircraft than the B-52D in almost every respect that can be measured: it burns about 30% less fuel¹⁷, flies to the South from Guam without tankers⁹¹, risks five men instead of six⁶, and carries twenty 2,000 lb GPS weapons⁵ where the D carried none. But the war Arc Light fought was a war of area bombing on targets located by intelligence hours or days old, and for that war the J is a worse carpet and an equal guess. Its real value is the GBU-31 and GBU-38 it carries, and their value depends on coordinates: on the U-2S and the Global Hawks of Memorandum 003, and on what the ground war can locate. Where Washington lets it strike fixed targets in the panhandle and the North, the J is a transformation of what one bomber can destroy in one night in any weather. In the South, against an enemy under canopy who chooses when to fight, it is a more efficient way to do what the D did.

## §1.2 GBU-31 Joint Direct Attack Munition, 2,000 lb class (GBU-31(V)1/B on the Mk 84 and GBU-31(V)3/B on the BLU-109) — P005b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**The GBU-31 is a 2,000 lb bomb with a GPS brain and four steering fins**. The Joint Direct Attack Munition is a tail kit built by Boeing, fitted to an existing bomb body. Development began in 1992, the first kits were delivered in 1997, and the weapon was deployed in 1999 and first used in combat in Operation Allied Force. The GBU-31 is the 2,000 lb member of the family, in two versions²⁶:

- the GBU-31(V)1/B on the Mk 84 general-purpose bomb, 2,036 lb and 152.7 in long;
- the GBU-31(V)3/B on the BLU-109 hard-target penetrator, 2,115 lb and 148.6 in long²⁶.

Both have a 25 in wingspan across the strakes²⁶. The Navy also lists the GBU-31(V)2/B and (V)4/B, its versions of the same pair⁶⁷. I assume a modern programmable fuze, the FMU-152 Joint Programmable Fuze or equivalent, with impact, delay and proximity options⁹⁵. This provision supplies the 2026 configuration of the kit. Kits with anti-jam GPS antennas and the latest receivers are irrelevant in a war without GPS jamming⁹⁶.

**Quantity.** The provision supplies the munitions with the bombers⁸, and A2 limits the supply to realistic production and distribution. JDAM has been produced in the hundreds of thousands. Its annual production rate has varied with demand, from a few thousand to several tens of thousands of kits a year⁹⁷. I take a production line of about 25,000–40,000 kits a year across both sizes⁹⁸, and assign about 8,000–12,000 GBU-31 kits a year to this theater⁹⁹. The bomb bodies themselves are no constraint: Mk 84s existed in quantity in 1964. BLU-109s did not exist until the 1980s¹⁰⁰, and must come with the provision.

**Organisation.** The kits are fitted to bombs at the munitions storage areas of Andersen and U-Tapao by munitions maintenance squadrons¹⁰¹. The coordinate for each weapon is produced by a weaponeering and mensuration cell (D2) and loaded into the aircraft's mission data before take-off, or sent by satellite in flight¹⁰².

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**The weapon has no crew. The coordinate does.** The GBU-31 puts its demands on two groups of men.

**Builders and loaders.** Kit build-up, testing, storage and loading at the bomber bases need about 150–250 more munitions men than the historical force had for unguided bombs¹⁰³. They serve on Guam and in Thailand, outside the Republic and outside the ceiling.

**The coordinate cell.** This is the part that counts. A GPS weapon needs a target coordinate accurate to a few metres in the same datum the GPS uses¹⁰⁴. In 1965 no one in Southeast Asia could produce one. The maps of the theater were the Army Map Service 1:50,000 series and the French surveys behind them, on local datums. Their absolute errors relative to the GPS datum ran to tens or hundreds of metres, and they were worse in the mountains where the Trail ran¹⁰⁵. A coordinate read off such a map is accurate only to the map's own error.

The cell that can do better needs three things: imagery precisely located in WGS 84, the software to measure target positions from it, and analysts trained to use it¹⁰⁶. The imagery exists in this theater only because Memorandum 003 put the U-2S and the Global Hawks over it³⁵, and their imagery is located by GPS. I estimate the cell at about 60 men at MACV J-2 and Seventh Air Force in shifts¹⁰⁷, counted against this provision. Its software and training must come with the provision. Its product is the limiting input of everything the GBU-31 does.

**Spaces.** About 60 in the Republic¹⁰⁸, and about 150–250 outside it¹⁰⁹. CONFIG sets no ceiling.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**Two warheads, one guidance system.**

- **Mk 84.** A 2,000 lb general-purpose bomb with about 945 lb of high explosive¹¹⁰. Against a steel truss bridge, a ferry landing, a petroleum storage tank or a building, it is the heaviest single blast in the Air Force's conventional inventory, apart from the 15,000 lb BLU-82¹¹¹. Its crater is about 15 m across and 10 m deep in soil¹¹².
- **BLU-109.** A 2,000 lb penetrator with a hardened steel case and about 530–550 lb of high explosive¹¹³. It penetrates about 1.8–2.4 m of reinforced concrete before detonating¹¹⁴. Against bunkers, caves and tunnel entrances it is the right weapon. Against the tunnels of Cu Chi and the Iron Triangle, dug 3–10 m deep in laterite clay¹¹⁵, it reaches the upper galleries and collapses sections. It does not reach the deepest levels unless it strikes directly above them, and no one knows where they are.

**The B-52J carries 20 of either type**⁵, about 40,700–42,300 lb.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**The GBU-31 has no seeker.** It is guided by a GPS-aided inertial navigation system, initialised by transfer alignment from the aircraft, and steered by its tail fins to a coordinate fixed before release. It sees nothing. Its performance is therefore the same under canopy, at night and in the monsoon. In each case it arrives at the coordinate within 5 m or less²⁶.

Every condition that affects the weapon therefore affects the coordinate instead:

- **Under triple canopy,** the weapon arrives exactly where it was told, through the trees. A bunker complex whose position was measured from a prisoner's sketch arrives as a crater at the sketch's position. A bunker complex measured from ground survey or from a defector's walk through it with a GPS receiver arrives as a crater on the bunker. In this war, however, no ground party has a GPS receiver (CONFIG conditions; A3a).
- **At night,** nothing changes for the weapon.
- **In the monsoon,** nothing changes. The JDAM's accuracy is the same in any weather⁶⁷. The fighter-bombers' great problem over the panhandle, months of cloud that hid every target, does not exist for this weapon.

**Which half of find-and-fix.** Neither. The GBU-31 strikes what has been found. It finds nothing.

### D5 · Performance

**Accuracy.** The circular error probable is 5 m or less with GPS, and 30 m or less for up to 100 seconds of free flight without GPS after a good hand-off from the aircraft. Initial operational testing recorded 9.6 m²⁶. The Navy gives less than 6 m in the most accurate mode⁶⁷.

**Range.** Up to 15 miles from the release point, depending on release altitude and speed²⁶.

**Release altitude.** Up to 45,000 ft and more²⁶. From a B-52J at 35,000–45,000 ft, the weapon can glide to a target well outside the envelope of the 37mm and 57mm guns defending it¹¹⁶.

**Terminal effects.** The Mk 84's blast and the BLU-109's penetration are as in D3. At impact angles of 60–90 degrees, which the kit can be programmed to achieve, the weapon strikes steeply. That suits bridge piers, cave mouths and bunkers¹¹⁷.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**None.** The weapon talks only to its aircraft, through the MIL-STD-1760 interface, before release²⁶¹¹⁸. After release it is autonomous. It cannot be retargeted, aborted or redirected in flight¹¹⁹.

**Talking to ARVN.** An ARVN unit can ask for a GBU-31 only through the American advisory and air-request chain, and only by passing a coordinate. That coordinate is a map grid reference, with the map's errors in it (D2). Nothing about this weapon is visible to ARVN except its effect.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Release conditions.** The GBU-31 must be released inside its launch acceptability region, which the aircraft computes from altitude, speed, heading and the target coordinate¹²⁰. From a B-52J this region is large: anywhere within about 15 miles of the target at high altitude²⁶.

**Survivability against the threat.** The weapon is a falling bomb. In the 1960s there is no defence against it¹²¹. Against fixed targets, the threat that matters is the threat to the bomber, set out in §1.1 D7. The GBU-31's contribution is that the bomber can release outside the range of the point defences of most targets in the South, the panhandle and Laos.

**Target classes that suit the weapon.** Bridges, including the Thanh Hoa and Paul Doumer bridges, which in the historical war fell only to laser-guided bombs in 1972. Ferry landings and fords on known crossings. Petroleum storage, power plants, rail yards, depots and truck parks in the open. Cave mouths and fortified bunker lines whose positions are known. Artillery positions north of the DMZ once they are located. Fixed SA-2 sites, as long as they remain where they were seen¹²².

**Target classes that do not suit it.** Moving vehicles and moving troops. Anything under canopy whose position is not measured. Anything that moves between the measurement and the strike.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Kits.** About 8,000–12,000 GBU-31 kits a year are assigned to this theater⁸¹. At 20 a sortie⁵, that is enough for about 400–600 full GBU-31 sorties a year, against a historical Arc Light rate of up to 1,800 sorties a month¹³. **The GBU-31 cannot equip Arc Light. It can equip a precision force inside Arc Light.** That is the most important logistic fact about it.

**Bomb bodies.** Mk 84 bodies exist in the 1964 inventory. BLU-109 bodies must come with the provision¹²³.

**Fuzes.** Programmable fuzes must come with the provision¹²⁴.

**Storage.** The kits are electronics. They need dry storage and periodic testing, which Guam and U-Tapao provide¹²⁵.

**Coordinates.** The coordinate cell (D2) needs imagery, software, computers and trained men. In 1964 all of these exist only through this provision and Memorandum 003³⁵. This cell is the true logistic tail of the weapon.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Everything, as with the bomber.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**Not under this provision.** The JDAM must be released from an aircraft that can align its inertial system and pass it a coordinate, which means a MIL-STD-1760 interface²⁶¹²⁶. No aircraft in the VNAF of 1964–75 has one. Its A-1 Skyraiders, A-37s and F-5s were built before digital weapons buses existed¹²⁷. In the 2020s interfaces have been improvised that let older aircraft release GPS weapons from pre-programmed coordinates¹²⁸, but none is part of this provision. A later provision could supply one. Without it, the GBU-31 leaves with the Americans.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**The GBU-31 performs to specification under four conditions.** There must be a good coordinate in the GPS datum, a target that has not moved, a release inside the launch acceptability region, and GPS available. The last of these is guaranteed in this war (CONFIG conditions). Weather, night and monsoon do not matter⁶⁷.

**Where performance collapses.** It collapses wherever the coordinate is poor. A coordinate read from a 1:50,000 map in the Annamite mountains may be 100–300 m off¹²⁹. The weapon then lands with a CEP of a few metres around the wrong point. Its effect falls to that of an unguided bomb dropped by a bomber that knows exactly where it is, which in this theater is still better than the historical bomber's¹³⁰. It also collapses against anything that moves, because the weapon cannot follow.

**Sustained tempo.** The weapon is not the limit on tempo. Kit production (D8) is, and above all coordinate production. A cell of about 60 can measure perhaps 100–300 good coordinates a day on fixed targets, with good imagery¹³¹. That supports perhaps 5–15 full GBU-31 sorties a day, comparable to the kit supply.

### D11 · Failure modes

**Wrong coordinate.** The dominant failure mode, as for the bomber: a perfect strike on the wrong place (§1.1 D11).

**Kit failure.** About 2–5% of weapons fail to guide and fall ballistically, landing hundreds of metres from the aim point¹³².

**Fuze failure.** A dud leaves a 2,000 lb unexploded bomb in a village or on a road, which the VC will recover and turn into mines, as they did with American duds throughout the historical war. Each dud is a gift to the enemy's engineers.

**Penetrator failure.** A BLU-109 striking at a shallow angle may skip or break up without penetrating¹³³. A BLU-109 striking laterite or rock may detonate high in the structure.

**A partial failure.** A cell's strike of 60 weapons on a bridge with three failures still drops the span¹³⁴. A coordinate error affects every weapon aimed at that coordinate; a kit failure affects one.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** The kits work to specification. The coordinate cell is new and slow. Its coordinates from map and old imagery are poor, and from U-2S and Global Hawk imagery good¹³⁵.

**Month twelve.** The kits still work to specification. JDAM kits do not wear. Stored correctly, they last years¹³⁶. The coordinate cell has built a target database and learned the enemy's habits. Its output and quality rise steeply¹³⁷.

**What declines first.** The stock of good targets declines first. The fixed targets of the South and the panhandle are few: the enemy builds few things he cannot move or replace¹³⁸. Once the bridges, the fords and the known depots have been struck, they are rebuilt, bypassed or replaced, and the coordinate cell must find new targets in a system designed to have none of permanent value. The weapon does not degrade. Its target set does.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against an unguided Mk 84 or M117 from a Skyspot-directed B-52D:** The unguided bomb lands within a few hundred metres of its aim point¹³⁹. The GBU-31 lands within 5 m²⁶. That is a margin of about 50 to 100 times in miss distance. To destroy a bridge span of about 10 m with a probability of one-half, the D's cell drops perhaps 150–300 unguided bombs¹⁴⁰. One J drops two or three GBU-31s¹⁴¹.

**Against the Paveway laser-guided bomb on the F-4 from 1968:** The laser-guided bomb is comparably accurate in clear weather, with a CEP of a few metres¹⁴². It cannot be used through cloud or smoke. In 1972 the Thanh Hoa bridge fell to laser-guided bombs after years of failure by unguided ones. With the GBU-31, the bridge could be struck from 1965 in any weather, if Washington allowed it¹⁴³.

### D14 · Negative specification
The GBU-31 has no seeker and finds nothing: it goes to a coordinate and cannot follow anything that moves. Its accuracy of 5 m or less²⁶ is relative to the coordinate given, and in 1965 the best coordinate available is a map grid with errors of 100–300 m in the mountains¹²⁹, unless the U-2S and Global Hawk imagery of Memorandum 003 is measured to produce it³⁵. It cannot equip Arc Light: about 8,000–12,000 kits a year⁸¹ support about 400–600 full sorties a year against up to 1,800 Arc Light sorties a month¹³. The BLU-109 penetrates about 1.8–2.4 m of reinforced concrete and does not reach the deep levels of tunnel systems 3–10 m down in laterite⁸². It cannot be released by any VNAF aircraft, none of which has a 1760 interface. It changes no political constraint: the bridges of the North can be struck only where Washington allows.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the protection of weather over his fixed targets, and the safety of point defences around them. It also denies him the safety of numbers: a bridge that survived hundreds of unguided bombs now falls to a handful. Fixed infrastructure with a known coordinate becomes untenable, which pushes the enemy towards infrastructure he can move, hide or rebuild overnight.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The GBU-31 makes every fixed target with a measured coordinate a target that dies in one strike, in any weather, from 1965. That changes the air war against bridges, ferries, depots, power plants and fortified positions as completely as the laser-guided bomb changed it in 1972, and seven years earlier. It does not change the war against an enemy whose valuable things are not fixed: men, trucks under trees, rice in caches and regiments in the forest. Its value in the South is limited by the coordinate, and in the North by Washington. Its supply limits it to a precision force inside Arc Light, not a replacement for it.

**Key figures — P005b**

- Weights: GBU-31(V)1/B (Mk 84) 2,036 lb, GBU-31(V)3/B (BLU-109) 2,115 lb 
- CEP 5 m or less with GPS; 30 m or less for 100 s without GPS; 9.6 m in operational test 
- Range up to 15 miles; release up to 45,000+ ft 
- B-52J load: 20 (8 internal, 12 external)⁵
- Mk 84 fill about 945 lb¹⁴⁴; BLU-109 penetrates about 1.8–2.4 m reinforced concrete⁸²
- Supply to theater about 8,000–12,000 kits a year⁸¹, enough for about 400–600 full sorties a year 
- Coordinate cell about 60 men at MACV J-2 and Seventh Air Force; 1:50,000 map coordinates in the mountains 100–300 m off¹²⁹
- Kit cost about $22,000 (FY2007)²⁶

## §1.3 GBU-38 Joint Direct Attack Munition, 500 lb class (Mk 82 or BLU-111 body) — P005c

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**The GBU-38 is the 500 lb member of the JDAM family.** It is the same Boeing tail kit of GPS-aided inertial guidance and steering fins, fitted to the Mk 82 general-purpose bomb or its insensitive-munition version, the BLU-111. The Navy also lists the BLU-126 low-collateral body. Development of the 500 lb version began in late 1999. The Navy's initial operational capability came in the second quarter of fiscal year 2005. The Air Force gives its weight as 558 lb and its length as 92.6 in, with a 14 in wingspan²⁶. The Navy gives 590 lb⁶⁷; the difference is the bomb body and fuze. I assume the same programmable fuze as for the GBU-31, with impact, delay and proximity options¹⁴⁵.

I do not assume the Laser JDAM, the GBU-54, which adds a laser seeker for moving targets⁶⁷. It is not named in the provision and is not supplied.

**Quantity.** About 15,000–25,000 kits a year for this theater¹⁴⁶. Mk 82 bodies existed in quantity in 1964. BLU-111 and BLU-126 bodies must come with the provision if wanted¹⁴⁷.

**Organisation.** As for the GBU-31: built and loaded at Andersen and U-Tapao and aimed by the coordinate cell at MACV (§1.2 D2).

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**The GBU-38 needs no men in the Republic beyond the GBU-31's coordinate cell.** It does need about 100 more loaders and kit builders at the bomber bases¹⁴⁸, outside the Republic.

The man on whom this weapon most depends is not on any of these rolls. He is the infantry officer, the forward air controller or the artillery forward observer who must give a coordinate for an enemy position near friendly troops. In 1965 he has a 1:50,000 map, a compass, a lensatic sight and his own reckoning. His coordinate is good to about 100 m on a good day in open country, and worse at night, under canopy or in contact¹⁴⁹. Nothing in this provision gives him anything better. The standing condition of the world gives the constellations to provisions built to use them, and specifically not to the historical force (CONFIG conditions; A3a).

**Spaces.** About 0 added in the Republic¹⁵⁰, and about 100 outside it¹⁵¹.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**The Mk 82 warhead.** A 500 lb general-purpose bomb with about 190 lb of high explosive¹⁵². Its blast and fragments are lethal to exposed troops out to several tens of metres and dangerous to a few hundred¹⁵³. Against entrenched troops, a direct hit or a near miss within a few metres is needed¹⁵⁴. Against bunkers with log-and-earth overhead cover of the kind the PAVN built around Khe Sanh and Dak To, it needs a direct hit with a delay fuze¹⁵⁵.

**Carriage.** About 30–40 per B-52J¹⁵⁶. A single J can therefore aim 30–40 separate 500 lb bombs at 30–40 separate coordinates in one pass¹⁵⁷.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**No seeker.** GPS-aided inertial guidance to a fixed coordinate, as for the GBU-31²⁶. Canopy, night and monsoon do not affect the weapon. They affect the coordinate.

The particular case for this weapon is the defence of a perimeter in contact at night. The enemy is in the wire, in the trenchline 200 m out, or in the treeline 400 m away¹⁵⁸. The defender knows the positions of his own bunkers exactly, because he surveyed them by day, or should have¹⁵⁹. He knows the enemy's positions only roughly, from muzzle flashes, from the sound of the assault and from the map.

For prepared fire, the weapon is excellent. A perimeter that has registered its likely approach routes, assembly areas and support-by-fire positions by survey in daylight can have those coordinates on file, and the coordinate cell can render them in the GPS datum from imagery¹⁶⁰. For fire called against an enemy who has just appeared, it is no better than the defender's estimate of where he is.

**Which half of find-and-fix.** Neither. The GBU-38 applies force to a found enemy. It is a fixing weapon in the narrow sense of punishing an enemy who has chosen to stay and fight.

### D5 · Performance

**Accuracy.** A CEP of 5 m or less with GPS²⁶.

**Range.** Up to 15 miles from release, depending on altitude and speed²⁶.

**Release altitude.** Up to 45,000 ft²⁶.

**Distance from friendly troops.** This is the figure that matters. The historical Arc Light was kept 3 km from friendly troops at first, and later about 1 km in the sieges of 1968 and 1972¹⁶¹. At An Loc and Kontum the boxes fell close to the defenders' lines. That was a deliberate acceptance of risk, because a box could fall a few hundred metres off its aim point¹⁶². A GBU-38 with a good coordinate can be placed much closer. I estimate the danger-close distance for a 500 lb bomb at about 400 m for a one-in-a-thousand chance of incapacitating a friendly soldier in the open, and much less for troops in bunkers¹⁶³. With good coordinates, GBU-38s from B-52Js can be laid inside 300–500 m of friendly troops in deliberate defence¹⁶⁴.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**None after release**, as for the GBU-31 (§1.2 D6). The coordinate reaches the J by satellite, or by the forward air controller's voice to the aircrew, who enter it by hand¹⁶⁵. A request from an ARVN perimeter passes through its American adviser, as all ARVN requests for B-52 fire did¹⁶⁶.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Release.** From a J at high altitude, at any time, in any weather, inside the launch acceptability region¹⁶⁷.

**Targets that suit it.** Assault positions, mortar and recoilless-rifle positions, trenchlines and siege works around a besieged base whose coordinates have been measured. Truck parks in the open. Bridge approaches. Infantry in the open on a known road. Cave mouths and bunkers that do not need the 2,000 lb weapon²⁶.

**Targets that do not suit it.** Anything moving. Anything whose position is known only to the nearest grid square. Anything inside a village, where the bomb's accuracy is real but the identity of the target is not.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Kits.** About 15,000–25,000 a year⁸¹. At 30–40 a sortie¹⁵⁶, that is about 400–800 full GBU-38 sorties a year. Combined with the GBU-31, the precision force can fly about 800–1,400 full guided sorties a year, against historical Arc Light rates of 800–1,800 sorties a month¹³. **In round terms, about one sortie in fifteen can be fully guided, or every sortie can carry a few guided weapons among its unguided bombs**.

**Bomb bodies, fuzes, storage.** As for the GBU-31 (§1.2 D8).

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**Not under this provision**, for the reason given under the GBU-31 (§1.2 D9). The 500 lb size would suit the VNAF's A-1 and A-37 if they had the interface¹⁶⁸. They do not, and this provision does not give it.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**The GBU-38 performs to specification against a measured coordinate**, as the GBU-31 does.

**Where it changes the war.** The case where it changes the war is the siege. Plei Me in 1965, Con Thien and Dak To in 1967, Khe Sanh in 1968, Kontum and An Loc in 1972 were battles in which the enemy closed on a fixed position. He dug siege works whose positions could be fixed by daylight observation and photography, and stayed long enough for the measurement to remain good¹⁶⁹. Against such an enemy, a J with 30–40 GBU-38s can strike 30–40 separate siege positions in one pass, in any weather, a few hundred metres from the defenders¹⁷⁰. Its effect is close to that of an artillery battalion's preplanned fire, with 500 lb bombs and no gun to supply¹⁷¹.

**Where it collapses.** It collapses in the meeting engagement, the ambush, the night attack from unregistered ground, and against any enemy who "hugs" friendly troops to stay inside the safety distance. That last tactic is the PAVN's answer to American firepower and its standing doctrine. Against that enemy the GBU-38 can be brought closer than any bomb before it, but not closer than the uncertainty of the coordinate allows.

**Sustained tempo.** The limits on tempo are kits and coordinates, as for the GBU-31.

### D11 · Failure modes

**Wrong coordinate near friendly troops.** This is the worst failure, and the GBU-38 brings it closer than any earlier bomb. At Dak To on 19 November 1967 a misdirected bomb fell on the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry and killed many of its men. A GPS weapon given a wrong coordinate near a perimeter repeats that, precisely. Mistakes in transcription, datum or the hand entry of a coordinate read over voice are the commonest source¹⁷².

**Kit failure.** About 2–5% of weapons fall ballistically¹³². Near friendly troops, the weapon that fails to guide lands hundreds of metres off. That is why danger-close distances are not reduced to the CEP.

**Duds.** Duds become the enemy's mines, as with the GBU-31.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** The kits work to specification. The coordinate procedures between ground units, forward air controllers and the bomber force are new and error-prone¹⁷³. The first fratricide is likely in the first months of use near troops¹⁷⁴.

**Month twelve.** Procedures have been written from experience, controllers trained, and perimeters surveyed in advance¹⁷⁵. The weapon's useful closeness to friendly troops grows as the procedures improve.

**What declines first.** The enemy's willingness to mass against fixed positions declines first, which is the weapon succeeding (§2 will weigh it). After it, the kit stock if tempo outruns production.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the B-52D Arc Light box in defence of a besieged base:** The box falls a few hundred metres to a kilometre from its aim point and covers about 1.2 by 0.6 miles¹³¹⁶². The GBU-38 falls within 5 m of each of 30–40 aim points²⁶¹⁷⁶. The box is better at destroying an enemy whose position is unknown within a kilometre. The GBU-38 is better at destroying an enemy whose positions are known, and can be used several times closer to the defenders¹⁷⁷.

**Against 155mm and 175mm artillery,** the historical force's close and continuous fire: Artillery fires on call in minutes, adjusts by observation, and can follow a moving enemy¹⁷⁸. The GBU-38 arrives only when a J is overhead or on call, and cannot be adjusted after release. The bomb carries about 190 lb of explosive¹⁷⁹; a 155mm shell carries about 15 lb¹⁸⁰. Against siege works, one GBU-38 is worth about a dozen 155mm rounds on the same point. Artillery remains the better weapon for the minute-to-minute fight, and the GBU-38 the better weapon for the heavy blow on a known position.

### D14 · Negative specification
The GBU-38 has no seeker and cannot follow a moving target; the Laser JDAM that can is not supplied⁶⁷. Its CEP of 5 m or less²⁶ applies to the coordinate it is given, and the 1965 infantryman, controller or forward observer can give a coordinate only to about 100 m by map¹⁸¹, because no historical ground unit has a GPS receiver (CONFIG conditions). It is not an artillery substitute for the minute-to-minute fight: it comes when a B-52J is overhead and cannot be adjusted. It does not defeat the enemy's hugging tactics; it narrows the gap he must close. Danger-close for a 500 lb bomb remains about 400 m in the open¹⁸². A wrong coordinate near a perimeter is a precise fratricide. Supply supports about 400–800 full sorties a year. It cannot be released by any VNAF aircraft.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the time and safety to dig siege works within a kilometre of a besieged base: positions he holds long enough to be measured can be struck one by one, at night and in cloud, a few hundred metres from the defenders. It narrows the safety margin his hugging tactics depend on, and it denies him safety for trucks, guns and parks in the open whose positions are known.

### D16 · Honest verdict
The GBU-38 gives the B-52 something it never had: the ability to put 30–40 separate 500 lb bombs on 30–40 known positions near friendly troops, in any weather¹⁵⁷. In the sieges of this war, at Plei Me, Con Thien, Dak To, Khe Sanh, Kontum and An Loc, where the enemy closed on a fixed position and dug in long enough to be measured, that is a large change in what the bombers can do for the men inside the wire. Elsewhere it is limited by the 1965 infantryman's map, by the enemy's choice to fight where he cannot be measured, and by supply.

- Weight 558 lb (Air Force) or 590 lb (Navy) on Mk 82/BLU-111; length 92.6 in⁶⁷
- CEP 5 m or less with GPS; range up to 15 miles; release up to 45,000 ft²⁶
- B-52J load about 30–40¹⁵⁶
- Mk 82 fill about 190 lb¹⁷⁹
- Danger-close for a 500 lb bomb about 400 m in the open¹⁸³; historical Arc Light safety distance 3 km, later about 1 km¹⁸⁴
- Supply to theater about 15,000–25,000 kits a year⁸¹; about 400–800 full sorties a year 
- Guided sorties of both sizes about 800–1,400 a year against Arc Light's 800–1,800 a month¹³
- 1965 ground coordinate by map about 100 m accuracy¹⁸¹

## §1.4 Quickstrike aircraft-laid bottom influence mines (Mk 62, Mk 63, Mk 64, Mk 65 and Quickstrike-ER, with Mk 57 and Mk 71 target detecting devices) — P005d

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**Quickstrike is a family of bottom mines made from bombs.** Each is an aircraft-laid bomb, or a purpose-built case, with an arming device in the nose and a target detecting device in the tail. It is dropped into shallow water, lies on the bottom, and explodes when a vessel's magnetic, pressure, seismic or acoustic signature meets its settings¹⁸⁵¹⁸⁶. The family entered service in 1983¹⁸⁷:

- **Mk 62:** a converted 500 lb general-purpose bomb, the Mk 82¹⁸⁶;
- **Mk 63:** a converted 1,000 lb general-purpose bomb, the Mk 83;
- **Mk 64:** a converted 2,000 lb general-purpose bomb, the Mk 84;
- **Mk 65:** a 2,000 lb class mine in a purpose-built thin-walled case, weighing 2,390 lb, 128 in long, 29 in across the fins, for depths up to 600 ft¹⁸⁸;
- **Mk 64 Mod 5 Quickstrike-ER:** the Mk 64 with the GBU-64v1 JDAM-ER wing and tail kit, for release at more than 40 miles¹⁸⁷. In 2026 it is in operational demonstration. Its fourth demonstration was off Kauai in March 2023, and certification for 12 stores on the B-52H is the step to initial operational capability²⁵.

A fourth configuration follows from the provision: **Quickstrike-J**, a Quickstrike body fitted with a JDAM tail kit, which can lay a precise field at higher altitude with less standoff than the ER¹⁸⁵. Since this provision supplies both the mines and the JDAM kits, I include it.

**Target detecting devices.** The Mk 57 is used in the Mk 63, Mk 64 and Mk 65. The Mk 71, which reached Milestone III in 1995, adds algorithms to detect, classify and localise ships, including quiet diesel-electric submarines, fast patrol boats and air-cushion vehicles. Arming delays, ship counts, activity windows, sterilisation and self-destruct can all be set¹⁸⁸¹⁸⁵. The Mk 75 safe-and-arming device and the Mk 176 lithium battery go into the Mod 3 kits of the Mk 62 and Mk 63¹⁸⁹.

**Ancestry.** The Quickstrike's direct ancestor is the Destructor of this war. The Mk 36 was a 500 lb bomb with a magnetic fuze, laid by Navy carrier aircraft by the thousand on the ferries, fords, waterways and roads of the panhandle in 1965–68. It did not stop the flow of munitions¹⁹⁰. The Quickstrike is the Destructor matured: more sensors, better discrimination, settable windows, and from altitude or standoff.

**Quantity.** About 3,000–6,000 mines a year for this theater¹⁹¹, of which a few hundred Quickstrike-ER¹⁹².

**Organisation.** Mines are assembled and preset at Guam and U-Tapao by Navy mine specialists, a Mobile Mine Assembly Group detachment¹⁹³, and laid by B-52Js or by Navy carrier aircraft. The Quickstrike Mk 62 and Mk 63, without the JDAM-ER kit, need no digital interface. Any aircraft that carries a Mk 82 or Mk 83 can lay them¹⁹⁴. That includes the Navy's A-4s, A-6s and A-7s in this war¹⁹⁵.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**The mine has no crew. The minefield has a planner.** A mining campaign needs a staff to choose the fields, set the devices, record every mine's position and settings, and track the fields' activity and sterilisation dates¹⁹⁶. That staff is part of the Navy's mine-warfare community, about 40–60 men at Guam, U-Tapao and Seventh Fleet¹⁹⁷, outside the Republic.

**Spaces.** About 0 in the Republic¹⁹⁸. CONFIG sets no ceiling.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**Explosive.** The Mk 62 carries the Mk 82's fill of about 190 lb, the Mk 63 the Mk 83's of about 445 lb, and the Mk 64 the Mk 84's of about 945 lb¹⁹⁹. The Mk 65's fill is not published in my sources¹⁸⁸. I estimate it at about 1,100 lb²⁰⁰.

**Lethality.** A bottom mine kills by the shock and bubble pulse of its explosion beneath a hull. In water of 2–10 m, which is the depth of the North's rivers, canals and coastal approaches²⁰¹, a Mk 62 breaks the back of a steel barge or a motorised lighter, and a Mk 64 or Mk 65 sinks a coaster or a large freighter²⁰².

**B-52J loads.** Mk 62s at the Mk 82 count of about 51¹⁵⁶. Mk 64 or Mk 65 on the 2,000 lb stations, at about 20²⁰³. Quickstrike-ER at 12²⁵. One J can lay a field of 20–51 mines in one pass²⁰⁴.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**The mine's sensors are its target detecting devices,** listening on the bottom for a vessel's magnetic, pressure, seismic or acoustic signature, and its programmed rules for which signatures to attack¹⁸⁹¹⁸⁶. Canopy is irrelevant to a mine in water. Night is irrelevant; a mine works in the dark as in daylight. The monsoon affects mines in three ways.

**Floods.** Rivers in flood carry silt that buries mines and currents that roll them. Both can degrade the sensors or move the mine off its recorded position²⁰⁵.

**Low water.** In the dry season, low water may leave a mine exposed on a bar, where it can be seen and removed²⁰⁶.

**Wooden craft.** Wooden sampans have almost no magnetic signature²⁰⁷. The North's waterway traffic in the panhandle was a mixture of steel barges, motorised lighters, wooden junks and sampans, and bamboo rafts²⁰⁸. A Quickstrike set to magnetic influence will fire on the steel craft and ignore the wooden. Pressure and seismic settings may detect the larger wooden junks²⁰⁹. Small sampans and rafts will pass over most settings²¹⁰. This is the mine's most important limitation in this theater, and I put it plainly in D14.

**Laotian waterways.** The rivers of the Laotian panhandle, the Xe Kong and its tributaries and the Xe Bang Fai, were used to float supplies downstream in drums and on rafts²¹¹. These have no signature a mine can detect.

**Which half of find-and-fix.** Neither. A minefield finds its own targets by their signatures, but only on the water where it lies.

### D5 · Performance

**Depth.** Standard Quickstrike mines are for depths of about 300 ft or less¹⁸⁵. The Mk 65 is for depths up to 600 ft¹⁸⁸. The waters of this war are far shallower.

**Delivery.** A standard Quickstrike is laid below about 500 ft at about 300 knots or less, with a ballute or folding-fin retarding tail. A B-52 lays it from about 1,000 ft at 500–600 km/h¹⁸⁶. Quickstrike-ER can be released more than 40 miles from the field, and at various speeds and altitudes inside its launch acceptability region²⁵. Quickstrike-J can be laid from high altitude with less standoff¹⁸⁵.

**Accuracy of laying.** Unguided Quickstrikes laid low fall within tens of metres of their intended positions²¹². Quickstrike-J and -ER are laid by GPS to within a few metres²¹³. A field laid by GPS can be recorded exactly, which matters for clearance after the war, and for not mining one's own channels.

**Persistence.** A mine lasts as long as its battery and its settings allow: weeks to months, or until its sterilisation date²¹⁴. A field must be reseeded as mines are swept, fired or expire. The historical reseeding of Haiphong's approaches in 1972 was routine¹⁹⁰.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**None.** A Quickstrike has no communication after it is laid²¹⁵. It cannot be switched off or retasked once in the water, except by the settings made before laying. This is the operational and the political weakness of mines. A field laid in a river on a Monday cannot be told on Tuesday that a ceasefire has been agreed. Every field must be laid with its sterilisation date chosen in advance²¹⁶.

**Talking to ARVN.** No role. The South Vietnamese Navy does not lay mines in the North. The VNAF could lay unguided Mk 62s from its A-1s²¹⁷, but this provision does not propose it and the political authority for any mining is American.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Waters.** The waters this provision can reach are set by CONFIG's switches. Under the modelled constraints:

- **Haiphong and the northern ports:** prohibited until May 1972 [CONFIG haiphong_mining MODELLED]. In May 1972 Navy aircraft laid Mk 52 mines in the Haiphong approaches, and over the following days thousands more were laid in the secondary harbours and waterways. Twenty-seven Sino-Soviet bloc merchant ships stayed in Haiphong for the rest of the year rather than risk the fields. Until then, Quickstrike sits unused for this, its most powerful mission.
- **The panhandle's rivers, estuaries, ferries and coastal lighterage south of the 20th parallel:** within the authorities under which the Navy historically laid Destructors there in 1965–68¹⁹⁰. Here the provision can work from the first campaign.
- **The waterways of the Laotian panhandle:** within the air authorities over Laos that allowed Steel Tiger and Arc Light strikes there. Their traffic, as D4 says, is largely undetectable by a mine.
- **Cambodian waters, Sihanoukville and the Mekong into Cambodia:** neutral waters, and no authority historically existed to mine them [CONFIG cambodia_air MODELLED]. Asking for it would be a large political request, and I note its cost in Part 2.
- **The coast of the Republic:** the enemy's seaborne infiltration came by trawler to remote beaches, and Market Time fought it. Mining our own coast would sink our own fishermen.

**Survivability of the layer.** A B-52J laying standard Quickstrikes at 500–1,000 ft¹⁸⁶ over a defended river crossing in the panhandle flies into the 37mm and 57mm and small arms. I will not do it. The J lays the field from altitude with Quickstrike-J, or from standoff with Quickstrike-ER¹⁸⁵²⁵. Navy carrier aircraft laying unguided Mk 62s at low level face the same guns the Destructor layers faced²¹⁸.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Mines.** About 3,000–6,000 a year⁸¹. The Mk 62, Mk 63 and Mk 64 use bomb bodies that exist in the 1964 inventory. Their target detecting devices, arming devices, batteries and tail kits must come with the provision²¹⁹. The Mk 65 case is a separate product and must come whole²²⁰.

**Assembly.** Mines are assembled and preset by Navy mine specialists at the bomber bases (D2)¹²⁹.

**Storage.** The detecting devices and batteries are electronics and need dry storage²²¹.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** All of it.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**Partly, in principle; not in practice.** The unguided Mk 62 and Mk 63 could be laid by VNAF A-1s at low level²²². The authority to mine the North, its rivers or anyone else's waters is not one the Republic holds independently of Washington, and after 1973 the Paris settlement made it moot. Quickstrike leaves with the Americans.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**Quickstrike performs to specification against steel-hulled vessels in shallow water,** in fields laid where the vessels must pass, and reseeded as they are swept or expire¹⁸⁸¹⁹⁰.

**Where it collapses.** Against wooden boats and floating drums (D4). In flooded rivers that bury or roll the mines (D4). Against an enemy with an organised sweeping effort, which the North developed historically for its waterways and coastal approaches²²³. And against an enemy with an alternative route, which in the panhandle was always the road.

**Sustained tempo.** The limits on tempo are the supply of detecting devices and the reseeding rate. A field in a river must be reseeded every few weeks as it is swept or fired²²⁴.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The mine that does not fire.** A sensor fault, a battery failure or a setting too narrow, and the boat passes²²⁵.

**The mine that fires on the wrong vessel.** A setting too broad, and the mine fires on a civilian craft, a fisherman, or after the war on a river ferry if its sterilisation fails²²⁶. After a ceasefire, unsterilised mines are both a hazard and a breach of the settlement. The historical Paris protocol required the United States to neutralise its mines¹⁹⁰.

**The mine that is found.** A mine exposed at low water, recovered by the enemy's engineers and studied²²⁷. The Quickstrike's 2026 detecting devices are the most sensitive technology in this provision that the enemy can physically recover intact. Soviet engineers would want them. I do not think this matters to this war, but it is a security risk.

**The field that does not matter.** A field closes a waterway that the enemy then stops using, shifting the load to roads and trucks. The field has worked, and the tonnage still arrives.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** Fields are new and their effect is large. The enemy has not yet learned where they are or how to sweep them, and boats are lost²²⁸.

**Month twelve.** The enemy has learned. He sweeps with whatever he can improvise, uses wooden craft, moves at times or by routes the field does not cover, and moves the load to the road²²⁹. The historical experience of the Destructor fields in the panhandle, which did not stop the flow¹⁹⁰, is the guide.

**What declines first.** The traffic on the mined waters declines first, which is the field working. Then the field's effect on what arrives, as the system adapts.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the Mk 36 Destructor of 1965–68:** The Destructor was a magnetic mine that fired on any sufficient magnetic signature, and it was laid at low level¹⁹⁰. The Quickstrike adds pressure, seismic and acoustic sensing, signature discrimination, ship counts and settable windows¹⁸⁹¹⁸⁶. Its Quickstrike-J and -ER versions are laid from altitude or from 40 miles away, by GPS, to within metres¹⁸⁵²⁵. Against steel craft it is a much better mine, laid by a much safer method. Against wooden craft it is about the same.

**Against the Mk 52 of the 1972 Haiphong mining:** The Mk 52 was a 1,000 lb mine laid at low level by A-6s and A-7s. The Quickstrike Mk 63 is the comparable weapon¹⁸⁷, with better sensors and, as Quickstrike-J or -ER, safer laying. In the 1972 mining the decision mattered and the weapon did not: the Mk 52 closed Haiphong, 27 ships stayed in port¹⁹⁰, and the mines were good enough. Quickstrike would have closed it equally, and from altitude.

### D14 · Negative specification
Quickstrike does not detect wooden sampans, bamboo rafts or floating drums: its magnetic setting ignores them, and pressure or seismic settings may catch only the larger wooden junks²⁰⁹. It does not close the Trail: it closes waterways, and the historical Destructor fields of 1965–68 in the panhandle did not stop the flow¹⁹⁰. It cannot be laid in Haiphong or the northern ports before May 1972, nor in Cambodian waters, under the modelled constraints. Standard Quickstrikes are laid at 500–1,000 ft¹⁸⁵¹⁸⁶, which over defended crossings means AAA; safe laying needs the Quickstrike-J or the Quickstrike-ER, which is still in demonstration in 2026²⁵. Its fields need reseeding every few weeks²³⁰ and cannot be switched off after laying. Its value against land vehicles is not established in my sources.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the use of steel-hulled craft on mined waterways and coastal approaches south of the 20th parallel, and the use of Haiphong from May 1972, without sweeping or loss. It forces him onto wooden craft, roads and trucks. It denies him safe passage at the ferries and fords where boats and barges must cross.

### D16 · Honest verdict
Quickstrike is a better Destructor and a better Mk 52. Its greatest use in this war, the closing of Haiphong, is prohibited until May 1972 by Washington, and when it comes the historical mines did the job. In the panhandle's rivers and coastal lighterage it raises the toll on the North's waterborne logistics from 1965 and forces the load onto wooden craft and trucks, but it does not reduce what arrives in the South by much, because the Trail has the road. It is a modest provision on its own and a valuable one only if Washington lets it be used against the ports.
