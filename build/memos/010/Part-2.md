# Memorandum 010 — Part 2 of 3 · §2 Integration and second-order effects · §3 Changes to existing forces


The NCO education completes something. Memorandum 009 trained every private since 1957; memorandum 007 educated every lieutenant and captain since 1957 and chose the battalion commanders by board¹. Between them sat the man who leads the private and carries out the lieutenant's plan, and the record has been saying for two memoranda that he was the gap. The chronicle of 1967 closes on an ARVN "trained to the man and as short of squad leaders as history's", and the chronicle of 1968 on squads "that have no sergeants"². This grant fills the gap in quality everywhere and in numbers partly, and it reaches the hamlet post as well as the battalion, because the Popular Forces platoon at night is led by a sergeant (E03). What follows is the work of finding where that matters and where it does not.

## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

The provision joins four systems and makes one newly viable.

**FIX.** Its main home. In a war where the enemy decides whether to fight, the fights he accepts are mostly small and mostly at night, and they are decided in the first minutes by the man in charge of the squad or the platoon. Both provisions improve that man. P010a improves the American squad leader, who was already the better-trained of the two; P010b improves the ARVN squad leader, who was historically the army's thinnest layer³.

**SECURE.** Under E03 the provision reaches the territorial platoon. The Popular Forces platoon in its post is about 35 men led by a sergeant or sergeant first class, with three squad leaders under him²; historically its leadership training ran up to 60% behind programme in 1964 and its quotas were never filled³. Every one of those men now holds the course for his grade. The post's night — its listening posts, its fire plan, its call for help and its discipline until the reaction arrives — is the platoon leader's work.

**ADVISE.** The provision opens a second channel of advice beneath the officers. The American NCO adviser and the Vietnamese NCO now share a curriculum, and the senior courses teach the Vietnamese sergeants to manage training, maintenance and readiness, which is the substance of self-sufficiency below the battalion staff.

**SUSTAIN.** In the American force it is a cost: instructors and men in school. In the Vietnamese force the senior courses make the supply and maintenance sergeants better at keeping the army running without American contractors.

**Newly viable: the acting squad leader.** Because the RVNAF Basic Leader Course gates the step from corporal first class to sergeant (§1.2 D1), every corporal first class who has passed it is a schooled squad leader before he holds the rank. That makes it possible, for the first time, to fill an empty sergeant's slot with a man who has been taught the job. It is the provision's answer to the numbers half of the NCO shortage, and it is a partial one.

The provision touches FIND only at the bottom, STRIKE only through the squad leader's call for fire, and SUBVERT and INTERDICT not at all, except as the enemy's proselytizing reaches the sergeant.

**F010-1** **The chain of leadership is complete below the company for the first time.** In the ARVN regular battalion of 1964–67, the private has had ten weeks of the 2026 course², his squad leader has had the Basic and Advanced Leader Courses, his platoon sergeant the Senior Leader Course, his lieutenant the branch basic course, and his battalion commander was chosen by a board¹. Each layer was historically the weakest in its turn; now none is untaught. The value of the layers is not additive. A trained private under an untaught corporal loses much of his training in the first minute of a fight, because nobody tells him where to fire or when to move; an educated lieutenant whose sergeants cannot carry out his plan has no plan. The sergeant is the joint between them, and with this grant the joint holds. I judge that this provision is worth more in combination with memoranda 007 and 009 than it would be alone, and that most of what those memoranda achieved below the battalion commander was being wasted at the sergeant⁴.

**F010-2** **The post's night has a schooled leader.** At a Popular Forces post in Dinh Tuong with a carbine it can shoot⁵, a radio that stays on the air⁶ and a trained platoon², the man who decides how the night is spent now knows how to spend it. He puts out the listening post at 300 metres on the canal path, not 50 metres inside the wire; he sites the M249 or the BAR to cover the dike approach; he keeps a third of the platoon awake on a roster; and when the 60mm rounds start falling he holds his fire for the wire instead of emptying the magazines into the dark. The record has already shown the post's first ten minutes made a fight by the trained platoon and its first call made a call for fire by the radio. This finding adds the man who orders both. It does not add the reaction force or put the platoon in the hamlet instead of behind its wire, and POSTNA-2 still governs: night control needs security resident in the hamlet with a reaction that arrives.

**F010-3** **Advice gets a second channel, sergeant to sergeant.** The American adviser has always been an officer advising an officer, with his NCOs teaching weapons on the side. With P010a and P010b, an American sergeant first class on a battalion advisory team and the ARVN battalion's senior *thượng sĩ nhất* have studied the same troop-leading procedures, the same training schedule and the same counselling form. The adviser need not persuade the commander that the battalion needs night training; he can work with the senior NCO who runs the training. This raises the advisory effort's reach without raising its authority, and it reaches below the officer whose face is at stake in accepting advice. It is moderate in weight, and largest where P006c's selected advisers speak working Vietnamese⁷.

**F010-4** **The sergeants who keep the army running are schooled for it.** The Senior and Master Leader Courses teach a platoon sergeant and a first sergeant to run a maintenance programme, account for property, manage supply and report readiness honestly³. In the ARVN, where trucks stood dead for want of an oil change and radios for want of a battery, this matters to self-sufficiency more than any single weapon. It meets memorandum 008's rebuild depots and battery works from the user's side: the depot can rebuild an engine, and the motor sergeant now knows when to send it⁶. I judge it a moderate gain to self-sufficiency from 1965, felt in vehicle and radio availability rather than in battle⁴.

**F010-5** **The gate needs an enforcer, and the record has one.** A course gate in the RVNAF can be opened by an across-the-board decree or bought at the academy (§1.2 D11). Memorandum 001's Commission and its Muster Service already verify that the man on the roll exists and has had his basic course⁸²; the course number for each NCO grade goes on the same roll. Memorandum 007's promotion board, which handles officers, sets the precedent for written criteria¹. Together they make the gate as strong as the American lever behind them, which is real until 1969–70 and lapses with the drawdown. The gate's wartime strength is therefore borrowed, and it will fail at the same time and by the same mechanism as the other institutions built behind the lever.

### Compound pairings
**Interactions with earlier provisions**

- P010b × P009b, P009c × P007b, P007c: the trained private, the schooled sergeant and the educated lieutenant under a board-chosen commander; the chain of leadership below the company is complete, and each layer's value depends on the one above and below it
- P010b × P009c × P008b × P004c: the Popular Forces post at night with a trained platoon, a radio on the air, a squad gun and a platoon leader schooled to site, roster and hold it
- P010a, P010b × P006c: sergeant-to-sergeant advice from the same curriculum; the selected adviser's Vietnamese reaches the battalion's senior NCO who runs its training
- P010b × P008a, P008b: the motor and signal sergeants schooled to run maintenance and report readiness meet the rebuild depot and the battery works from the user's side
- P010b × P001b × P007c: the Muster Service puts each NCO's course number on the roll and the Commission pursues bought passes and decree promotions; the gate is as strong as the lever behind them
- P010b × P007c: the educated sergeant is the strongest candidate for the field commission; the board takes the best platoon sergeants faster, and the Basic Leader Course's acting squad leaders fill behind them
- P010a × P006b: the educated sergeant trains his squad collectively for six to seven months before the battalion deploys and keeps it for the tour; the Basic Leader Course's situational exercise becomes the squad's rehearsal
- P010a × P009a: every American drill sergeant has passed the Advanced Leader Course; the 3,000–5,000 added drill sergeants and 4,000–7,000 added NCO instructors draw on the same senior NCOs
- P010b × P009b: the drill sergeants who return to the battalions as platoon sergeants hold the Senior Leader Course before they take the platoon; the drill sergeant's circle runs through the academy

The pairings that produce compound effects are the ones above. Two deserve a sentence of their own. The first is the chain in F010-1: three memoranda that each made one layer of the ARVN better have, with this one, made the layers fit, and the fit is worth more than the sum. The second is the post in F010-2: four memoranda have now equipped, trained and connected the Popular Forces platoon, and this one gives it a leader who knows what to do with all of it. In both cases the binding constraint moves above what any of them reaches.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

The provision touches only the last link. It does not find a PAVN regiment, and it does not change whether the regiment accepts battle. What it changes is what happens in the first minutes after contact, and that is where the ARVN historically lost the fights the regiment chose. When a battalion of the 7th Division on a sweep north of Cai Lay walks into a VC battalion's L-shaped ambush, the company that survives is the one whose squad leaders get their men off the road and into a base of fire, whose platoon sergeant gets the M60 into action on the flank, and whose radio operator sends a usable contact report. The educated lieutenant knows that; the schooled squad leader does it.

The American side gains less, for two reasons: the American squad leader was already better schooled than his ARVN counterpart, and the American fights the enemy accepted were mostly decided by fire support arriving in minutes, which the squad leader calls but does not own. The gain is real in the patrol base, the night ambush and the meeting engagement in the canopy, where a lost squad or a squad that loses its fire control is the cost.

Neither provision changes which fights happen. The enemy's control of engagement stays where the record has it.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

Nothing direct. The VCI is identified by the Special Branch, the census and the district centre⁹⁷, not by a squad leader. There is one indirect effect. The squad leader schooled in the 2026 course's conduct and detainee handling is the man who controls what his squad does to the people of a hamlet it passes through. A squad that does not loot, does not beat the old man who will not say where his son is, and delivers a detainee alive to the district centre makes the Special Branch's work easier and the Front's propaganda harder. That is small and real, and it is the same effect as memorandum 009's conduct block, one level up.

### Dependence on SECURE

Everything this provision does in the countryside depends on security already present. The schooled PF platoon leader matters in a hamlet his platoon holds and a district where the reaction comes. Outside the held belt he leads a platoon in an outpost that the Front can ignore, isolate or overrun at its choosing, and his schooling changes how long it holds, not whether the hamlet outside the wire is the government's.

The effect runs differently in the four regions the record now separates:

- **The Delta.** The provision's largest effect. The Delta's war was the PF post on the canal and the RF company on the road, and the post's night is the platoon leader's work. At Ap My Loi on the canal north of Cai Lay, where a mortar section of the 514th Local Force Battalion opened on the post in March 1967⁶, the post's sergeant is now a man who has been taught to put a listening post on the canal bank and a roster on the wall. I judge the Delta's posts lost to night attack fall by a further tenth to a fifth beyond what the platoon's training and radio achieved⁴.
- **The coastal lowlands of I and II Corps.** Smaller. In Quang Ngai, Quang Nam and northern Binh Dinh the enemy was the PAVN and VC main force in the peopled lowlands, and the posts fell to battalions, not to mortar sections. A schooled sergeant holds a post against a company longer; he does not hold it against a regiment. The mine, which the enemy turned to in the lowlands in 1966–67, is taught in the Basic Leader Course's lanes³ and is not defeated by it.
- **The Highlands and their peoples.** Least. The Montagnards fought mostly in the CIDG, which is not RVNAF and is outside the grant, and the course is taught in Vietnamese, which a Rhade or Jarai territorial sergeant may not read. Where Montagnard RF companies exist in Darlac and Pleiku, their sergeants take a course in a language that is not theirs, at a centre staffed by Kinh, and FULRO reads that as it read the rest of the Republic's institutions. I judge the effect there small and in part negative.
- **The Saigon belt.** Real and visible. The RF companies around the capital fought the Front's local forces in Hau Nghia, Long An and Gia Dinh, and their sergeants are now schooled; in Tet the RF companies that held their compound walls in the district towns did so on their sergeants' fire discipline². The belt is also where the trained deserter carried the course to the Front's guerrillas, and a schooled sergeant who deserts there carries more.

### The ADVISE cluster

The provision feeds the ADVISE cluster more than it starves it. It feeds it in three ways: by the sergeant-to-sergeant channel, by the senior courses' work on maintenance and readiness, and by giving the advisers a counterpart layer that can carry out what the officer agrees to. It starves it in one way: the instructors, who are the best senior NCOs in a corps short of them. And it adds to the stock of institutions protected by the American lever, which means it will be captured with them when the lever lapses, judged together and not alone.

The soldier's family belongs here. The NCO is older than the private, married, and his family lives near his unit, in the dependents' quarters of the base camp or the town beside it. His pay as a sergeant first class was eaten by the inflation of 1965–68 in the same proportion as the private's, and a master sergeant with five children could not feed them on it¹⁰. He moonlights, his wife trades, and the sergeant who has been to the academy is a better manager of the platoon and no better able to buy rice. The course does not touch pay. It touches one thing that matters to the family: the counselling and leave practice the course teaches gives the platoon sergeant the habit of knowing where his men's families are, which in March 1975 was the question every soldier was asking. That is not a remedy for the dissolution of the corps; it is the sergeant who will know why his men are leaving.

On rice and the piaster: the provision's only direct effect is on the soldier's conduct in the hamlet, where a disciplined squad does not take rice, chickens or pigs from the farmers it passes. That is a small gain to the government's procurement and a small loss to the Front's argument. Its effect on the NCO's pay is nil.

### Employment concepts

#### EC010-1 · The leader's cycle at Dong De

The RVNAF Basic Leader Course runs as a six-week cycle at the National NCO Academy at Nha Trang and at the division NCO schools. A class of about 150 corporals first class reports on a Monday from the division's units, by truck from the division base or by C-123 from the more distant ones; it is organised into platoons of about 30 under a Vietnamese instructor who has been a platoon sergeant, with an American NCO adviser to each two or three platoons. Weeks one to three are classroom and practical work: troop-leading procedures, the five-paragraph order, the after-action review, counselling, the squad's weapons on the academy ranges (the M1 carbine or M16A1, the BAR or M249, the M60, the M79 and the M18A1 Claymore), the AN/PRC-10 and AN/PRC-25 and the contact report. Weeks four and five are the field: leader lanes on the individual tasks, land navigation by day and night in the hills west of the city with the AN/PVS-2 Starlight scope where the division has them, and a patrol base exercise. Week six is the situational exercise, in which each student leads a squad through an order, a movement and an ambush, graded by the instructors, and the evaluation goes into his file before the promotion board. The academy runs about three classes in parallel at the 1970 capacity of about 4,500³, so it graduates about 450 a week, about 20,000 a year, with the division schools carrying as much again¹¹. The minimum viable package is one experienced Vietnamese instructor for each 12–16 students and a range; without the American adviser the course runs, more slowly to correct. Without the land-navigation area, the course becomes a classroom and loses its most valuable two weeks.

#### EC010-2 · The platoon leader's course at the province centre

Under E03, the Popular Forces platoon leader takes the Advanced Leader Course before he takes the platoon. It runs four weeks at the regional RF/PF training centres, for classes of about 60 sergeants from one province or two, so that the sergeants who will reinforce each other at night have trained together. Week one is the platoon's night defence: siting the post, the listening post and the roster, the fire plan on the wire and the dike approaches, the BAR or M249 and the M2 60mm mortar where the post has one. Week two is the call: the AN/PRC-25 or the province-made hamlet radio⁶, the call for fire on a grid fixed by map and compass to the district's 105mm M101A1 section, and the report that brings the reaction. Week three is the platoon's day: the patrol, the ambush on the canal path, the search of a house with the family present and the handover of a detainee to the police post. Week four is the exercise, at night, against an instructor force playing a VC local-force company. The cycle produces about 1,500–2,500 platoon leaders a year from 1964 across the centres¹². It depends on P009c's trained platoon and P008b's radio; without them the sergeant knows what to do and has nothing to do it with.

#### EC010-3 · The acting squad leader

An ARVN rifle squad with no sergeant is commanded by its senior corporal. Historically that corporal had nothing beyond basic training. Under the grant, the corporal first class who has passed the Basic Leader Course is a schooled squad leader awaiting the stripe, and the battalion's personnel officer assigns him to the empty slot with the title of acting squad leader. On an operation he leads the squad as a sergeant would; on the next promotion board, his performance as acting leader is the strongest evidence in his file. This is the concept that turns a quality provision into a partial answer to the numbers shortage. In the mobilisation of 1968, when the field commissions had taken about 5,500 of the army's best sergeants² and the new territorial platoons were short of men who had held the rank, about two in three of the empty squad leader slots in the regular battalions can be filled by a schooled acting leader (on the basis of the share of corporals first class holding the course after four years of the gate, against the slots the commissions and the expansion emptied)⁴. Without the gate, the concept is simply the historical one: the senior man, untaught.

#### EC010-4 · Sergeant to sergeant

The American sergeant first class on a battalion advisory team, a graduate of the Senior Leader Course and, under P006c, of the adviser's course and 24 weeks of Vietnamese⁷, spends the first month of his tour with the battalion's senior NCO. Together they write the battalion's training schedule for the coming quarter on the format both learned, set the standard for each company's night defensive position, and inspect the motor pool's maintenance log. On operations the adviser goes with the company whose first sergeant asked for him. The concept's minimum is an American NCO adviser at each battalion and a Vietnamese senior NCO who holds the Master Leader Course; without P006c's language it degrades to the same work through an interpreter, about half as effective¹³, and without P010b's counterpart it degrades to the American teaching a man who has no frame for what he says.

#### EC010-5 · The academy at Bien Hoa

An American soldier promoted to sergeant in Vietnam must pass the Basic Leader Course before he can become a staff sergeant. MACV runs it in theater at an NCO academy at Bien Hoa, in classes of about 150, four to six in parallel, on the full 29-day schedule³, with land navigation in the controlled ground of the Long Binh complex. A sergeant is released by his battalion between operations, flies in by C-7A Caribou or drives from his base camp, and returns a month later. The academy graduates about 600–900 a month¹⁴. In 1967–68 that is short of the demand, and the battalions with the heaviest losses wait two to three months for places; the concept settles on a priority for rifle companies¹⁵. With P006b's unit rotation, the battalion sends its sergeants to the academy during the training months before deployment rather than in theater, and the Bien Hoa academy serves mainly battle-loss replacements and men promoted in the field.

#### EC010-6 · The returning instructor

From mid-1966 every American NCO academy in the United States takes a share of its instructors from men returning from a tour in Vietnam, on a one-year assignment before their next unit¹⁶. They bring the war into the case studies the course teaches by³: the patrol base at night in War Zone C, the booby-trapped trail in Quang Nam, the dustoff in the monsoon. The Advanced and Senior Leader Courses of 1967 are teaching this war, not the European one. The concept costs the units the instructor for a year and gives the next class's sergeants a year's lead on their predecessors. It is the course's best information input (§1.1 D4), and it loops: the instructor of 1967 trained the squad leaders of 1968.

#### EC010-7 · The readiness report

Under the Master Leader Course, each ARVN battalion's senior NCO reports the battalion's vehicles, radios and weapons by serviceability each month to the regiment, on a form designed so that the man who fills it in is not the man rated on it¹⁷. The report is checked against the rebuild depot's intake⁶ and, from 1965, sampled by the Commission⁸. Its value is in the honest picture of the army's equipment. Its cost is that the honest picture is unwelcome to the commander, and the senior NCO who files it needs the commander's protection or the Commission's. It is the course turned into a metric, and A13's warning applies to it as to every metric: it will be gamed where the commander wants it gamed.

#### EC010-8 · The course in the battalion's lull (abandoned)

The first plan for the division schools, in 1964, was to teach the Basic Leader Course at each battalion's base camp between operations, so that no man left his unit. It was abandoned within a year. Battalions recalled their students on the first alert, classes were cut short in their fourth week and certified as complete, and the division commanders used the courses as a reward for the men they wanted to promote. The concept named a constraint the dossier had stated only in passing: a course the unit controls is a course the unit can cancel. The courses moved to the division schools and the academy, under the training command, with the gate counting only a course completed there.

#### EC010-9 · The staff sergeant's waiver (abandoned)

In 1966, with the American Army short of staff sergeants and the Bien Hoa academy full, MACV asked for authority to waive the Basic Leader Course for sergeants in rifle companies in contact. The Army granted it for six months, and the waivers reached about one in four promotions in the infantry battalions of II Field Force by the end of the period¹⁸. The concept was abandoned in favour of a priority at Bien Hoa and a deferral: a sergeant may be promoted to staff sergeant and must complete the course within 90 days or lose the stripe. The deferral worked where the academy had places and became a second waiver where it did not.

#### EC010-10 · The American instructor at Dong De (abandoned)

For the first classes after the reorganisation, MAAG proposed that American NCOs teach the Basic Leader Course at Nha Trang through interpreters until a Vietnamese faculty was ready. It was tried with one class and abandoned for the reason memorandum 009 abandoned the American drill sergeant: a Vietnamese sergeant taught by a foreigner through an interpreter learns that leadership is an American matter, and the Vietnamese instructors lose face before the men they will later command. American NCOs became advisers to the Vietnamese faculty, not instructors.

#### EC010-11 · The battalion sergeant major (abandoned)

The 2026 American model gives each battalion a command sergeant major who advises the commander directly on the soldiers and their training, a channel the United States Army created only in 1967³. MACV proposed in 1964 that the senior NCO course's graduates fill such a post in each ARVN battalion. The Joint General Staff accepted the course and refused the post. Battalion commanders would not accept an NCO with a direct line to them on the soldiers' welfare, which they read as a channel for complaint and a check on their control of rations and leave, and several corps commanders read it as an American informer in every battalion. The senior NCO course ran; its graduates served as the battalion's senior sergeant with the authority the commander chose to give them. The abandoned concept names the limit on the whole provision: the sergeant's authority is what the officer allows.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

The enemy does not stop fighting the squad that is better led; he fights it less, and fights something else more.

**F010-6** **The enemy moves further to the targets the grant does not reach.** The record already has the enemy moving to forces outside memorandum 009's grant — CIDG, police posts, hamlet militia and, from 1968, the People's Self-Defense Force — and to mines and stand-off fire. This provision narrows the list by including the RF and PF, so the territorial post with a schooled sergeant drops lower on the Front's list and the CIDG camp and the self-defence group rise. In the Delta that means the Hoa Hao and Cao Dai militia villages and the PSDF hamlets of 1968–70; in the Highlands it means the CIDG camps. The enemy also moves up: to the company and battalion level, where the decision to relieve a post or to drive down a road is an officer's, and where an ambush on the relief force catches men the sergeant does not command. I judge the displacement moderate. It is the familiar pattern of every provision that improves one class of target in this war.

**F010-7** **The enemy's proselytizing aims at the sergeant.** COSVN's military proselytizing sections had worked on ARVN soldiers through their families and their grievances for years. A schooled NCO is a more valuable recruit or informant than a private: he knows the post's roster and fire plan, the battalion's training schedule and, as an acting squad leader or platoon sergeant, the movement order. From 1966 the B-2 Front's proselytizing cadres in the Delta give the sergeant priority over the private, approaching him through his wife's market stall and his family's village. Their yield is small, because the sergeant has more to lose and a career the gate has made visible, but each success compromises a post's or a platoon's routine. Minor to moderate.

### Bottleneck migration

**F010-8** **The ARVN's binding constraint moves from the sergeant to the sergeant's officers and the battalion's collective training.** Before the grant, the record named the squad leader as the ARVN's constraint once the private was trained. With the squad leader schooled and acting leaders filling empty slots, the constraint moves in two directions. Upward, it moves to what the officer lets the sergeant do and to the command above the battalion, which memorandum 007 left with the palace. Sideways, it moves to the battalion's collective training: the squad leader knows his drills, and the battalion still does not train as a battalion, because nobody in this grant or the last two gave it the time, the ranges or the doctrine to do so. The abandoned battalion sergeant major is the clearest sign of the upward limit. This is the memorandum's most useful output and I state it at full weight: the ARVN below the company is now led as well as the American Army of 2026 teaches, and above the company commander nothing has changed that this memorandum reaches.

**F010-9** **The territorial post's constraint moves to the reaction, the district chief and the family.** With a trained platoon, a radio and a schooled leader, the post holds longer and calls better. What decides whether it holds the night is whether the call brings fire or men in time, which is the district chief's 105mm section and the RF company on the road, and whether the platoon's families, who live in the hamlet beside the post, are safe enough that the platoon stays. The schooled sergeant can hold his men to their posts for an hour after the attack begins; he cannot hold them if their families' houses are burning on the other side of the canal. Moderate.

**F010-10** **The American constraint moves to the number of sergeants and the 12-month tour.** The American squad leader was competent before the grant and is more competent after it. What remained short in 1966–69 was the number of experienced sergeants, emptied by the tour, the separations of senior NCOs and the absence of reserve mobilisation³; and from 1969 the discipline of the conscript army in a war it had stopped believing in. The grant touches neither. With unit rotation the tour's effect is softened inside the battalion's year⁷; across years it is not. Moderate.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F010-11** **The best Vietnamese sergeants teach, three ways at once in 1964–66.** The RVNAF's best senior NCOs are now drawn on by three programmes in the same years: memorandum 009's drill sergeants, about 2,000–3,000; this grant's instructors, about 1,300–2,500 before 1964 and 3,500–6,000 at the peak with the territorial courses (§1.2 D2, E03); and memorandum 007's field commissions, which took about 600 in 1966 and about 5,500 by the end of 1968¹². In 1964–65, when the battalions were being broken at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, about 5,000–8,000 of the army's best NCOs were teaching or in officer school rather than in a platoon (on the basis of the sum of the three programmes' 1964–65 demand)⁴. That is a real cost and I do not wave it away. It is smaller than the gain, because the NCOs who remain in the platoons have all passed the courses for their grades, and because the instructors return to units: the drill sergeant's circle now runs through the academy, and from 1966 the instructors coming back hold the Senior Leader Course. But in the two years the war was nearest to being lost in 1964–65, the price of building the army's spine was a thinner spine in the battalions that were fighting.

**F010-12** **The American NCO corps pays in instructors and time, in the years it was shortest.** About 4,000–7,000 added American instructors at the peak and about 2–3% of the NCO corps in school at any time (§1.1 D2), on top of memorandum 009's 3,000–5,000 added drill sergeants and memorandum 006's selected advisory corps, which draws on the same senior NCOs. In 1966–67 the Army's shortage of experienced sergeants first class and master sergeants comes about three to six months earlier than history's and is somewhat deeper¹⁹. The build-up of 1966 slips by perhaps one to two months more from this grant²⁰. I judge the cost moderate, and real in the year it falls.

**F010-13** **The gate holds promotions in a shortage.** In the American Army, no sergeant becomes a staff sergeant without the Basic Leader Course. In 1967–68, with Bien Hoa full and the deferral in force, about one in five rifle-company promotions to staff sergeant waits one to three months for a place²¹. The man does the job at his old grade while he waits; the cost is in pay and morale, not in the field. Minor.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F010-14** **The untaught NCO is erased, and with him the historical NCO programmes' late starts.** The following no longer happen as they did: the 1964 leader programme of seven and ten weeks that was "under consideration" with no final action; the 1966 three-week leadership bonus for the top tenth of each training company, which becomes the ordinary road to the academy³; the PF leadership training 60% behind programme, which the gate fills³. In the American Army, the creation of the NCO Education System in 1971 and of the Sergeants Major Academy in 1972 are erased as events, because both have existed since 1957³. The NCO Candidate Course of 1967 still happens — it answers the numbers shortage, which this grant does not — but its graduates take the Basic Leader Course's lanes as a final module (§1.1 D5), and the instant sergeant of 1968 is a schooled one. What becomes of the attention those programmes consumed is small: they were never large. Moderate in weight, because what is erased is the condition, not the programmes.

### Perverse effect

**F010-15** **The best-schooled sergeants are the first commissioned.** Memorandum 007's field commission takes the sergeants with a combat record and a commander's recommendation¹. With this grant, the board can see which sergeants passed the Advanced and Senior Leader Courses and how they did, and it takes them first. The number commissioned is set by the officer corps' demand, not by this grant, so the drain does not grow in size²². It grows in quality: the 5,500 sergeants made lieutenants by 1968 are now precisely the 5,500 best-schooled platoon sergeants in the army². The platoons lose better men than history's to the commissions, and the officers' corps gains better lieutenants. The net is positive for the army and negative for the squads in 1966–68, and acting leaders under EC010-3 fill some of the gap. Moderate.

**F010-16** **The gate becomes a toll and the course a ticket.** Where corruption reaches the academy, places and passes are sold (§1.2 D11); where the government wants the army's gratitude, a decree promotes across the board³; where the commander wants the readiness report to read well, it does. The Commission and the Muster Service catch some of this while the lever lasts. I judge that about a tenth to a fifth of the RVNAF's course passes in 1964–69 are bought or unearned, rising after the lever lapses in 1970 (on the basis of the share memorandum 009 judged false in the training centres' figures, F009-17, applied to a course whose pass carries a promotion and is therefore worth more to buy)⁴. The American gate suffers the softer form: the course becomes a ticket to punch, and the graded evaluation becomes a pass for all who attend (§1.1 D12). Moderate.

**F010-17** **The schooled territorial sergeant is drawn away from his hamlet.** A PF platoon leader who has passed the Advanced Leader Course is a man the RF, the ARVN and the police all want. Some are recruited to the regular forces at the rank they hold, and the province chief who sees an able sergeant moves him to the RF company that guards the province headquarters. The hamlet that depended on him loses him. This is memorandum 009's perverse effect one rank up, and of the same small weight. Minor.

**F010-18** **The educated American sergeant enforces standards in an army that stops accepting them.** In 1969–71, the American sergeant who has been taught to hold his squad to standards on drugs, weapons discipline and conduct is the man the squad's worst soldiers resent most, and some of the fraggings of those years were directed at exactly such sergeants. The course gives him the counselling and the judgment to handle a soldier in trouble before it comes to that, and it does not make him immune. I judge the net effect on discipline modestly positive, with some schooled sergeants dead because of what they were schooled to do. Minor.

### Anticipation

**F010-19** **The enemy declines the well-led squad, and the gain is invisible.** The Front's local forces and the PAVN's reconnaissance know within a season which posts keep listening posts out and which patrols vary their routes. They do not attack the post whose sergeant sets a roster; they attack the one beside it whose sergeant does not, or the CIDG camp, or the PSDF hamlet. They do not ambush the patrol that moves off the trail at dusk; they mine the trail it would have used. The result is the most important second-order effect of this provision, as of memoranda 007 and 009 before it: the improvement appears as attacks that do not happen and ambushes that are not laid, and no metric this headquarters keeps measures a post not attacked. The body count, which rises when the enemy attacks a well-defended post and loses, may fall when the enemy stops attacking it, and the Hamlet Evaluation System will score the quiet hamlet as it scored every quiet hamlet. I state this plainly: the provision's gain will be largest where it is least visible, and the statistics will show at most the casualty-care half of the improvement that memorandum 009 already gave. Moderate in weight, because it is the honest description of how the main gain will appear.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F010-20** **The enemy reads the schooled sergeant from his manuals.** There is no detection moment. The NCO corps of 1964 has been schooled since 1957, and the Front has fought it for seven years. What the enemy gains is the curriculum: the Basic Leader Course and its manuals are in Vietnamese, printed in thousands, and carried in sergeants' packs. The first captured copies reach COSVN's military staff in 1964²³. By 1965 the B-2 Front's training sections are using the ARVN's own troop-leading procedures and patrol-base routine to teach their cadres what an ARVN platoon will do: when it stands to, how far out its listening posts sit, how it moves to a contact. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee draws the correct conclusion — that the ARVN's small units are more competent and more predictable — and orders the local forces to study the ARVN's routines as they study the terrain. It draws one wrong conclusion: that the change is an American imposition that will last only as long as the advisers, which leads it to expect the course to collapse after the drawdown, which it does not, because the course is Vietnamese (§1.2 D9). Military Region 5 learns faster than the B-2 Front, because its war in the lowlands of Quang Ngai and Binh Dinh is fought more often against ARVN regulars²⁴. Moderate.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F010-21** **The enemy's adaptations are cheap, but beating the schooled post costs more.** Switching targets, proselytizing the sergeant, mining the trail the patrol avoids and studying the manual cost the enemy little: they are what his local forces and cadres do anyway. The expensive adaptation is the one he needs to take a well-led post at night: more reconnaissance, a sapper team instead of a mortar section, a company instead of a platoon, and a longer preparation. In the Delta in 1966–67 that means about one more night of reconnaissance and about one more sapper cell per attack on a schooled post²⁵, and a tenth to a fifth fewer attacks the local force battalion can mount in a season (on the basis of same basis)⁴. The exchange is about even on the cheap adaptations and favours the Republic on the expensive one, but only where the post and its reaction are in place. Moderate.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F010-22** **A small saving of American lives that does not reach the clock.** P010a saves American lives in the patrol, the night ambush and the meeting engagement, where a schooled squad leader keeps his squad together and calls fire well. I judge the saving at about one to three percent of American deaths in small-unit actions in 1965–71, overlapping with memorandum 007's educated lieutenants and memorandum 009's trained soldiers (on the basis of the share of small-unit deaths attributable to squad leaders' errors in navigation, fire control and patrol-base discipline, set against what the course teaches; POS007-4's rate for the lieutenant, at one to three percent, applied to the sergeant with the overlap taken out). That is perhaps 300–900 Americans (on the basis of the same rate on the historical small-unit deaths)⁴, below a band. It does not move political tolerance: the record holds that the clock was spent by the casualties of 1965–68 and by Tet. The softer effect of the late-war discipline crisis, if the schooled sergeant holds a few more squads together in 1969–71, reaches the press as fewer stories of a collapsing army; it narrows the credibility gap by little and leaves tolerance where it was. The loop returns nothing that moves a band. Minor.

### Open channel

**F010-23** **The NCO corps becomes a ladder for the rural man.** The RVNAF's officers came from the educated, urban and largely Catholic or northern class; its enlisted ranks came from the farms of the Delta and the coast²⁶. Memorandum 007's field commissions and the end of the diploma bar began to open the officer corps from below¹. This grant builds the step beneath that: a sequence of courses by which a farmer's son can rise from private to master sergeant first class on his performance, with a national academy that teaches him to lead and a promotion board that must see his course record. For the first time the RVNAF has an institution for its rural soldiers that is theirs and is national. I judge it real and slow, felt by 1968–70 in the regard in which the senior NCOs are held in the hamlets they come from, and in the number of men from the Delta and the coast who choose to re-enlist⁴. It does not reach the Montagnards, for the reason in the regional analysis above.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F010-24 · Counter-directional** **The squad leader learns the American way of war, and the ARVN's dependence on American fire reaches the squad.** The 2026 Basic Leader Course's leader lanes and situational exercise teach the squad leader to call for fire on a grid, to call the medevac helicopter and to report a contact so that fire support can be brought to bear in minutes³. That is what a 2026 American squad leader does, and in 1964–72 it is what an ARVN squad leader with an American adviser in the battalion can do, with American artillery, American helicopters and the American coordinate cell behind him²⁷⁷. The effect is to carry the dependence on American fire, which memoranda 004, 005, 006 and 007 have each deepened at their level, down to the squad. A squad leader taught that the answer to contact is to fix the enemy and call the guns fights well with the guns and less well without them. In 1972 the ARVN squad on the perimeter of An Loc calls fire it has, and the dependence is a strength; in 1974, when the ammunition ration falls hardest on the squad gun and the artillery, and in 1975, when the VNAF cannot fly and the American fire is gone, the squad leader has been taught to fight a way his army can no longer supply. I state it at its true weight: it does not reduce the squad's competence in 1964–72, which is the larger effect; it makes the squad's competence depend more on what the Americans and the 1974 appropriation provide. A Vietnamese course could have taught the squad to fight on its own fires first, and this grant, being the American course, does not. Moderate.

**F010-25 · Strongest positive** **The ARVN and the territorial forces are led at the squad and platoon by men schooled for the job, from 1964 to the end.** The record's last two memoranda trained the private and educated the officer and recorded each time that the army remained short of squad leaders and that the mobilisation of 1968 produced squads with no sergeants². This grant gives every regular and territorial NCO the course for his grade since 1957, and through the acting squad leader it fills about two in three of the empty squad slots with a schooled man⁴. Combined with memoranda 007 and 009, it completes the chain of leadership below the company. I judge three effects:

- **Battalions not broken.** In the ambushes the enemy still lays on ARVN battalions in 1964–68, about one in six to one in five fewer battalions broken beyond what memoranda 007 and 009 saved, because the squads hold their base of fire and get off the road after the officers are hit (on the basis of the NCO's share of the small-unit failures the advisers recorded, M010·S10, against the record's existing reductions in F007-18 and F009-25).
- **Posts held.** In the Delta and the Saigon belt, a further tenth to a fifth fewer PF and RF posts lost to night attack in 1965–71 than the trained platoon and the radio already achieved (on the basis of the platoon leader's share of the post's first hour, against POS008-2 and F009-2).
- **Soldiers' lives.** About 3,000–9,000 RVNAF and territorial soldiers' lives over 1964–72, in fights the enemy accepted and the squad held (on the basis of a rate of one to three percent of RVNAF killed in small-unit actions, the same rate I judged for the American sergeant in F010-22, set against an army whose NCOs historically had far less schooling)⁴. Under DIRCP01-2 this figure is a judgment on its rate and must be shown in dated events before it is repeated.

This is the largest gain to the RVNAF's fighting power at the level where most of its war was fought, and it is held in the men and in a Vietnamese institution, not in American equipment or an American lever. It survives the drawdown in a way most of this force does not. It does not reach the division and corps commands that decided 1972 and 1975, so it does not move the rung by itself; it moves what the ARVN can do below them. Major.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

The advisory effort gains about 210–420 Americans at the NCO academy, the division and service schools and the RF/PF centres (§1.2 D2, E03), and a channel of advice from sergeant to sergeant. The battalion advisory teams' NCOs now advise a counterpart who has the same frame as they do. MACV's training directorate takes on the course gate as a reporting problem: it must check the academy's pass rates and the promotion boards' respect for the gate against the Muster Service's roll and the battalions' performance, with the scepticism A13 asks of every figure produced by the organisation measured by it. MACV also runs the Bien Hoa academy for American sergeants.

### US conventional forces

Every American NCO who arrives has passed the course for his grade, and every American squad in the field is led by a man schooled in land navigation, patrolling, fire control and leader-level casualty care³. The squad is better in the patrol base, the night ambush and the meeting engagement in the canopy. Its losses to mines are unchanged in kind. The Army's experienced sergeants are spread thinner in 1966–68 by instructors, drill sergeants and advisers, and some promotions wait. The Marine Corps' corporals, who led its squads in Quang Nam and Quang Tri, have all passed the Corporals Course³. The late-war crisis of discipline is eased at the margin and not prevented.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

The Special Forces A-teams' sergeants are American NCOs and have passed the courses for their grades, including the Senior Leader Course for the team sergeants. That improves the teams' management of their camps and their training of the strikers, modestly. The CIDG strikers' own leaders are not RVNAF and are outside the grant, and the CIDG camps rise on the enemy's list. The Vietnamese Special Forces, the LLDB, are RVNAF regulars and their NCOs are schooled.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

Every regular battalion's NCOs hold the courses for their grades; empty squad slots are filled by acting leaders who hold the Basic Leader Course; the division NCO schools and the academy at Nha Trang are among the army's largest institutions. The Central Training Command enforces the gate against corps and division commanders who want promotions for their favourites, with the Muster Service and the Commission as its witnesses. The battalion's binding constraint moves to its officers and to collective training. The Joint General Staff refused the battalion sergeant major, and the divisions and corps are commanded as they were.

### RF/PF and territorial security

Under E03 every Regional and Popular Forces NCO holds the course for his grade, and every PF platoon leader the Advanced Leader Course. The post's night is led by a schooled man, and the territorial forces lose fewer posts to night attack, most of all in the Delta and the Saigon belt. The constraint moves to the reaction, the district chief and the families beside the post, and the best schooled territorial sergeants are drawn away from their hamlets. The territorial forces' presence after dark, which decides the hamlets, is unchanged by this provision.

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. The air campaign against the North is planned by officers and flown by officers; the Air Force's and Navy's enlisted maintainers are schooled leaders under P010a, which improves the management of the flight lines at the margin and does not change a sortie.

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged. The Trail is attacked by air and by the sensor system, and no squad leader reaches it. The ARVN's Lam Son 719 in 1971, with its M113 cavalry squadrons and M41A3 tanks, is fought by battalions whose sergeants are schooled; its outcome was decided by the corps commander and the PAVN's armour, which this grant does not reach.

### Arc Light

Unchanged in employment. The ARVN squad leader is better at reporting contacts that bring the B-52J's strikes in close support²⁷, and more dependent on them.

### Airmobile formations

Modestly improved. The airmobile infantry's squad leaders are schooled in the landing-zone drills off the UH-1H and CH-47A and the patrol base, and the helicopter units' crew chiefs and maintenance sergeants are schooled leaders, which improves aircraft availability at the margin²⁸. The 1st Cavalry Division's war is unchanged in shape.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The Navy's petty officers who command the PBR Mk II river patrol boats of Game Warden and the craft of the Mobile Riverine Force have passed the Navy's leadership courses [§1.1 D1]. The Vietnamese Navy's enlisted boat captains of the River Assault Groups are schooled under P010b. The effect is small and positive.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Unchanged in its mechanism. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units' leaders are not RVNAF NCOs and are outside the grant; the district centres' work rests on the police and the Special Branch⁷. The disciplined squad that delivers its detainee alive to the district centre helps at the margin (the identify-to-neutralise chain above).

### Third-country forces

Unchanged. The Korean, Australian and Thai NCOs are their own armies'; the grant reaches only US and RVNAF NCOs.

### Prior provisions

- **P009b, P009c** (RVNAF and RF/PF basic training): the drill sergeants hold the Senior Leader Course and return to the battalions as platoon sergeants who have been schooled for the platoon; the trained private now has a schooled corporal. The drain of the best NCOs from the battalions is deepened in 1964–65.
- **P009a** (US basic training): the drill sergeants of the expanded cadre have passed the Advanced Leader Course; they and the NCO instructors draw on the same senior NCOs.
- **P007b, P007c** (RVNAF officer education and the promotion board): the educated lieutenant has a schooled sergeant to carry out his plan; the board's field commissions take the best-schooled sergeants first; the board's written criteria are the precedent for the NCO gate.
- **P007a** (US officer education): the lieutenant and the sergeant share doctrine from their first courses; the platoon is taught one way.
- **P006b** (unit rotation): the battalion sends its sergeants to the Basic Leader Course during the training months before deployment; the educated sergeant trains his squad collectively and keeps it for the tour.
- **P006c** (selected advisory corps): the adviser NCO and his counterpart work from one curriculum; the advisory corps, the drill-sergeant cadre and the NCO academies compete for the same American sergeants.
- **P008a, P008b** (rebuild depots, battery and radio works): the motor and signal sergeants schooled to run maintenance meet the depots from the user's side.
- **P004a, P004b, P004c** (carbine, marksman's rifle, squad gun): the squad leader employs the marksman and sites the squad gun as his course teaches; the rearmament's practice is held by the man who leads the squad.
- **P001b** (Commission and Muster Service): the course number for each NCO grade goes on the muster roll and bought passes become Commission cases.
- **P005a, P005b, P005c, P003b** (B-52J, guided bombs, Global Hawk): the ARVN squad leader reports contacts that bring their fire in close and depends on it more.
- Unchanged: P001a, P002a, P002b, P003a, P005d, P006a.
