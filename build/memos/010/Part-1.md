# Memorandum 010 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


This grant is about the sergeants, and in this war the sergeants are the men who fight it. The battalion commander plans the operation and the company commander calls for the fire, but the contact itself, in the canopy west of Dau Tieng or on a paddy dike in Dinh Tuong at two in the morning, is a squad's fight, and a squad is led by a sergeant or a corporal. The grant gives every American NCO, and every NCO of the South Vietnamese regular forces, the education the United States Army gives its noncommissioned officers in 2026, one course for each grade, each course a gate for the next promotion. I read it as two provisions, because the two corps are taught in different languages, by different men, in different countries: the American services' enlisted professional military education, and the RVNAF regular forces' NCO education on the 2026 American Army model.

Three readings govern everything below, and I state them once here. First, "the same date as officer education" is 1957, as it was for the basic training of memorandum 009. The officer education of memorandum 007 runs from 1957 and its first seven classes stand on 1 January 1964 as a condition of the world; this grant runs from the same date and I have recorded it in CONFIG as a standing condition of its own. Second, the grant says *all* NCOs, where memorandum 007's said new officers¹. I take that literally. The NCOs already serving in 1957 went through the course for their grade in 1957–60, and every man promoted since went through his course before he put on the stripe². On the opening date, therefore, every serving NCO in both forces holds the course for the grade he wears. Third, "ARVN" means the regular forces, as it did in memorandum 009: the ARVN with its Airborne, Rangers and Vietnamese Marines, and the enlisted NCOs of the VNAF and VNN. The Regional and Popular Forces are not named, and memorandum 009 needed the grant to name them before it reached them³. Their sergeants keep the schooling history gave them, and I say what that costs where it binds.

One further reading runs through both dossiers. "2026 US NCO education" is a curriculum, a method and a set of gates, not equipment. Where the 2026 course teaches a task on a system that does not exist in 1957 — a digital mission-command network, a small drone, a vehicle the period does not have — the task is taught on what does exist, and I say in each dossier what survives that substitution.

## §1.1 US enlisted professional military education to the 2026 standard, from 1957 — P010a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** Every American noncommissioned officer, in every service, from 1957, is educated for each grade by the course his service runs for that grade in 2026, and may not be promoted to the next grade until he has passed it². For the Army, which fights most of the ground war and makes most of its sergeants, that is the NCO Professional Development System under the rule called Select, Train, Educate, Promote, which since 1 January 2016 has tied each promotion to a school⁴. The sequence runs from the Basic Leader Course through the Advanced Leader Course, the Senior Leader Course and the Master Leader Course to the Sergeants Major Course, under one schoolhouse, the NCO Leadership Center of Excellence at Fort Bliss⁵.

**The Army courses as they stand in 2026.** The Basic Leader Course is the new 29-day course that replaced the 23-day one in 2026. It keeps everything the old course taught — leadership, counselling, training, written and oral communication, doctrine through real case studies⁵ — and adds six or seven days of field training: eight days in the field rotating through leader lanes on the individual soldier's tasks, among them casualty care, weapons, patrolling and vehicle recovery, then graded land navigation, then a culminating situational training exercise in which the students receive fragmentary orders, plan, brief their subordinates and execute⁶. The Advanced and Senior Leader Courses, which ran eight to sixteen weeks by specialty, were cut in the 2025 overhaul to three weeks resident regardless of specialty, and the Master Leader Course was lengthened from two weeks to three. The Sergeants Major Course is unchanged at ten months⁷. My sources show the 2025 overhaul announced and expected to be finalised within weeks; I found nothing reversing it, and I take it as the 2026 configuration⁸.

**The gates.** Under the 2016 rule each course gated the promotion to the grade it served: the Basic Leader Course for sergeant, the Advanced for staff sergeant, the Senior for sergeant first class, the Master for master sergeant and first sergeant, the Sergeants Major Course for sergeant major⁴. The 2025 change removed the school requirement for sergeant and made the Basic Leader Course the prerequisite for staff sergeant⁷. I read the 2026 gates accordingly, one grade later than in 2016: a corporal or specialist may be made sergeant on merit, but no sergeant becomes a staff sergeant without the Basic Leader Course, no staff sergeant becomes a sergeant first class without the Advanced, no sergeant first class becomes a master sergeant without the Senior, no master sergeant becomes a first sergeant or sergeant major without the Master and the Sergeants Major Course⁹.

**The other services.** I read "all US NCOs" as all four services, as memorandum 009 read "all US forces"³. The Marine Corps' 2026 requirements, set by its December 2024 message, are a resident Corporals Course for corporals, Sergeants School resident or seminar for sergeants, the Career School distance programme for staff sergeants, the SNCO Leadership School and the Advanced School distance programme for gunnery sergeants, and the First Sergeants School¹⁰. That matters almost as much as the Army's system here, because the III Marine Amphibious Force fights the northern war and its corporals lead its squads. The Navy's and the Air Force's enlisted leadership continua — the Air Force's Airman Leadership School, NCO Academy and Senior NCO Academy, the Navy's petty officer leadership courses — matter to the riverine force, the Seabees, the security police at the air bases and the maintenance chiefs of the fighter wings, and I treat them as present without developing them¹¹.

**What it is not.** It is not a school for making NCOs. It educates the men the services promote; it does not add a single stripe, choose the man, or shorten the time a private takes to become a sergeant². It is not the basic training of memorandum 009, which every one of these men also had³, and not advanced individual training. It is not a counterinsurgency course: the 2026 curricula are built first for large-scale combat against a peer, with the lessons of Iraq and Afghanistan behind them⁵¹².

**In-service date.** A 2026 configuration of a system whose promotion gates date from 2016⁴ and whose Basic Leader Course was rebuilt in 2026⁶. The NCOs who served in 1957–63 completed it before the campaign opened².

**Quantity.** Every US NCO from 1957². In 1957 the Army's NCO corps, sergeant to sergeant major, numbered about 250,000–320,000 men and the Marine Corps' corporals and above about 60,000–80,000¹³. Those men went through their grade's course over 1957–60, a catch-up of roughly 330,000–400,000 Army and Marine course places in about four years, alongside the men promoted each year¹⁴. In steady state, before the war, promotion and first-course entry puts about 120,000–180,000 Army and Marine NCOs a year through a course, most of them through the Basic Leader Course and the Corporals Course¹⁵. At the 1967–69 peak, when the Army was making sergeants of draftees in their first year, the figure rises to about 250,000–350,000¹⁶. The Navy and Air Force add about as many again¹⁷.

**Organisation in theater.** Almost none. The courses are taught in the United States: at the NCO academies of the Army's divisions and posts, which by 1959 had already put more than 180,000 soldiers through some academy course¹⁸; at the Sergeants Major Academy, which history opened at Fort Bliss only in 1972 and this grant opens in 1957; at the Marine Corps' schools at Quantico, Camp Lejeune and Camp Pendleton¹⁹. Its product reaches Vietnam inside every squad, section, platoon and battalion staff. The one exception is the man promoted in Vietnam: a specialist made sergeant in the 1st Cavalry Division in An Khe cannot become a staff sergeant without the Basic Leader Course, and the 12-month tour means he must either take it in theater or wait until he goes home. I put a MACV NCO Academy at Bien Hoa, teaching the Basic Leader Course to about 600–900 men a month²⁰, and accept that the Advanced and Senior courses are taken in the United States between tours.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The provision consumes about 250–450 US spaces in the Republic²¹. No troop ceiling is set, so those spaces cost only the men themselves (CONFIG).

Its real cost is in the United States, outside the ceiling. A schoolhouse for every grade of NCO in four services needs about 4,000–7,000 added instructors and staff at the 1967–69 peak²². Every instructor is a sergeant first class or master sergeant taken out of a unit, in the years when the Army was shortest of exactly those men²³.

The larger cost is the time of the students. With the course lengths in D1 and the promotion rates in D5, about 2–3% of the NCO corps is in school at any moment instead of in its unit²⁴. In 1967, when the Army's NCO shortage was critical enough to produce the NCO Candidate Course²³, that is about 8,000–12,000 sergeants absent from units at any time²⁵. I count that cost plainly. It is smaller than it sounds — most of those men are in school between assignments, not taken out of a squad in contact — but it is real.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Instruments and authorities.* The provision's only instrument is the gate: the authority to refuse a promotion to a man who has not passed his course⁴. Everything else in it depends on that power being used.

The gate brings three things with it. The first is a standard: the Academic Evaluation Report, which since the 2016 rule grades the student on written work and a measured performance as well as completion⁴, so that the promotion board sees not only that the sergeant went to school but how he did. The second is a common doctrine: troop-leading procedures, the operations order, the after-action review, the counselling session and the training schedule, taught the same way to every sergeant in the Army⁵. The third is a lever on the man's career that his commander cannot override.

What it cannot compel is the assignment. It does not decide whether the educated staff sergeant goes to a rifle platoon in the 25th Infantry Division at Cu Chi or to a supply room at Fort Hood. It does not decide who is promoted, only who is eligible. And the gate can be waived. In 2016, with the rule in force, about 14,000 Army NCOs had been promoted without the education the rule required⁴. A gate that its own Army waived 14,000 times in peacetime will be waived in a war, and D11 treats that as the first failure.

What the courses teach the man to do with his weapons is the period's, not 2026's. The Basic Leader Course's leader lanes and situational exercise are run on the M14 rifle and, from 1966, the M16A1, the M60 machine gun, the M79 grenade launcher, the M18A1 Claymore and the M72 LAW, with the AN/PVS-2 Starlight scope from 1967; its field radio is the AN/PRC-10 and, from 1965–66, the AN/PRC-25²⁶. The tasks survive the substitution. What the squad leader learns is how to place the M60 on the trail junction, where to put the Claymores around a patrol base, how to call for fire on a grid he has fixed by map and compass, and how to send the report that brings the M102 battery's rounds or the UH-1D for the wounded, and how to bring an M113 section up a dike without losing it in the paddy. The 2026 system the course assumes for that last task does not exist; the task does.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Information inputs.* The course learns what its faculty knows, and in 1957–64 its faculty knows Korea and the Second World War, not Vietnam²⁷. The 2026 curriculum brings with it twenty years of Iraq and Afghanistan and the lessons of the war in Ukraine — small-unit patrolling under constant observation, the improvised mine, the casualty who must be held for hours — set inside a curriculum built for large-scale combat⁶⁵. Some of that is close to this war, notably the mine and the held casualty; some of it is not.

From 1965, the course can learn Vietnam, because the NCOs who teach it are rotating home from the war. That is the provision's best information input and its slowest: the first instructors who have led a squad in the Iron Triangle are teaching in 1966–67²⁸. The case studies the course teaches by⁵ can then be our own war's, and the Bien Hoa academy in D1 can teach the Basic Leader Course with this war's terrain on its land-navigation course.

It touches the find-and-fix problem only at the bottom. A squad leader who can navigate in the canopy at night, run a patrol base without noise and lay an ambush that holds is a better instrument for finding the local force that moves on the trails near his base. He does nothing for finding a PAVN regiment that has decided not to be found, and nothing for identifying the VCI in the hamlet.

### D5 · Performance

*Throughput and capacity.* The rate limit is the instructor base, not the student. Before the war, the courses in D1 put about 120,000–180,000 Army and Marine NCOs a year through school²⁹. At the peak the requirement nearly doubles, and the instructors in D2 must come from the same senior NCOs the units need.

The promotion clock is where the provision binds. Historically, the Army promoted men to sergeant inside their first year during 1967–69, and its NCO Candidate Course made sergeants of privates in 21 weeks²³. Under this grant a man may still be made sergeant on merit, but he is not a staff sergeant until he has passed the 29-day Basic Leader Course⁶⁷. For the soldier in Vietnam that means either the MACV academy at Bien Hoa or a delay until he leaves. The NCO Candidate Course itself is historical and remains; its 12 weeks at Fort Benning are longer than the Basic Leader Course, and I read its graduates as taking the Basic Leader Course's field lanes and evaluation as a final module, which adds about two weeks to their pipeline³⁰. Its top graduates, promoted straight to staff sergeant¹⁸, must hold the course before the promotion takes effect.

The output, in sergeants made better, is large, because it is everyone. By 1964 the provision has touched about 330,000–400,000 Army and Marine NCOs²⁹. By 1968 every American squad leader in Vietnam, including the instant sergeant of eight months' service, has had at least the Basic Leader Course or the Corporals Course, and every platoon sergeant at least the Advanced Leader Course³¹.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.* The course reports to the promotion system: the Academic Evaluation Report goes into the man's file and before his next board⁴. It reports nothing to MACV; I learn of its effect only through the units.

Its interface with the South Vietnamese is through the advisers. A large share of the Americans who advise the RVNAF are NCOs: the weapons and operations sergeants of the battalion advisory teams, and from 1967 the NCOs of the five-man mobile advisory teams sent to the Regional and Popular Forces. An adviser who has passed the Senior Leader Course knows how to plan a unit's training, read its readiness and counsel its leaders, and that is what his Vietnamese counterpart needs from him. The interface is strongest with P010b, which teaches the same curriculum in Vietnamese: an American sergeant first class and an ARVN *trung sĩ nhất* who have studied the same troop-leading procedures can work a combined ambush from the same vocabulary.

The language barrier is not touched. The course teaches no Vietnamese and no French³², and the adviser still works through an interpreter.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Reach and security preconditions.* It reaches every American NCO in every service, at home and abroad, from 1957². It needs no security in Vietnam because it is not taught there, except for the MACV academy at Bien Hoa, which sits inside the defended perimeter of the Long Binh–Bien Hoa complex and needs only a land-navigation and patrolling area of a few square kilometres inside the base's controlled ground³³.

Its reach in the war depends on the 12-month tour. The educated sergeant serves twelve months in Vietnam, or thirteen if he is a Marine, and then goes home. The provision makes every sergeant who arrives better; it does nothing to keep him. Memorandum 006's unit rotation decides whether he stays with the men he leads³⁴.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The footprint is people and time. In the United States it is the instructor base of about 4,000–7,000 at the peak³⁵, the land-navigation courses and field areas the 2026 Basic Leader Course needs⁶, classrooms and barracks for the students, and the travel that the 2025 overhaul was designed to cut⁷. The money is modest by the standard of this war³⁶.

In Vietnam the footprint is the Bien Hoa academy: barracks for about 600 students at a time, about 150–250 cadre, a range for the M16A1 and M60, a land-navigation course and a ration and transport allocation²⁹. It needs nothing the RVNAF must supply, and nothing that the port and road system in this war cannot carry.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The American courses do not transfer; their Vietnamese counterpart is P010b, granted alongside. What transfers is the faculty. Every American NCO adviser at the Nha Trang NCO Academy, and every adviser to an ARVN battalion who must teach his counterpart to plan training, has been through the course the Vietnamese are learning, and through the more senior courses beyond it.

Under Vietnamization the American provision leaves with the American units. The men it educated remain in the advisory teams until 1972–73, and then they are gone.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The provision performs to specification when three things hold: the gate is enforced, the educated NCO stays with a unit long enough to use what he learned, and his officers let him lead.

The first holds in peace and weakens in war. The second is the 12-month tour's problem, not the course's: a staff sergeant who arrives educated and leaves after a year has given his platoon one year of better leadership, not a tradition. The third varies by unit and by lieutenant; memorandum 007's educated lieutenants make it more likely¹.

Its performance collapses where the problem is not competence. The 1969–71 crisis in the American Army in Vietnam — drugs, racial violence, refusals, the fragging of officers and NCOs — was a crisis of the conscript army in a war its soldiers had stopped believing in. An educated sergeant is better equipped to hold a squad together through it, because the course teaches counselling, standards and the handling of a soldier in trouble⁵. He is not immune to it, and some of the men killed in that crisis were exactly the conscientious sergeants who enforced standards.

It is weakest in the specialist trades. The 2025 overhaul cut the Advanced and Senior Leader Courses to three weeks regardless of specialty⁷, which means the technical content the old courses carried by branch is no longer taught there. The maintenance sergeant of a helicopter company or an artillery battery is educated as a leader, not as a technician.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The waiver.** The first failure is the gate itself. When the Army is short of staff sergeants and a battalion in contact needs one, the commander asks for a waiver, and the Army grants it; the 14,000-man "black log" of 2016 is the peacetime proof⁴. A partial failure is the gate kept for the senior grades and waived for the junior, which keeps most of the value of the senior courses and loses some of the junior. A total failure is the gate abandoned for the duration, which turns the provision back into the historical academies with better curricula.

**The course that teaches the wrong war.** The second failure is the curriculum. The 2026 courses teach large-scale combat⁶. Until the instructors are men back from Vietnam, the sergeant learns how to lead a squad in a patrol base against a peer with drones, which is closer to Vietnam than it sounds, but not the same.

**The man in school when he is needed.** The third failure is the sergeant who is at Fort Benning in the month his old platoon goes into the A Shau. It is the provision's cost working as designed, and in the 1967–68 shortage it will sometimes be the wrong cost³⁵.

**The educated man in the wrong job.** The fourth is assignment: the staff sergeant with the Basic Leader Course running a motor pool in Germany while a specialist runs a rifle squad in Quang Tri. The provision does not touch the personnel system, and the historical system put men where the requisition was.

What the force does while the provision fails is what it historically did: it promotes the best men it has, and they learn on the job.

### D12 · Degradation curve

For an institution, month one against month twelve becomes 1964 against 1968. In 1964 the provision is at its best: seven years of courses complete, every NCO educated for his grade, instructors chosen from a stable peacetime NCO corps, gates enforced³⁷. By 1968 it is stretched: the instructor base has nearly doubled with men pulled from units, class sizes have grown, waivers have begun, and the students are younger and less experienced³⁸.

What declines first is the field portion. The eight days in the field that the 2026 Basic Leader Course added⁶ cost the most in land, instructors and time, and a schoolhouse under pressure cuts them first. The classroom core survives longest, which means the sergeant who learned to write an operations order but did less land navigation than he should have.

The second thing to decline is evaluation. The graded Academic Evaluation Report⁴ becomes a pass for everyone who attends when the Army needs every man. The courses survive; their standard sags.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The historical benchmark is the system history had in 1964: local NCO academies of uneven quality, run by divisions and commands under Army-wide standards set only in 1958 by AR 350-90, through which more than 180,000 soldiers had passed by 1959. There was no Army-wide system, no gate and no sequence; a sergeant first class might have been to an academy or not, and his promotion did not depend on it¹⁸. The NCO Education System came only in 1971, with the first Army-wide basic courses in May 1971 and the first advanced courses in January 1972. The Sergeants Major Academy opened in 1972. The war's own answer to the shortage was the NCO Candidate Course, which trained about 33,000 sergeants in 12 weeks at Fort Benning and 9 weeks on the job from 1967²³.

Against that, the margin is large at the top and real at the bottom. Every platoon sergeant and first sergeant in 1964 has been through a course his historical counterpart never had, and every sergeant major has had ten months at an academy that did not exist⁷. A staff sergeant who has passed the 2026 Basic Leader Course in his first month leading a squad is, I judge, about as competent as a historical one after three or four months of leading it in the field, a judgment that rests on the course's field lanes, land navigation and situational exercise set against the time historical squad leaders took to learn the same tasks in contact³⁹. The NCO Candidate Course graduate gains less, because his 21 weeks already covered much of the same ground²³.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It does not make more NCOs.** The NCO shortage of 1966–69 was a shortage of men, made by the 12-month tour, the separations of senior NCOs and the build-up without reserves; the provision educates the men promoted, and its gates slow some promotions.
- **It does not keep the sergeant in the war.** The 12-month tour still takes him home; memorandum 006's unit rotation, not this, decides whether he stays with his men.
- **It does not cure the crisis of 1969–71.** Drugs, racial violence and fragging were the conscript army's crisis; an educated sergeant handles it better and is not immune to it.
- **It is not a Vietnam course.** The 2026 curricula teach large-scale combat; the war's own lessons enter only when men back from Vietnam teach it, from about 1966–67.
- **The gate will be waived.** The Army waived it about 14,000 times in peacetime; in a shortage it will be waived again, first for the junior grades.
- **It does not teach the trades.** The 2026 Advanced and Senior Leader Courses are three weeks of leadership, not technical schooling.
- **It costs sergeants.** About 2–3% of the NCO corps is in school at any time, and 4,000–7,000 senior NCOs teach instead of lead³⁵.
- **It does not reach the Regional and Popular Forces, or the officers.** The first is outside the grant; the second is memorandum 007's.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy some of the American squad's mistakes: the patrol that is lost in the canopy and walks into a base camp, the ambush laid without security that is itself ambushed, the squad that loses its fire control in the first minute and shoots its own flank, the casualty left to bleed while the sergeant calls for help on the wrong frequency. It denies him the leaderless American platoon after its lieutenant is hit. It denies him nothing at the operational level and nothing in the sanctuary.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a real provision for the American Army and a modest one for the war. It makes every American sergeant better at the work he was already doing, and in a war fought by squads and platoons that saves American lives and some Vietnamese ones. Its largest value falls in 1967–71, when the historical NCO corps was thinnest and youngest, because the instant sergeant now has at least the Basic Leader Course and the platoon sergeant the Advanced. It does not make more sergeants, keep them in Vietnam past their year, or touch the strategy they fight inside. Its indirect value may be the larger: every American NCO adviser has been through the courses his Vietnamese counterpart is learning, and that is what makes P010b work.

## §1.2 RVNAF regular NCO education on the 2026 US Army model, from 1957 — P010b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** Every NCO of the South Vietnamese regular forces, from 1957, is educated for each grade by a Vietnamese course built on the 2026 American Army sequence, and may not be promoted until he has passed it². The NCO grades of the RVNAF run from corporal (*hạ sĩ*) and corporal first class (*hạ sĩ nhất*) through sergeant (*trung sĩ*), sergeant first class (*trung sĩ nhất*) and master sergeant (*thượng sĩ*) to master sergeant first class (*thượng sĩ nhất*), the senior enlisted grade⁴¹. I map the American courses onto them:

1. **Basic Leader Course**, for every corporal first class before he is made sergeant: the American 29-day course⁶, lengthened to six weeks for translation, interpretation and the literacy of its students⁴².
2. **Advanced Leader Course**, for sergeants before sergeant first class: the squad and section leader's course, four weeks⁴³.
3. **Senior Leader Course**, for sergeants first class before master sergeant: the platoon sergeant's course, four weeks.
4. **Master Leader Course**, for master sergeants before master sergeant first class: the company first sergeant's and battalion operations sergeant's course, four weeks⁴⁴.
5. **A senior NCO course** at the academy for the master sergeants first class who serve as the senior enlisted men of battalions, regiments and divisions: four to five months, compressed from the American Sergeants Major Course's ten⁴⁵.

**The school.** The ARVN already had the institution. The National Noncommissioned Officers Academy was founded by the French Army in 1951 at Quang Yen in the North as the Commando School, moved south to Nha Trang, and was reorganised at the end of 1958 to train selected NCOs in leadership and command. Under this grant, that reorganisation comes in 1957, and the academy at Dong De becomes the home of the sequence above, with the basic course also taught at division NCO schools and the VNAF and VNN courses at their own service schools⁴⁶. In 1970 the academy taught 16,522 students, in barracks enlarged from 3,320 to 4,520 places⁴⁷. That is the scale this grant needs, but it needs it from 1957⁴⁸.

**What it is not.** It is not the basic training of memorandum 009, though every NCO made after 1957 had that too³. It does not reach the Regional and Popular Forces, whose NCOs are outside the grant [U: reading of "ARVN" in the preamble]. It does not commission anyone; the academy's historical officer-candidate classes⁴⁷ are a separate matter and I leave them as history made them.

**In-service date.** No real-world equivalent; an adaptation of the 2026 American system⁴⁶⁷. The NCOs of 1957–63 completed it before the campaign opened².

**Quantity.** Every regular NCO from 1957². In 1957 the RVNAF regular forces numbered about 150,000 men, of whom about 18,000–28,000 were NCOs⁴⁹. They went through the course for their grade in 1957–60. Thereafter the academy and the division schools put about 8,000–14,000 men a year through a course before 1964, about 15,000–25,000 a year in 1964–67, and about 30,000–45,000 a year at the 1968–70 peak⁵⁰. On 1 January 1964 the regular forces hold about 35,000–50,000 NCOs⁵¹, every one of them educated for his grade.

**Organisation in theater.** The academy at Nha Trang with its advisory detachment; NCO schools at the ten ARVN divisions, the Airborne and the Vietnamese Marines; the VNAF and VNN enlisted leadership courses at their training centres; a curriculum and translation cell at the Central Training Command in Saigon⁵².

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The American cost is small: about 150–300 US personnel, of whom 130–270 military spaces⁵³. No troop ceiling is set (CONFIG). Most of these advisers should be NCOs, and they are the products of P010a.

The Vietnamese cost is the cost that matters. At the 2026 ratio of about one instructor to 12–16 students⁵⁴, the sequence needs about 800–1,500 Vietnamese NCO instructors before 1964 and 2,000–3,500 at the 1968–70 peak⁵⁵. Those are the army's best sergeants first class and master sergeants. The ARVN was short of exactly those men; its NCO grade structure was out of balance in the middle grades, with most NCO input coming in at the bottom⁵⁶. In 1964 the basic training of memorandum 009 already holds 2,000–3,000 of the army's best NCOs as drill sergeants³. This grant takes another 1,000–1,500 of them in the same year⁵⁷. That double drain is the provision's sharpest cost, and Part 2 must weigh it.

About 3–5% of the NCO corps is in school at any time⁵⁸.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Instruments and authorities.* As in the American provision, the instrument is the gate, and here it is a weaker instrument. RVNAF promotion relied more on seniority than on merit; its enlisted promotions were made by annual centralised boards. The government also ordered across-the-board promotions of one grade for all enlisted ranks on national occasions⁵⁶. A gate that a decree can override is a gate the government can open whenever it wants the army's gratitude. The 1967 NCO Annual Promotion Procedure, which considered NCOs on merit⁴¹, shows that the system could hold a standard when the Joint General Staff wanted it to. The grant gives the advisers a standard to argue for; it does not give them a veto.

The course brings the same common doctrine as the American one⁵, in Vietnamese: troop-leading procedures, the operations order, the after-action review, counselling, training management and the readiness report. It brings the weapons drill on the RVNAF's own weapons: the M1 carbine and M1 rifle, the Browning Automatic Rifle, the M1919 machine gun and the 60mm M2 mortar in 1964, the M16A1, M60 and M79 from 1968⁵⁹. The squad leader learns to control their fire, site them in a night defensive position and call for the 105mm M101A1 battery's support on a grid he has fixed himself.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Information inputs.* The Vietnamese course has a better information input than the American, because its students and instructors are in the war. A sergeant at the Nha Trang academy in 1964 has spent years in the field against the VC; the instructor who teaches him patrolling has done it in the U Minh or the Ca Mau peninsula. The 2026 curriculum gives them a frame — case studies, after-action reviews, a standard for each task — into which their own experience can be put⁵.

What the course cannot learn from is the ARVN's own reporting, which was shaped by the men it reported on⁶⁰. An NCO course built on the after-action review needs a culture that tells the truth about failure in front of officers. That culture did not exist in most ARVN units⁴¹, and the course can teach the technique without creating the culture.

Like the American course, it touches the find-and-fix problem only at the bottom. A Vietnamese squad leader who can navigate at night and lay an ambush without noise finds the local force on the trails near his post. He does nothing about the main-force regiment and nothing about the VCI, except that the VCI's own reports about his platoon change.

### D5 · Performance

*Throughput and capacity.* The rate limit is the instructor, as in the American provision, and the literacy of the student. The 2026 curriculum assumes a soldier who can read doctrine and write a counselling record and an order; the Academic Evaluation Report grades writing⁴. Many ARVN NCOs of 1957–64 had a primary education or less⁶¹. The Vietnamese course must teach more by demonstration and less by reading, and it loses some of what the American course's written work does for the student's ability to plan.

The throughput in D1 is achievable. In 1970 the academy alone taught 16,522 students; the grant needs that rate from 1964, with the division schools carrying the basic course. In 1957–63 the requirement is smaller, and the academy's history shows a school that existed and could grow⁴⁷.

The output is a different NCO corps. On 1 January 1964 every corporal first class who has been made sergeant has had six weeks at a leader's course, and every platoon sergeant has had a squad leader's course and a platoon sergeant's course. Historically, a leader programme of seven weeks for squad leaders and ten for platoon leaders was only "under consideration" in 1964, with no final action recorded⁶². In 1966 the ARVN gave its best trainees three weeks of leadership training before sending them to the NCO Academy or officer school⁴¹. This grant gives every NCO, from 1957, what the army was still discussing in 1964.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.* The course reports to the Central Training Command and, through the promotion files, to the Joint General Staff's boards⁵⁶. The advisers report to MACV through the training directorate.

Its interface with the Americans is the best of any provision so far for the men below the officers. The American NCO advisers of P010a and the Vietnamese NCOs of P010b have studied the same troop-leading procedures and the same orders format, and a combined patrol or a joint fire plan can be worked out in a shared structure even across the language. When an American platoon from the 9th Infantry Division and an ARVN platoon from the 7th Division share a night ambush in Dinh Tuong, the two platoon sergeants are reading the same sketch.

Its interface with the Vietnamese officer is the hard one. The 2026 model gives the NCO responsibility for individual training, discipline and the welfare of the soldiers, and gives the officer the plan and the decision⁵. In the RVNAF, officers came largely from the educated urban class and NCOs from the rural ranks, and the gap between them was social as well as military⁶³. A lieutenant who does not let his sergeant run the platoon's training wastes everything the sergeant learned. The officer education of memorandum 007 is the counterpart that makes this work¹.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Reach and security preconditions.* It reaches every regular NCO in every service and division, from the 1st Division at Hue to the 21st at Bac Lieu². Its schools need security: the academy at Nha Trang sat on the coast in a secure city through the whole war until 1975, and the division schools sit at division base camps. Their security is the army's own.

The course demands that the man be released from his unit, and a commander short of sergeants does not release his best one. Historically, leadership training quotas for the Popular Forces were never filled in 1964⁶²; regular units filled their school quotas with the men they could spare⁶⁴. The gate fixes that in principle, because a man who has not gone cannot be promoted, and so his commander cannot reward him without sending him.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The footprint is mostly Vietnamese: instructors, barracks, rations and transport for 1,000–2,000 students at a time before 1964 and 3,000–5,000 at the peak⁶⁵. The academy's 1970 barracks for 4,520⁴⁷ shows that the scale is within the army's reach. The American contribution is advisers, curriculum, translation and printing, and the weapons and ammunition for training, which the American aid programme already supplies⁶⁶.

The soldier's family is part of the footprint. An ARVN NCO's family lived near his unit, often in the dependents' housing around the post or in the town. Six weeks at Nha Trang separates him from it, and a staff sergeant from the 7th Division in My Tho is a day's travel away. His pay does not change while he is in school, and by 1967–68 inflation had made his pay insufficient to feed a family without other income⁶⁷. A sergeant whose wife sells goods in the market while he is away is a sergeant whose family has learned to live without him, and some of those men will not come back from the academy to the unit.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

This is the Vietnamese provision; transfer is its premise. The question is what happens when the Americans leave. The academy, its curricula and its instructors are Vietnamese and stay. What leaves is the adviser who argues for the gate at the promotion board. After 1972–73 the gate's survival depends on the Joint General Staff alone. I judge it would hold for the senior courses, which the senior NCOs who took them value as markers of status, and loosen for the basic course in the emergency of 1974–75⁶⁸.

### D10 · Employment envelope

It performs to specification when the gate holds, when the NCO stays, and when the officer lets him lead. All three are weaker in the RVNAF than in the American Army. The gate can be opened by decree⁵⁶. The NCO can be drained by desertion and by commissioning: in 1966 alone the RVNAF commissioned over 2,000 men from the ranks, including 401 regular and 100 Regional Forces master sergeants first class directly commissioned⁴¹. Every one of those men was a senior NCO the army needed as a sergeant, and the education this grant gives makes him a better candidate for commission. And the officer, as D6 says, must be willing.

Its performance collapses where the problem is political. The regimental commander who keeps his unit out of a fight to protect his position, the division commander chosen for loyalty, the order from the palace to hold or to withdraw — none of these is the sergeant's, and none of them is touched¹. In the battles of 1975 the ARVN units that broke broke above the NCO, mostly.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The decree.** The first failure is the across-the-board promotion, which history shows the government ordering on national occasions⁵⁶. A single decree that promotes every corporal first class to sergeant without the course undoes a year of gating. A partial failure is one such decree in a crisis; a total one is the gate abandoned.

**The purchased place.** The second failure is corruption. If places at the academy or a passing grade can be bought, the gate becomes a toll⁶⁹. A partial failure is a small number of purchased passes, which cheapens the course; a total one is a course whose pass the boards stop respecting.

**The double drain.** The third failure is the cost in D2. In 1964–65, with 2,000–3,000 of the best NCOs teaching basic training³ and 1,000–1,500 more teaching NCO courses³⁵, the battalions lose the sergeants who would have led their best platoons. If the expansion of 1965 drains the schools in turn, the army has neither the instructors nor the platoon sergeants.

**The educated deserter.** The fourth is the family. An NCO whose pay does not feed his family, and whose family is far away, deserts or takes a second job. His education goes with him.

**The officer who will not delegate.** The fifth, and the most common, is the lieutenant who does the sergeant's work himself or lets no one do it. That NCO's education is wasted inside the platoon.

While the provision fails, the army has what history gave it: NCOs promoted by seniority, trained on the job, and thinnest in the middle grades⁵⁶.

### D12 · Degradation curve

In 1964 the provision is at its best: seven years of courses complete, every NCO educated for his grade, the academy at a scale it can sustain³⁷. In 1965–67 it is strained by the expansion of the forces, by the double drain of D2 and by the commissioning of the best senior NCOs⁴¹. In 1968, after Tet, the mobilisation brings the largest intake of the war, and the historical army cut its basic training from 12 weeks to 9 and sent untrained men straight to units³. The NCO courses will be cut in the same way.

What declines first is the length and the field portion, as in the American provision. What declines second is the gate, through waivers and decrees. What survives longest is the sequence itself — the man who knows he will go to school before his next stripe — and the doctrine the academy teaches, which outlasts any one class.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The historical benchmark is the RVNAF's own NCO system. It had an academy, reorganised in 1958 to teach leadership and command⁴⁷, and division training. It had no sequence of courses by grade, and promotion did not depend on schooling. Its middle grades were thin and its NCO input came in at the bottom⁵⁶. Its leaders' programme of seven and ten weeks was still under consideration in 1964⁶². American advisers reported poor leadership as the main cause of unit ineffectiveness, and a 1967 review found deficient leadership in all but seven of the units judged unsatisfactory⁴¹.

Against that, the margin is larger than in the American provision, because the gap is wider. I judge that an ARVN rifle company whose NCOs are all educated for their grades closes about a third to a half of the gap between an average ARVN company of 1964–67 and the best ones, at squad and platoon level, in patrolling, night defence, fire control and the care of its own men. That is a judgment built on the NCO's share of the small-unit failures the advisers and the 1967 review recorded, set against what a course can teach³⁹. It closes none of the gap above the company commander.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It does not reach the Regional and Popular Forces.** Their NCOs, who led the platoons that held the hamlets, keep the schooling history gave them; the territorial war's sergeants are unchanged.
- **It does not choose the officer.** An educated sergeant under a political or incompetent commander is still sent down the road into the ambush; memoranda 007 and 002 change the commander.
- **The gate can be opened by decree.** Across-the-board promotions on national occasions were historical; a gate the government can override is a standard, not a law.
- **It costs the best sergeants.** About 800–1,500 Vietnamese instructors before 1964 and 2,000–3,500 at the peak, on top of memorandum 009's 2,000–3,000 drill sergeants.
- **It does not cure desertion.** Pay, family and posting drive it; the course touches the leader who counsels the soldier, not the pay that feeds his family.
- **It does not stop the commissioning drain.** The best senior NCOs are the best candidates for commission, and over 2,000 were commissioned in 1966 alone.
- **It teaches the technique of the after-action review, not the culture of telling the truth.** The course can teach a sergeant to report failure; it cannot make his officers want to hear it.
- **It does not reach above the company.** The battles the ARVN lost above the NCO are lost the same way.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the ARVN platoon that sleeps in its night position without listening posts, the squad that fires all its ammunition in the first minutes and then runs, the company that loses its fire control when its officer is hit, and the patrol that takes the same path every evening. It denies him some of the ARVN's tactical predictability below the company commander, and some of the looting and abuse in the hamlets that a sergeant who controls his men prevents. It does not deny him the decision above the company.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a substantial provision for the ARVN, larger than P010a is for the Americans, because the ARVN's NCO corps was its thinnest layer and nobody before this grant had built it. With memorandum 009's trained private and memorandum 007's educated lieutenant, the regular army from 1964 has, for the first time, a trained leader at every level from the squad to the company. That changes how ARVN small units fight at night, how they keep their weapons and trucks running, and how they treat the people in the hamlets they pass through. Its costs are the best sergeants taken to teach, in the year the army can least spare them, and a gate the government can open whenever it wishes. It does not reach the territorial forces, the commanders above the company, or the pay that keeps the soldier's family fed. A better sergeant under a bad colonel is still under a bad colonel; but in a war of squads and platoons, a better sergeant is most of what the soldier sees of the army.
