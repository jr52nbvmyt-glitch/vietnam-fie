# Memorandum 001 — Part 2 of 3 · §2 Integration and second-order effects · §3 Changes to existing forces

*Audit — Memo 001, Part 2/3 · v1.0.1 · cards loaded: none · channels: 10/10 (floor 9) · findings numbered: 17 · employment concepts: 6 + 4 abandoned (floor 4) · counter-directional: yes · strongest positive: yes · designations: ~21 · structural deviation: none*

## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

The cumulative force is the historical force of 1 January 1964 and these two provisions; there is nothing earlier to integrate with. The provisions join no weapon system. They join the systems that the theater needs assessment ranked first and that the arsenal does not reach.

**SECURE.** The land programme (P001a) is a SECURE provision of a kind the system has never held: it changes what a hamlet's people stand to lose if the government is driven out, rather than how many armed men stand between them and the enemy. It does not add a single man to the hamlet at night. It makes the men who are there, the Popular Forces squad raised from the hamlet's own families, defenders of their own land rather than of a landlord's rent. It also makes every titled hamlet a place the government must hold or visibly fail.

**SUBVERT.** The land programme is the largest SUBVERT instrument in theater on the opening date, larger than the Chieu Hoi programme begun in 1963 [H] and the leaflet and loudspeaker effort, because it is aimed not at the enemy's soldiers but at the reason their families give them to him. The anti-corruption programme (P001b) is a modest SUBVERT instrument: it attacks the Front's most effective theme after land.

**ADVISE.** The anti-corruption programme is an ADVISE provision first. It gives the advisory effort two things it has never had: a physical count of the units it advises, made outside their chain, and a lever between persuasion and the withdrawal of all aid. It is newly viable as a means of relief for cause, which advisers have been unable to obtain because they could neither prove a commander's failure nor offer a replacement (G-4, G-6).

**SUSTAIN.** Both consume small amounts of the scarcest things in theater: helicopter hours, escort companies, honest administrators and American linguists. Neither is a logistic burden of any size by the standards of a combat provision.

**FIND, FIX, STRIKE, INTERDICT.** Not joined. The main force, the Trail and the sanctuaries are untouched in the first order. What reaches them reaches them through the population, slowly, and is analysed under the channels.

### Compound pairings
**Interactions with earlier provisions**

- P001a × P001b: the Commission guards the land programme against fees, collusion and false worship-land claims, and the land programme gives the Commission about a million families with a reason to complain and the courts to complain in (F001-1)
- No earlier provision exists; pairings with historical programmes (Chieu Hoi, Hop Tac, the Strategic Hamlet remnant) are analysed in §3

**F001-1** (SECURE, ADVISE) **The land programme and the Commission are each other's precondition.** The 1970 programme's failures were failures of honesty: officials charging fees to accept applications and discounting compensation cheques, landlords and officials colluding, rented land reclassified as worship land, and tenants too intimidated to testify [S3]. Its own auditors judged the lack of grievance machinery perhaps its most important remaining problem [S3]. The anti-corruption programme is that machinery's enforcement arm: a complaint of a fee demanded at a village land committee goes to the land court and the Commission at once, and the Commission has a case that is documentary (a posted list, a title, a cheque), local and cheap to prove. The compounding runs the other way as well. A commission that begins by prosecuting generals dies young; a commission that begins by protecting a million peasant families from the officials who would extort them builds a constituency and a record of convictions on cases nobody powerful will defend. The land programme is the Commission's best first customer, and the Commission is the land programme's guard. Neither provision delivers its own verdict without the other at anything like the scale claimed in §1. Weight: major.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

Untouched in the first order, and I will not pretend otherwise. The VC main force of 21,000–23,000 that this headquarters carries on the opening date [H] chooses whether to fight, moves under canopy and at night, and is found no better because peasants own their fields. The chain is reached only at its far end and only over years: the main force's replacements, rice and porters come from the hamlets, and its local intelligence comes from the population. A titled peasant has a reason to report a battalion's passage to the district; an untitled one does not. That reason operates only where reporting is safe, which is where the government already holds the hamlet, and therefore mostly where the main force does not move. The honest first-year effect on FIND.main_force is nil, and I have pushed nothing on it.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

This is the half of the find-and-fix problem the land programme touches, and it touches it in two ways that must be kept apart. First, a by-product: the parcel register, the claims and the posted lists are a household-by-household census of every hamlet the teams enter, the first the Republic has made since the Strategic Hamlet records were lost with the old regime. A census is not a list of cadres. It is the frame against which strangers, absences and the households that refused to claim can be noticed. Second, a motive: a family with a title and a grievance against the infrastructure, which may now be taxing land the family owns in law, has a reason to name the tax collector. Neither half neutralises anyone. The neutralising half of the chain, a rural police that arrests on evidence and a court that convicts, does not exist in 1964 (G-3), and the Commission's court is not that court. The Commission touches the chain in one narrow place: officials who sell documents, passes and information are the enemy's easiest point of entry into the government, and some of the Commission's cases will turn out to be the enemy's agents.

### Dependence on SECURE

Total for the land programme, partial for the Commission. Every title the programme issues is worth what the night security of its hamlet is worth (POSTNA-2). In a hamlet held by night, the title is property. In a hamlet contested by night, it is a claim the holder may be killed for. In a hamlet the enemy holds, it is a promise. The programme therefore adds nothing to SECURE's capacity and raises the stakes of SECURE's failures: a district that loses its hamlets after titles are issued loses them with a list of names. The programme also feeds SECURE in the one way SECURE needs most and the arsenal cannot supply: it gives the Popular Forces squad, recruited from the hamlet, a stake in staying there at night. The Commission depends on SECURE only for the safety of its musters at isolated posts and of its witnesses.

### The ADVISE cluster

The anti-corruption programme feeds the cluster more than any combat provision could. The physical muster gives every adviser the true strength of the unit he advises; the Board gives the Mission a graduated lever; the Commission's case files give an adviser who reports a counterpart's theft a channel that does not destroy his relationship with the counterpart. Each of these increases advisory leverage and each costs the Republic part of its claim to be its own government (POSTNA-6). The cost is lowest where the lever is pulled by a Vietnamese body on a Vietnamese finding and the American role is to guarantee the body's independence; it is highest where Americans are seen to decide who is relieved. The design keeps the Americans in the second position, and the employment concepts below keep them there.

The land programme feeds the cluster indirectly but deeply. It is a political act of the Vietnamese government, not an American programme, and it is the first thing since 1956 that gives the Republic's soldiers, most of whom come from peasant families [H], something their government did for their fathers. It also starves the cluster in one place: it injures the landed families from which a share of the officer corps comes, and the advisory effort will hear about that grievance from the counterparts it is trying to keep.

### Employment concepts

#### EC001-1 · Rent stop by broadcast and decree

*P001a.* On promulgation day the Chief of State reads the decree on Radio Saigon and the province radio stations; within a week leaflets and the Vietnamese Information Service's loudspeaker teams carry it to every district town [E: the information apparatus of 1964]. The message is three sentences: from this harvest no tenant pays rent on rice land; the land he works is his; land the Viet Minh or the Front gave him is his by the Republic's law. The province chief's orders to the Regional Forces and the courts follow: no rent collection, no evictions, no landlord's agent escorted anywhere. The cycle is a single event with a weekly repetition in the provinces through the first main-season harvest, December 1964 to January 1965 [E: the Delta harvest calendar], when the first test comes: whether any landlord's agent collects. Minimum viable package: the decree, the broadcast, and the province chief's order to the Regional Forces. Without the anti-corruption programme it degrades to a law that district chiefs enforce where they choose.

#### EC001-2 · The land oil-spot: titling behind the security line

*P001a.* A mobile team of 25 deploys from the province capital at first light by road, boat or helicopter, with a Regional Forces company as escort, to a village on the inner edge of contested territory. It works the village for two to three weeks by day [M001], returning each night to the district town or the nearest post: day one, a meeting with the village committee and the enlarged photograph; days two to ten, walking the fields with the tillers to mark boundaries; days eleven to thirteen, entering claims; then a 30-day posting [M001] during which the team works the next village, coming back for the hearing of objections and the delivery of titles. Two villages a team-month [M001]. The team never works a village whose hamlets the district cannot reinforce by day within about an hour [E: the escort company's ability to hold until relief], and works outward from the province capital and the main roads. The Popular Forces squad of each titled hamlet is raised or reinforced from the titled families at the same time. Minimum viable package: the team, an escort company, the photograph, and a district chief who will send help. Without helicopter lift it degrades to the villages on roads and waterways, which in the Delta is most of them.

#### EC001-3 · Title and militia together

*P001a with the territorial forces.* In a hamlet where the government can hold at night, titles are delivered at a public ceremony at which the hamlet's Popular Forces squad, recruited from the titled families, is sworn in and armed. The title-holders' sons guard their own fields. The cycle is the titling cycle; the effect is the conversion of a Popular Forces post from an outpost of a distant government into the hamlet's own defence. Minimum viable package: titles, rifles for a squad, a radio that works, and a reaction force that comes at night. Without the reaction force it degrades into arming a squad that will be overrun, which is worse than not arming it.

#### EC001-4 · Named titles in contested hamlets (abandoned)

*P001a.* Posting claimants' names and delivering titles in person in hamlets the government holds only by day. Abandoned within the first months because it produced lists of targets: I expect the enemy's security sections to kill title holders and committee members in the first districts where names are posted [E: the predictable response of an infrastructure that kills hamlet officials]. Replaced by posting parcel numbers only and holding titles at the district office until the hamlet is secured or the tiller asks for his. The constraint it names, which the dossier described but did not weigh, is that information made public by the government in a hamlet it does not hold at night belongs to the enemy too.

#### EC001-5 · Land for loyalty (abandoned)

*P001a.* Withholding titles from families with a son in the Front, or making titles conditional on the hamlet's "loyalty". Proposed by province chiefs in the first weeks [E: the reflex of an administration used to land as patronage] and abandoned before it was tried at scale, because it destroys the programme's central claim: that the Republic confirms land to whoever tills it, including land the Front gave. A conditional title is a hostage, and the enemy would say so. The constraint it names is that the programme's political value lies entirely in its unconditionality.

#### EC001-6 · The dawn muster

*P001b.* A muster team of eight arrives at a battalion's base at first light without notice, by road or by helicopter [M001], with the battalion's adviser informed only on arrival. The battalion parades by company; every man presents his card; fingerprints are checked against the offline roll on the team's laptop; absent men are listed with the commander's explanation. A battalion takes about a day; with travel, eight battalion-equivalents a team-month [M001], so the 60 teams muster about 480 units a month [M001]. The results go the same evening by the Commission's own radio net to the Commission, the Joint General Staff and this headquarters at once. The cycle: every unit mustered about every six months, with surprise re-musters of units whose first result was bad. Minimum viable package: eight people, a vehicle or a helicopter seat, a laptop with the roll and a radio. Without the biometric roll it degrades to a head-count, which a commander defeats by borrowing men from the next unit for the day.

#### EC001-7 · Pay at the parade

*P001b.* Once a unit is enrolled, its pay no longer passes through its commander. The Muster Service's paymaster arrives with the month's pay under guard and pays each man on his card and fingerprint, and the soldier's family allotment is paid to the family at the district office on the family card. Monthly, by unit. Minimum viable package: a paymaster, a guard, the roll and the cash. It depends on the dawn muster (EC001-6) for its roll. It degrades, where the paymaster cannot reach, to the old system, which is the reason the Popular Forces in isolated posts are the last to benefit.

#### EC001-8 · The graduated lever

*P001b.* The sequence by which the Commission's findings reach the Board: a muster or audit finding is sent to the responsible Vietnamese authority with 30 days to act [U]; if nothing is done, the Board issues a notice naming the command, importer or province; if nothing is done in a further 30 days, the Board suspends the named flow, and resumes it when the Commission certifies action. The sequence is designed so that most findings are acted on at the first step by a Vietnamese authority that would rather act than be named. Cycle: a handful of notices a month, and very few suspensions [E: a lever that works is one that is rarely pulled]. It depends on the Board's American co-chair being willing to suspend; without that it degrades to a reporting system.

#### EC001-9 · The exemplary general (abandoned)

*P001b.* Opening the Commission's work with a public case against a general officer, to show that it reaches the top. Abandoned after the first attempt or never begun, because the first general accused is a member of some faction of the ruling council and his prosecution is read in Saigon as that faction's defeat, not as justice; it produces a coup alert, not a conviction. Replaced by working upward from documentary cases at battalion, district and province level. The constraint it names is that the Commission's reach at the top is set by the junta's balance of factions, not by evidence.

#### EC001-10 · American arrests (abandoned)

*P001b.* American investigators accompanying Vietnamese officers on arrests, to stiffen them. Abandoned in the first weeks because a photograph of an American agent at the arrest of a Vietnamese officer is the most valuable piece of propaganda the Front could be given, and because it makes the arresting Vietnamese officer an American agent in his own service's eyes. Americans investigate, audit and advise; Vietnamese arrest.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**F001-2** (SUBVERT) **The enemy's land argument is displaced, not destroyed.** Denied the claim that the government's return means the landlord's return, the Front moves to three positions. It claims the reform as its own victory: the puppets were forced by the revolution to give the land, and will take it back the day the revolution is defeated, so the land is still safe only with the Front. It shifts its agitation to grievances the reform does not touch: conscription, taxation by the government, the conduct of troops, corruption, and the American presence. And it shifts its taxation from consent to coercion in the hamlets it controls. The first position is the most dangerous, because it is half true: the Republic's reform was a response to the revolution, and the next junta may reverse it. The displacement costs this command something. While the land argument dominated, the Front's agitation was predictable; once it diversifies, the propaganda that answers it must answer many things. Weight: moderate.

**F001-3** (ADVISE) **Corruption moves to what the muster cannot count.** Ghost soldiers and pay theft are the forms of corruption the provision counts physically, and they are where it will bite. Corruption that is measured moves. I expect it to move to diversion of rice, fuel and ammunition, which leaves no absent man to find; to the sale of offices and transfers, which leaves no record; to kickbacks on construction and on Commercial Import Program licences, where on the historical course importers paid 4–5 piastres per dollar for a licence [S17]; and down the scale to the Popular Forces posts the muster teams reach least. Displaced landlord capital moves too: compensation cash and the rents no longer collected leave the countryside for Saigon property and import licences, feeding the urban corruption the Commission must then chase. The net reduction in corruption is real but smaller than the visible reduction in ghost soldiers will suggest, and the Commission's statistics will overstate it. Weight: moderate.

### Bottleneck migration

**F001-4** (SECURE) **In the countryside the binding constraint moves from the offer to the delivery.** Before the provision, the constraint on the government's standing in the hamlets was what it offered: a landlord's return, a tax and a draft (G-1). After it, the offer is better than the enemy's on paper [M001], and the constraint becomes whether the government can deliver it and protect it. That is night control of the hamlet (POP.night_control) and the Regional and Popular Forces who provide it (RFPF.effectiveness), and in the programme's second year the land courts' capacity to hear disputes [M001]. The provision does not loosen that constraint; it exposes it. This is the most useful sentence in this memorandum for every provision that follows: from 1964, a provision that holds hamlets at night is worth more than it was the day before, because what it holds is now worth having. Weight: major.

**F001-5** (ADVISE) **Relief for cause moves from a problem of evidence to a problem of replacements.** Before the provision an adviser could not prove a corrupt commander's corruption. After it, the muster and the Commission prove it, and the binding constraint becomes the one the theater needs assessment named: unsatisfactory commanders are seldom relieved because there are no replacements [H]. A commission that exposes faster than the officer corps can supply honest successors produces vacancies, acting commanders and units commanded by the deputy who was splitting the ghost pay with the man removed. The provision makes the officer pipeline (ARVN.leadership) the bottleneck on its own effect. Weight: moderate.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F001-6** (SUSTAIN, SECURE) **The escort companies are the real cost, not the helicopters.** The land programme's 120 teams each need a Regional Forces company by day [M001]; if all were in the field, that is up to 120 companies, roughly 12,000–15,000 men [E: companies of 100–130], out of about 96,000 Regional Forces in 1964 [S24], or one in seven [D]. They return to their posts at night, so night security is not drained, but the province chief's daytime reserve, his reaction force for posts attacked the night before, is. I judge the programme can sustain about 60–80 teams in the field in 1964, not 120, without stripping provinces of their reaction forces [E: half to two-thirds of the teams escorted at any time], which is a further reason the 1964 titling figure sits at the bottom of the §1 range. Helicopter sorties, about 200–300 a month for the land teams and about 40 for the musters [M001], are a real but secondary bid on the scarcest lift in theater (G-15). The third cost is people: the two programmes take the most honest and able of the Republic's young administrators, perhaps 2,000–3,000 of them [E: the share of the two programmes' staff drawn from the existing civil service], from the districts that need them for everything else. Weight: moderate.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F001-7** (SUBVERT) **The landlord's return behind the troops stops happening, if I stop it.** On the historical course, government clearing operations repeatedly let landlords reclaim land the insurgents had distributed [S4], and each reclamation was a recruiting event for the Front in the hamlet where it happened. The provision erases that event, but only if the RVNAF and, from 1965, American units refuse to escort landlords and their agents; the decree makes it an offence [M001], and the order is mine to give to the advisory effort and, later, to American units. What becomes of the attention it consumed: the Front loses a recurring, local, visible proof of its argument. Two historical events are erased with it: the 1970 Land to the Tiller law, which now has nothing left to do, and the long period of Ordinance 57's disuse [S1]. Weight: moderate.

### Perverse effect

**F001-8** (SECURE) **Success in a contested hamlet makes its people targets, and leaves more rice within the enemy's reach.** Two perverse effects run from the land programme's success. First, a titled family in a hamlet the government does not hold at night has accepted something from the government, and the enemy's security sections will make examples of some of them; the more successful the programme in contested hamlets, the more such killings, until the titling team learns to stop at the security line (EC001-4). POP.civilian_harm rises locally. Second, the rent the tenant no longer pays used to leave the hamlet for a landlord in the district town or Saigon. It now stays in the hamlet, and in hamlets the Front taxes, part of it reaches the Front. In the first year, while much tenanted land lies in contested or enemy hamlets, the reform modestly increases the rice available to the enemy's tax collectors [E: the share of tenanted land outside government control at night], until the hamlets are held. Weight: moderate.

**F001-9** (ADVISE) **The muster shrinks the army in the year Washington is counting it.** If 5–10% of the rolls are ghosts [M001], the first round of musters strikes 25,000–50,000 men from an army whose paper strength in 1964 is about 514,000 [S24]. The RVNAF has not lost a soldier; it has learned its size. But the strength return falls in the year in which Washington measures Vietnamese effort by force levels, and in which the junta's legitimacy with Washington rests on promises of expansion. The commanders whose ghost income is cut lose a share of their pay they had treated as their due; some will compensate by stealing elsewhere (F001-3) and some will look to a different junta. A perverse but honest result: the provision's most reliable success is reported as a reverse. Weight: moderate.

### Anticipation

**F001-10** (SUBVERT) **The enemy's best response is not to fight the titles but to let them be counted.** The decline-battle adaptation has a political analogue here. The infrastructure does not need to stop peasants taking titles. It can tell them to take the puppet's paper, keep it, and keep paying the Front, and wait to see who is in the hamlet in two years. Titles issued then rises steadily while the allegiance the titles were meant to change moves much more slowly, and "titles issued" becomes this programme's Hamlet Evaluation System: a figure produced by the organisation rated on it, rising while the war does not move. Under A13 I will not cite titles issued as evidence of progress. The indicators that will show whether the reform is changing allegiance are the ones the enemy cannot help generating: tax receipts in captured VCI ledgers, rice sold to government markets in titled districts, voluntary Popular Forces enlistment, and unprompted information from titled hamlets. Weight: major (for the measurement of the war, not its course).

**F001-11** (ADVISE) **The muster works most where it catches least.** Commanders who know a muster team may arrive at dawn on any day strike their ghosts from the rolls before it comes. In the units where this happens the muster records no ghosts, the Commission brings no prosecutions, and the provision's effect, pay and rations for men who exist and a strength return that is true, is complete and invisible. The improvement will be under-reported by every measure the Commission keeps, and I record now that a falling rate of ghosts found is evidence of success, not of a problem that was smaller than estimated. Weight: moderate.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F001-12** (SUBVERT) **Hanoi detects it on the day of the broadcast, and draws a correct conclusion that makes 1964 more dangerous.** There is nothing covert about a land law. COSVN and the Party's southern committees know its terms within days, and the district cells within weeks [E: the Party's courier network and its access to government radio]. The Party's Ninth Plenum resolved in December 1963 to seek decisive military victory in the South before the United States could intervene in strength [H]. The correct reading of the reform, from Hanoi, is that the Front's political base is now perishable: a Republic that gives land to the tiller and survives a few years will erode the southern movement from beneath. The lesson the Party draws is to win quickly, before the reform takes hold, and to keep the government out of the hamlets so that it cannot. That reinforces the decision to commit northern regulars and to move to mobile warfare in 1964–65. What the enemy gets right, and what hurts: the reform is worth exactly what night security is worth, and it depends on a junta that may reverse it, so the counter is to deny the government the hamlets and wait for the coup. What it may get wrong: Party doctrine treats peasant support as a function of class consciousness organised by the Party; it may underestimate how quickly a family's support follows a title it can keep, and so underestimate the erosion in the hamlets the government holds. The doctrine propagates from COSVN to district cells within one or two months [E: the period's Party directive cycle], and its application everywhere is the same: hold the night. Weight: major.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F001-13** (SUBVERT) **The cheap counters are waiting and coercion; the expensive one is competing.** Claiming credit for the reform costs the Front nothing. Waiting costs it nothing in the hamlets it holds. Coercion, killing title holders and committee members in contested hamlets, is cheap in cadre and expensive in standing, because the Front must now kill peasants for owning land it says it gave them. Competing on terms, lowering its own taxes, which Race found sometimes five to seven times the government's [S5], is expensive: it cuts the revenue that pays the main force's expansion in exactly the year the Party has decided to expand it. The exchange rate is therefore favourable to the Republic where it holds the hamlets, roughly neutral where they are contested, and favourable to the enemy where the enemy holds them, because there the reform costs the enemy nothing and the government nothing it can deliver. For the anti-corruption programme the enemy's adaptation is almost free: it loses some purchased agents and some black-market supply, and gains new grievances among the officers the Commission injures. Weight: moderate.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F001-14** (SUSTAIN) **The loop through Washington is slow, small, and positive on balance, with two negative spikes.** Theater effect: a visible reform programme and an anti-corruption commission. Return path: a Congress and press that, on the historical course, would come to see the Republic as a regime of landlords and grafters gain evidence to the contrary, which modestly lengthens tolerance (US.political_tolerance) and narrows the credibility gap (US.credibility_gap) as the musters give this headquarters strength figures that do not need defending. Two spikes run the other way. The first round of corruption cases produces headlines about corruption, not about reform, and the muster produces a smaller army (F001-9). And the compensation bonds need appropriations for eight years [M001], which makes the reform's credibility with the landlords hostage to each year's Congress. The loop returns to theater as a small increase in Washington's willingness to fund the pacification side of the war and to accept the Board's suspensions. It does not change troop decisions, the air war or the clock's rate in 1964. Weight: minor.

### Open channel

**F001-15** (ADVISE) **The provisions change what a coup is for.** Until now a junta in Saigon has had no constituency in the countryside and no programme that a successor would hesitate to repeal; coups changed the men at the top and nothing below. The reforms give the junta that executes them something to lose and something to claim: a rural constituency of titled families and a record that the United States has made the condition of its support. A coup against a reforming government now has to say whether it will keep the reform. If the United States says plainly that it will support no government that reverses either programme, a coup becomes harder to justify and less rewarding, because its makers inherit the reforms' enemies without the option of placating them. If the United States says nothing, the reforms become a coup programme for the injured. This channel runs in either direction according to a decision in Washington, which I will ask for in §4. Weight: moderate.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F001-16 · Counter-directional** (ADVISE, SECURE) **The two provisions make the Republic's political centre less stable in the year it is weakest.** On the opening date the junta is two months old and, on the historical course, will be overthrown by its own members on 30 January [H]. The provisions injure at once the two groups from which coups are made: the landed families of the officer corps, whose rents end on promulgation, and the commanders whose income comes from ghost pay, purchased commands and diverted supplies. Each provision alone is a coup grievance; together they unite grievances that would otherwise be held by different men. The Board, the provision's own enforcement mechanism, adds the charge of American control (POSTNA-6), which the coup-makers can turn against the reforming government as easily as the Front can. And the reform's first-year effects, rice left within the enemy's reach in contested hamlets (F001-8) and Hanoi's decision to hurry (F001-12), run against the Republic before its benefits arrive. I weigh this as a major effect against a major primary effect, and it is concentrated in 1964–65: the provisions make the next eighteen months more dangerous politically and militarily, in return for a foundation that pays over years. The command will not welcome it, because the period it worsens is the period in which the historical war was very nearly lost.

**F001-17 · Strongest positive** (SECURE, SUBVERT) **The government's arrival becomes a guarantee instead of a threat, six years early, for most of the tenant families in the South.** The most significant thing the provisions change is not titles issued but the meaning of the government's presence to about 855,000 Delta tenant families and several hundred thousand in the Centre [M001]. From the day of the decree, a tenant whose hamlet the government enters stops paying rent and keeps whatever land the Front gave him; a tenant whose hamlet the government leaves risks nothing from the government but keeps the Front's tax and conscription. For the first time since 1954, the Republic's offer to the peasant is better than the enemy's, and the Commission makes it more likely that the offer arrives without a bribe attached (F001-1). On the historical course this happened only in 1970, after the Front's southern base had been gutted in 1968 and when the American withdrawal was already under way; the record's verdict on it was "too little, too late" [S1]. Here it happens before the Front has committed its main force to mobile war and before a single American combat unit has landed. I estimate that by the end of 1965 the programme can have titled about 400,000–600,000 hectares [E: the 1964 rate rising as security spreads around Saigon and along the Bassac], with rent ended in law and largely in fact across government-held territory. It closes no gap by itself. It is the first provision that touches G-1, the deficit POSTNA-1 says must move before any rung above (b) is reachable, and it creates the precondition on which every SECURE provision that follows will build. Weight: transformative in potential, realised slowly and only behind security.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

Three changes. First, every battalion and province adviser gains a true strength return for his unit from the dawn muster (EC001-6) and a channel to the Commission that does not run through his counterpart. Advisers' reporting of corruption, which on the historical course was suppressed because it destroyed the relationship (G-6), now has somewhere to go. Second, province advisers acquire a new line of work: watching the land programme in their provinces for fees, escorts of landlords and reversals, alongside the aid mission's land advisers. Third, this headquarters issues a standing order, through the advisory chain, that no adviser will accompany, and no American aircraft or vehicle will carry, any operation whose purpose or effect is to restore a landlord or his agent to land the decree has transferred (F001-7). MACV's personnel staff gains a muster cell to receive the Commission's returns, and the J2 a small section to read the land register for what it says about hamlets. The advisory effort still does not reach the districts, the police or the Popular Forces in strength; nothing in this memorandum extends it.

### US conventional forces

None are in the country on the opening date; on the historical course the first arrive at Da Nang in March 1965 [H]. When they come, they inherit two obligations from this memorandum: no escort of landlords, and respect for titled land in the conduct of operations and the siting of bases, with damage to titled crops paid for through the land courts rather than through a claims system the peasant cannot reach. That is a change in conduct, not capability.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

Largely unchanged. The CIDG camps are in the highlands and on the frontiers, and the land programme excludes the highlands and Montagnard land [M001]. One change: the CIDG is paid through Special Forces and American channels rather than the RVNAF, so the Muster Service does not reach it, and the dawn muster would expose nothing there that the A-teams do not already know. The Nung and Montagnard strikers are untouched by both provisions, and the separate problem of Montagnard land rights, which the provision explicitly does not address, remains a grievance the Front can work.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

The divisions are mustered within about nine months [M001] and their pay goes to their soldiers. The three divisions in the Delta, the 7th, 9th and 21st [H], operate in the provinces that hold most of the tenanted land, and their conduct toward the land programme is the single largest factor in whether it works there: an operation that clears a district and lets the landlord back undoes a year of titling in a week. The Joint General Staff carries a new political weight: its chief is the officer who must order the dawn muster's right of entry, and the corps commanders, who are also the junta's power brokers, will judge him by how he uses it. The divisions' immediate fighting power, their infantry battalions and the M113 mechanized rifle squadrons of their armored cavalry [H], changes little; their strength returns become true; some commanders are relieved, and the replacements' quality is the constraint (F001-5).

### RF/PF and territorial security

This is where both provisions land hardest on an existing force. The Regional Forces, about 96,000 in 1964 [S24], carry the land programme by day as escort (F001-6), and the province chief's daytime reaction force shrinks accordingly. The Popular Forces, about 168,000 [S24], recruited from the hamlets, gain the thing that pay alone never gave them: in titled hamlets, the fields they guard belong to their own families (EC001-3). They are also the last to benefit from pay at the parade, because the paymasters reach the isolated posts least (EC001-7). The posts remain as vulnerable as they were, held by men armed with M1 and M2 carbines [H]: they still need a radio that works, an AN/PRC-10 that is charged or, from 1965, an AN/PRC-25 [H], and a reaction that comes at night. Neither provision supplies those. The night reaction that exists is VNAF A-1H and A-1E Skyraiders and T-28s when they can fly at night, and from December 1964 the first AC-47 gunships [H]; the land programme makes their arrival over a titled hamlet worth more and their failure to arrive cost more.

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. The air campaign against the North begins on 2 March 1965 on the historical course [H], flown largely by F-105D and F-100D squadrons from Thailand and South Vietnam [H], and nothing in either provision bears on it.

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged. The Yankee Team reconnaissance flights over Laos that begin in May 1964 with RF-101C and Navy RF-8A aircraft [H], and the strikes that follow, are untouched. Tonnage arriving from the North is not affected by who owns rice land in the Delta. The one indirect link runs the other way: the Front's southern rice and revenue, which the reform will slowly erode, are what make the Trail's small external requirement sufficient, and a southern movement with less rice and fewer recruits needs more from the North. That effect is years away and is carried into §4 as a possibility, not a fact.

### Arc Light

Unchanged in capability. When B-52F strikes begin in June 1965 [H], their targeting must take account of titled land in the same way artillery and tactical air must: a strike that destroys titled hamlets in a contested province spends what the reform bought there.

### Airmobile formations

The CH-21C and UH-1B companies of the advisory effort, and in I Corps the Marine UH-34D squadron, are asked for about 240–340 sorties a month for land teams and musters [M001]. That is a real bid on lift that the operations staff will resist, and I have allocated it below the ARVN's operational needs and above training lifts [U: COMUSMACV's priority]. The 1st Cavalry Division, when it arrives in 1965 with its UH-1D and CH-47A fleet [H], is unaffected.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The ARVN River Assault Groups' LCM-6 and LCVP craft [H] carry land teams on Delta waterways, a modest additional task that fits their patrol schedules. The Junk Force is unchanged. The Market Time patrols of 1965 onward are unaffected.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Does not exist on the opening date; on the historical course it is created in 1967 [H]. When it comes, it inherits from this memorandum a land register that is also a household census of every titled hamlet, a habit of complaints to an independent body, and a Commission whose court has a record of convicting on evidence. Each of these could make the later programme better; the Commission could also become the body that watches it.

### Third-country forces

The only third-country military presence on the opening date is the small Australian Army Training Team [H]; it is unaffected. When Korean forces arrive in 1965 [H], their own country's land reform of 1950 makes them a source of example to Vietnamese officers, though not one I would rely on.

### Prior provisions

None. This is the first memorandum, and no earlier provision exists whose employment could change.
