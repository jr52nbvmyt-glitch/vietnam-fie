# Memorandum 008 — Part 2 of 3 · §2 Integration and second-order effects · §3 Changes to existing forces


## §2 Part A — Integration

The verdict first. The electronics base is a hamlet-security provision delivered through a factory, and its first-order effect is a radio that works at two in the morning in a Popular Forces post, two to four years before history put one there. The metallurgical base is a sustainment provision whose weight is deferred to 1973–75 and is held hostage to a decision this headquarters has to make in 1964: whether the Republic's armed forces will be made to buy from it while American stocks are free. If we make them, it is the first thing in eight memoranda that reaches the Republic's ability to fight after the money shrinks. If we do not, it builds bridges and outpost wire for ten years and is an empty arsenal when it matters.

Neither base finds, fixes or kills anyone. Both depend on the night security of the countryside for their reach and on the Saigon–Bien Hoa triangle for their existence. Both put new things into the hands of a population the Front taxes and an army the Front has penetrated, and some of what they make will come back at us in mines, booby traps and captured radios.

### Functional systems

**SUSTAIN** is the system both bases join directly. The metallurgical base adds a second, Vietnamese tier under the American theater base: construction and fortification steel, prefabricated short bridge spans, engine and transmission rebuild, the rebuild of M113 carriers and M41 light tanks, cartridge cases and bullets for the 5.56mm and 7.62mm, and the bodies of 60mm and 81mm bombs for the M2 and M29 mortars and of 105mm shells for the M101A1 howitzer, all filled with imported propellant and explosive. The electronics base adds WD-1/TT field wire, BA-386 dry cells for the manpacks, AN/VRC-46 and AN/VRC-12 vehicle sets, and depot repair of radios, generators, switchboards and AN/PPS-5 radar modules in days rather than months¹. Neither touches the American theater base itself, which runs on its own historical lines. What changes is that the RVNAF has a supply system that is partly its own.

**SECURE** is the system the electronics base strengthens most, and the reason it matters. The settled position of the Theater Needs Assessment is that a hamlet is held at night only by security resident in it, with a means of calling help, and a reaction that arrives in the dark. Earlier memoranda supplied the first term in the held belt: the police post and its Popular Forces platoon, rearmed so that it is not outgunned, under a district chief chosen for ability. Nothing supplied the second term at scale. The record puts an AN/PRC-10 or an HT-1 village set in each police post from 1964², and the Popular Forces post of memorandum 004 is drawn with a PRC-10 to the district³. That is the radio on the inventory. What was missing was the radio on the air: the battery that has not run flat, the handset that has not drowned, the set that comes back from repair in a week rather than a season, and a set for the second and third posts in the village that the police never covered.

**STRIKE** is touched at its lowest rung. More territorial stations on the district nets mean more calls for fire reaching the district's M29 81mm mortars, the province's M101A1 105mm howitzers and, where the net plan allows, American fire-direction centres. The fire is no faster than before once a call arrives. More calls arrive, and they arrive from posts that were silent.

**ADVISE** is where the metallurgical base's fate is decided. It compels nothing; it is a supplier, and the RVNAF's habit, which the Americans built, is to draw American stock that costs it nothing¹. Whether the arsenal runs depends on whether the American lever is used to make the RVNAF order from it. The ordnance advisers at MACV J-4 and the area logistics commands, the signal advisers at J-6, and the Aid Compliance Board of P001b are the instruments.

**Newly viable** is one thing only: a Republic that can, in 1974–75, turn an aid dollar into more ammunition, more spare parts and many more radio batteries than the historical Republic could. Nothing else in the force has made that possible, and nothing else does it now.

**Untouched** are FIND, FIX, INTERDICT and SUBVERT, with the small exceptions below.

**F008-1** **The radio is the middle term of the settled position, and this is the first provision that supplies it industrially.** In the held belt from 1965–66 the post has its platoon, its rifles, its chief and now a means of calling help that is kept working by a battery works at Bien Hoa and a rebuild depot at Long Binh. That completes two of the three terms of POSTNA-2. The third, the reaction that arrives in the dark, is not made in any factory. Where it exists, I expect the posts to hold measurably more often and the Front to attack them less. Where it does not, this provision gives the post a way to report its own fall⁴.

**F008-2** **The base gives the American lever its first object in the Republic's self-sufficiency.** The checkpoint found that the lever was used to protect honest institutions and never to make the Republic own the instruments of its war, and the meta-checkpoint said the rung would not move until a provision reached sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation. This base is such a provision only if the lever is used on it. The decision is mine to propose and CINCPAC's to approve, and it costs money and Vietnamese pride in the years when neither seems to matter.

### Compound pairings

Only these pairings produce compound effects.

- **P008b with P002a, P004a and P002b: the post that can call.** The constabulary post with its Popular Forces platoon beside it, rearmed with carbines, under a merit-selected chief who keeps his reaction force awake. Each was worth less alone. The radio is the hinge between the post and the chief: a call that reaches a district operations room staffed by a chief who has held the district two years, and who knows which RF company can reach which post by which canal, is a call that produces a boat at 0300.
- **P008b with P006c: the subsector team that answers.** The selected adviser on an eighteen-month tour, qualified to call fire, sits in the district operations room the radio feeds. His standing order to hand over the radio now has more Vietnamese radios to hand over to, and Vietnamese fire to call.
- **P008a with P004a and P004c: the carbine's cartridge and the squad gun's links.** The arsenal makes 5.56mm cases and bullets, M249 links, magazine bodies and springs¹. In 1974 the ration fell first on the squad gun, and the arsenal is the only thing in the force that can put more rounds behind it for the same dollar.
- **P008a with P001b: the Commission and the scrap trade.** The arsenal's contracts, its scrap licences and its draft deferments are the next great field of corruption, and the Commission's audit and the Muster Service's identity roll are the instruments that police them.
- **P008a with P001a: the pump on the titled field.** Small engines and pumps from the agricultural shops reach families who own their land and keep their surplus, and an owner invests where a tenant would not.
- **P008a and P008b with P007b: the technical-service officer.** Staff, logistics and signal officers educated since 1957 to run their own systems⁵ are the customers the plants need: men who can write a specification, accept a lot and refuse a bad one.

**Interactions with earlier provisions**

- P008b × P006c: the subsector adviser's handover of the radio now has Vietnamese sets and Vietnamese fire to hand over to; the territorial net feeds the district team that stays

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

Neither provision touches it, and I will not pretend otherwise. A PAVN regiment under the canopy of War Zone C or the Chu Pong massif is exactly as hard to find as it was. Two small effects exist and both are second-hand. The Long Binh depot repairs AN/PPS-5 radar modules and helicopter avionics units on the bench in country, so a few more ground-surveillance radars and a few more VNAF and ARVN-supported helicopters are serviceable on a given night⁶. More radios in more hamlets also mean more reports of armed men moving at dusk. Those reports come from a population that does not volunteer what it knows and over a net the enemy can hear, and I expect them to locate local-force platoons, not main-force regiments. The find-to-fix chain is where it was.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

Here the effect is small and runs partly the wrong way. A radio does not tell the Special Branch who in the hamlet is the Front's finance cadre; the informant who knows will not say it over a set the whole hamlet can hear. What the radio adds is speed: a post that holds a man caught in the night check can tell the district at once, and a warrant raid can be called off by radio when the target moves. What it subtracts is secrecy. The territorial net has no encryption below division, the plant cannot build the KY-38 NESTOR¹, and the Front listens. A district centre that coordinates a raid on the Popular Forces net announces it. The rule has to be that nothing about a Phung Hoang target goes on the territorial net, and the centres must keep their own couriers and their own few secure sets. I count this as a cost the provision imposes and the procedure can contain, not as a contribution.

### Dependence on SECURE

The electronics base is almost wholly a SECURE provision, and its dependence on hamlet security is total. A radio in a post the Government holds at night is a lifeline; a radio in a post that falls is the Front's radio, and its battery is the power for a booby trap⁷. The metallurgical base depends on security in two places: the triangle between Saigon, Bien Hoa and Thu Duc where it stands, and the roads and the Da Nhim line through which its power, inputs and output move.

Consider a post on the Mang Thit canal in Vinh Long in the dry season of 1966. It has a Popular Forces platoon of about thirty, an AN/PRC-25 from Thu Duc on the district net, two spare batteries and a third on order⁸. At 0150 a sentry hears men in the water hyacinth. The platoon leader calls the district at Tam Binh; the operations room, which the district chief has run for two years, raises the Regional Forces boat company and the two M29 81mm mortars that cover the canal, and the first rounds land within about fifteen minutes⁹. The company of the local-force battalion that had planned to assault at 0200 does not. In 1964 the same post had a PRC-10 whose battery had died on the Tuesday, and the district learned of the attack at dawn from a woman on a sampan. The difference is not the radio on the inventory. It is the battery and the repair cycle, and the district chief at the other end.

The effect differs by region, and the record has too often told only the Delta.

- **The Delta.** The effect is largest here, because the posts are many, the reaction can travel by water at night, and the territorial forces carry the war. A post on a canal in Kien Hoa or Vinh Long that can call the boat company is the case the provision was built for. I expect the Delta's held belt to show it first and most.
- **The coastal lowlands of I and II Corps.** In Quang Nam and Binh Dinh the posts are thick in the rice land, but the enemy there includes PAVN and main-force regiments, as in the northern districts of Binh Dinh around Hoai Nhon and Tam Quan, where a regiment in the hamlets in 1972 undid eight years of posts. A radio lets a post report that a regiment has arrived. It does not let the post survive the regiment.
- **The Highlands and their peoples.** Montagnard hamlets and the CIDG camps are far from any reaction that comes in the dark, and the Ministry of Interior allots the hamlet sets through province chiefs who distrust the highland peoples. After the FULRO rising of September 1964, I expect Saigon to withhold radios from Rhadé and Jarai hamlets as it withheld arms. The radio will reach the Highlands' Vietnamese settlements and the camps, not the Montagnard villages, unless this headquarters makes it a condition. I intend to try, and I do not expect to win the argument quickly.
- **The Saigon belt.** Here the provision's effect is the plants themselves: about 55,000–85,000 jobs in Bien Hoa, Thu Duc, Long Binh and Saigon¹, a target the Front must decide whether to attack, and a security task for III Corps and the Hop Tac ring. The radios matter less here, because the reaction is close and the posts are fewer.

On rice and the piaster, the effect is real and modest. The agricultural shops' motor pumps and small diesel engines reach the held Delta's owner-cultivators, whom the land programme gave a surplus to invest. Pumps make a second crop possible on fields that can be watered in the dry season, and from 1967 the IR8 rice that historically reached the Delta needs controlled water. I expect the held belt's rice output to rise by a few percent more than the land programme alone would have raised it, and the Front's take to rise with it in the villages where the Front still taxes, because a pump is a visible asset and its diesel passes the Front's tax gates on the canals⁴. Procurement by the Government benefits only where the Government holds the night. On the piaster, the base pays about 55,000–85,000 wages in piasters and absorbs piasters by selling pumps, radios and steel at home, and on balance I put its effect on inflation at a small downward pressure, far below the war's spending¹.

On the soldier's family: neither plant raises a soldier's pay, which inflation still erodes as before. What the electronics base does is employ about 15,000–25,000 young women on assembly¹⁰, many of them within reach of the base camps and dependants' quarters of III Corps at Bien Hoa, Long Binh and Thu Duc. For those families there is a second wage. For the families of I and II Corps, there is nothing.

### The ADVISE cluster

The ADVISE cluster is where the metallurgical base will live or die, and the base gives the cluster a question it has not faced in seven memoranda: whether to use the lever to make the Republic own something.

The RVNAF in 1964 draws its ammunition, spare parts and radios from American aid, free at the point of issue. An arsenal round costs it piasters. A 105mm shell body made at Bien Hoa and filled with imported explosive costs less than the American round delivered, but it costs the Vietnamese budget something and the American stock costs it nothing¹. Every incentive the RVNAF's quartermasters face points to the American depot. In 1965–67, when American ammunition is short enough that MACV imposes controlled supply rates, the arsenal will be used because it must be. In 1968–72, when the American pipeline is full, it will idle unless someone makes it run.

I propose to make it run. From 1965, a fixed and rising share of the RVNAF's consumption of small-arms cartridges, mortar bombs and 105mm rounds, of its rebuild of engines and carriers, and of its radios and all of its batteries and field wire is bought from the domestic base with Military Assistance Program funds converted to piasters, and the corresponding American deliveries are reduced. The share would start at about a fifth and rise to about half by 1969 for the items the base can make¹¹. The Aid Compliance Board of P001b enforces it as it enforces appointment through the list. This is advisory leverage spent on self-sufficiency, and the settled position says no use of the lever is free. Its price is plain: higher costs per round in the years we are paying for everything, a quarrel with the American ammunition and electronics industries that lose orders, and Vietnamese generals who will see an American instruction about where to buy their shells as an affront. Its gain is a working arsenal in 1974.

The advisory corps of P006c has a part here too. The ordnance and signal advisers at the depots and area logistics commands, on eighteen-month tours with working Vietnamese, are the men who will see whether lots are honestly accepted and whether the radios reach the Popular Forces platoons or stay in headquarters. Their reports go to the Board. The educated technical-service officers of P007b are their counterparts. This is the first time in the exercise that the cluster's people and the cluster's lever point at the same physical thing.

### Employment concepts

Both bases are institutions, so their concepts are routines: what a lot, a span, a radio or a battery does in a week.

#### EC008-1 · The ninety-day road

The bridge works at Bien Hoa builds prefabricated steel spans of 10–30 metres to three or four standard designs, galvanised and stocked at the area logistics commands at Da Nang, Qui Nhon, Saigon and Can Tho¹². When the Front drops a bridge on Route 4 or Route 1, the province engineer reports it by radio; a span set leaves the nearest stock within 24–48 hours on two or three trucks with a crane truck; an ARVN engineer platoon with a security company places it on the existing abutments in one to two days, or on timber cribs where the abutments are gone¹³. The cycle from demolition to traffic is about five to ten days for a short span, against weeks to months when steel had to be ordered from abroad or a Bailey bridge borrowed from an American engineer battalion¹⁴. At full rate the works turns out 150–300 spans a year, three to six a week¹. The minimum package is a span set, a crane truck, an engineer platoon and a company to guard it. Without P008b's radio the report reaches the province a day later; without an engineer platoon the span sits in a depot.

#### EC008-2 · The domestic share

From 1965 a fixed share of the RVNAF's consumption of items the base makes is bought from it with aid funds converted to piasters, and American deliveries are reduced by the same amount; the Aid Compliance Board enforces the share. Each month the arsenal presents lots; an inspectorate of Vietnamese quality engineers with American ordnance advisers draws samples, proof-fires every barrel and a sample of every shell-body lot, and accepts or rejects. Accepted lots go to the area logistics commands on the same trucks and barges as American stock. The cycle is a month from order to acceptance and two to six weeks from acceptance to the division's ammunition point. The share is about a fifth in 1965 rising to about half by 1969 for cartridges, mortar bombs, 105mm bodies, batteries and wire¹⁵. The concept depends on P001b's Board for enforcement and on P007b's officers for honest acceptance. Without the Board it degrades into the starvation of orders that Part 1 named the likeliest failure¹.

#### EC008-3 · The closed rebuild loop

A division turns in an M113 with a cracked transmission at its area logistics command; it goes by low-boy or barge to the rebuild works at Go Vap or Bien Hoa, where the carrier is stripped, its engine and transmission rebuilt or replaced from rebuilt stock, its hull welded, and it is returned in about 60–90 days¹⁶. The same line takes the M35 trucks that carry the divisions' rice and ammunition. Historically such work for the RVNAF went offshore or waited on American depots and took six to nine months, if it happened¹⁷. At full rate the works returns 400–600 carriers and tanks, 3,000–5,000 engines and 1,500–2,500 transmissions a year, about ten carriers a week¹. The loop also rebuilds trucks, generators and the small diesels of the Popular Forces' boats. It needs parts and bearings bought abroad, and it needs the division to turn the vehicle in rather than cannibalise it.

#### EC008-4 · The outpost kit

A standard kit of barbed and concertina wire, steel pickets, reinforcing bar, culvert sheet and roofing for a Popular Forces post, issued by the district to the platoon when it builds or rebuilds a post, with sandbags and cement from the Government's other channels¹⁸. Each kit is a few tonnes, two truckloads or a barge¹⁹. At 120,000–180,000 tonnes a year of construction steel the base could, in principle, build or rebuild every post in the country in a year¹; in practice the limit is the district's trucks and the province chief's honesty. The kit is issued against the post, counted by the Muster Service's inspectors on their visits, and its theft is a Commission case. Without that, about a quarter of the wire is sold before it reaches the post²⁰.

#### EC008-5 · The Vietnamese carbine (abandoned)

In 1965 the Directorate of Defence Industry proposes to make the whole M4A1 upper receiver in country rather than its small parts and, later, its barrels. The arsenal's first barrel lots fail proof at a high rate and its receivers are out of tolerance, as Korea's first M16s were slow and imprecise in 1971–72²¹, and the URG-I's components are 2026 machining the 1964 base cannot match¹. The project consumes the arsenal's best machinists for a year. It is stopped in 1966. The arsenal keeps magazines, springs and small parts, and after 1968 chrome-lined barrels for rebuild. The constraint it names is precision, which a generation's leap in a steel mill does not deliver.

#### EC008-6 · Open-market scrap (abandoned)

In 1966 the steelworks buys battlefield scrap from anyone who brings it to the yards at Bien Hoa, by weight, at a fixed price. Within months the scrap yards are receiving brass from American firing points, pierced steel plank lifted from airstrips still in use, and new pickets pulled from outpost wire, and the Front's district finance sections are selling scrap through intermediaries to fund themselves²². The concept is replaced by scrap bought only from the American and RVNAF property-disposal yards and from licensed dealers under the Commission's audit. The constraint it names is that anything the base buys at a fixed price, the country will strip to sell it.

#### EC008-7 · The night net

Every Popular Forces platoon post and every Regional Forces company in the held belt carries a PRC-25 class set from 1966 (a PRC-10 or hamlet set before, an AN/PRC-77 from 1969), on the district's territorial net. At 1800 the post checks in; through the night it answers the district's check every hour or two, and a post that misses two checks is treated as under attack and the district calls it on the alternative net and alerts the reaction²³. On contact the post calls fire and the reaction force by pre-arranged codes for its own registered targets. The cycle is nightly, every night. A post on listening watch burns a battery in about three to five nights¹, so a district of forty posts uses roughly 300–400 batteries a month. The minimum package is a set, an antenna that reaches the district, two spare batteries, and a district operations room that is manned all night. Without P002b's chief, the room is empty after midnight. Without the reaction force, the net is a list of posts falling in order.

#### EC008-8 · The battery run and the exchange

Each week the district's resupply, by road with the rice convoy or by boat, carries fresh batteries to every post and takes back the spent ones, which are counted and destroyed so that they do not become booby-trap power sources⁷. A set that fails is exchanged at the district from a float of about one in ten, and the failed set goes to the province and on to Long Binh, which returns it in one to three weeks²⁴. The concept is what turns a radio on the inventory into a radio on the air, and its tempo is weekly. It depends on the road or the canal being open, on P001b's muster inspectors counting the batteries, and on the district chief not selling them.

#### EC008-9 · The hamlet alarm

The village and hamlet set from Thu Duc, about 20,000–30,000 a year¹, goes to hamlet chiefs and village offices in the held belt on the Ministry of Interior's net through the district. It carries one message that matters: armed men are here. It is used for the alarm, the request for a medical evacuation and the morning report. It is not used for anything the Front should not hear, because the Front hears it.

#### EC008-10 · The handover of the call

The territorial net feeds Vietnamese fire first: the district's mortars, the province's howitzers, the VNAF's C-47 flare ship and the RVNAF's boats. The subsector adviser listens and helps; he calls American fire only when Vietnamese fire cannot reach²⁵. This is the practice P006c established for the regular units, extended to the posts, and it is possible because the posts now have radios on a Vietnamese net that Vietnamese fire answers. Where the province has no artillery within range of a post, it degrades to the American adviser's call, and the dependence returns.

#### EC008-11 · A radio in every hamlet (abandoned)

In 1965 the Ministry of Interior, with ample sets from Thu Duc, issues hamlet radios to every hamlet with a hamlet chief, including those in the contested belt the Front holds at night. Within a year several thousand are captured, surrendered or sold²⁶, and the district nets in the contested provinces carry the Front's own traffic and its false alarms, one of which in the concept's history draws a Regional Forces company into an ambush²⁷. The issue is restricted in 1966 to hamlets the district rates as held at night, and the sets in contested hamlets are withdrawn where they can be. The constraint it names is POSTNA-2 restated: the radio belongs to whoever holds the hamlet after dark.

#### EC008-12 · The post on the artillery net (abandoned)

In 1966 a few provinces put Popular Forces posts directly on the American and ARVN artillery fire-direction nets, to cut out the district. The nets are flooded with untrained calls, the clearance rules of the standing rules of engagement still require the district chief's approval for fire near a hamlet²⁸, and captured sets on the fire net let the Front call fire on its own terms. The concept is abandoned for the district relay of EC008-7. The constraint it names is that the call for fire is a political act in a populated country, and the district chief is where that act is cleared.

## §2 Part B — Channels

The traverse covers SECURE, SUSTAIN and ADVISE, which the bases touch heavily, and STRIKE and SUBVERT, which they touch lightly. The primary contribution is the radio on the air at the post from 1964–66 and the stretch of the aid dollar in 1974–75; every effect below is weighed against those two.

### Displacement

**F008-3** **The Front's attack moves from the post to the relief it summons.** The Front had a name for it long before this provision: attack the post to destroy the relief. A post that can call help changes the attack's object. A district company no longer assaults a post with its platoon, its carbines and a working radio. It fires a few mortar rounds and a B40 rocket at it, waits for the call, and lays its ambush on the canal or the road the boat company or the Regional Forces company must use. In the Delta the ambush is at a bend in the canal where the boats slow; in Binh Dinh it is at the culvert on the district road. The radio has not removed the danger; it has moved it from thirty men behind wire to a hundred men on a road at night. Where the district chief varies the route, sends the reaction by water when the road is likely, and holds the reaction until the post's own fire and the mortars have done their work, the exchange still favours us. Where he sends the same company by the same road every time, the Front kills it. The attack also moves to the posts that have no radio or a dead battery, which in 1964–65 means the posts of the contested belt and the Highlands, and from 1966 means the posts whose batteries were sold.

**F008-4** **Sabotage moves to the Da Nhim line and the plants' inputs.** The line from Da Nhim to Saigon crosses about 250 km of the Highlands and the eastern provinces, and the Front can drop a pylon with a few kilograms of explosive. It will. The steelworks stops when the line is cut, and the arsenal and rebuild works run on the thermal station at Thu Duc¹. A cut pylon costs days; a destroyed transformer costs months. I expect the line cut a few times a year from 1965, and the steel output to run a tenth to a fifth below rated in consequence⁴. The plants themselves, inside the Saigon triangle, are attacked rarely before 1968 and at Tet, when Bien Hoa and Long Binh are fought over, they lose weeks.

**F008-5** **The Front's metal and battery economy moves onto our output.** The Front's workshops in the base areas make mines, grenades and rocket tubes from scavenged metal and need batteries for detonators and radios: the DH-10 directional mine, the Front's answer to the M18A1 Claymore, is fired by a battery and a length of wire²⁹. In 1964 they scavenge from American waste and from what the Trail brings. From 1965 the Republic's own market is full of Vietnamese-made wire, steel plate, brass and dry cells, sold by quartermasters and by pilferers from the depots and the posts¹. The Front's district finance sections buy them through the same merchants who buy its rice. The volume that leaks is small against the base's output, perhaps a few percent of its wire, brass and batteries, but small against the base is large against the Front's needs: a few percent of 1.5–3 million dry cells is tens of thousands of batteries a year⁴. Every booby trap triggered by a Thu Duc battery will be reported, and the press will find it.

### Bottleneck migration

**F008-6** **At the post, the binding constraint moves from the means of calling to the reaction that answers.** Before this provision, in much of 1964–65, the call often never went out: the battery was dead, the set was in repair, or the post had none³⁰. After it, from 1966 in the held belt, the call goes out. What limits the post then is whether anything answers: a Regional Forces company within an hour by road or water, a mortar or howitzer within range, a flare ship, and a district operations room awake to hear the call. In the Delta, the boats and the canals make the answer possible at night. In the coastal lowlands, the answer is a company on a road the Front can mine. In the Highlands there is often no answer at all within the hours of darkness. This is the most important single output of this memorandum's traverse, because it says where the next provision must go: to the reaction and to the fire within range of the posts, not to more radios.

**F008-7** **For the Republic's self-sufficiency, the constraint moves from capacity to the order book, and after 1973 to dollars for what the base cannot make.** Before this provision the Republic could not make shell bodies, cartridges or batteries at all. With it, it can make them by the million. In 1964–72 what binds is whether it is made to: the domestic share of EC008-2, or nothing. In 1973–75 what binds is the foreign exchange to buy propellant, explosive, primers, graphite electrodes, ferro-alloys, semiconductors and crystals. The base turns a dollar into more rounds; it does not make the dollar, and the arsenal's real endurance without imports is its three to six months of propellant and explosive¹. The constraint after 1973 is therefore the same one the historical Republic faced, the appropriation, but each dollar of it is worth more.

**F008-8** **In the steelworks the constraint is electricity and electrodes, then foremen.** Part 1 named the rate limits: 70–100 MW at peak against a national grid built around one line from Da Nhim, graphite electrodes consumed at a few kilograms a tonne and all imported, and about 2,000 first-line foremen the country has to find. None of these binds the radios or the batteries, which run on a few megawatts¹. The steelworks will run below its rating more often than the arsenal or the electronics plants, and its shortfall falls on construction steel, which the American base can supply in the years it is in theater.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F008-9** **The bases take about 31,000–45,000 men of military age from the RVNAF, and the ones they take are the technicians its own services wanted.** The metallurgical base holds about 25,000–35,000 men of military age and the electronics base about 6,000–10,000¹, all needing deferment under a law on the Korean model³¹. That is three to four divisions' worth of men by count. The honest weight is lower: most of the skilled men would never have been riflemen, and a fitter in the rebuild works keeps more carriers running than he would have as a private in one. The real loss is narrower and sharper. The ARVN Signal Corps and the Ordnance Corps compete with the plants for the same radio technicians and machinists, and the plants pay more. The second loss is the deferment itself, which becomes a commodity: a certificate of employment at Bien Hoa will be bought by sons of families who never enter the plant, and the Muster Service of P001b will find them only if it is set to look³². I count this as a moderate cost and expect the Commission to have a deferment case in its first two years.

**F008-10** **The bases cost aid dollars and port tonnage in the years when neither is scarce, and save them when both are.** In 1964–68 the base imports inputs worth about 40–60% of the metallurgical output and about half of the electronics output by value, and the domestic share of EC008-2 costs more per round than drawing on an American stock already paid for. By weight the inputs are a quarter to a third of the steel they become, and in the Saigon port congestion of 1965–66 they queue behind American cargo; I expect the steelworks to lose a month or two of output to the queue in 1965–66⁴. The thermal station's 120,000–150,000 tonnes a year of fuel oil¹ is a small claim on a port that handles millions. Against all of this, every tonne of rebar, wire and bridging made at Bien Hoa is a tonne not shipped from the United States or Japan. On balance the port gains from 1966; the budget pays from 1965 to about 1970 and gains thereafter.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F008-11** **Offshore rebuild, the years of the dead radio at the post, and the late crash programme for depots no longer happen.** Historically the RVNAF's vehicle, engine and electronics rebuild was partly done out of country, and in 1970 the Americans began an eighteen-month, $30.6 million programme to upgrade the RVNAF's rebuild depots by mid-1971, with about 1,100 more depot civilians still needed³³. In this war that programme is erased: the depots exist from 1964 and their civilians are trained by 1968. The territorial forces' years with too few radios and too few batteries, which historically ran until the PRC-25 reached them late in the decade³⁴, are erased in the held belt from about 1966. The attention those problems consumed was American attention in 1970–71, at MACV J-4 and the area logistics commands, during the withdrawal. It is freed for the problem the withdrawal actually posed, which was what the RVNAF would do when the money shrank. I cannot say the Americans of 1970 would have used it well. I can say they would have had it.

### Perverse effect

**F008-12** **The radio can become the post's wall.** A platoon that can call help has a reason to stay inside its wire and call, rather than go out at night to lay its own ambush on the path the Front uses. Night control of a hamlet is held outside the wire, on the paths and at the edges, as much as inside it, and a post that has turned into a fort with a radio holds itself, not the hamlet. I expect some platoons to make exactly that trade, particularly where the district reaction is good and the platoon leader is weak, and I expect district chiefs who measure their posts by calls answered rather than by patrols laid to encourage it⁴. The cure is the platoon leader and the subsector adviser who requires the night ambush, and the provision supplies neither. The weight is moderate: the radio's gain is larger, but this effect eats into it in exactly the districts where the reaction makes the post feel safe.

**F008-13** **Captured and leaked sets put the Front on every district net in the contested belt.** The Front prized captured PRC-25s, and the issue of thousands of sets to territorial forces in contested districts gives it hundreds more a year⁴. Every one is a monitor on a net with no encryption: the district's reaction, its check-in schedule and its fire plans are heard by the Front's listening teams, and its false calls can draw a reaction into an ambush. The plant does not build NESTOR¹, and American control of communications security does not reach the territorial nets in any case. Code words, changing frequencies and a separate net for the reaction reduce the damage; they do not end it. The net effect is still strongly in our favour in the held belt, where few sets are lost, and close to even in the contested belt, where many are.

**F008-14** **The Directorate of Defence Industry becomes a prize for the generals.** State enterprises with tens of thousands of workers, contracts in the hundreds of millions of piasters, scrap licences and deferment certificates are the richest patronage the Republic has outside the import licences¹. Each coup's winners will want the Directorate, its plants' managements and its contracts. The Commission of P001b polices the contracts while it is protected; after it was curbed in 1971, the plants' managements become a palace appointment like the Board's, and their lot acceptance becomes what the palace's men make of it. This is the mechanism by which three earlier provisions' institutions fell in 1970–71, and the base will fall to it too. I judge the plants' production survives the capture, because the palace wants the plants to produce. Their honesty of acceptance does not.

**F008-15** **One bad lot of shell bodies can make the gunners refuse domestic rounds.** An in-bore premature from a cracked shell body kills a gun crew¹. A single such accident in an ARVN artillery battalion, traced to a Bien Hoa lot, will make every battery commander in the corps ask for American rounds and hide the domestic ones, and the domestic share collapses for a year while the inspectorate proves its lots again. I expect at least one such incident in 1965–67⁴. The weight is minor if the inspectorate is independent and the lot is traced and published; it is moderate if the accident is hidden and the rumour does the work.

### Anticipation

**F008-16** **The Front declines the attack on a post that can call, and the radio's success is the attack not made.** Memorandum 004 found that the Front stopped assaulting posts with rearmed platoons and went for softer targets. The radio does the same at a second remove: a district committee that knows a post can bring mortars and a boat company within half an hour does not spend a company on it. The posts that can call are attacked less, and when attacked are attacked by fire rather than by assault. The measure that would show this is posts not attacked, which no report records. The measure that will be reported, posts overrun, falls, and the fall will be credited to the rifles, to the chiefs, to the police and to the weather. I am fairly sure that from 1966 to 1971 the held belt's posts are attacked a third to a half less often than in the war as it now stands, and I cannot prove it⁴. This is the effect that makes the electronics base the more important of the two, and it is invisible by construction.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F008-17** **COSVN reads the radio within a season and reads it rightly.** The authors are COSVN's Military Affairs Committee and the regional military commands below it, in the Delta Military Region 9 and around Saigon Military Region 7³⁵; the Front's own technical reconnaissance teams supply the evidence. By the end of 1965 their directives say what any competent enemy would: kill the radio operator first, cut the antenna, listen to the district net and learn its check times, attack on the fourth night when the battery is weak, and, above all, attack the post to destroy the relief³⁶. All of that is right, and the part that hurts is the last. What COSVN gets wrong, for about a year, is the source: its propaganda describes the radios as American equipment and the plants as an American enterprise, which is untrue and which the workers of Thu Duc know to be untrue. That costs the Front a little with the urban workforce and nothing in the hamlets.

**F008-18** **Hanoi reads the arsenal late, and its error matters in 1974.** The Politburo in Hanoi and the General Staff under Van Tien Dung see the Bien Hoa steelworks as an American base dressed in Vietnamese clothes and a target for sabotage, not as a change in the Republic's endurance, until the Paris Agreement makes the Republic's supply its own problem⁴. Historically their judgment in 1974 was that the aid cuts had forced Thieu to fight "a poor man's war," with his firepower down by nearly 60 percent and his mobility halved. In this war the firepower in the categories the arsenal makes has not fallen so far, and the radios still work. Hanoi will see that by the end of 1974 and will draw one of two conclusions. It may conclude that waiting costs it, and accelerate; or that the Republic is harder to break than its plan of October 1973 assumed, and add forces. Either is a decision with authors, and Part 3 must make it.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F008-19** **The cheap counters are listening, cutting the line and ambushing the relief; the expensive one is giving up the post as the Front's arsenal.** Listening on captured sets costs the Front almost nothing. A pylon on the Da Nhim line costs a few kilograms of explosive and a sapper team's risk. An ambush on the relief costs what any ambush costs, and against a careless district chief it is profitable. The expensive adaptation is the one the Front cannot avoid where the radio works: it stops overrunning posts, and overrun posts were one of its main sources of weapons and ammunition in 1964–65. The record has the Front taking tens of thousands of modern carbines by the end of 1965 from fallen posts, deserters and ambushed columns³. Every post that holds because it called keeps its carbines and its 5.56mm. On balance the exchange favours us where the reaction exists and roughly breaks even where it does not, which is the same answer every memorandum on the countryside has reached, and for the same reason.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F008-20** **The loop out of theater runs through the 1974 appropriation, and it changes what the vote buys, not the vote.** Follow it. The bases make the RVNAF's ammunition, parts and communications cheaper per unit in 1973–75. The meta-checkpoint judged that the 1974 vote stands as history's until a provision changes what the Republic needs Congress to pay for. This provision does exactly that. It does not change the vote itself: Congress cut military aid to about $700 million for fiscal 1975 for reasons of its own, Watergate and a war it wanted to be done with, and nothing in the Republic's factories reaches those. What it changes is the vote's purchasing power. If about half of what the fiscal 1975 appropriation bought was ammunition and spare parts³⁷, and the domestic base stretches that half by 1.4–1.8 times and communications by about two¹, the $700 million buys what about $850 million to $1 billion would have bought in the historical Republic⁴. That is most of the way back to fiscal 1974's $1.1 billion. It does so only if the domestic share has been run since 1965. The loop returns to the theater as ammunition and batteries in the divisions in 1974–75. It does not return to Washington: a Congress that cut a billion to seven hundred million was not watching the Republic's factories, and the American clock is not moved by them in any phase.

### Open channel

**F008-21** **The factory wage enters the soldier's household and creates a working class the Front must organise.** About 55,000–85,000 industrial jobs in the Saigon belt, two-thirds of the electronics jobs held by young women¹, put a second wage into some thousands of RVNAF households near Bien Hoa, Long Binh and Thu Duc, where III Corps units and their dependants live. Where the soldier's family has that wage, his pay's erosion by inflation hurts less and his reason to desert to protect his family is weaker. It is a small mechanism on the problem that dissolved the corps in 1975, and it reaches only III Corps. The same jobs create an industrial workforce of a size the Republic did not have, organised in part by the Vietnamese Confederation of Labour under Tran Quoc Buu, and the Front's worker-proselytising sections will try to organise it too. A strike at Bien Hoa is a political event in a way a strike in a rice mill is not. The plants also absorb some of the refugees who historically filled Saigon's slums, though at their scale they take a few tens of thousands from flows that ran into the hundreds of thousands³⁸.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F008-22 · Counter-directional** **The stretch is an argument for the cut, and the arsenal hides a dependence it cannot end.** The base's case to Washington in 1974 is that every aid dollar buys more in the Republic than it did. That is true, and it is also the best argument a Senator who wants to cut can find: if they make their own shells, they need less money. The Defense Attaché Office warned that cuts in support could be equated with loss of territory; in this war its argument is weaker, because the Republic is visibly better able to manage. I expect the appropriation to stand where it stood, for the reasons of F008-20, and the argument from the base to be used to defend that figure against any supplemental. The second half of the finding is quieter. Every shell body and cartridge case from Bien Hoa is filled with imported propellant and explosive¹. The more the RVNAF fights with domestic rounds, the more its endurance in 1975 rests on a three-to-six-month stock of chemicals the Republic cannot make and an import pipeline Congress controls. A Republic that feels self-sufficient in ammunition is not, and its commanders will plan as if it were. The weight is moderate: it does not undo the stretch, and it moves the dependence from the finished round to its filling rather than creating a new one.

**F008-23 · Strongest positive** **The RVNAF's communications and ammunition do not go dark in 1974–75, provided the domestic share has run since 1965.** In 1974 the historical Republic rationed artillery rounds, cut patrols and units cut their radio use to save batteries. In this war, with the domestic share running, the battery works supplies nearly all of the dry cells the RVNAF needs from inputs that are small in weight and value, the rebuild depot returns sets in days, and the arsenal turns each ammunition dollar into about 1.4–1.8 times the rounds¹. The squad gun the ration fell on first gets more of its links and cases from Bien Hoa. This is the first provision in eight memoranda that reaches one of the three conditions the meta-checkpoint said the rung requires: sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation. I state it at that weight, and with its limits beside it. It does not reach the other two conditions: an air arm the Republic owns, and the choice of the corps commanders. It does not touch the palace's orders of 13–14 March 1975 or the families on the road, and I do not expect it by itself to delay the collapse of I and II Corps by the three weeks that would push the last assault into the rains. It changes what the Republic fights with in 1974; whether that changes how long it fights is for Part 3 to test against the critical path, and I will not claim it before the test.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

MACV J-4 gains a responsibility it historically did not have until 1970: running the domestic share with the Aid Compliance Board, which means advisers at the arsenal's lot acceptance, at the rebuild works and at the area logistics commands, and a monthly reckoning of what the RVNAF drew from each source. J-6 gains the territorial nets: frequency plans for thousands of new stations, net discipline, and the rule that keeps Phung Hoang traffic off them. No new American spaces are needed; the existing ordnance and signal advisers carry it, at the cost of some of their time for other tasks¹.

### US conventional forces

Little changes. American units draw American stock. Their engineers lend fewer Bailey bridges and their depots repair fewer RVNAF vehicles, because the Vietnamese rebuild works does it. American units operating in the Delta and around Saigon find more territorial posts on the air and able to report; the Front's listening on captured sets reaches some American nets too, as it historically did¹.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

The CIDG camps receive Vietnamese-built sets and batteries through the Vietnamese Special Forces, and their depot repair moves from the Special Forces' own channels to Long Binh in part. The Montagnard villages around the camps are where the Interior Ministry will be slowest to issue hamlet radios (§2 Part A), and the Special Forces' own issue to the villages they work with is the only reliable path until Saigon is made to change.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

The divisions gain a rebuild loop for carriers, tanks, trucks and engines that returns vehicles in about 60–90 days, and a supply of radios and batteries that is no longer last in line behind American units. The JGS gains a Directorate of Defence Industry and its politics. Its quartermasters must now choose between domestic and American stock, and will choose American unless the share is enforced. The ARVN's Signal and Ordnance Corps lose technicians to the plants.

### RF/PF and territorial security

This is where the provision acts. From 1966 in the held belt every Popular Forces platoon and Regional Forces company has a working PRC-25 class set and a weekly battery run, and posts are built and rebuilt with steel wire and pickets from Bien Hoa. The territorial forces' posts are attacked less and hold more often where the reaction exists, and some platoons stop patrolling because they can call.

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. The bases make nothing the air campaign against the North uses, and nothing in the North's response is touched by them.

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged. The bases do not touch the Trail, the sanctuaries or the tonnage arriving.

### Arc Light

Unchanged. The B-52J and its weapons are American and draw nothing from the Republic's plants.

### Airmobile formations

Very little changes. American airmobile divisions are supported by American depots. The Long Binh depot's bench repair of avionics units serves the VNAF's UH-1H fleet as it grows¹, which adds a few serviceable aircraft on a given day and does not change how the airmobile war is fought.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The steel boatyard builds and rebuilds the RVN Navy's river craft and the Regional Forces' boats, and the rebuild works keeps their diesels running¹. The Delta's reaction by water, on which the night net depends most, is the main beneficiary. The American Mobile Riverine Force is unchanged.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

The district and province centres must keep their traffic off the territorial nets, which the Front monitors (§2 Part A, F008-13). The centres use their own couriers and secure sets. Otherwise unchanged.

### Third-country forces

Unchanged in employment. The Korean divisions in II Corps bring a country that built exactly this kind of base in the early 1970s³¹; their officers will recognise the plants and may be the best judges of them.

### Prior provisions

- **P002a** · National Police Reform Programme: the post's base sets, the HT-1 village sets and their batteries now come from Thu Duc and Bien Hoa and are repaired at Long Binh; the constables' 2026 solar-charged handhelds remain imported and unchanged². The post rule's means of calling is kept on the air.
- **P002b** · Provincial Administration Reform: the district operations room becomes the hub of the night net; the chief's reaction force and his net discipline decide what the radios are worth.
- **P001a** · Land-to-the-Tiller Programme: titled families in the held belt buy pumps and small engines from the agricultural shops; a few percent more rice in the held Delta, and a few percent more for the Front to tax where it still taxes.

- **P004a** · M4A1 carbine: the arsenal makes 5.56mm cases and bullets, magazines, springs and small parts from 1964, and chrome-lined barrels for rebuild after 1968; the whole upper is not made. The ration of 1974 falls on fewer empty magazines.
- **P004c** · M249 light machine gun: links and linked 5.56mm components are made at Bien Hoa; the squad gun's 1974 ration is the first place the stretch shows.
