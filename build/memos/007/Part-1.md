# Memorandum 007 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


The Joint Chiefs have granted me something I did not expect to be given in any form: an officer corps that went to school. Not equipment and not a programme with a budget line, but seven years of lieutenants who learned their trade before they were asked to practise it, and a rule that no officer in either army rises a grade without finishing the course for it. I read the grant as three provisions. The first is the education of new American officers to the 2026 standard. The second is the same education built for the Vietnamese and given to every new ARVN officer. The third is the reform of how the ARVN chooses and promotes its officers, which the grant names separately and which I treat as a system of its own.

The education is stipulated from 1957. The campaign opens on 1 January 1964, and nothing in this exercise acts before it, so the seven classes commissioned in 1957–63 enter as a standing condition of the world: on the morning I take command, the lieutenants and captains of both armies who were commissioned in those years have been through the courses, and the gates have governed their promotions. Nothing else from before 1964 changes. Ap Bac was fought, the Diem government fell, and the generals who run the Republic were commissioned before 1957 and never sat in any of these classrooms. The selection reform carries no date in the grant, and I have read "from 1957" as belonging to the education alone, so P007c arrives with the campaign on 1 January 1964¹.

These are institutional provisions, and the sixteen fields are read as the procedure directs for them: D3 as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput, D6 as the reporting chain, D7 as reach and security preconditions. Each field says so in its first line.

## §1.1 US officer education to the 2026 standard, Army and Marine Corps, commissioned from 1957 — P007a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**What it is.** Every officer commissioned into the United States armed forces from 1957 passes through the officer education continuum as the services run it in 2026, and must complete each course before the promotion it governs¹. For the Army, which fights most of this war on the ground, that continuum is three resident courses with distributed-learning phases between them. The Basic Officer Leader Course comes in two parts: BOLC A is the pre-commissioning education at West Point, ROTC or Officer Candidate School, and BOLC B is the branch course that follows commissioning, at Fort Benning for infantry and armor, Fort Sill for field artillery, and the other branch schools for the rest. BOLC B must be completed within two years of commissioning or the officer is separated². The Captains Career Course follows at about four years of service, now preceded by a 75-hour distributed-learning phase on the Army profession, mission command, operations, the operations process and unit training management³. The Command and General Staff Officer Course, the Army's Intermediate Level Education, is a 10-month resident course at Fort Leavenworth in three phases: a common core, the Advanced Operations Course and electives⁴.

**The Marine Corps.** Every Marine officer goes through The Basic School at Quantico, whose mission is to train new officers as company-grade officers with particular emphasis on the rifle platoon commander⁵. Its course runs about six months⁶. The Expeditionary Warfare School is the captains' course, 41 weeks resident, taught in conference groups of about 16⁷. The Command and Staff College at Quantico is the majors' course⁸.

**The Air Force and the Navy.** The grant covers new US officers without naming a service, and I read it as reaching all four. The Air Force's and Navy's 2026 continua come with it. They change less in this war because the Air Force already ran Squadron Officer School and Air Command and Staff College before 1964, and because the air war's failures were failures of policy and target selection more than of the education of captains⁹. I do not develop them further in this dossier, and I say so here so that no later section assumes I have.

**The gate.** The provision's force lies in the rule that each course must be completed before the next promotion¹. I read the gates as 2026 practice sets them: BOLC B before first lieutenant, the Captains Career Course or EWS before major, and the staff course before lieutenant colonel¹⁰. In 2026 the Captains Career Course is already required of captains who seek early promotion¹¹. The grant makes every gate absolute.

**In-service date.** The continuum is a 2026 configuration of a system rebuilt between 2006 and 2022: BOLC from 2006–10, with BOLC II merged into BOLC B in 2010², and the career course modernized with its distributed-learning phase in 2022³. The officers commissioned in 1957–63 completed it before the campaign opened¹.

**Quantity.** Every new American officer commissioned from 1957¹. In round terms that is about 15,000–20,000 Army and Marine Corps officers a year in 1957–64, rising to about 40,000–50,000 a year at the 1967–69 peak, when Officer Candidate School was expanded to feed the war¹². By January 1964 the seven classes of 1957–63 are about 100,000–130,000 officers, of whom perhaps two-thirds remain on active duty¹³.

**Organisation in theater.** None. The provision is taught in the United States. Its product reaches the Republic of Vietnam inside every platoon, company, battalion staff and advisory team that holds an officer commissioned since 1957. In January 1964 those officers are second lieutenants through senior captains. By 1968 the 1957 class has reached major and the battalion executive officer's chair; the first of them command battalions about 1971–72¹⁴.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Staff and complement.** The courses are staffed by the service schools. The 2026 small-group method sets the faculty load: a BOLC B platoon of about 40 lieutenants has a small-group leader and platoon trainers, a Captains Career Course small group has about 16 students to one instructor, and the staff college teaches in staff groups of about 16¹⁵. At the 1967–69 accession peak the Army's and Marine Corps' training base would need about 1,500–2,500 more instructors and staff than it historically had¹⁶.

**Personnel spaces in the Republic.** None in theater. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, and the provision consumes no space in the Republic in any case¹⁷.

**The real cost in spaces is time.** At any moment about 4–6% of the company- and field-grade officers of the Army and Marine Corps are in school rather than in units¹⁸. Historically many officers also went to the career course and Leavenworth; the change is that nobody skips them. In 1966–68, when the Army was short of captains and promoted first lieutenants at about two years of service¹⁹, this gate takes captains out of the line exactly when the line most needs them. That is the price, and it is real.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The provision's instruments are the curricula, the method and the gate.

**The curricula.** BOLC B teaches the platoon leader's trade in his branch: small-unit tactics, land navigation by day and night, weapons qualification, call for fire, troop-leading procedures, maintenance and supply discipline, and the law of armed conflict with escalation-of-force and detainee handling²⁰. The Captains Career Course's distributed phase covers the Army profession, mission command, operations, the operations process and unit training management³, and its resident phase teaches the company commander and battalion staff officer to plan and run combined-arms operations²¹. The staff course teaches operational art, mission command and joint and Army doctrine, and the history of war⁴.

**The doctrine they teach.** The 2026 courses are built on ADP 6-22 (Army leadership and the profession), ADP 6-0 (mission command), FM 3-0 (operations), FM 5-0 (planning and orders production), FM 6-27 (the law of land warfare), ATP 3-21.8 (the infantry platoon and squad) and ATP 3-21.10 (the infantry rifle company), and, for partnership, FM 3-22 (Army support to security cooperation) and FM 3-24 (counterinsurgency)²². In 1964 the lieutenant applies that doctrine with the period's equipment: the AN/PRC-25 radio, the M101A1 105mm howitzer, the UH-1D, the M60 machine gun and, in this war, the M4A1 carbine and M249 of P004a²³.

**The method.** The 2026 courses are taught in small groups, by practitioners, through repeated planning exercises and situational training lanes, and they assess their students on performance rather than attendance²⁴. This is the part of the provision that 1964 most lacked: a common professional language in which a lieutenant writes an operations order his company commander can read, and an expectation that he will be told the intent and act on it.

**The gate.** No promotion without the course¹. It is the only compulsion the provision carries. It does not compel a commander to use what his lieutenant learned, and it does not compel the personnel system to post the educated officer where his education is needed.

**The authority.** The provision lives inside the service personnel systems; it requires no new law and no new money beyond the training base²⁵.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.* The courses learn what their faculty and their doctrine know, and from 1964 they learn what the war teaches them.

**The doctrinal inheritance.** The 2026 curriculum carries the Army's and Marine Corps' twenty years of counterinsurgency and stability experience in Iraq and Afghanistan: population-centred operations, partnership with host-nation units, the cost of indiscriminate fire, detainee operations and the ethics of the long war²⁶. It also carries the turn since 2017 back to large-scale combat operations against a peer, which now dominates the curriculum²⁷. A 2026 captain is educated first for a division fight against a modern army and second for a war among the people.

**What it learns in 1964–75.** The schools receive after-action reports from the theater, as they did historically, and the 2026 method folds them into the instruction faster because its instructors come from the units²⁸. The courses learn about Vietnam from Americans who served there. They do not learn about the hamlet from Vietnamese who live in it.

**Night, canopy and monsoon.** The training lanes at Fort Benning, Fort Polk and Quantico teach night movement, patrolling and ambush in woodland; they cannot reproduce triple canopy, the paddy dyke or the monsoon²⁹. The Jungle Operations Training Center in Panama and the Army's jungle courses existed in the period and remain the nearest substitute.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Throughput.** At the peak the system must educate about 40,000–50,000 new Army and Marine officers a year through the basic course³⁰. BOLC B runs about four to six months depending on branch³¹; TBS about six months³². At the career-course level about 12,000–18,000 captains a year pass through a course of about six months for the Army and 41 weeks for EWS³³. At the staff course about 4,000–6,000 majors a year attend for 10 months³⁴.

**The rate limit is the wartime pipeline.** Officer Candidate School in 2026 runs 12 weeks³⁵, and the grant leaves commissioning sources as they were. In 1966–69 the Army commissioned large classes of OCS lieutenants through a short course and sent many of them to Vietnam within months³⁶. Under the provision every one of them first completes BOLC B. That adds about three to four months to the pipeline of each new platoon leader³⁷. In 1966 that is a delay; by 1968 it is a steady state, and the platoon leaders arriving in the Republic are older by a season and better trained by a great deal.

**The rate limit at the top is captains.** With captains promoted fast and needed in every company, the career-course gate pulls about one captain in eight out of the force at any time from 1966³⁸. The Army can meet it, at the cost of more first lieutenants commanding companies, which happened historically for other reasons³⁹.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as the reporting chain and the interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

The provision reports through the services' training commands to the service chiefs. In theater it reports nowhere; its product is the officer.

**Talking to the ARVN.** The provision teaches partnership and security force assistance at the level 2026 doctrine teaches them, which is a block of instruction, not a qualification⁴⁰. The advisers of P006c, who are selected and trained separately⁴¹, are the provision's best channel to the ARVN. Where the US officer and the ARVN officer of P007b have read the same doctrine in two languages, they share the vocabulary of an operations order, a fire plan and a commander's intent. That shared language is the most useful thing the pairing of P007a and P007b buys, and it is easy to overlook.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Every new American officer in every branch and every service¹. It reaches the Republic only as far as those officers go: a platoon of the 173rd Airborne Brigade in War Zone D, a company of the 1st Infantry Division in the Iron Triangle, a battery of the 12th Marines at a fire support base in Quang Tri, an advisory team in a Delta subsector.

**Preconditions.** The schools are in the United States and need no security. The precondition that binds is time: a wartime army that needs lieutenants now must accept them three or four months later⁴². The second precondition is that the personnel system honours the gate when the war presses against it. In 1966–67, when commanders in Vietnam ask for captains and the career course holds them at Fort Benning, the pressure to waive the gate will be heavy. The grant says the gate holds¹. I record that it will be tested.

**Ground the provision does not reach.** The officers commissioned before 1957. In 1964–68 these are the battalion, brigade and division commanders, the staffs of MACV and the field forces, and every general⁴³. The provision educates the men who carry out this war's operations and not the men who design them.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Fuel, spares, specialist personnel.** None in the Republic. The provision consumes classrooms, training areas, ammunition for training and instructors in the United States.

**The cost.** About 1,500–2,500 instructor and staff positions at the peak⁴⁴, plus the student man-years: at about 4–6% of the officer strength in school, roughly 8,000–14,000 officer man-years a year at the 1968 peak of about 170,000 Army and Marine officers⁴⁵. Much of that load existed historically, because most officers who made a career went to the career course and many went to Leavenworth. The added load is perhaps a third to a half of it⁴⁶.

**What the RVNAF cannot supply.** Nothing is asked of it.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**It transfers by its sister provision.** The education of ARVN officers to the same standard is P007b, and it is a separate system with a separate dossier because its throughput, its language, its schools and its politics are all different. What transfers from P007a directly is the American officer's ability to teach. A US captain educated in 2026 small-group method, posted to the faculty of the ARVN Infantry School at Thu Duc as an adviser, can teach the method as well as the content.

**What happens under Vietnamization.** The American courses do not transfer; they stay in the United States. Their product leaves the Republic with the American units in 1969–72⁴¹. Under this grant the advisers who remain are, by 1970, entirely officers commissioned since 1957 and educated to the standard, so the advisory effort's quality rises at the end of the war as the American combat effort falls⁴⁷.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** where the officer is posted to the work his course taught: a lieutenant leading a rifle platoon, a captain commanding a company or serving as a battalion operations officer, a major on a brigade staff.

**It degrades** where the war departs from the curriculum. A 2026 education built for large-scale combat operations and informed by Iraq and Afghanistan fits the main-force battles of 1965–68 and the conventional war of 1972 well, and fits the village war of 1964–65 and the pacification of 1969–71 only partly⁴⁸. It degrades also under the individual replacement system, which breaks every unit's cohesion each year; P006b's unit rotation answers part of that from 1965⁴¹.

**It collapses** where the senior commander directs operations the education warns against. An educated company commander ordered to sweep a free-fire zone at a body-count quota is still ordered to do it. The provision makes him more likely to report honestly what the sweep achieved. It does not make him the man who set the quota.

**Over weeks rather than once.** The education does not decay in the field as a machine does; it hardens into habit or erodes into the unit's practice. A lieutenant who joins a battalion whose officers learned the same doctrine keeps it; one who joins a battalion that does things its own way loses it within a few months⁴⁹.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The gate is waived.** The most likely failure. In 1966–67 the Army, short of captains and majors, suspends the career-course gate "for the duration" or grants blanket waivers⁵⁰. A partial failure looks like waivers for officers serving in Vietnam; a total failure looks like the gate abolished for the war. The grant says the gate holds¹, and I plan on it holding; I note that Washington would have had every incentive to break it.

**The course is compressed.** In the wartime surge the schools shorten the courses to move more officers through them, as the ARVN historically cut its officer candidate course from 37 to 24 weeks in June 1968⁵¹. The officer then holds the qualification without the education. This is the quieter failure and the more dangerous one, because the record shows the gate as met.

**The education is ignored.** The educated officer is posted to work it did not prepare him for, or is commanded by men who did not share it. This is not a failure of the provision but a limit on it, and in 1964–68 it applies to every battalion commander and above⁵².

**What the force does meanwhile.** It fights with the officers it has, as it did historically.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** On 1 January 1964 the provision is at full strength in the company grades, because seven classes are already through it¹. It does not degrade in the first year.

**The wartime years.** From 1966 three things decline. Course quality is the first to go: the instructors who should come from the units are needed in the units, and the schools are staffed with officers who have not yet served in Vietnam⁵³. Course length is the second, under the pressure in D11. The gate is the third, and the last to go if it goes at all. By 1969–70 the US Army's officer corps is strained by turbulence and by the drawdown, and the education cannot hold up a unit whose soldiers no longer believe in the war.

**What lasts.** The officers of 1957–63, who were educated before the war distorted the schools, are the provision's best product. They are captains and majors in the hardest years of the ground war⁴³.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the 1964 system.** In 1964 a new Army officer went to a branch basic course of a few months, most career officers later went to the branch advanced course, and selection for the Command and General Staff College was competitive, so many officers who later commanded never attended⁵⁴. The schools taught in large classes by lecture, and promotion did not depend on completing them⁵⁵. The 2026 continuum adds three things: universal attendance enforced by the gate, the small-group performance-based method, and a curriculum carrying sixty more years of war, including the long counterinsurgencies.

**The margin.** I estimate that a 2026-educated platoon leader of 1966 is about as competent in his first month as a historical platoon leader in his fourth to sixth month in command⁵⁶. For a war in which platoon leaders often served six months in the field, that is half again as much useful command time. At the company level the gain is smaller, because historical captains had usually been to the advanced course. At the battalion level in 1964–70 there is no gain at all⁵².

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It does not reach the men who run the war.** Battalion, brigade and division commanders and every general in 1964–70 were commissioned before 1957 and are untouched; the first educated battalion commanders arrive about 1971–72.
- **It is not a counterinsurgency course.** The 2026 curriculum is built first for large-scale combat against a peer; the Iraq and Afghanistan lessons are present but secondary.
- **It does not change the strategy, the body count or the replacement system.** It makes better junior officers inside them; P006b's unit rotation, not this, addresses the replacement system.
- **It does not raise commissioning standards.** It educates officers after they are commissioned; it does not impose the 2026 degree requirement on Officer Candidate School.
- **It costs captains.** At any time about one captain in eight is in school from 1966, and the wartime pipeline for platoon leaders is three to four months longer.
- **It does not teach the hamlet.** Its knowledge of Vietnam comes from Americans who served there, not from the Vietnamese.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy some of the young American officer's mistakes: the platoon walked into a horseshoe ambush on a trail, the company that loses its fire support plan in the first minutes of contact, the lieutenant who cannot read the map in the canopy. It denies him nothing at the operational level.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a modest provision for the American side of the war and a real one. It makes the platoon leaders and company commanders of 1964–70 markedly better at their work, which saves American lives at the margin and some Vietnamese civilian lives where the lieutenant's discipline over fire and detainees holds. It does not touch the men who decide how the war is fought until the war is nearly over, and it is built for a different war from the one of 1964–65. Its largest value may be indirect: as the faculty and the common language for its ARVN counterpart, and as the education of the advisers who remain when the American units leave.

- Courses: Army BOLC B (branch, ~4–6 months, separation if not done within 2 years of commissioning)²; Captains Career Course with a 75-hour distributed phase³; CGSOC 10 months resident⁴; USMC TBS ~6 months⁵⁷, EWS 41 weeks⁷
- Gates: BOLC B before 1LT, CCC or EWS before MAJ, staff course before LTC⁵⁸

## §1.2 RVNAF officer education to the 2026 standard for new ARVN officers commissioned from 1957 — P007b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**What it is.** Every officer commissioned into the Army of the Republic of Vietnam from 1957 completes an officer education continuum built on the US Army's 2026 courses, translated, taught in Vietnamese and adapted to the Republic's war, and must complete each course before the promotion it governs¹. I read "2026 officer education for new US and ARVN officers" as one standard applied to two armies, and the ARVN version as the American courses' structure and method carried into the ARVN's own schools, not as Vietnamese lieutenants sent to Fort Benning⁶⁴.

**The commissioning schools stay.** The ARVN's officers came from two schools. The National Military Academy at Dalat commissioned regular officers through courses of one year from 1955, two years from 1956 and three under Diem, reverting to two years in 1963, and produced fewer than 200 officers a year before its four-year course began in 1967⁶⁵. The reserve officer school at Thu Duc, founded in 1951, was the largest source of officers and the main source of small-unit leaders; it graduated over 40,900 students from 1952 to November 1969⁵¹. Its course was nine months for baccalaureate holders⁶⁶. The provision does not replace these schools. It adds the education that follows commissioning.

**The continuum.** Three courses, each a gate¹:

1. **The branch basic course**, the ARVN equivalent of BOLC B, at the ARVN branch schools — Infantry at Thu Duc, Artillery, Armor, Engineer, Signal and the service schools — taken immediately after commissioning and before the officer joins his first unit or within his first year⁶⁷. Gate: promotion from aspirant or second lieutenant.
2. **The captains' career course** at about four to six years of service, at the branch schools with a common core, preceded by a correspondence phase in Vietnamese on the model of the 2026 distributed-learning phase⁶⁸. Gate: promotion to major.
3. **The command and general staff course**, resident at the RVNAF Command and General Staff College at Dalat, about nine to ten months on the CGSOC model⁶⁹. Gate: promotion to lieutenant colonel. Historically the Joint General Staff created command and staff schools for field-grade officers at Dalat only in 1967, with attendance then required for promotion⁷⁰; this provision does from 1957 what the ARVN did from 1967, to a higher standard and with no exemption.

**In-service date.** The design is the 2026 US curricula adapted; no real-world ARVN equivalent existed. The 1957–63 classes completed the basic course before 1964, and the earliest of them the career course [U: standing condition].

**Quantity.** Every new ARVN officer from 1957¹. About 2,000–3,000 commissions a year in 1957–63, about 4,000–6,000 a year in 1964–67 as the army expanded, and about 8,000–12,000 a year at the 1968–71 peak of the general mobilization⁷¹. The seven classes of 1957–63 are about 13,000–18,000 officers⁷², of whom perhaps 10,000–14,000 are serving on 1 January 1964⁷³.

**Organisation in theater.** A Central Training Command curriculum cell at the Joint General Staff owns the courses. The branch schools teach the basic and career courses. The Command and General Staff College at Dalat teaches the staff course. A US faculty advisory detachment works at each school⁴⁴.

**What the seven classes mean.** On 1 January 1964 the ARVN's company-grade officers are, for the most part, these classes. A Thu Duc graduate of 1958 is a captain commanding a company or serving as a battalion operations officer; the 1957 class are senior captains and the first majors⁷⁴. Since in 1970 37% of infantry battalions were still commanded by captains⁷⁰, and the shortage was worse earlier, the 1957–60 classes are commanding battalions by 1965–66⁷⁵.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Vietnamese staff.** Each branch school adds a faculty for the basic and career courses taught in small groups: at about 1 instructor to 16 students for the career course and 1 to 40 for the basic course with platoon trainers, the schools need about 600–900 more Vietnamese instructors by 1967 and about 1,200–1,800 at the peak⁷⁶. The Dalat staff college needs about 60–90 faculty⁷⁷.

**US staff.** About 350–500 US personnel in the Republic, of whom about 300–450 military spaces⁷⁸. No ceiling is set. These advisers are a better use of American captains and majors than many of the posts they otherwise fill, and they come from P007a's educated cohorts.

**The cost in ARVN officers.** About 5–8% of ARVN company- and field-grade officers are in school at any time⁷⁹. In an army whose battalions were commanded by captains for lack of majors, that hurts.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.*

**The curricula.** The basic course teaches the ARVN platoon leader his trade: patrolling and ambush by night, the call for fire on American and ARVN artillery and on the VNAF, land navigation, weapons, first aid and evacuation, the treatment of the population and of prisoners, and leadership by example⁸⁰. The career course teaches the company commander and the battalion staff officer to plan an operation, run a fire support plan, train a unit, and keep its weapons, rations and pay honest⁸¹. The staff course teaches the division and corps staff officer to plan and sustain operations at scale, including the defence against a conventional offensive⁸².

**The equipment the ARVN officer learns to employ.** The weapons of his platoon, which in this war are the M4A1 carbine, the M110A2 marksman's rifle and the M249 of P004²³; the M60 machine gun, the M79 grenade launcher and the M2 60mm and M29 81mm mortars; the AN/PRC-10 and later AN/PRC-25 radios; the AN/PVS-2 Starlight scope where he has one; the M101A1 105mm and M114A1 155mm howitzers of his supporting artillery; the M113 carrier and the M41A3 tank of the armored cavalry; the H-34 and UH-1 helicopters that lift him; and the VNAF's A-1H Skyraider overhead⁸³.

**Adaptation to the Republic's war.** The ARVN version adds what the American course leaves out: the territorial war, the RF/PF, the sector and subsector, the hamlet, the land reform and the police of P001a and P002a, and the officer's relation to the province chief⁸⁴. It drops nothing of the conventional content, which the ARVN needs in 1972 and 1975.

**The gate.** No promotion without the course¹. It is a stronger instrument in the ARVN than in the US Army, because historically the ARVN's promotions ran on seniority and patronage, with across-the-board promotions ordered for national holidays⁷⁰. A gate on education is the first objective criterion the system has.

**What it cannot compel.** Command selection. The educated captain is eligible for major; whether he gets a battalion is decided by whoever controls appointments, which until P007c is the President and the corps commanders⁶⁶⁸⁵.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.*

**What the schools learn.** The courses learn from their own graduates, who come back from the units as instructors, and from the US advisers⁸⁶. Here the ARVN version has an advantage over the American one: its instructors are Vietnamese who fought in the Delta and the Highlands, and the course learns about the hamlet from men who were born in one.

**What the schools do not learn.** They learn what officers are willing to say in a classroom. Where an officer's career depends on his patron, he will not tell a seminar that his division commander's operations are staged for the reports. The 2026 method assumes candour among professionals, and candour depends on a selection system that does not punish it; that is P007c's work⁸⁷.

**Infiltration of the classroom.** The schools are open to the enemy's penetration agents like every ARVN institution⁸⁸. A course that teaches the plan for a division's defence teaches it to whoever is in the room.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Basic course.** About 2,000–3,000 lieutenants a year in 1957–63, 4,000–6,000 in 1964–67 and 8,000–12,000 at the peak⁸⁹. At about four to five months, the branch schools must carry about 1,500–2,000 students at a time in 1965 and about 3,500–4,500 at the peak.

**Career course.** About 1,500–2,500 captains a year from the mid-1960s, rising to about 3,000–5,000 by 1970 as the mobilization's lieutenants reach captain⁹⁰. At about five to six months that is about 700–2,500 in residence at once.

**Staff course.** About 400–700 majors a year by 1966 and 1,000–1,500 by 1970⁹¹. At about nine to ten months that is the largest single load, and the binding one: the Dalat college must grow by several times its historical size⁹².

**The binding rate.** The career-course gate at major. In 1969 the regular army had only 63% of its authorized senior officers, and in May 1971 46 of 133 maneuver battalions were still commanded by captains⁷⁰. A gate before major slows the creation of majors in exactly the grade the army is shortest. This is the provision's central cost, and it is offset only because the 1957–63 classes, already through the gate, fill the majors' grades in 1964–67⁴⁷.

**The 1968 mobilization.** Historically the ARVN cut its officer candidate course from 37 to 24 weeks in June 1968⁵¹ and recalled about 1,055 reserve officers in early 1968, nearly exhausting the reserve⁷⁰. Under the provision the basic course follows commissioning whatever the candidate course's length, which protects the platoon leader's education at the cost of three to four months more before he joins his platoon⁹³.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as the reporting chain and the interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** The schools report to the Central Training Command and the Joint General Staff. Course results go to the personnel directorate and, under P007c, to the selection board⁹⁴. The US faculty advisers report through the MACV training directorate.

**Talking to the Americans.** This is the provision's quiet strength. An ARVN battalion commander educated from the American curriculum writes his orders in the same format, uses the same fire-support terms and understands the same intent as his American adviser and the American brigade beside him⁹⁵. Combined operations in 1965–68, in which ARVN and American units fought side by side with poor coordination, become easier to plan⁹⁶. The cost is a deeper habit of planning around American fire support, which the record already holds as the war's central dependency.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Every ARVN officer commissioned from 1957¹. It reaches the regular army and, through officers posted there, the Regional Forces' company and battalion commanders and the sector and subsector staffs⁹⁷. It does not reach the Popular Forces, whose leaders are not officers.

**Security preconditions.** The schools are at Thu Duc near Saigon, at Dalat in the Highlands and at the branch schools around Saigon and Vung Tau⁹⁸. They need local security, which they had historically through most of the war; Dalat saw fighting during the Tet Offensive of 1968.

**Ground the provision does not reach.** Every officer commissioned before 1957: the generals of the juntas, the corps and division commanders of 1964–70, most regimental commanders until about 1967⁹⁹. The ARVN's leadership problem was worst at the top, where command was given for loyalty⁸⁵. This provision cannot touch that. It is felt from the bottom up, and reaches regiment about 1966–68 and division only in the early 1970s⁶¹.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Costs.** Classrooms, barracks, training areas and training ammunition at the branch schools and Dalat; translation and printing of the curricula; about 600–1,800 more Vietnamese instructors; about 300–450 US military spaces⁴⁴. The money is modest: in 1964 prices perhaps $10–20 million a year at the peak, mostly construction and instructor pay¹⁰⁰.

**What the RVNAF cannot supply.** The curricula and the first faculty, which come from American advisers through the 1957–63 classes and are then Vietnamized as graduates return as instructors⁹⁴. By the late 1960s the RVNAF can run the courses itself, with US advisers in support.

**The hidden cost.** The officers in school are not commanding. In the worst year, 1968, that is several thousand officers out of the line⁴².

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**It is already the ARVN's.** The courses are Vietnamese courses in Vietnamese schools from the start. What transfers under Vietnamization is the last of the US faculty advisers⁹⁴. By 1970 the ARVN's schools can run the whole continuum without American instructors.

**What happens after transfer.** The schools survive as long as the gate does. If a government in Saigon suspends the gate to promote its loyalists, the courses become a formality, attended by men who know attendance is optional, and the provision becomes a building¹⁰¹. This is the failure that matters after 1973, when the American lever is gone.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** where an educated officer commands under a superior who lets him. In a division whose commander was chosen for competence, the educated battalion commander plans, coordinates and fights his battalion. In a division whose commander was chosen for loyalty and fears casualties, the same battalion commander is ordered not to close with the enemy, as at Ap Bac in January 1963, where the 7th Division's operations were constrained from above.

**It degrades** in the territorial war at night, which no classroom fully teaches and which depends on the officer living among the population and the RF/PF trusting him¹⁰². It degrades also in the political war: an officer educated to fight is not thereby educated to stay out of coups.

**It collapses** where the officer is not paid enough to live. ARVN officer pay lost much of its value to the inflation of 1965–72, and an educated captain whose family cannot eat on his salary sells rations and gasoline like an uneducated one. Education does not reach that problem; P001b's pay verification touched only part of it¹⁰³.

**Over weeks rather than once.** As with P007a, the education hardens into the unit's practice or is lost to it. The difference is that in the ARVN the unit's practice is set by a commander chosen by a political system, so the education's survival depends on P007c more than on anything in the schools.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The gate is bought.** The first failure, and the most likely. Seats in the courses and passes are sold, or a general's protégé is passed without the work¹⁰⁴. A partial failure is a course with a quiet lane for the connected; a total failure is a diploma with no education behind it.

**The gate is suspended by a junta.** After a coup the new government promotes its supporters, as coup governments did historically⁸⁵. If the gate is suspended for that list, it is suspended for good. The American lever can hold it where the US is willing to make aid conditional on it¹⁰⁵.

**The educated officers become a faction.** Officers educated together, sharing a doctrine and a contempt for their superiors, are a political force. The "Young Turks" of 1964–65 were officers frustrated with the generals above them. Educated captains and majors with no path to command are more likely, not less, to join a coup that promises one¹⁰⁶. This is the counter-directional risk in the provision, and it is real.

**The education is wasted.** The educated officer is posted to a staff or a province job because he can write, while the battalions are commanded by men who cannot¹⁰⁷. Under the record this already happens: the honest, able field-grade officers are spent on every reform at once, and the divisions lose each time.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** On 1 January 1964 the company grades are full of educated officers¹. Through 1964 and into 1965 the provision is at its strongest relative to the force: the army is still of its 1964 size and the classes of 1957–63 are a large share of its officers⁸⁹.

**The expansion dilutes it.** From 1965, as the army grows, the new classes are educated but young, and the experienced educated officers are spread thinner across more battalions¹⁰⁸. By 1968, with the mobilization's lieutenants pouring in and the officer candidate course cut, the share of officers with full education and combat experience falls even though everyone has been to the basic course⁴².

**What declines first.** The career course, under the pressure to make majors. Then the staff course's length. The basic course is the last to go, because it is the cheapest¹⁰¹.

**Losses.** Educated officers die in the lead. The 1957–63 classes are the company commanders of 1964–66, the years of the ARVN's heaviest losses before Tet¹⁰⁹. The provision spends its best product early.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical ARVN.** Historically, in 1969 most ARVN officers still lacked formal training beyond commissioning; command and staff schooling for field-grade officers began at Dalat in 1967; promotion to major required a ninth-grade diploma rather than a course, with officers already holding their rank exempt; and over 8,000 officers and men trained at American schools in 1957–69, a small fraction of the corps⁷⁰. Against that, every ARVN officer commissioned from 1957 holds three levels of professional education before he reaches lieutenant colonel.

**The margin.** I estimate that the ARVN company-grade officer of 1964–67 under this provision is roughly the equal, in tactical competence, of the better historical ARVN officers who had been to Fort Benning's infantry course¹¹⁰; and that the share of battalion commanders with that standard rises from perhaps one in five historically to four in five by 1967⁶³. That is a large change in a narrow band of the army. Above regiment it is nil until the 1970s.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It does not reach the generals.** Corps and division commanders and the juntas of 1964–70 were commissioned before 1957; command at the top stays a political appointment until P007c reaches it.
- **It does not choose commanders.** It makes officers eligible; it does not put them in command. Without P007c the educated captain can be passed over for a loyalist.
- **It does not pay officers.** An educated captain whose salary has lost its value to inflation is as corruptible as an uneducated one.
- **It does not teach night security by itself.** The territorial war is learned by living in it; the course gives the vocabulary, not the trust of a hamlet.
- **It costs majors.** The career-course gate slows the making of majors in the grade the army was shortest of; only the 1957–63 classes, already through it, cover the gap in 1964–67.
- **It does not keep officers out of politics.** An educated officer corps with blocked promotion is a coup faction.
- **It deepens the habit of American fire support.** Common doctrine makes combined operations easier and the dependence on American fire deeper.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the ARVN battalion that walks down the road into the ambush the regiment laid for it: the incompetent company commander, the fire plan never made, the relief column that does not scout its route. It denies him some of the ARVN's tactical predictability, which the enemy's regiments exploited from Binh Gia onwards. It does not deny him the political general who orders the battalion to stand still.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a substantial provision. With the 1957–63 classes in place, the ARVN's company-grade officers in 1964 are educated to a standard the historical army never reached, and from 1965 the battalions are commanded by them. That attacks the ARVN's most cited weakness — poor leadership, which American advisers named as the foremost reason for ineffective units — from below, and it does so in the years the war was lost in the countryside. Its ceiling is set above it: education does not choose commanders, pay officers or govern the generals. It makes P007c possible, because a merit system needs a pool of qualified officers to choose from, and it enlarges the small pool of able field-grade officers the record says every reform drew on. Alone it is a +1 to ARVN effectiveness; with a selection system that uses it, much more.

**Key figures — P007b**

- Historical sources of officers: Thu Duc, over 40,900 graduates 1952–Nov 1969; Dalat under 200 a year before 1967⁶⁵; candidate course cut 37 to 24 weeks in June 1968⁵¹
- Historical schooling: field-grade command and staff schools only from 1967; ninth-grade diploma required for major; most officers without formal training in 1969⁷⁰
- Commissions under the provision: ~2,000–3,000 a year in 1957–63, ~4,000–6,000 in 1964–67, ~8,000–12,000 at the 1968–71 peak¹¹¹
- 1957–63 classes: ~13,000–18,000 commissioned, ~10,000–14,000 serving on 1 Jan 1964¹¹²
- Gates: branch basic course (~4–5 months) before 1LT, career course (~5–6 months) before MAJ, Dalat staff course (~9–10 months) before LTC¹¹¹³
- US spaces: ~300–450 military of ~350–500 US personnel¹¹⁴; Vietnamese instructors ~600–1,800 added¹¹⁵
- Battalion commanders with full education: ~1 in 5 historically, ~4 in 5 by 1967⁶³
- Officers in school at any time: ~5–8% of company- and field-grade¹¹⁶

## §1.3 RVNAF Officer Selection and Promotion Reform, 1964 — P007c

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**What it is.** A reform of how the ARVN chooses, rates, promotes, assigns and removes its officers, decreed on 1 January 1964 [U: arrival as the campaign opens; the grant gives no date]. The grant says "officer selection and promotion reform for ARVN" and no more. I have designed it to 2026 practice and to the Republic's politics, and I state the design so that every later section knows what was granted¹¹⁷.

**The historical system it replaces.** Under Diem the personnel directorate compiled a confidential annual promotion list and the President decided, often after consulting the head of military security, and personally chose officers for key posts⁶⁶. After 1963 the presidents and chiefs of state insisted on appointing corps and division commanders mainly on personal loyalty, and the coups of 1960, 1963 and 1964 drove them to appoint and supervise the key commanders personally⁸⁵. Promotion below the top ran largely on seniority, with across-the-board one-grade promotions sometimes ordered for national events. The system was last substantially reformed in 1965, with changes in 1968 and 1969, centralizing promotions in the Joint General Staff with annual boards. Promotion to major required a ninth-grade diploma, from which serving officers were exempt. Unsatisfactory commanders were seldom relieved, for lack of replacements⁷⁰.

**The reform's eight parts.**

1. **The Officer Selection and Promotion Board**, a permanent body under the Joint General Staff, appointed for fixed terms and removable only for cause, with panels for each grade and branch. Its members are serving and retired officers drawn by lot from a qualified list, with a member of the Commission Against Corruption of P001b sitting on each panel for integrity¹¹⁸.
2. **Written criteria.** Promotion on performance, potential, education and time in grade, published in the decree. Education is P007b's gate.
3. **The officer evaluation report.** A two-tier report by the officer's rater and senior rater on the 2026 US model, with the senior rater's top block capped at about a third of the officers he rates, so that a rating means something¹¹⁹. Reports go to the board directly and are not seen by the officer's patron.
4. **Command selection lists.** Battalion and regimental commanders are chosen centrally by the board from eligible officers, for a fixed command tour of 18–24 months, and the corps and division commanders must appoint from the list¹²⁰. Historically in May 1971, 80 of 133 battalion commanders had held command for a year or less and 52 for under six months⁷⁰; the fixed tour answers that.
5. **Relief for cause**, by the division commander with the board's review, and the relieved officer's evaluation going to the board⁹⁴.
6. **An open path to commission** for proven NCOs and enlisted men without the baccalaureate, through the officer candidate school on merit and combat record¹²¹.
7. **The diploma bar removed.** The ninth-grade requirement for major is replaced by P007b's career-course gate¹²².
8. **General officers and division and corps command.** The President still appoints, but only from a board list of three ranked candidates for each post, and the list is published to the National Security Council and the US Mission¹²³. An American aid condition protects the board, as with P002b's Selection Board: the United States will fund no RVNAF expansion and no new equipment for a division whose commander was not appointed from the list¹²⁴.

**In-service date.** No real-world RVNAF equivalent; the US systems it is modelled on are 2026 practice.

**Quantity.** One national system¹. It handles about 12,000–16,000 officer promotions and about 150–250 battalion and regimental command selections a year in 1964–67¹²⁵.

**Organisation.** The board and its secretariat at the Joint General Staff in Saigon; evaluation records held centrally; a small inspection staff that audits the rating profiles of senior raters⁹⁴.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Vietnamese staff.** The board's secretariat, panels and records staff: about 200–350 people, mostly officers and clerks¹²⁶. Board panels sit for several weeks a year each.

**US staff.** About 60–90 US personnel, of whom about 40–60 military spaces: an advisory cell to the board and the JGS personnel directorate, trainers for the evaluation system, and a share of the senior advisers' time spent on evaluations and the aid condition⁹⁴. No ceiling is set.

**The pool it draws from.** The board itself consumes able officers: perhaps 40–80 field-grade and general officers of integrity at any time¹²⁷. These come from the same small pool the record already counts as spent several times over.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.*

**What it can compel.** Promotions from second lieutenant through colonel, by the board's list⁹⁴. Battalion and regimental command, by the command list. Relief, by the division commander with review. Entry to the officer corps from the ranks, on merit.

**What it can influence.** General-officer promotion and division and corps command, through the ranked list of three and the aid condition⁹⁴.

**What it cannot compel.** The President's choice among the three; the decision of a junta to suspend the decree; the honesty of a senior rater who wants his protégé promoted. The capped top block limits the last: a senior rater who gives his protégé the top block must deny it to someone else, and the inspection staff sees the profile¹²⁸.

**The forms and rules it borrows.** The officer evaluation report follows AR 623-3 and its DA Form 67-10-1 (company grade), DA Form 67-10-2 (field grade) and DA Form 67-10-3 (strategic grades), with the senior rater's profile, translated and adapted¹²⁹. Command selection follows the US Army's centralized selection list, and the officer's career path follows the logic of DA PAM 600-3¹¹.

**The lever behind it.** An American aid condition⁹⁴. It is the same lever that protected the Selection Board of P002b through the coups of 1964–65 in this war's record, where the US pulled it at once and the board survived¹³⁰. The lever is strong while American aid is large and the American mission is willing to use it, and it weakens as both fall after 1969¹³¹.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.*

**What the board learns.** The evaluation reports, course results from P007b, the unit's combat record, the inspection staff's audits, the Commission's integrity checks, and the American adviser's evaluation of the commander, which the board receives as an input and not as a veto⁹⁴.

**How reliable.** In a quiet division, the reports tell the board who is competent. In a contested one at night, they tell it who reports well. A battalion commander whose district is quiet because he has a private arrangement with the local VC battalion reads as a good commander on paper¹³². The adviser's report is the board's best check on that, and P006c's selected advisers on 18-month tours make it more reliable than the historical adviser's six-month impression⁴¹.

**What it cannot see.** Loyalties. The board sees performance, not a man's private commitments to a faction, a religion, a region or the enemy¹³³.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Throughput.** About 12,000–16,000 promotions a year in 1964–67 and about 20,000–30,000 at the 1968–71 peak, when the mobilization drives promotion¹³⁴. About 150–250 command selections a year in 1964–67, rising to about 300 by 1970 as the number of battalions grows¹³⁵.

**The rate limit.** The board's time and the quality of its inputs. A board reviewing a thousand captains for major in three weeks reads each file for minutes¹³⁶. It can do that well only if the reports are honest and short.

**Speed of effect.** The command list changes battalion command within about two years, as tours end and commanders are relieved¹³⁷. Promotion by merit changes the field grades within about three to five years¹³⁸. The general officers change only as fast as the President chooses among the ranked candidates and as the old generals retire or are removed, which in 1964–67 means slowly and by coup¹³⁹.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as the reporting chain and the interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** The board reports to the Chief of the Joint General Staff, and its lists go to the Minister of Defense and, for general officers, to the President⁹⁴. Its decisions are published inside the RVNAF.

**The interface with the US.** The US Mission receives the general-officer lists and the record of appointments against them; MACV's advisory chain feeds evaluations of commanders⁹⁴. The aid condition is administered by the Mission and MACV together.

**The interface with the provinces.** Province and district chiefs are officers, and in this war they are chosen by P002b's Selection Board¹³⁰. The two boards draw on the same officers and must coordinate their claims; otherwise the province board takes the best majors and the command list gets the rest, which the record says already happens.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Every ARVN officer, by grade. Firm through colonel; partial at general officer⁹⁴. It reaches the Regional Forces' officers through the same board; it does not reach the Popular Forces, whose leaders are not officers.

**Preconditions.** Political: a government in Saigon that accepts the board, or an American lever strong enough to make it accept. Institutional: an evaluation culture, which takes years to build; an officer who has never been rated honestly does not rate honestly the first time¹⁴⁰. Security: none special; the board sits in Saigon.

**Where it does not reach.** The coup. A coup is a promotion list written by force¹⁴¹. The decree does not stop a coup; the aid condition raises its price.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Costs.** Small: staff, records, the inspection staff, the evaluation forms, and the training of every rater in the corps⁹⁴. Perhaps $1–3 million a year in 1964 prices¹⁴².

**What the RVNAF cannot supply.** Not money or equipment, but the first honest ratings and a culture of relief for cause. These come from the advisers and from the 1957–63 classes of P007b, who learned the rating system's logic in their courses⁹⁴.

**The hidden cost.** Every officer the reform removes or passes over has a patron, and the patron's displeasure is the reform's real cost¹⁴³.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**It is already Vietnamese.** The board, the reports and the lists are Vietnamese institutions; the US role is advisory, with the aid condition as its lever⁹⁴.

**What happens when the Americans leave.** The aid condition weakens as aid falls; after 1973 it is the only American lever left and Congress controls the aid. The board survives only if the Republic's government values it for itself. If the President after 1973 returns to appointing generals for loyalty, the general-officer tier reverts at once, and the battalion tier slowly, as commanders appointed on merit are replaced¹⁴⁴. Historically, flag appointments under Thieu were made for loyalty rather than competence⁷⁰.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** in quiet years and in corps whose commanders accept it. Battalion command goes to officers chosen for it; commanders serve their tours; failures are relieved; the able NCO becomes a lieutenant.

**It degrades** under coups and political crises, which in 1964–65 were constant. Each new junta wants its own men in the divisions near Saigon. The board can resist below regiment; at division it can only list, and the aid condition must do the rest.

**It collapses** if the United States declines to use the lever at the first test, because no later test will be taken seriously¹⁴⁵. It collapses also if a junta finds it convenient to blame the board for a military defeat, as governments under pressure find scapegoats.

**Over weeks rather than once.** The reform is a slow-working institution. Its effects on battalions appear over two years, on field grades over five, and on the general officers over the whole war⁴².

### D11 · Failure modes

**Capture by a faction.** The most dangerous failure. The board is captured by the faction in power, and the reform becomes the instrument of the next purge, under the authority of merit¹⁴⁶. The capped top block and the Commission's integrity member make capture visible, not impossible.

**Rating inflation.** The second most likely. Every senior rater gives every protégé the top block until the cap is enforced, and then he moves officers between raters to evade it¹⁴⁷. The inspection staff is the answer; it must be honest.

**The general-officer tier fails while the rest works.** The most probable outcome in 1964–67. The battalions improve; the divisions are commanded by the President's men; and the educated, merit-selected battalion commander is ordered to do something stupid by a general appointed for loyalty¹⁴⁸.

**The coup of the passed-over.** Officers denied promotion by the board, with patrons in the junta, conspire against it¹⁴³. The reform raises coup risk while it beds in; that is why the manifest pushes political stability down.

**What the force does in the window.** It reverts to the old system: seniority and patronage, as historically.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** In January 1964 the board has no ratings to work with and must start with records, course results and adviser reports⁹⁴. Its first year is its weakest, and it must survive two coups in it. By the end of 1964 the first evaluation cycle gives it data; by 1966 it has two full cycles and a command list with a record.

**What declines first.** The general-officer list, as each junta tests it. Then the relief for cause, as division commanders find it politically costly to relieve the cousin of a minister. The battalion command list is the most durable part, because it is technical and lower in the political temperature¹⁴⁹.

**After 1969.** As American leverage falls, the reform depends on the officers it has promoted. By then the field grades are largely its product and P007b's¹⁵⁰, which is the reform's best chance of outlasting the Americans.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical system.** Seniority, patronage and presidential choice; reforms in 1965, 1968, 1969 and 1971 that centralized boards, added points for combat service and cut time-in-grade requirements; battalion commanders of a year or less in command, 80 of 133 in May 1971; commanders seldom relieved for lack of replacements⁷⁰. Against that, this reform puts merit selection and fixed tours in place seven years early, relieves failures with a pool from which to replace them, and opens command to competent officers regardless of their diploma.

**The margin.** I estimate that by 1967 the share of ARVN battalion commanders who would have been rated competent by their advisers rises from perhaps half historically to about three-quarters, and that the median battalion command tour rises from under a year to about 18 months. At regiment the change is smaller and later; at division and corps it depends on the President, and I estimate only one or two division commands in ten changed by 1967⁶³.

### D14 · Negative specification
- **It does not take command of divisions and corps out of politics.** The President still appoints generals, from a ranked list of three; the aid condition can enforce the list, not the choice.
- **It does not stop coups.** A coup is a promotion list written by force; the reform raises its price and raises the risk of one while it beds in.
- **It does not create able officers.** It chooses among those that exist; its pool is P007b's educated classes and the small pool of able field-grade officers the record says every reform already draws on.
- **It does not see loyalty or penetration.** The board reads performance, not a man's faction, his patrons, or his contact with the enemy.
- **It does not work without the American lever,** and the lever weakens after 1969 and is gone after 1973.
- **It does not end corruption.** It makes the sale of commands harder and visible; it does not raise pay.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the enemy the ARVN battalion led by a commander who bought his command, holds it for six months and is never relieved; and, slowly, the division led by a general chosen because he would never move against the palace. It denies the enemy part of the predictability he exploited — the commander who would not close with the enemy because his career depended on avoiding losses.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is the provision that decides whether the education is used. Below the generals it works: battalions get chosen commanders who serve full tours and are relieved when they fail, and educated officers rise. At the top it is as strong as the American will to enforce a list, and no stronger; the junta years of 1964–65 will test it at once. It is the first serious answer in this war to the gap in the officer corps that the record holds open, and it is the reform most likely to provoke the generals it threatens. I judge it worth that risk. Its effect is institutional and accrues over years: it will show in battalion outcomes from 1966 and in the divisions only if the lever holds.

- Historical: Diem's President chose from a confidential list⁶⁶; corps and division commanders appointed for loyalty⁸⁵; ninth-grade diploma for major; 80 of 133 battalion commanders in command a year or less in May 1971; 46 of 133 battalions captain-led⁷⁰
- Board: ~200–350 Vietnamese staff; ~40–80 senior officers on panels 
- Throughput: ~12,000–16,000 promotions and ~150–250 command selections a year in 1964–67¹⁵¹
- Command tour: fixed 18–24 months 
- OER: senior-rater top block capped at about a third¹⁵²
- General officers: President chooses from a board's ranked list of three, enforced by an aid condition 
- US personnel: ~60–90, of whom ~40–60 military⁹⁴
- Margin: competent battalion commanders ~1/2 historically to ~3/4 by 1967; median command tour under 1 year to ~18 months; 1–2 division commands in 10 changed by 1967⁶³
