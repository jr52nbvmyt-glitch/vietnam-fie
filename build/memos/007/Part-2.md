# Memorandum 007 — Part 2 of 3 · §2 Integration and second-order effects · §3 Changes to existing forces


## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

These three provisions belong first to ADVISE, and they are the first in this war to reach the part of ADVISE that the record has always named as the RVNAF's central weakness: the men who command its companies, battalions and regiments. Every earlier provision that touched the ARVN gave it something to use — a rifle, a squad gun, a true muster roll, a better adviser. These give it officers who know how to use them and a system that puts those officers in command. Cao Van Vien, who chaired the Joint General Staff for most of the war, judged the lack of leaders to be the RVNAF's central problem, and American advisers named poor leadership as the foremost reason for ineffective units¹. I am now attacking that problem directly, from below, and I want to be precise about where the attack lands and where it stops.

**ADVISE** is joined and strengthened at its root. P007b changes what the ARVN officer knows; P007c changes who among those officers commands. Together they change what an adviser is for. In 1964 the selected adviser of P006c sits beside a battalion commander who, in four cases in five by 1967, has been through a branch course and a career course built on the same curriculum the adviser was taught at Fort Benning². The adviser stops being the man who explains how to lay a fire plan and becomes the man who argues about which fire plan. That is a different job, and a better one.

**FIX** is strengthened at the battalion and company level on both sides of the alliance. The ARVN battalion that is moved by helicopter or truck to meet an enemy who has chosen the ground is led by a man who has been taught to scout his route, put security on his flanks, keep his companies within mutual support and plan his fires before he needs them. The American platoon that walks into the canopy is led by a lieutenant who has been taught the same, longer and harder than his historical counterpart. Neither provision touches the decision that matters most in FIX in this war: the enemy's choice of whether to fight.

**SECURE** is strengthened through the territorial forces' officers. The sector and subsector staffs and the Regional Force company commanders are drawn, from 1964, from the educated classes of 1957–63, and the board's open path to commission brings proven Regional Force, Popular Forces and ARVN sergeants into the officer corps for the first time on any scale. The district war is fought by lieutenants and captains, and these are now better lieutenants and captains.

**SUSTAIN** is joined on the Vietnamese side and charged on the American side. P007b educates the RVNAF's logistics, signal and staff officers to run their own systems, which is the only kind of self-sufficiency an officer school can give. P007a costs the American training base about 1,500–2,500 instructor and staff spaces in the United States and about one captain in eight in school at any time from 1966³, and that charge falls on the same pool of company-grade officers that the advisory corps and the rotating battalions draw on.

**STRIKE** is touched only through the company commander's ability to plan and call fire, which both curricula teach. It improves the use of artillery and air in the fight; it does not add a gun or a sortie, and on the Vietnamese side it deepens a habit I discuss under the counter-directional finding.

**FIND, INTERDICT and SUBVERT** are not joined. An educated officer patrols better and reports more honestly, and that is a marginal contribution to finding; it is not a sensor. Nothing here touches the Trail, the sanctuaries or the infrastructure's tax system.

**F007-1** The three provisions are one instrument in three parts, and none of them works alone. P007b makes officers eligible and P007c decides which of them command; P007a educates the Americans who teach at Dalat and advise in the battalions. Without P007c, the educated captain of P007b can be passed over for a loyalist, as he would have been under Diem's confidential list¹; without P007b, the board of P007c chooses among the same thin pool every earlier reform drew on. With both, the ARVN has, for the first time, a pipeline that produces educated officers and a selection system that rewards them. I judge the compound to be roughly twice the sum of the parts at the battalion level, and at the division level it is worth exactly as much as the American lever behind the general-officer list².

**F007-2** The pool that every reform has drawn on is enlarged, but not until 1966. The Commission, the Provincial Administration Selection Board and the advisory effort each needed honest, able field-grade officers, and the record holds that there were perhaps 900–1,200 of them in 1964–66 and that the divisions lost every time the pool was spent. P007b changes the arithmetic from below. The 1957 class enters the major's grade in about 1964–66 and the classes behind it follow at roughly 2,000–3,000 a year³; from 1966 the pool of educated majors and lieutenant colonels grows by several hundred a year, and the province list of P002b and the command list of P007c stop competing for the same few men⁴. In 1964 and 1965 nothing in this memorandum adds a single field-grade officer, and the competition the checkpoint recorded stands through the worst two years of the war.

**F007-3** The selected adviser and the educated counterpart speak the same professional language, and that compounds both. An adviser of P006c with 24 weeks of Vietnamese⁵ who sits with a counterpart who has been through the career course at Thu Duc on a curriculum translated from his own can argue doctrine rather than teach it. The friction that defined the historical advisory relationship — a young American captain telling an older Vietnamese major with a decade of war how to do his job — does not vanish, but its ground changes: the two men are now arguing from the same book. Influence rises. Authority does not, and the adviser still cannot relieve anyone.

### Compound pairings
**Interactions with earlier provisions**

- P007b × P007c: education makes an officer eligible and the board decides whether he commands; each is worth a fraction of the pair, and together they give the ARVN its first pipeline that produces and rewards able officers
- P007b, P007c × P002b: the educated classes enlarge the pool the province list draws on from 1966; the evaluation reports give the Selection Board a record of each officer's field performance; in 1964–65 the two boards still compete for the same few field-grade men
- P007b × P006c: the selected adviser advises a counterpart educated on the same curriculum; the argument moves from teaching doctrine to applying it, and the adviser's Vietnamese reaches a counterpart who has the vocabulary to answer
- P007a × P006c: the Captains Career Course is the pipeline of the selected advisory corps; the advisers of 1966–70 are career-course graduates, and the course and the corps compete for the same captains
- P007a × P006b: the educated lieutenant joins a battalion that trains collectively for six to seven months before it deploys and keeps him for the whole tour; the platoon leader and the platoon learn together, once
- P007c × P001b: the Commission is the board's enforcement arm against sold promotions and commands, and the Aid Compliance Board carries the aid condition that protects the general-officer list; the evaluation report gives the Commission a paper trail it never had
- P007b, P007c × P004a, P004b, P004c: an educated company commander employs the marksman as an overwatch and the squad gun as a base of fire because both are in his course; battalion sniper pools and volume-of-fire doctrine, which 004 recorded as abandoned, are not tried by officers taught why they fail
- P007c × P006a: army officers posted to the district centres are rated, and their reports are a target; the board's files and the Phung Hoang file are two lists of loyalties in two buildings, and the enemy wants both
- P007b × P005c, P003b: the company commander educated to call fire uses the American fire the adviser brings more effectively, and depends on it more

The pairing that matters most beyond the provisions' own interlock is with P002b, and it runs in both directions. The province and district chiefs are officers; they are rated on the same evaluation report as battalion commanders from 1964, and their reports are read by both boards. A district chief who held his district at night — Major Le Cong Phuong of Can Giuoc is the record's example — now has a written record of it that a command board can read, and an officer who ran a battalion well has a record that the Selection Board can read. Until 1966 that pairing hurts the divisions, because the province list takes the best of a pool that has not yet grown. From 1966 it helps both, because the pool is growing and the two boards stop bidding for the same man.

The pairing with P001b is quieter and probably as important. Historically a command could be bought because nobody wrote down why a man got it. The evaluation report and the published command list make the transaction visible: a lieutenant colonel whose senior rater placed him in the bottom third and who nonetheless appears at the head of a regiment is a case on Commissioner Khoa's desk, and the Aid Compliance Board can suspend the counterpart funds of the corps that put him there. That is not an end to the sale of commands, but it moves the sale from the clerk's office into the light.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

These provisions do nothing to find the main force and something real to fix it once found. The PAVN regiment under the canopy, the VC 9th Division in War Zone C, the 324B in the hills south of the DMZ are exactly as hidden as they were in Memorandum 006; an educated officer has no sensor. Sanctuary is untouched: the enemy's base areas in Cambodia and Laos, his regeneration there, and his ability to decline battle by withdrawing across a border are all where they were, and nothing in an officer school changes the throughput of the Trail or the size of the reserve the enemy rebuilds behind it.

Where the change lands is in the minutes after the enemy chooses to fight. The ARVN's characteristic defeat from Binh Gia onward was not a lost firefight; it was a relief column driven down the road the enemy had chosen for it, without route reconnaissance, without flank security, its companies strung out in march order, its fires unplanned. At Binh Gia the relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal and the 4th Marine Battalion was ambushed in the rubber going to recover the bodies of a helicopter crew. At Ba Gia the 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry was cut in two on the road it had to use. At Dong Xoai the 7th Airborne Battalion walked into the 271st Regiment's ambush in the Thuan Loi rubber. The rearmament of Memorandum 004 made each of those battalions survive the first quarter-hour better. It did not make them less likely to walk into it.

An officer educated in the career course has been taught, as a matter of drill, that the relief of a besieged post is the enemy's favourite ambush, and that the answer is a column that moves by bounds with its own security out, or does not move by the road at all. A battalion commander selected by a board, rather than by a corps commander who wants no losses reported this month, has a career interest in doing it right rather than in doing it fast. I expect the pattern of the road ambush to weaken from about 1965 in battalions commanded by educated officers, and the enemy to need more force and better bait to spring it². I do not expect it to disappear, because the decision to send the column down the road at all is taken at division and corps, by generals this memorandum does not reach until about 1971–72.

On the American side the effect is the same in kind and smaller in degree. The lieutenant who leads an American platoon in 1966 was taught by a 2026 course to rehearse, to plan his fires before he leaves the wire, to keep his machine guns where they can support, and to read a map under the canopy. The historical lieutenant learned those things in his first months in country, and some of his men paid for the lesson. The improvement is in the first-month mistakes; the margin, stated in Part 1, is that an educated platoon leader is about as competent in month one as a historical one in months four to six³. It does not change which contacts the enemy offers the platoon.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

The chain against the infrastructure is touched at two points, both small. First, the army officers who sit in the district centres of P006a and command the Regional Force companies that escort its warrant raids are educated, from 1964, in the law of war, in detention and in the handling of evidence; the curriculum's escalation-of-force and detainee training is a modest reinforcement of the capture-first rules the centres already run on. Second, relief for cause gives a province a way to remove the officer whose company burns a hamlet or loots a market, which historically almost never happened, and every such removal is a small subtraction from the Front's best recruiting material. Neither changes how a cadre is identified, which is the work of the Special Branch, the census and the files. This memorandum's contribution to that half of the war is in the conduct of the men around the identification, not in the identification itself.

### Dependence on SECURE

The provisions improve the officers who command the territorial war, and they depend on that war being winnable at night. A subsector adviser of P006c will meet, from 1964, a district operations officer and a Regional Force company commander drawn from the educated classes: men who have been taught how a perimeter is sited, how a reaction force is rehearsed, and how a night ambush is laid and checked. That is a real improvement to the posts and their reaction. But an educated captain cannot make a Popular Forces platoon sleep in its hamlet or a province chief release his reserve at 0200, and the record is plain that the decisive variable at the post is presence after dark and the reaction behind it.

The more important effect on SECURE runs through the board's open path to commission. Historically an ARVN officer needed a baccalaureate for the reserve officer course¹ and a ninth-grade diploma to make major⁶, which closed the officer corps to the rural men who did most of the fighting. A Regional Force platoon sergeant who has held a post in Chuong Thien for three years knows its families, its paths and its informers better than any graduate of Thu Duc; under P007c he can be commissioned through a twelve-week course and returned to his province as a lieutenant. Several hundred such men a year, most of them returning to the territorial forces, change who leads the posts. The dependence runs the other way too: the board's command lists cover the ARVN's battalions and regiments, not the Regional Force companies, which stay under the province chief. The territorial war gains educated officers by assignment and promoted sergeants by commission; it does not gain the board's protection against a chief who gives his companies to a cousin, which P002b's list has to supply.

### The ADVISE cluster

This memorandum is the ADVISE cluster, and its effect on the cluster is the largest of any provision so far. Every earlier provision either fed ADVISE or starved it: the rearmament needed the adviser to enforce zeroing, the Commission needed honest replacements it could not find, the province list took field-grade officers from the divisions, and the advisory corps of P006c bought a better adviser for a counterpart who might not listen. P007b and P007c attack the counterpart problem itself. They change who the counterpart is, what he knows, how he got his command and what happens to him if he fails.

The record now holds four instruments of leverage over the RVNAF's officers: the Aid Compliance Board's power over funds, the published list for province and district chiefs, the selected adviser's honest report, and now an aid condition behind the general-officer list. They are additive and they are all American. Each one trades the Republic's short-run effectiveness against its claim to be its own government, and the general-officer condition is the most exposed of them, because it reaches the men who make governments. I judge the trade worth making below the generals, where the board is a Vietnamese institution run by Vietnamese officers on written criteria and the American hand is invisible. At the top it is visible, and it will be named.

What the cluster still lacks is anything that teaches the ARVN to fight without American fire. The 2026 curricula assume artillery, air and medical evacuation on call, as the American Army does; an ARVN company commander educated on them learns to use those things well, and the record has already found that the ARVN's dependence on American fire deepens with every improvement in its ability to request it. I come back to this under the counter-directional finding.

### Employment concepts

#### EC007-1 · The gate and the branch course

The branch basic course is the ARVN officer's first five months after commissioning, and it is the gate to first lieutenant. A new second lieutenant commissioned from the Thu Duc reserve officer course or from Dalat reports to his branch school — the Infantry School at Thu Duc, the Artillery School at Duc My, the Armor School at Thu Duc, the Signal School at Vung Tau, the Quartermaster School at Saigon — within a month of commissioning. The infantry course, the largest, runs about four and a half months on the 2026 Infantry Basic Officer Leader Course model translated into Vietnamese: about six weeks of individual skills, land navigation and weapons on the M4A1, M249, M110A2 and the M60, then about twelve weeks of platoon operations in the field, with night patrols, ambushes, a defended post, a relief column and a call-for-fire range where each student adjusts 105mm rounds from the M101A1 by voice over an AN/PRC-25⁷.

At 4,000–6,000 commissions a year in 1964–67, about 350–500 lieutenants start a branch course each month across the schools; at the 1968–71 peak of 8,000–12,000 a year, 700–1,000 a month. The infantry school runs classes of about 150 starting every second week, with about four classes in the course at once. The minimum viable package is a company of students, a cadre of about 25 Vietnamese instructors, four to six American faculty advisers, a training battalion's worth of weapons and ranges, and a 105mm battery firing for the call-for-fire week. The gate is the course certificate: no second lieutenant is promoted to first lieutenant without it, and an officer who fails twice is reassigned to a non-commissioned post or released. Without P007c's board, the gate is enforced by the JGS personnel directorate alone and can be waived by a corps commander; with the board it cannot.

#### EC007-2 · The Dalat staff year

The resident Command and General Staff course at Dalat is the gate to lieutenant colonel and the place where the ARVN learns to plan above the battalion. It runs about nine and a half months for a class of about 250–400 majors a year by 1966, on the CGSOC common core: operations at division and corps, combined planning with American and allied forces, logistics, fire support coordination, intelligence preparation and the territorial war, with a module on pacification written by MACV and the JGS together⁸. Its faculty is Vietnamese with about 30–40 American faculty advisers, most of them P007a's Command and General Staff graduates on their first post after Leavenworth.

The cycle is a year: a class enters in September, after the monsoon's worst, and graduates in June; about one in ten is sent instead to Fort Leavenworth, continuing the historical practice of US schooling for the most promising. The staff year's product is not only the graduates but their exercises: each class plans a corps operation on a real map — the Binh Dinh coast, the Plain of Reeds, the approaches to Saigon — and the best plans are sent to the JGS. A major taken out of a battalion for nine months in 1965 is a heavy charge; without the staff year, the ARVN had no formal field-grade command and staff schooling until 1967⁶.

#### EC007-3 · The board season

The Officer Selection and Promotion Board works on a fixed calendar, which is itself the reform. Promotion boards sit by grade: captain twice a year, major and lieutenant colonel once a year in the dry season, colonel once a year in the autumn. Command selection boards sit twice a year, in February and August, and select about 75–125 battalion and regimental commanders each, who take command on fixed dates in April and October for tours of 18–24 months⁹.

A lieutenant colonel's board is the clearest example. About 1,500–2,000 eligible majors' files are reviewed by three panels of five colonels and general officers, each panel drawn by lot from a standing roster of about 40–80 senior officers and including at least one officer from each corps area¹⁰. Each file holds the officer's evaluation reports with the rater's narrative and the senior rater's placement in the top third, the middle or the bottom; his course certificates; his citations; and any finding by the Commission or a court-martial. A panel scores about 40 files a day on a written scale; two scores more than two points apart send the file to a third panel. The board takes about three weeks; the list is published in rank order in the official gazette, with the cut line. An officer passed over twice may request a review of his file and see its contents. The American advisory cell of about 60–90³ sits in the building and can read the procedures; it does not read the files and cannot see the scores until the list is published.

#### EC007-4 · Relief for cause and the thirty-day review

A battalion or regimental commander can be relieved by his division commander for cause — failure in action, failure to train, misconduct toward the population, theft — in writing, with reasons. The relief takes effect at once and goes to the board within thirty days for review; the board can confirm it, which bars the officer from command for two years, or overturn it, which returns him to the command list and puts a written finding against the officer who relieved him. A relief without a written reason is void. The thirty-day review is the protection against the historical relief, which was a political act; it is also what makes a division commander think before he relieves, which can cut the wrong way.

The expected rate in 1965–66 is about 20–40 battalion commanders relieved a year out of about 150–180, falling to about 10–20 once the first command lists have run a full tour¹¹. Each relief needs a replacement already on the list; the board's command lists therefore include an alternate list of about a quarter of the principal list, and the relieving division draws from it. Where the alternate list is exhausted — it will be in I Corps in 1965 — the senior company commander commands until the next board, and the battalion is captain-led for a season.

#### EC007-5 · The field commission

The open path to commission takes NCOs with at least three years of service, a combat or post-defence record, and their battalion or province chief's recommendation through a twelve-week officer candidate course at Thu Duc that substitutes demonstrated performance for the diploma. A candidate is tested on reading, writing and arithmetic to a primary standard and taught what a platoon leader needs; he is then commissioned as a second lieutenant and goes through the branch course like any other officer. About 500–1,500 a year from 1965, two-thirds of them returning to the Regional and Popular Forces of their own province¹². The course runs in classes of about 120 every month. Without P007c's removal of the diploma bar, these men cannot make major, and the path becomes a dead end at captain; with it, the best of them command battalions by 1970.

#### EC007-6 · The platoon leader's first month

The American lieutenant educated under P007a joins his platoon in a different way. Under the unit rotation of P006b, the battalion he joins is in its six-to-seven-month collective training cycle in the United States⁵; he arrives from the Infantry Basic Officer Leader Course at Fort Benning, or the Basic School at Quantico, having spent about five to six months on the platoon in the field³. His first month is spent on his platoon's live-fire exercises, a battalion field exercise, and the rehearsal drills his course taught him: the actions on contact, the immediate action against an ambush, the casualty evacuation, the call for fire, the night defence. When the battalion deploys he deploys with it, as its platoon leader, and stays for the tour. The minimum package is the lieutenant, his platoon sergeant and a battalion that keeps them together; without P006b, the lieutenant arrives as an individual replacement to a platoon he has never seen, and his education is worth perhaps half as much in his first month, because he cannot rehearse with men he does not know².

#### EC007-7 · The career course to the advisory corps (P007a with P006c)

The Captains Career Course is the selected advisory corps' recruiting ground. A captain in the course from 1965 is offered the advisory board at about week 10 of the resident phase, and those selected go from the course to the 32–36 weeks of language and adviser training of P006c⁵ and then to a battalion or subsector in the Republic. The career course gives him what the 2026 SFAB recruit has: a company command behind him, staff planning, fire support coordination, and enough doctrine to argue it. The cycle is a year from course entry to the adviser's arrival in country. About 1,200–2,000 advisers a year come by this route at the 1967–69 peak, about a third of the advisory corps' intake¹³. Without P006c the course produces educated captains who go to their units; with it, it produces the advisers.

#### EC007-8 · The adviser's rating (abandoned)

In the first months of 1964 the US Mission proposed that the senior adviser of each ARVN division and province contribute a written assessment to each Vietnamese commander's evaluation report. It would have given the board an honest second view of every battalion commander, written by a man with no stake in the Vietnamese patronage system. It was abandoned before the first board sat. A Vietnamese commander whose career depended on his American adviser's opinion would have stopped treating the adviser as a counsellor and started treating him as a superior or an enemy; the advice relationship, which depends on the counterpart's willingness to listen, would have become a reporting chain. The Front would have had, in writing, the proof that the Americans chose the Republic's officers. And the advisers themselves, asked to rate men they had to live beside, would have inflated the ratings as historical advisers inflated their reports. The adviser's assessment goes instead to his own chain, as P006c provides, and the board reads only Vietnamese evaluations.

#### EC007-9 · Promotion on the scoreboard (abandoned)

The second abandoned concept was rating battalion commanders on results: enemy killed, weapons captured, ralliers, hamlets rated secure. Several corps commanders asked for it in 1964 and the JGS personnel directorate drafted the criteria. It was dropped after the first season of trial ratings in IV Corps showed what it would do: it rewarded the commander who reported most, not the one who achieved most, and it gave every battalion commander in the Republic a career interest in inflating the body count and the Hamlet Evaluation scores. An officer rated on his kills will produce kills, and the dead will be whoever can be counted. The written criteria rate the officer on the state of his unit (strength present, training completed, desertion, the condition of weapons and equipment), the judgment of his rater and senior rater on his leadership in action, and his conduct toward the population, as attested by the province chief. They do not count bodies.

#### EC007-10 · Leavenworth for all (abandoned)

The third was to send the whole staff course to the United States, continuing the historical practice by which more than 8,000 RVNAF officers and men went to American schools in 1957–69⁶. It would have saved the cost of building the Dalat course and given every graduate an English-language education. It was abandoned on capacity and language. Leavenworth can take perhaps 30–60 Vietnamese officers a year without displacing its own students; the ARVN needs 250–400¹⁴. An officer educated in English on an American curriculum about American divisions comes home with a vocabulary his staff does not share. And an officer corps whose senior education happens in Kansas is an officer corps the Front can call American with some justice. The staff course is taught at Dalat in Vietnamese; about one in ten graduates goes on to Leavenworth.

#### EC007-11 · The wartime waiver (abandoned)

The fourth was the waiver: allowing a corps commander to promote an officer to major or lieutenant colonel without the career course or the staff course if he was in command in combat and the school seat could not be spared. The JGS granted waivers through the summer of 1965, when the battalions broke at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and every officer was needed in the field. By September there had been about 300, and the Commission's sample of them showed what everyone already knew: about one in three had gone to officers whose combat command was nominal, and the waiver had become the new thing for sale¹⁵. The board ended it and replaced it with deferral: an officer in command may defer his course by one board cycle, once, and is promoted on return. The cost is that the gate holds in the summer of 1965 when the battalions most need their majors.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**F007-4** Patronage leaves the promotion list and gathers where the list stops: at the general officers, at assignments, and at the course seats. A system that publishes its promotions and its battalion commands takes the junta's most valuable everyday currency, and the currency moves. It moves first to the top, where the President chooses one of three names for each general officer and each division, and where the choice is still his (P007c's negative specification). It moves sideways to assignments the board does not make: the division staffs, the corps logistics commands, the Saigon headquarters, the province chief posts, the training centres, the posts with contracts and rice and fuel. A board-selected lieutenant colonel can be given a regiment in the U Minh or a staff post in Saigon, and which he gets is still a favour. And it moves down to the schools: a seat in the Dalat staff course, a certificate, a pass mark. The Commission of P001b can follow the course seat, because the seat leaves a paper record; it cannot easily follow an assignment, because every assignment can be defended on the needs of the service. I judge that the reform removes perhaps half of the sale of commands below the general officers by 1966 and concentrates the remainder in assignments and at the top².

**F007-5** The enemy moves his ambush from the educated battalion to the one that is not, and from the battalion's road to the general's plan. COSVN's practice from Binh Gia onward was to attack a post and ambush the relief; the relief came, because the generals sent it, and it came badly, because its commander had not been taught otherwise. Where the battalion commander now scouts his route and moves by bounds, the enemy needs more force and better bait; he can still get the column sent, because that decision is the general's. He also looks for the units the reform does not reach in 1964–65: the Regional Force companies under province chiefs who still choose their own commanders, the CIDG camps outside the RVNAF chain, the ARVN battalions in I Corps where the alternate lists run out first. This is the same displacement as F004-1, at a different level: the denied target is not the rearmed post but the well-led battalion, and the enemy goes around it.

**F007-6** Enemy penetration moves to the board and the personnel directorate. A board that decides every promotion and command in the army is a better target than any division staff. Its files hold every officer's ratings, every commander's weaknesses, every senior officer's patrons, and the order in which colonels will rise. An agent in the personnel directorate can see who will command which regiment six months before he does; an agent on a panel can push an officer up or down. The record holds that Colonel Pham Ngoc Thao, a communist agent, rose to take part in the coup attempt of February 1965. The board reads performance, and Thao had been, by American accounts, an effective province chief of Kien Hoa; nothing in an evaluation report reveals a man's other loyalty. The Military Security Service vets the board's staff and panels; it was itself penetrated (INTEL.penetration). I expect the enemy to have a source inside the board's secretariat within about eighteen months and to treat its lists as his best guide to the ARVN's future leadership².

### Bottleneck migration

**F007-7** The binding constraint on ARVN leadership moves up, from the battalion to the division and the corps, where it was always heaviest. In 1964 the ARVN's leadership was weak at every level, and the constraint a battalion's adviser felt was the battalion commander. From about 1966, with four in five battalion commanders educated and about three in four competent³, the constraint the adviser feels is above him: the division commander who sends the battalion down the road, the corps commander who holds his reserve in the provincial capital, the general who will not accept a casualty list before a coup. Those men were commissioned before 1957, are untouched by P007b, and are chosen by the President from a list of three under P007c. The Binh Gia pattern was a general's plan executed by battalions; the reform fixes the battalions. The plan remains the general's until the 1957 classes reach division command in about 1971–72, and the record's most damaging ARVN failures of 1972 and 1975 were failures of corps and division command. This is the memorandum's most useful bottleneck: the ARVN's leadership problem becomes, from 1966, a problem of generals, which no school can solve and only the President and the American lever can.

**F007-8** In 1964–66 the constraint inside the officer corps moves to the field grades and to officer pay. The seven classes of 1957–63 fill the lieutenants' and captains' grades with educated officers in 1964, but they reach major only from about 1964–66 and lieutenant colonel from about 1966–68¹⁶. The career-course gate slows the making of majors in exactly the grade the ARVN was shortest of, and the waiver that would have relieved it was ended. So the field-grade shortage of the record continues through 1965 and is, if anything, sharper for a year; the battalions are led by captains more often in 1965 than they were historically, by educated captains². The second constraint is pay. An educated captain whose salary has lost half its value to inflation (ECON.inflation) faces the same choice as an uneducated one, and the reform does not pay him (P007b's negative specification). Where the Commission musters the pay and the board makes promotion depend on a clean record, the incentive to take money weakens; where pay falls further behind prices, as it will from 1966, it returns.

**F007-9** In the American force the constraint moves from the lieutenant's competence to the strategy above him and the captains beside him. An American platoon led by an educated lieutenant in 1966 still conducts the search-and-destroy operation its brigade commander planned, against an enemy who decides whether to fight, under a body-count system the battalion commander is rated on. The lieutenant's education is worth a great deal inside that operation and nothing about whether the operation should be conducted. Meanwhile the company commanders the lieutenant needs are captains, and P007a takes one in eight of them into school. The American Army's binding constraint at the tactical level shifts from platoon leaders who learn on the job to a thinner layer of company commanders, and at the operational level it does not move at all.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F007-10** The schools take the ARVN's best captains as instructors and 5–8% of its officers as students, in the two years when the battalions most need them. A 2026 small-group course needs instructors who have done what they teach, and the Vietnamese faculty of about 600–1,800³ must come from the company commanders and battalion staff officers with the best records — the same men the board would put in command. In 1964 and 1965, while the Republic loses battalions at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, perhaps 1,500–2,500 officers are in school or teaching at any time instead of in units¹⁷. I judge this the provision's heaviest cost, and I accept it, because the alternative is to spend the classes of 1964–65 the way the historical army spent its officers: in the field, untaught, and gone. But the cost is real. The battalion that lost its best captain to the Infantry School in March 1965 fought at Ba Gia without him.

**F007-11** In the American Army the career course, the advisory corps and the rotating battalions compete for one pool of captains, and the shortage that F006-10 put a year early arrives harder. About one captain in eight is in school from 1966³; the selected advisory corps takes about a third of its 5,500–6,000 a year from captains; and the battalions of P006b need their company commanders for a full year's training and tour. The historical Army met its captain shortage in 1967–69 by promoting lieutenants after about two years of service and by giving companies to lieutenants; under P007a's gates a first lieutenant cannot be made captain without his basic course and cannot command a company as a captain without the expectation of the career course before major. The wartime Army will need to defer the career course, as the ARVN defers its own, and I expect the deferral rate to reach about half of eligible captains in 1967–69¹⁸. The cost is a thinner, younger company-command layer in the American battalions of the peak years than P006b alone would have given.

The American training base costs about 1,500–2,500 instructor and staff spaces in the United States by 1967. With no troop ceiling, these are not spaces against the Republic's ceiling; they are officers and NCOs not in units anywhere, and their cost is real in a force that is expanding without the reserves. The US personnel in the Republic for P007b and P007c — about 350–500 and 60–90³ — come from the same advisory pool as P006c and are, at about 3% of the field advisory strength at its peak, a modest charge.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F007-12** The historical ARVN's command churn, its captain-led battalions and its late schools are erased, and most of the 1968 crash commissioning is erased with them. In May 1971, 80 of 133 ARVN battalion commanders had been in command a year or less and 46 of 133 battalions were commanded by captains. Under P007c the command tour is 18–24 months and the battalions are commanded by board-selected officers; the captain-led battalion survives where the alternate list runs out, but it is the exception by 1967 rather than the rule. The ARVN's field-grade command and staff schooling begins in 1964 rather than 1967. The diploma bar to major is gone. The 1968 mobilization, which cut the officer candidate course from 37 to 24 weeks¹⁹, still needs its officers, and the gate holds them for their branch course; the mobilization's lieutenants of 1968–69 are fewer and better taught, and some ARVN platoons are led by sergeants for a season in their place². The attention that the historical reforms of 1965 and 1968–71 consumed⁶ — repeated JGS directives on promotion, repeated American pressure to promote on merit — is released for the war, and so is the advisers' time spent on the counterpart who could not plan a fire.

**F007-13** In the American force the wartime officer who was commissioned in 23 weeks and sent to a platoon with nothing more is erased. The historical Officer Candidate School at Fort Benning produced, in 1966–68, lieutenants who went to their platoons with no branch course at all; the most notorious of them, Second Lieutenant William Calley, was commissioned from it in September 1967. Under P007a every one of those lieutenants goes through the branch course, which includes the law of war, escalation of force and the treatment of detainees, before he leads a platoon. I state plainly what this does and does not erase. It erases the lieutenant who was never taught; it does not erase the lieutenant who was taught and does not care, or the company whose captain and battalion commander make clear what they want. Whether the massacre at Son My in March 1968 runs differently is a question for Part 3 and the chapter, and I will not answer it here by assumption; the provision makes such an event less likely at the margin and does nothing about a command climate set by officers it did not educate.

### Perverse effect

**F007-14** An educated officer corps whose top is chosen by the palace is a coup faction in waiting. By 1967–68 the ARVN has several thousand educated, board-promoted majors and lieutenant colonels who have been told, in writing and for years, that command goes to merit. They will watch the President choose division commanders from a list of three for loyalty, and they will watch corps commanders like the ones the record names — appointed for loyalty, protected in their businesses²⁰ — survive defeats that would have ended a battalion commander's career under the board's rules. That is the grievance of the Young Turks of 1964 with an education and a written standard behind it. I do not expect it to produce a coup while the Americans are present in strength and the aid condition is enforced; I expect it to produce one or two serious plots in 1970–72 as the lever weakens, and a faction in the officer corps that the President must buy or purge². In 1964–65 the danger is different: the generals of the juntas see the board as an American instrument aimed at them, and its first general-officer list is a coup grievance.

**F007-15** Success takes the best battalion commanders away from their battalions. An officer who commands a battalion well for eighteen months is promoted, sent to Dalat, and given a regiment, a province or a division staff. That is how an army should work, and it is how this one now works; but the ARVN's best battalions historically depended on a single commander who stayed, and the board's fixed tour removes him on a calendar. The Delta district that depended on a 21st Division battalion commander who knew its paddies loses that knowledge when he goes. This is the same perverse effect as F002-10, at the battalion level, and it is minor because the man who replaces him is chosen by the same board.

**F007-16** In the American Army the gates turn education into a ticket to be punched. The historical Army's officer corps in 1970 was found by the Army War College's own Study on Military Professionalism to be careerist: officers chasing the assignments and schools that led to promotion, commanding for six months to get their command ticket, and reporting what their superiors wanted. P006b already ended the six-month command. P007a adds gates: the basic course before first lieutenant, the career course before major, the staff course before lieutenant colonel. A gate that is a hard bar makes the course a box to be ticked; an officer in 1969 will ask whether he has his career course before he asks what he learned in it. I judge that the gates make the careerism the 1970 study described somewhat worse in its form and somewhat better in its substance, because the officer must at least sit the course². The net effect on the officer corps' integrity is close to nil.

**F007-17** Relief for cause and a capped evaluation can teach caution as easily as courage. The historical ARVN commander avoided losses because Diem punished them; at Ap Bac in January 1963 the 7th Division's officers would not close with the enemy because their careers depended on avoiding casualties. A board that relieves for failure in action will, unless its criteria are written well, teach the same lesson: the commander who never attacks is never relieved. The written criteria rate failure to engage as failure, and the thirty-day review can overturn a relief for a loss honestly taken; but the senior rater's top third is a scarce good, and a battalion commander who loses a company in a bold action and one who loses nothing by staying in his base will not always be told apart. I expect the reform to make battalion commanders more willing to fight and somewhat more careful in reporting; it does not end the habit of caution that the generals above them reward². A second, smaller perverse effect is that the capped top block becomes a regional and factional quota in some corps, the senior rater sharing it out among the officers of his own region or religion. The board can see it, and the Commission can investigate it, and it will not be stopped entirely.

### Anticipation

**F007-18** The enemy declines the road ambush against the battalion that would have defeated it, and the reform's largest gain becomes a disaster that did not happen. The improvement this memorandum makes to the ARVN in the field is mostly negative: battalions not cut in two on the road, relief columns that arrive, posts relieved before they fall. The enemy, who chooses most engagements, reads the educated battalion's route security and its fire plan and does not spring the ambush; he waits for another column, or attacks another post. The metric that registers this is nothing. The body count does not rise; it may fall, because the battalions are fighting fewer of the enemy's chosen battles. The Hamlet Evaluation scores do not move. What moves is a number nobody reports: battalions lost per year, which should fall by a third or more from 1966². This is the improvement without a metric that A13 names, and I want it stated at full weight here so that when the chronicle shows an ARVN battalion that came home, nobody reads it as a quiet week.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F007-19** COSVN reads the reform within a season, through its agents and its battlefield, and draws one right conclusion and one wrong one. The right conclusion arrives from the battlefield in 1965: the ARVN battalions in the Delta and around Saigon are harder to ambush than they were, and the generals above them have not changed. The enemy's military proselytizing apparatus (binh van) knows the officer corps' politics intimately, and it reports by mid-1964 that a board is choosing battalion commanders and that the generals hate it. The doctrine Hanoi derives is the one F007-5 and F007-7 describe: aim at the general's plan, not the battalion's march; attack where the reform has not reached; wait for the top. It propagates through COSVN's military staff to the regiments of the 9th Division by 1965 and to the local-force battalions within a year.

The wrong conclusion is political. The Front reads the board as an American selection system for the Republic's officers and makes that its line: officers chosen by Americans, promoted for serving them. In 1964 there is enough truth in it — the aid condition is American — to hurt. But the board's lists are Vietnamese and visibly so, the field commission brings sergeants of the people into the officer corps, and the educated captain of 1966 is a man whom his soldiers see promoted for what he did. The line works against the generals, who are visibly chosen under American pressure; it works poorly against the battalions. What the enemy gets right that hurts most is the target he chooses for proselytizing: the passed-over, the relieved, and the educated colonel who will not be made general. Those men existed historically; now there is a written list of them.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F007-20** The enemy's counters are mostly cheap, and the one that is not is the one that matters. Penetrating the board's secretariat costs him an agent he probably already has. Proselytizing the passed-over costs him a cadre's time. Shifting his attacks to the Regional Forces and the CIDG costs him nothing he was not doing. Waiting for the generals to send a column costs time, which he has. These are cheap, and they mean the reform does not win the exchange outright. The counter that is expensive is the one he must pay to beat the educated battalion itself: a better ambush, a larger force, a second regiment to block the column's alternative route, a longer reconnaissance. Where a regiment sufficed in 1964, a division's worth of planning is needed by 1966 to destroy an ARVN battalion led by an officer who will not take the road blind². Each such operation consumes main-force strength, time and tonnage that he would otherwise have spent elsewhere. The exchange modestly favours the Republic at the battalion level and is neutral above it.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F007-21** The loop through Washington runs late and in two directions. In 1964–65 nothing here returns: the ARVN's improvement is below the level Washington measures, the July 1965 decision rests on the PAVN regiments in the Highlands and the broken general reserve, and American tolerance is spent, as the checkpoint recorded, by the casualties of 1965–68 and by Tet. The American lives saved by P007a's lieutenants are real and small, a fraction of the rate the record has already credited to P006b, and they do not move the casualty band. The loop returns in 1969–72, with Vietnamization. An ARVN whose battalions and regiments are commanded by educated, board-selected officers serving full tours is a better case for Vietnamization than the historical one, and the honest evaluation reports and command lists give Washington a truer picture of the ARVN's leadership than it historically had. That narrows the credibility gap a little in 1969–71, when the question in Congress is whether the ARVN can stand. It also gives Congress a target: an aid condition on the Republic's choice of generals is easy to describe as an American hand on an ally's throat, and in 1971–73, when Congress is cutting aid, it is a reason to stop paying for an army whose officers Americans choose. The return path ends where the American lever ends.

### Open channel

**F007-22** The field commission and the removal of the diploma bar change who the Republic's officers are. The historical ARVN officer corps was drawn from the baccalaureate-holding urban and town middle class, disproportionately Catholic and Northern in its upper ranks, and its officers commanded soldiers from a world they did not share¹. The Front made that distance one of its strongest themes, and the soldiers felt it. Several hundred field commissions a year from 1965, most of them sons of the southern countryside returning to the territorial forces of their own provinces, and the end of the diploma bar to major, begin to change the composition of the officer corps from below. By 1970 perhaps one ARVN lieutenant or captain in eight was a sergeant in 1964²¹. That is slow and it does not reach the generals; but it is the first change in this war to the class character of the Republic's army, and it weakens one of the Front's arguments where it is most true.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F007-23 · Counter-directional** The provision makes the ARVN's officers better at fighting the American way, and so more dependent on the American way, in the years when they should have been learning to fight without it. The 2026 curricula, American and Vietnamese, are built for an army with artillery on call, close air support within minutes, medical evacuation by helicopter, and an abundant supply chain. An ARVN company commander educated on them learns to plan and call those things, and does it well; the record has already found that every improvement in the ARVN's ability to request American fire deepens its dependence on that fire, and that the dependence was never contested by the instrument that could have contested it. This memorandum is that instrument, and as written it deepens the dependence rather than contesting it. The Dalat staff course teaches the corps operation as the Americans run it; the career course teaches the fire plan with American guns behind it. P007b pushes the RVNAF's self-sufficiency up by educating its staff and logistics officers³, and that is real; but the self-sufficiency it gives is in running the systems the Americans provide, not in fighting when they stop. When the ration of 1974 falls and American air is gone, these educated officers will know precisely what they lack. I state its weight as major for the war's last phase and minor before it, against a primary effect that is major from 1966. The remedy is in the curriculum: a module on fighting with Vietnamese fires alone, taught from 1966 at the career course and at Dalat, would cost nothing and was not written into the provision. I record the gap rather than invent the module.

There is a second, nearer counter-directional effect: in 1964–65 the reform sharpens the generals' coup grievance and takes the best captains out of the battalions in the worst two years. Both are real and both are temporary. The first is the one that will outlast the war's middle years.

**F007-24 · Strongest positive** From 1966 the ARVN's battalions are led as the historical army's never were, and the first serious narrowing of the gap in the officer corps begins. On the record's own historical figures, more than half the ARVN's battalion commanders in 1971 had held command a year or less and more than a third of battalions were led by captains⁶. Under these provisions, by 1967 about four battalion commanders in five have been through the branch and career courses, about three in four are competent by an adviser's judgment against about one in two historically, the command tour is about eighteen months, and a commander who fails is relieved in writing and replaced from a list³. That is the difference between a battalion that walks down the road into the ambush and one that scouts it; between a post relieved and a post lost; between a battalion that knows its province and one whose commander arrived in the spring. I put its value at a band in ARVN effectiveness and two in ARVN leadership, in combination with the earlier provisions, and I judge it the largest single gain to the ARVN's fighting power of any provision so far². Its effect accrues from 1966, peaks in 1969–72 when the 1957–63 classes reach regimental and the first division commands, and is felt in the conventional war of 1972 if the President chooses from the board's list. It does not reach the corps commanders of 1972 or the President of 1975, and those are where the Republic's last battles were lost. It moves the ARVN's capability inside the rung; whether it moves the rung depends on the generals, which is to say on the lever, and the record already says what happens to the lever.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

The advisory effort changes more than in any memorandum since 006. About 350–500 Americans go to the faculties of Thu Duc, Dalat and the branch schools and to a curriculum cell at the JGS Central Training Command, and about 60–90 to the board's advisory cell and the JGS personnel directorate³; these posts replace and enlarge the historical school advisory detachments. The field adviser's job changes in kind: with an educated counterpart he spends less time on basic tactics and more on operations, fire coordination and the territorial war, and his reports carry more weight because they can be checked against the counterpart's own evaluation report. MACV's J-1 and the senior advisers at corps acquire a new task: watching the board's procedures, enforcing the aid condition on the general-officer list, and reporting, in writing, when a corps commander overrides a relief or a list. That last task is the most politically delicate any American officer in the Republic has, and it falls on the senior adviser of each corps.

### US conventional forces

American battalions are led from 1964 by platoon leaders who have been through a 2026 branch course and from 1966 by company commanders, most of whom have been through a career course or will defer it. The first-month mistakes of the historical lieutenant are fewer; the battalion's fire planning, ambush drills and night defence are better from its first contact, especially in combination with P006b's collective training. Battalion and brigade commanders are unchanged until about 1971–72, and the strategy, the search-and-destroy operations and the body-count system are unchanged. The captain shortage of 1967–69 is sharper.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

Special Forces officers commissioned from 1957 are educated under P007a; the A-detachment commanders of 1964–68 are captains and lieutenants of those classes. The effect is small, because Special Forces selection and training already set a high standard. The CIDG is outside the RVNAF chain and is not reached by P007b or P007c; its camps are among the targets to which the enemy displaces, and the LLDB officers who command it alongside the Americans are ARVN officers who, from 1964, are educated and rated like any other.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

This is where the change lands. The ARVN's battalions are commanded from 1965–66 by educated, board-selected officers on 18–24-month tours; its regiments from about 1967–69; its divisions, in part, from about 1971–72. The JGS gains a personnel directorate that works on written criteria, a Command and General Staff course at Dalat from 1964, and a Central Training Command that runs the branch and career courses. Corps and division commanders are unchanged until the board's general-officer list begins to reach them, and the President chooses among the three names. The 1965 reform and the 1968–71 changes to the promotion system⁶ do not occur, because they are superseded.

### RF/PF and territorial security

The territorial forces' officers improve by assignment and by field commission. Sector and subsector staffs and Regional Force company commanders are drawn from the educated classes from 1964, and from 1965 several hundred field-commissioned lieutenants a year return to their provinces' Regional and Popular Forces. The Regional Force company is not on the board's command list; its commander is still chosen by the province chief, which makes P002b's list the protection that matters at that level. The improvement is real and moderate: better-led posts, better-rehearsed reactions, and officers who come from the countryside they guard.

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged in its conduct. The squadron and wing commanders who fly and plan it in 1965–68 were commissioned before 1957; the junior pilots of the Air Force and Navy continua are educated under P007a, which does not change the targeting, the restrictions or the air war's results.

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged. The provisions do not touch the Trail, its throughput or the forces that attack it.

### Arc Light

Unchanged. The bomber force and its targeting are untouched; the ARVN battalion commander who requests a strike through his adviser and the coordinate cell does so better, which is part of the dependence in F007-23.

### Airmobile formations

The lieutenants and captains of the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) and the aviation battalions are educated under P007a; the ARVN battalions that are lifted by them are led by educated officers from 1965–66 who plan the air assault with their American aviation commanders from a common doctrine. The change is in the quality of the combined air assault, not its scale.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The Vietnamese Navy's and Marine Corps' officers commissioned from 1957 are educated under P007b's extension to their services as far as the provision's text reaches, which is the ARVN and, by inference, the Vietnamese Marine battalions that fight as infantry. The Vietnamese Navy's river assault groups are not explicitly covered and are unchanged. The American Mobile Riverine Force of 1967 has educated platoon leaders like any other American unit.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

The army officers posted to the district and provincial centres of P006a are educated and rated from 1964, and their conduct on warrant raids is somewhat more disciplined by training in the law of war and detention. The board's files and the Phung Hoang file are two separate targets for the enemy's agents and for the palace. Otherwise unchanged.

### Third-country forces

Unchanged. The Korean, Australian, New Zealand, Thai and Philippine contingents are not reached by these provisions.

### Prior provisions

- **P001b** · GVN Anti-Corruption Programme · The Commission becomes the board's enforcement arm against sold promotions, commands and course seats, and investigates the cases the evaluation report makes visible; the Aid Compliance Board carries the aid condition behind the general-officer list. The Commission's chronic shortage of honest replacements for officers it removes eases from 1966 as the educated pool grows.
- **P002b** · Provincial Administration Reform · The Selection Board reads the officers' evaluation reports from 1964 and competes with the command board for the same field-grade officers until 1966, after which the pool grows. Chiefs who are officers are rated by the same system.
- **P004a, P004b, P004c** · Small arms · Employed by company commanders who were taught why the marksman stays with his squad and why the squad gun needs fire discipline; the abandoned concepts of 004 are not retried.
- **P006a** · Phung Hoang · Army officers in the centres are educated and rated; the board is a second target beside the national file.
- **P006b** · US unit rotation · The educated lieutenant joins his platoon during its collective training and stays for the tour; the battalions compete with the career course for captains.
- **P006c** · Selected US Advisory Corps · The career course becomes the advisory corps' main recruiting ground for captains, and the advisers advise educated counterparts; the competition for captains sharpens.
