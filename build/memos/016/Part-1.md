# Memorandum 016 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier


## §1.1 Glock 21 Gen5 MOS.45 pistol with Glock night sights and SureFire X300T Turbo weapon light — P016a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

This is the smallest weapon I have been sent and the one that will reach the most hands. Every pistol and revolver carried by an American serviceman in Southeast Asia and by every man of the Republic's armed forces is withdrawn, and in its place each holder receives the same thing: a Glock 21 in.45 Auto with factory tritium night sights and a SureFire X300 Turbo light on its rail. The National Police receive it as their standard handgun as well¹.

**The pistol.** The Glock 21 Gen5 MOS is the fifth-generation full-size.45 pistol made by GLOCK Ges.m.b.H. of Deutsch-Wagram, Austria, and by GLOCK, Inc. of Smyrna, Georgia. The Glock 21 entered production in 1990², and its Gen5 version, with the flared magazine well, the nDLC finish, the Marksman barrel and no finger grooves, was announced in February 2023³. It is a striker-fired, polymer-framed, short-recoil, locked-breech pistol with Glock's Safe Action trigger: three passive safeties and no manual safety lever. It feeds from a 13-round double-stack magazine⁴. The slide is cut for a reflex optic (the MOS cut) under a cover plate; no optic is part of this provision [U: "standard night sights"].

**The sights.** "Standard night sights" I read as Glock's own factory night sights (GNS): a steel front post and a steel rear notch, each holding a sealed tritium vial that glows green without light or batteries, set on a sight radius of 174 mm⁴. Tritium's half-life is about 12.3 years, so a vial fitted in January 1964 gives a little over half its first brightness by the end of 1975⁵.

**The light.** The SureFire X300T Turbo is a 650-lumen white LED weapon light with a turbo reflector that throws a narrow, intense beam: 66,000 candela at its peak, reaching 514 metres to the standard illumination threshold. It runs 1.5 hours on high from two 123A lithium cells, weighs 124.7 g with them and is 95.5 mm long. Its body is hard-anodised aluminium and it is rated IPX7, watertight to one metre for 30 minutes. I take the X300T-A lever-latch version, which clamps to the Glock's accessory rail in a few seconds without tools⁶. The pistol and light ride in a light-bearing duty holster of Safariland 6360 class, a holster moulded to the pistol-and-light combination with a retention hood⁷.

**What it replaces.** In the American forces it replaces the M1911A1.45 pistol, the standard sidearm since 1926 and the pistol of the whole war. It also replaces the.38 revolvers carried by aircrew, by some advisers and by the military police of some commands, the M1917.45 revolvers still found in the tunnels, and the special-purpose pistols of the raiders and tunnel clearers: the suppressed.38 revolvers, the High Standard HD.22s and the Navy SEALs' suppressed Smith & Wesson Mk 22 Mod 0, the "Hush Puppy"⁸⁹. The aircrews' revolver was usually the Smith & Wesson Model 15 Combat Masterpiece, carried in the survival vest beside the AN/PRC-90 radio¹⁰. In the RVNAF — the ARVN, the Vietnamese Air Force and Navy, the Marine Division and the Regional and Popular Forces — it replaces the M1911A1, the revolvers and the French MAC Mle 1950 and MAS Mle 1935S pistols still in the territorials' armouries¹¹¹². I read "ARVN" in the provision as the whole RVNAF, the same reading memorandum 004 gave the rifle¹³; if the user means the army alone, the territorials keep the M1911A1 and the count falls by about a tenth. In the National Police and the Police Field Force it replaces the.38 Smith & Wesson Model 10 and M1917 revolvers of the constables and the M1911A1 of the officers¹⁴.

**What it does not replace.** The police keep their historical long arms. Erratum E05 ruled that the constable carries the M1 or M2 carbine and a pump shotgun where his station issues one, the station's armoury holds the M3A1, and the Police Field Force carries carbines, the M1918A2 automatic rifle and the M79¹⁴. The user's provision names the handgun only, so I change the handgun only and E05 otherwise stands. The CIA-armed Provincial Reconnaissance Units, the CIDG, the hamlet militia and the People's Self-Defense Force are not the RVNAF or the police, and keep what they hold¹⁵. Third-country forces keep their own sidearms.

**Arrival.** 1 January 1964, the campaign's opening and the default for an undated provision (CONFIG `default_arrival`). Issue to the Americans in country and to the RVNAF's regular units runs through the first quarter of 1964; to the territorials and the police through the summer¹⁶.

If this war's forces are larger or smaller than history's at a date, the count follows them.

**Organisation.** None: it is an individual weapon. Each holder signs for a pistol, a light, three magazines and a holster, as he signed for the M1911A1 and two magazines²³. Spare cells and magazines are held at company, district and police-station level.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

One man per pistol. No new crews: every holder already exists and already carries a sidearm or, in the police, a revolver.

The spaces it consumes are a training and armament detachment: about 40–60 Americans in 1964 to train the RVNAF's and the police's instructors and armourers, set up the issue at the corps depots and the National Police's provincial headquarters, and write the Vietnamese manual and range course²⁴. From 1966 the work passes to the existing advisory structure and the Vietnamese schools at Thu Duc, Quang Trung and the National Police Academy, and the spaces end²⁵. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so the spaces cost only their training pipeline.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**The pistol and its round.** The Glock 21 fires the.45 Auto, the same cartridge as the M1911A1, the M1917 revolver, the M3A1 sub-machine gun and the Thompson⁴¹¹. That is the most valuable single fact about the choice: no new round enters the theater's ammunition system. I assume the period's M1911 ball round, a 230-grain full-metal-jacket bullet at about 255–260 m/s, for every holder, the police included²⁶. Its muzzle energy is about 490–510 joules against about 270 for a.38 Special 158-grain round from a 4-inch revolver²⁷.

Each holder carries 39 rounds in three magazines, or 40 with one chambered: 13 in the pistol and two spares⁴. The M1911A1 holder carried 21 in three seven-round magazines, the revolver-armed constable six in the cylinder and perhaps twelve loose in a pouch¹¹²⁸. A man with a Glock 21 therefore goes into a fight with about twice the cartridges of an officer with a.45 and about twice to three times those of a constable with a.38.

**The light as a tool, not a weapon.** The X300T's 650 lumens and 66,000 candela light a man at 25 metres at about 100 lux, a bright office, and at 50 metres at about 26 lux. Both follow from the candela figure divided by the square of the distance. In the face of a man at five metres it dazzles: he cannot see past it to the shooter for a second or two²⁹. It is a momentary-or-constant switch worked by the trigger finger or the support-hand thumb without shifting the grip⁶.

**Practice ammunition.** A 2026-standard pistol qualification and refresher takes about 150–200 rounds a year per holder³⁰. At the peak that is about 35–45 million.45 rounds a year in practice alone, about 730–940 tonnes at about 21 g a cartridge. Combat expenditure is a fraction of that.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The pistol has two "sensors" in the plain sense: a set of sights that glow and a light that illuminates. Neither finds anyone; both help a man see what is in front of him once he is in front of it. This provision touches neither half of find-and-fix as RULES A6 frames it. It does not find the main force and it does not identify the VCI. It touches the moment after identification: the arrest at a door at 0300, the clearing of a bunker, the search of a hut.

**Night.** This is where the provision does its work. The tritium sights let a man align his pistol on a dark target with no light at all; the post and notch glow as three dots³¹. The light identifies the target before he fires. Under a moonless sky in a hamlet the X300T turns an indistinct shape at 15 metres into a man whose hands can be seen, at about 290 lux. In history, a man in that position held a flashlight in one hand and a.45 in the other, and fired one-handed or not at all²²⁸.

**Triple canopy.** Canopy does not matter to a pistol's light at pistol distances; the beam is not looking up. Under canopy at night the darkness is total, and there the light and the tritium sights are the whole difference between seeing and not seeing.

**Monsoon.** Rain scatters the beam: in heavy rain the light reflects off the drops and back into the shooter's eyes, and usable distance falls to about 15–30 metres³². The pistol itself is indifferent to rain. The light is watertight to a metre for 30 minutes, which covers a paddy fall or a tunnel's flooded U-bend but not a long river swim⁶⁸.

**Tunnels.** The underground is the extreme case: no ambient light at all, distances of one to five metres, a man crawling on his belly. The tunnel clearer of history went in with an M1911 or revolver in one hand and a flashlight in the other²²⁸. Here the light is on the gun, the beam goes where the muzzle goes, and his other hand is free for the bayonet, the probe or his own balance.

The pistol's protection is its finish. The Gen5's nDLC coating on the slide and barrel and the polymer frame do not rust in the humidity that pitted the 20-year-old blued and parkerised steel of a rebuilt M1911A1⁴. Most M1911A1s in Vietnam were Second World War guns, some rebuilt more than once¹¹.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

A pistol has no communications. What matters here is that every man who carries a sidearm in the theater, American, Vietnamese soldier and Vietnamese policeman, carries the same one, with the same magazines, the same cells and the same cartridge. A captain advising a battalion can hand his magazine to his counterpart. A district police chief's armourer and the subsector's American adviser read the same parts list. The Vietnamese manual, range course and armourer's guide are written once in 1964³⁴.

The fit to Vietnamese hands is the one place where talking to the ARVN is a problem. The Glock 21's grip wraps a double-stack.45 magazine and is one of the largest of any service pistol; the Gen5's modular backstraps only add to it⁴³⁵. The average Vietnamese soldier of the period stood about 160 cm and had a smaller hand than the average American³⁶. A man with a small hand reaches the trigger of the Glock 21 with the pad of his finger rather than the joint and shoots it less well than he shot the slimmer.45; a minority will struggle with it at all³⁷. The tunnel clearers, chosen for being short, are the same men⁸. This is a real cost of this pistol in this army, not a footnote.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Ground.** A pistol goes where a man goes; there is no mobility question. The conditions that matter are heat, wet, mud and laterite dust. The Glock's polymer frame and nDLC finish resist the corrosion that ruins steel in the Delta's humidity, and its design tolerates dirt better than an old, loose M1911A1⁴³⁸. A Glock dropped in paddy mud and wiped off fires; a light dropped in the same mud works after it is rinsed⁶.

**Heat and the cells.** Lithium 123A cells keep most of their charge for ten years on a shelf at room temperature, but lose it faster stored at 45–60°C in an unventilated steel container in the sun, which is how much of the theater's stock will be stored³⁹. A cell left a year in a Conex at Long Binh may deliver perhaps two-thirds to three-quarters of its rated runtime⁴⁰.

**Underground.** In a tunnel the.45's muzzle blast is the old problem, unchanged by the new pistol: the same cartridge makes the same overpressure in a confined space and can leave the shooter deaf for minutes⁸. The light's 650 lumens reflected off laterite walls at two metres also dazzle the man behind it unless he uses it in short bursts⁴¹.

**At sea and on rivers.** The Navy's boat crews and the River and Coastal Police carry the same pistol. Salt water is harder on the light's switch and contacts than on the pistol; the lights of the riverine forces need fresh-water rinsing and more frequent replacement⁴².

### D8 · Logistics footprint

Small and mostly familiar. The pistol fires the theater's existing.45 round and needs no fuel. It adds three things to the supply system.

**Cells.** Two 123A lithium cells per light. A constable or a unit working nights uses the light for perhaps 10–20 minutes a night of real use, and a fresh pair lasts 1.5 hours on high⁶⁴³. At the peak I estimate about 1.5–2.0 million cells a year across about 230,000 lights, or about 25–35 tonnes⁴⁴. The tonnage is trivial. The hard part is that the cell has to reach the village police station at Long Dinh and the PF post at Long Huu, not the depot at Long Binh, and nothing in the Republic's own industry makes it⁴⁵. The record already carries this problem for the M4A1 carbine's CompM5 sight, whose battery supply after 1973 is untraced¹³ (studies/us-and-arvn-small-arms-and-individual-weapons-ag).

**Parts.** A Glock is maintained by replacing springs, the striker, the extractor and the recoil spring assembly at long intervals⁴⁶. A pistol fired 150–200 rounds a year in practice will not need a spring in the war's twelve years. Lights are replaced when they break, not repaired.

**Ammunition.** The practice ammunition in D3, about 730–940 tonnes a year at the peak, is the largest addition by weight and is still well under a thousandth of the theater's monthly port clearance⁴⁷. It can be cut sharply in a crisis without harm.

**Specialists.** Glock's own armourer course takes a day; any RVNAF or police armourer who can maintain an M1911A1 can be taught it in that time⁴⁸.

What the RVNAF cannot supply: the cells, the lights and the pistols' replacement parts, all imported throughout.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

Complete and immediate. This is the first provision in this exercise that the Republic holds from the first day in its whole quantity: three-quarters of the pistols issued in 1964 are Vietnamese [D: D1 table]. There is nothing to Vietnamise.

Training: a two- to three-day course takes a man who has fired an M1911A1 to qualification on the Glock with the light; a constable who has carried a revolver needs four to five days, half of it on safe handling, because the Glock has no manual safety and its trigger is the only thing between a man's finger and a discharge⁴⁹. The officer and NCO education programmes standing since 1957 give the RVNAF instructors to teach it, but the National Police have none of those programmes⁵⁰⁵¹ (CONFIG `[conditions]`). Their training is the soft point.

After 1973: the pistols last as long as the war does. The lights last as long as the cells do. If the American supply of 123A cells stops, the lights go dark one by one over about a year, and the pistol becomes a well-made.45 with glowing sights, which is still better than what it replaced⁵².

### D10 · Employment envelope

**Where it performs to specification.** At up to 25 metres³³, by day and especially at night, in the hand of a man trained to use the light in short bursts and to shoot with both hands. That describes the military policeman at a gate, the constable at a door, the tunnel clearer in Cu Chi, the officer in a perimeter overrun at 0300, the pilot of an F-4C or an A-1H on the ground in Laos waiting for the HH-3E, the door gunner of a downed UH-1D, the crew of a burning M113 or M48A3, and the post commander in his bunker when the wire is cut. The small arms study's finding that most of this war's fights were fought at 10–30 metres is a rifle finding, but it says the pistol's distance is the war's distance more often than a reader would guess (studies/us-and-arvn-small-arms-and-individual-weapons-ag)¹³.

**Where performance collapses.** Beyond about 25–40 metres a pistol is a poor weapon whoever makes it³³. At night, a light switched on and left on tells every man within a few hundred metres where the shooter is; in an ambush or on a perimeter at night the light is a beacon, and a man who uses it as a flashlight to walk by is a target⁵³. In heavy rain the light's reach falls to 15–30 metres⁵⁴. Tempo matters through the cells: a police station that runs night searches six nights a week uses cells three or four times faster than my average⁵⁵.

**Weeks rather than once.** The pistol keeps its performance indefinitely. The light keeps it as long as cells arrive. The training decays fastest: a constable who fires his pistol once a year forgets the light's switch under stress⁵⁶.

### D11 · Failure modes

**What breaks first: the cells.** A dead light is the commonest failure and also the gentlest: the pistol still fires and the tritium sights still glow.

**Second: the negligent discharge.** A striker-fired pistol with no manual safety, carried by men who learned on a.45 with a thumb safety and a grip safety or on a double-action revolver with a heavy trigger, will produce negligent discharges in the first year, most of them when the pistol is holstered with a finger on the trigger or taken from a holster in a hurry⁵⁷. The training in D9 is what holds this down; the police, trained least, will suffer it most.

**Third: loss and capture.** Every pistol carried by a man who is killed, captured or overrun is a pistol the Front takes. The pistol is the cadre's weapon and the assassin's weapon; history's Front carried the K-54, the Chinese Type 54 copy of the Soviet TT-33, as a mark of rank, and a captured American.45 was a prize¹¹⁵⁸. A Glock with a light and glowing sights is a better prize. The tritium keeps glowing in the Front's hands for years; the light dies with its first set of cells unless the Front captures cells too⁶.

**Partial failure.** A light that flickers because its contacts are corroded; a magazine spring weakened by being left loaded for years in a police station armoury; a sight vial cracked by a fall. Each leaves a working pistol.

**What the force does while it is unavailable.** Nothing changes. A man without a working light is a man with a pistol and glowing sights, still better than history's.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** Every pistol and light works; every cell is fresh; the holders are new to the weapon, the police most of all, and the negligent discharges are at their worst.

**Month twelve.** About 5–8% of the lights are lost, broken or dead, concentrated in the territorials and the police posts⁵⁹. The pistols are nearly all serviceable. Proficiency has risen in units that practise and fallen in police stations that do not.

**What declines first.** The cell supply to the bottom of the system: district police stations and PF posts. That decline is visible by mid-1965 if nothing is done about it⁶⁰. Proficiency in the police declines second.

**Over the war.** The tritium halves in brightness by about 1976⁶¹. The pistols do not wear out. The lights are replaced at about 5–8% a year⁶².

**The margin.** By day, at the distances pistols are used, the margin is the 13-round magazine and a newer, more reliable gun: in a fight that lasts past seven shots, the Glock holder is still firing when the.45 holder is reloading. At night, the margin is much larger. Against a target that cannot be seen, the M1911A1 holder fires at a sound or a flash; the Glock holder sees the man and aligns glowing sights on him. I estimate a trained man's chance of hitting a man at 7–15 metres in darkness on the first shot at about twice what it was with a flashlight in the other hand and a black front sight, low confidence⁶⁴. For the constable the margin is larger still: he goes from six.38 rounds and a flashlight to thirteen.45 rounds with the light on the gun.

What the benchmark is not: the pistol is not compared with the carbine, the M4A1, the M249, the M60 or anything else that defends a post, because it does not do those things. Nor is it compared with the M3A1 sub-machine gun, which fires the same round from a 30-round magazine and stays in the station armouries and the vehicle crews' racks¹⁴.

### D14 · Negative specification
- It does not defend a post, a hamlet or a perimeter. A pistol is the weapon of the last few metres, and nothing about it changes who holds a hamlet after dark.
- It does not find anyone. It touches neither the main force nor the identification of the VCI; it helps only at the moment of arrest or clearance, after someone else has done the finding.
- It does not rearm the police. Under erratum E05 the constable keeps his carbine and the Police Field Force its carbines, BARs and M79s; only the handgun changes.
- It does not reach the CIDG, the PRUs, the hamlet militia, the People's Self-Defense Force or third-country forces.
- It carries no suppressor. The suppressed revolvers, the High Standard HD.22s and the Smith & Wesson Mk 22 Mod 0 pistols of the tunnel clearers and raiders are withdrawn with the rest, and the standard Glock 21 is louder underground than they were.
- It does not fit small hands well. Its grip is one of the largest of any service pistol, and many Vietnamese and some American holders shoot it less well than they shot the slimmer M1911A1.
- Its light betrays its user at night when misused; it is a weapon light, not a flashlight.
- Its light needs American 123A lithium cells. The Republic cannot make them, and the light dies with the cell supply.
- It has no manual safety; in poorly trained hands it produces negligent discharges.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
It denies the dark doorway and the dark tunnel: the man who waited in a hut or a tunnel bend for the searcher with a flashlight in one hand now faces a light on the gun, glowing sights and thirteen rounds. It also denies the assassin his easiest assumption, that the village official or the constable has six rounds and no light. It gives the enemy something back: every pistol lost with a killed official or an overrun post is a prized weapon for the Front's cadres and security sections.

### D16 · Honest verdict
This is a modest provision that does real good at the very bottom of the war, and I will not dress it up. The Glock 21 with night sights and a light is a better pistol than the M1911A1 in every way except the size of its grip, and for the constable it is a much better weapon than the.38 he replaces. Its effect is concentrated where pistols are actually used: in tunnels, in night arrests and house searches, in the last minutes of an overrun bunker, and in the hands of a downed pilot. A tunnel clearer at Cu Chi now goes into the dark with the light on his muzzle and his other hand free. A district police sergeant at a door at 0300 can see whether the man inside is holding a grenade or a child. Those men will live, and arrest instead of kill, a little more often. It will not change who holds the hamlets, who initiates the war's fights, or the end state, and I expect its effect on the American dead to be measured in dozens a year, not hundreds. Its costs are small and real: a cell supply the Republic cannot make, negligent discharges in the first year, a grip too large for many Vietnamese hands, and better pistols for the Front's assassins wherever an official dies with one on his belt.
