## §4.0 The course of the war

The war this command has fought since the first day of 1964 is a war in which the Republic was given, in a single session of decrees, most of the institutions history let it build too late, and lost anyway, a year later than history's Republic and in the same way. The pistol of this memorandum does not change that sentence. It changes something at the bottom of the war, where a man stands at a door at night, and I will say where.

On 1 January 1964 Saigon made land to the tiller law, gave the Commission Against Corruption an American power to suspend aid behind it, created a resident rural police and a Special Branch with the files on the infrastructure, and put its province and district chiefs on a ranked list chosen by examination. The same week the first ship of IRON GATE berthed at Khanh Hoi with carbines for the whole army and the territorials, and the gunships of the 14th Air Commando Wing flew their first night over the posts. The pistols came through the same gate: every officer, gunner and post commander of the RVNAF, every American in the country and, by August, every constable of the new police drew a Glock with glowing sights and a light, in exchange for a worn.45 or a six-shot revolver. Khanh's coup came on 30 January and used the combined command's network rather than breaking it. The Republic's governments went on falling for a year and a half, as history's did, but its new institutions did not fall with them.

The first year in the countryside was the year of the posts. About 600 village police posts were settled by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by July 1965, each with ten constables and a Popular Forces platoon beside it, and the constable and the PF corporal walked the hamlet every night with the census booklets. From the summer the constable lit the face at the door with the light on his pistol and held the booklet in his other hand, and the night check became a thing a ten-man post could do in two hours and believe. The Front read the posts within weeks and set out to kill the constables before they learned the village. It overran Long Dinh on Route 4 on 3 October and killed seven of its nine constables, and by July 1965 it had taken about 85 posts and killed about 410 constables, almost all where the posts had been settled ahead of the night security that should have stood beside them. Where the platoon was there, the post held, as Long Huu held on 17 November 1964, and from December the Front stopped assaulting such posts and went for whatever had been left out: the constables on the road, the camps, the militia with the handed-down Garands.

The pistols had their own first season, and it was not a clean one. The PF leaders took the lights on ambush and used them as torches, and four platoons in Long An and Dinh Tuong were fired on from the next dike before the province chiefs banned it in June. The police, who drew last and were trained least, shot their own feet and colleagues with a pistol that has no safety lever, until the Directorate doubled its course in October. A gunship over a Dinh Tuong post held its fire for six minutes in November because its crew could not tell the post's lights from the enemy's. And the Front took its first Glock at Rach Kien on 22 March and by June had told its security sections to hold fire until the lamp came on and then fire at it. By the end of 1964 the light was used for two seconds at a door or a bend and for nothing else, and both sides knew it.

The American war arrived in 1965 on the historical timetable and with a different air campaign. Washington approved an opening strike on the whole target list, and between 2 and 9 March the B-52Js destroyed the North's power, its fuel, its great bridges, its jet base and Haiphong's cranes in a week. The North dispersed, Moscow and Beijing rebuilt its sky, and the flow south never noticed. In the South the summer offensive broke battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, the Marines came ashore, Thieu and Ky took power in June, and in July the President sent the Army. The advisory war was over.

The main-force war of 1965–67 was history's war fought by a better-armed, better-led, better-supported American and Vietnamese force against an enemy who chose when to fight and usually chose not to. The Ia Drang cost about 165–170 American dead rather than 237. Behind the big-unit war the allied campaign against the Front's political apparatus drove its district committees out of the villages of the Saigon belt and the upper Delta, and they went on governing them from further away: from the Ho Bo woods, from the contested edge, and after COSVN's circular of April 1967 from across the Cambodian border. Under the woods the American tunnel teams went down with the light on the muzzle and their left hand free, and at Phu Hoa Dong on 19 April 1966 a specialist of the 65th Engineers saw the man in the gallery four metres off and fired first. They lost about a third fewer men underground than history's teams in two years, and the Front answered by leaving the first galleries empty and wired.

The Front's war on the list-appointed district chiefs in 1965–66 killed nineteen of them and two province chiefs, and four of those were shot in their own houses at night with pistols taken from the Republic's dead. At Cai Be on 2–3 March 1966 a three-man team of the Dinh Tuong security section used a Glock from the Long Dinh post, its light still working on stolen cells, to pick the district chief from his wife in the doorway. The Republic's best pistol in its enemy's hands changed the manner of those deaths and not their number, which COSVN's decision and the sections' intelligence set; the chiefs' deputies acted at once and the Board filled each vacancy within about three weeks. And at Tan Thoi Nhi in Hoc Mon on 14 August 1967 a police sergeant lit an old man with a sickle at a door, saw what he was holding and did not fire, and at the tenth house lit a courier of the district finance section against a wall with his hands empty and took him alive. That night is the measure of the pistol: about 150–200 fewer civilians shot at night a year by soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadres taken alive at their doors rather than lost in the dark.

The year ended with 486,000 Americans in the country and, on the record's rates, about 8,435–9,060 American dead in 1967 against history's 9,378, of which the pistol accounts for about 10–15. The air war over the North had destroyed everything that could be destroyed. The Trail carried about as much as history's, and the Politburo had set Tet for the General Offensive and General Uprising.

Tet in this war was history's Tet fought against a Republic that held its district towns more often. About two-thirds of the 64–72 district towns attacked were penetrated rather than nearly all, and the infrastructure's files told the allies where the political half of the offensive would come and not when, so the surprise stayed national. The national police chief, Loan, shot a captured officer in a Saigon street before a camera on 1 February, and the photograph's effect on the American public was history's, whatever pistol was in his hand. The Americans lost 14,450–14,560 dead that year, close to history's 14,592, and the public's tolerance was spent on the historical schedule. The Paris talks opened in November with no partial halt of the bombing to trade.

Vietnamization in this war handed over a better army and a countryside held more firmly than history's. The land programme of 1964 had finished its work with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, the territorials were more present in the hamlets than ever, and from 1969 the Vietnamese Air Force's radar gunships held the posts' nights through the monsoon cloud in their own language. The Commission survived below the rank of minister and general and lost its reach above it when the American lever lapsed. In 1972 the Easter Offensive met an ARVN that held An Loc and Kontum with American air power and retook Quang Tri City, as history's did, and lost the north of Quang Tri, the western Highlands and the border of III Corps, as history's did.

The end came differently, and for reasons a long way above the level of anything this memorandum touches. Nixon did not fall. The fiscal 1975 appropriation came near the level of 1974, and Thieu, believing on that evidence that he need not give anything up, refused the withdrawal of 14 March 1975. The Highlands went in a five-week siege rather than a ten-day rout, Hue in an ordered withdrawal on 18 April rather than a collapse on 25 March, and with no double collapse to resolve on, the Politburo put the offensive on Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, under Thieu, with no American air power, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976. In the spring of 1974 the Vietnamese gunships had beaten Military Region 9's rice campaign in Chuong Thien; in 1975 they could not fly over a PAVN division, and Thuong Duc and Pleiku went as before.

The pistol is in that war everywhere a man in a government uniform carries a sidearm, and it decided nothing at the level where the war was decided. It made the government's presence at night a little more decent and a little more effective in the villages the government already held, it saved a few dozen American lives and a few hundred Vietnamese civilians a year, and it put several hundred of the country's best pistols into the hands of the men who killed its officials. It did not move a post, a battalion, a battle or the date.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control +2 to +6 against history (low) · POP.night_control.I_corps 0 to +3 against history (low) · POP.night_control.II_corps 0 to +3 against history (low) · POP.night_control.III_corps +3 to +8 against history (low) · POP.night_control.IV_corps +3 to +9 against history (low) · GVN.political_stability 3–5 (history 4; low) · VCI.strength -20% to -10% against history (medium) · TRAIL.tonnage_arriving -5% to +5% against history (low) · FIND.vci +0.3 to +1 against history (medium) · ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit +1 to +5 against history (low) · ARVN.effectiveness.fire_coordination -4 to -3 against history (low) · ARVN.self_sufficiency.fire_support 0 to +2 against history (low) · US.casualties 1,250–1,335 (history 1,369; low).*

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. The coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the Republic's administrative continuity. MACV under General Paul D. Harkins is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; about 23,300 Americans are in the country by the end of 1964, none in combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year¹. The fighting is done by the ARVN, the Regional and Popular Forces and the CIDG camps against a Viet Cong movement rebuilt since 1959 on the Viet Minh's old infrastructure and on cadres returning down the Trail.

The phase is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon the state comes apart, coup after coup, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field the Viet Cong form regiments and then a division around Saigon, and at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve on ground they chose. Hanoi commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. Washington answers from the air, first with reprisals and then, from 2 March 1965, with an opening strike on the North's whole target list and a campaign run from Saigon without zones or pauses; and on the ground, from two Marine battalions guarding an airfield in March to the decision of July to fight the war with American troops. Every constraint that will shape the war is drawn here except the one this war never drew, the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, and so is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: close in, choose the time of battle, keep the base areas beyond the border.

Most of what is new in this war stood on its first day, and none of it decided the coups or the summer offensive. The Republic itself was changed by one session of decrees on 1 January 1964: land to the tiller and a Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it, a resident rural police with a Special Branch, province and district chiefs chosen from a ranked list, Phung Hoang centres built on the police's files, an officer board and a combined command. Behind those decrees stood what had been built since 1957 and was already in the ranks: every private and territorial trained on the 2026 course, every NCO schooled for his grade, every officer educated before each promotion, and an industry a generation above the country's own²³. By July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the musters have found the RVNAF about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls⁴, and about 1,100 village posts stand in the held belt, where a post with its trained and rearmed Popular Forces platoon now holds about nineteen attacks in twenty⁵. The Front's answer is to move its committees out of the held villages, and the first campaigns of the task force, of Delta and of the district centres follow them out.

The command can also see and strike as history's could not. The Global Hawk orbit closes the trawler route within a year and moves Hanoi's southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months early; on the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats, and Pierce Arrow is not flown. Sixty-four AC-130J gunships fly over the posts from the first week, and by the summer of 1965 the Front has stopped assaulting the posts within their net on clear nights. The B-52Js wait on Guam for sixteen months with a target list and no authority, and then destroy in a week what history's fighter-bombers spent years on. The North disperses, Moscow and Beijing rebuild its sky, and nothing that reaches the South changes: the porters, the trucks and the Front's regiments notice nothing at all.

#### The session of 1 January 1964 and the institutions it made
*1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Joint General Staff at Tan Son Nhut; Quang Trung, Dong De and the training centres; the Bien Hoa and Go Vap works · new · EVR05-1*
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh, with Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho's cabinet, signed in one session the decrees that this war's Republic was built on, each drafted with the US Mission. The land decree ended rent on tenanted rice land from the coming harvest, made every present tiller the owner of up to 3 hectares in the Delta and 1 in the Central Lowlands, compensated the landlord at 2.5 times a year's paddy, 20% in cash and the rest in indexed bonds, and confirmed by name every distribution the Viet Minh or the Front had made. Minh read it on Radio Saigon that evening, and its first sentence was that confirmation. The same session created a Commission Against Corruption under Judge Truong Vinh Khoa, with a Special Court, a Pay and Muster Verification Service and a Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board whose American co-chair could suspend aid to a named command, importer or province and whose concurrence was needed to remove the Commissioner⁶.

The police decree put a resident post of 8 to 12 constables in every village the government held, gave a Special Branch sole charge of intelligence on the infrastructure, raised a Police Field Force at once, and held every arrest to a magistrate's review within 72 hours; its Director General was Vo Dinh Cuong, a career inspector who had kept out of Nhu's political service⁷. Its companion made unlawful the appointment of any province or district chief not taken from the ranked list of a Selection Board under Judge Dinh Quang Tuyen. About 1,640 candidates sat the Board's first examination on 2 March, and its first list, for 31 posts, appeared on 27 April; by the end of 1964 about 95 of the Republic's roughly 290 chiefs had come from it⁸. The Phung Hoang decree put an intelligence and operations centre in every province and district as the Special Branch opened there, under Lieutenant Colonel Tran Dinh Hieu, with an American exploitation detachment under Lieutenant Colonel Martin J. Ashby and about 1,500 biometric kits; it forbade monthly targets, required a magistrate's warrant on three sources for every arrest, and opened the centres' reports to the Commission's audit⁹. The first district centre opened at Phu Tan on 16 March, two weeks after the first village post, and about 100 were open by December, every one behind a post; Hieu refused the coastal province chiefs who asked for centres before they had posts. From October the first of about 1,100 selected advisers, trained for eight months and kept for eighteen, reached the subsectors, among them Captain Samuel J. Whitcomb at Can Giuoc, who asked the district chief on his first night, in Vietnamese, which families still held their titles at the district office and why¹⁰.

The officer decree made promotion and battalion and regimental command the decision of an Officer Selection and Promotion Board under Brigadier General Phan Khac Minh, with rated evaluations, relief for cause after thirty days, a commission for proven sergeants, and generals chosen from a ranked list of three behind an aid condition. It inherited the seven educated classes of 1957–63, about 10,000–14,000 officers, who already held most of the platoons and companies; above them the army was what it had been². The first season published 61 battalion and 9 regimental commands on 30 June, and the career-course gate kept captains in command of a third of the battalions for another year and a half. When Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc moved on Saigon in September, two of their battalions were commanded by officers the board had refused for the June list, and none by an officer it had chosen¹¹.

The Combined Operations Command was proclaimed in the same session, the one decree the council signed with visible reluctance: Harkins as combined commander, Major General Le Van Kim as Vietnamese deputy, Korean and Australian deputies on paper, and a staff of about 260 at Tan Son Nhut by the end of January¹². Its first operations order was drafted on 22 January and never issued, because Khanh's coup came eight days later.

Two things stood already. The Republic began the war with a steelworks, an arsenal and rebuild works at Bien Hoa and a radio plant, battery works and wire mill at Go Vap, placed under a Directorate of Defence Industry under Vuong Dinh Toan¹³. For fourteen months it had more capacity than customers, because no quartermaster would pay for what American aid gave free, until Colonel Howard B. Teague's rule, made a condition by the Aid Compliance Board on 1 March 1965, required a quarter of the RVNAF's ammunition bodies, batteries, wire, radios and steel to be bought at home in 1965, a third in 1966 and half from 1968. Its price was in men: 31,000–45,000 workers of military age needed deferments, and a place on the deferment list cost two months' wages in Bien Hoa by the spring of 1965. By July the PF platoons of the held belt carried about 5,000 Go Vap radios and the hamlets 6,000 more, and the Front had put cells into the arsenal's case lines¹⁴. The ranks stood too. Since 1957 every regular recruit had passed the ten-week basic course at Quang Trung or a division centre and every territorial a six-week version, about 120,000–160,000 regulars and 60,000–100,000 territorials by 1964³, and every NCO had passed the course for his grade at the National NCO Academy at Dong De under Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc. Both cost the battalions their best sergeants as instructors, about 2,000–3,000 drill sergeants and 1,300–2,500 NCO instructors in the two years the army could least spare them; the Joint General Staff asked for a third of them back in June and November 1964 and got a few hundred, replaced from the battalions' own corporals. In November the Armed Forces Council's National Day promotion left the gate standing: every NCO with his course went up a grade, and no corporal without it¹⁵. From April 1964 every man on a muster roll carried the number of the cycle he had passed; in September the musters found 212 privates of a 22nd Division battalion whose cycle the training centre's ration accounts showed had lasted four weeks, not ten, and the commandant was charged in October and relieved in November⁵.

#### Operation TIDE TABLE
*6 Jan 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · The II Corps coast from Qui Nhon to Cam Ranh Bay and the Ca Mau coast; Haiphong and the Gulf of Tonkin; Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Cambodia · new · EV003-1*
*Initiative: both*

The first Global Hawk orbit took station off the II Corps coast on 6 January 1964, sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep for the steel-hulled trawlers that Group 759, later Group 125, ran south from Haiphong¹⁶. Before January nobody had been looking for them on a coast of 2,000 kilometres and tens of thousands of junks. The first track came on the night of 18–19 February: a contact at a steady ten knots that did not fish, turning west at dusk toward Phu Yen. The radar held it for seven hours, so the Vietnamese Navy's PC-461 from the Tuy Hoa patrol line did not have to search, only steam, and came up with the trawler at 0310 four miles off Vung Ro Bay. The crew fired her demolition charges; the forward hold blew and the after hold did not, and a boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of carbines, B-40 launchers, 82mm rounds and 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm¹⁷. Seven of the crew were taken from the water and nine died.

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven trawlers on the II Corps and Ca Mau coasts. Four were intercepted, one of them nearly intact off Binh Dinh by USS *Edsall*'s boarding party, whose crew gave up the landing beaches and two years of earlier voyages; six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon; one landed unobserved in May while the orbit was down with a generator fault. Hanoi tried again in the autumn, while the orbit was held in the Gulf after the August crisis: one trawler landed at Vung Ro on 21–22 September, a second was beached and burned off Tam Quan on 9 November with sixteen of her crew dead, and a third turned for home in December¹⁷.

The Central Military Commission concluded that winter that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night", and changed routes. Sihanouk was moving toward Beijing and would break with Washington on 3 May 1965; Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and the first arms cargo came ashore there in late June 1965, about eighteen months before history's, to be trucked by night to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak¹⁷. There was no trawler at Vung Ro for Lieutenant James Bowers to find on 16 February 1965, and Market Time opened on 11 March on TIDE TABLE's record with less and less to find. TIDE TABLE never starved the Front. It moved the enemy's principal line of supply to the southern half of the country into a neutral port that no American could stop or seize.

#### ◆ Operation IRON GATE
*6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Saigon port and Tan Son Nhut; the Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the division training centres and the province RF/PF training camps of IV and III Corps, then countrywide · new · EV004-1*
*Initiative: allied*

MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez* berthed at Khanh Hoi on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines and their CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249s and 320 M110A2s. The issue was called IRON GATE because no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate, and every company zeroed its carbines at 25 metres before it left, on a five-day course that Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn refused to shorten. The Delta divisions and the territorials of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac drew first, the general reserve in February and March, and I and II Corps from April. Khanh's coup stopped the issue for nine days, and a fifth of the first half-year's training was lost to units that drew carbines and went back to their posts without zeroing them¹⁸.

The pistols came through the same gate. The first 18,000 Glock 21s, each with its tritium sights and a SureFire light on the rail, came ashore at Khanh Hoi in the second ship of January, and the rule was the carbine's: an M1911A1 or a revolver in, a Glock, a light, three magazines and a holster out¹⁹. The officers, crews and senior NCOs of the Delta divisions drew with their companies' carbines; the issue to the whole regular RVNAF, about 25,000 pistols, was done by the end of March, and to the territorials' leaders, about 10,000–11,000, by the end of May. Each holder fired 50 rounds on the third day of the carbine course, after a morning on the light and an afternoon on the one rule the Glock imposes, that the finger stays straight until the sights are on the target. The Americans in the country, about 3,000–4,000 pistols' worth, exchanged theirs at Tan Son Nhut in a week. The National Police, rising through 30,000 men by June, drew theirs last, from May to August, on a four-day course that the Directorate of Police ran at Rach Dua and in the province towns without the army's instructors⁷²⁰.

By 30 June about 310,000 carbines were issued, 180,000 of them to the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles to the regular infantry; the squad gun and the marksman stayed with the regulars, and the outposts kept the Browning as their automatic rifle²¹. About a third of the 290,000 old weapons went, over this headquarters' objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia. The old pistols went the same way: of about 40,000 M1911A1s, revolvers and French pistols handed in by the RVNAF and the police by August, about 9,000 were given by the Directorate to village chiefs, hamlet chiefs and the Combat Youth's leaders, again over this headquarters' objection, and the rest went back to American depots. A PF platoon at Can Duoc that had hit a 100-metre target about once in four shots with its Garands put most of its rounds into it by the third morning and asked to keep the spent cases to show the village. The Long An province committee called the carbines "a trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty" and took its first on 22 March in an ambush at Rach Kien, and with it its first Glock, from the belt of the platoon's dead sergeant¹⁸²⁰.

#### The gunship wing's first year
*8 Jan – 16 Dec 1964 · Bien Hoa, Nha Trang, Da Nang and Qui Nhon air bases; the posts of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and Vinh Long; Tam Quan and Hoai Nhon, Binh Dinh Province · new · EV015-1*
*Initiative: both*

The 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship) flew its first sortie on the night of 8–9 January 1964 with 64 AC-130J Ghostriders under Farm Gate's rules: a Vietnamese observer aboard, support of the RVNAF only, and fire only on the province chief's clearance. At 0140 on 9 January the PF post at Phu Tuc on the Ham Luong called on its PRC-10 that its wire was being cut. Ghostrider 41 was over the post at 0209, its infrared turret showing about sixty men in two lines among the palms and a mortar team in a ditch beyond them; the clearance came at 0221 and the 105mm put eighteen rounds on them by 0240. The district reported 70 enemy dead. The video showed 18 to 25, and the video's figure went to MACV²².

Through the spring the wing flew 400–600 sorties a month and answered 120–180 calls from posts under attack, and it learned two things fast: the gunship was worth as much as the radio that called it, and the province chief's clearance took a median 35 minutes while posts were overrun beneath circling guns. In July clearance within 300 metres of a post was delegated to the district chief and the wait fell to about 12 minutes; the price of that rule was paid later, in hamlets beside the posts. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee answered in March: no assault on a post with a radio on a clear night within reach of the airfields, attacks in rain and on posts that could not call, and the wall crossed before the aircraft came. By June assaults on posts with radios on clear nights had nearly stopped in Long An and Dinh Tuong, and the local companies had turned to the mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the dike²².

The limit was found in the first north-east monsoon. At 2310 on 14 December the post at Tam Quan on Route 1 called that a company of the 2nd Regiment was on its wire, under a cloud base of 1,800 feet. Major Thomas R. Gaither took Ghostrider 63 down through the overcast to 1,500 feet to see, and found two 12.7mm guns sited on the dikes, which put thirty rounds through his left wing and tanks in six minutes while he broke the assault on the southern wall. He brought the aircraft home to Qui Nhon on three engines. The attack was renewed in the rain at 0400 with nothing overhead, and the post fell with nineteen of its thirty men. On 16 December the wing forbade attacks below 3,500 feet, and the provincial units of the coast learned over the winter what they had not believed since March: the aircraft did not see through cloud²².

#### Khanh's coup
*30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · changed from history · EV000-1*
*Initiative: neither*

Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from Minh's council without firing a shot, with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were arrested on a charge of plotting a neutralist settlement; Minh's aide, Major Nguyen Van Nhung, was executed; Minh stayed on as a figurehead²³. The coup came on history's date for history's reasons, and Khanh used the combined command's own network to take the state: the combined centre at Da Nang passed I Corps traffic to Saigon that night, and for nine days the command had no Vietnamese deputy and issued no orders¹².

The decrees were tested within a week. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay backed Khanh in the hope that the decrees would lapse. His officers detained Cuong, and he named acting chiefs in eleven provinces and districts; when he asked Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals, Khoa replied that he had no evidence, and the American co-chair refused the concurrence Khanh needed to remove him. Johnson's personal note of 2 February carried the condition that the United States would support no government that repealed the decrees⁴. Cuong was back in his office the same day, the acting chiefs were replaced by the list in April and May, and the officer board confirmed eleven of Khanh's twenty-three promotions and refused the rest¹¹. Lodge told Khanh on 4 February that the combined command had not been designed to command a coup and would not be used against one, and that the aid that paid the army was conditional on it. Khanh accepted on terms: his man Thieu as Vietnamese deputy and his own chief of staff, so that every combined order passed through Khanh's office. The first combined order went out on 14 February. By March an order cleared his office in about thirty hours rather than four days, and on 26 March the 21st Division crossed a corps boundary into the Ca Mau forests on a combined order, the first time in the war¹².

The pattern held until 1970. Each government that inherited the reforms found them cheaper to keep than to break, and learned that the board and the list could be bargained with and could not be ignored. In March McNamara endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 committed the United States to an independent South Vietnam; Westmoreland took MACV in June and Taylor replaced Lodge in July.

#### ◆ The first posts and the Front's answer
*1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · new · EV002-2*
*Initiative: both*

The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, and the posts followed the titling line into villages the government held at night⁸. Each was a brick house with a radio and ten constables carrying the carbines of 1950, and from the summer each constable's revolver was a Glock with a light on it. Beside each post, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon trained as a platoon on the territorial course, rearmed with M4A1s under IRON GATE, and from April carrying its own Go Vap PRC-10¹⁴. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by July 1965, with the census registering about 45 percent of the accessible adults and a constable and a PF corporal walking the hamlet each night with the household booklets⁷. From August 1964 the constable held the booklet in one hand and lit the face at the door with the pistol in the other, and the night check became something a ten-man post could do in two hours and believe²⁰.

The Front read the programme within weeks. A COSVN directive of March 1964 put the rural police above the PF as a target and ordered the committees to kill constables before they learned the village. On 3 October a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4, settled three weeks earlier without its platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables. The company took nine carbines and seven Glocks; the Dinh Tuong province security section took the Glocks from the company within the month. By July 1965 about 85 posts had fallen and about 410 constables were dead, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without their platoon⁸. The new pistols changed none of those numbers: the posts fell to company assaults and the constables died on the roads to rifle fire at 50–150 metres, where a pistol does not reach, and the pistol only decided how the last man in a breached post died. What the falls did change was the Front's armoury. About 280–320 of the constables' Glocks went with their posts and their bodies by July 1965, and with the officers' and post commanders' pistols taken in ambushes and the sales of the Saigon black market the Front held about 1,000–1,200 by the end of 1965, at low confidence; it could plausibly be half or double²⁰.

Where the platoon was there, it held, and one night showed why. At 0140 on 17 November 1964 a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at Long Huu in Kien Hoa. The platoon's corporal, Pham Van Tho, had zeroed his carbines in March and been taught at the Ben Tre centre to fire in pairs and keep a third of his ammunition for the second assault; the first assault broke on fire that did not pause, and the second was not pressed. The platoon's own radio called the Ham Long boats at 0143, and the two PF soldiers wounded on the bund were alive with tourniquets on when the boats came at 0320¹⁴. Across the held belt in 1964–65 the posts with their platoon held about nineteen attacks in twenty, and the platoons lost about a fifth fewer of their wounded; that figure is the combined judgment of the carbine, the radio and the course, and replaces the separate estimates first made for each⁵.

The Front learned faster than this headquarters expected. From December 1964 its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps, the hamlet militia with the Garands the army had handed in, and the posts that held by mortar from 300 metres and by the mine on the path to the market. By the summer of 1965 posts that could call were attacked about a third less often than posts that could not, about two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts, and Go Vap dry cells were turning up in the firing circuits of the Front's mines¹⁴. The outposts that fell, the deserters and the ambushed battalions gave the Front 10,000–30,000 M4A1s by the end of 1965, the best rifles any guerrilla carried, each needing the Republic's own 5.56mm¹⁸. Some of the men sent against the posts were PF deserters who had walked home with their training, and the district companies made them squad leaders⁵.

Behind the posts the Special Branch and the centres took their first harvest: about 1,900 arrests on three sources in the held belt by July 1965, a third released by the magistrates, and about 260 of them members of district or village committees against perhaps 100 the Branch's own folders would have reached. The biometric kit caught its first case at Cho Moi in December 1964, a finance cadre released on a bribe and rearrested under a new name, found in forty seconds¹⁰. The Branch found its first penetration in February 1965, a clerk in Dinh Tuong who had copied informant cards since the summer; eleven of the informants named on them were dead⁸. From January 1965 the Front's district committees in the held belt began moving their offices out of the villages to the contested edge.

#### The titles, the musters and the Vinh Long suspension
*16 Mar 1964 – 11 May 1965 · An Giang, Phong Dinh, Vinh Long, Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An, and the coast from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh; the ARVN and territorial units of IV Corps, then countrywide · new · EVR05-2*
*Initiative: allied*

The first 40 titling teams of about 25 men took the field on 16 March 1964, each under a Regional Forces escort, working outward from the province capitals into villages the district could reinforce by day. The first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen on 21 April and the first in a contested district at Phung Hiep on 11 May. By July 70 teams were out, the most the provinces could escort, and by 31 December about 210,000 hectares were titled to about 170,000 families, almost all in hamlets the government held at night⁴. The rent stop held at the December harvest in most of those districts. The Mission reported titles issued to Washington each month, and this headquarters reported them with the warning that they measured the government's paperwork, not the peasants' allegiance.

The Pay and Muster Verification Service's first teams paraded the 21st Division at dawn around Bac Lieu and Soc Trang on 7 April, unannounced, and by the autumn 60 teams were mustering about 480 units a month; from August the enrolled units of IV Corps were paid at the parade rather than through their commanders. It cost what had been predicted: Liberation Radio said for a month that the "American masters" had dismissed a puppet, and the officers who resented the Board said the same.

#### ◆ The lamps on the guns: the first season
*1 Apr – 31 Dec 1964 · The PF posts and police posts of Long An, Dinh Tuong and Kien Hoa; the National Police stations of the Delta towns; the COSVN security directorate in Tay Ninh; Bien Hoa air base · new · EV016-1*
*Initiative: both*

The new pistol's first season was spent learning what its light was for, and the Vietnamese paid for most of the lessons. In April and May 1964 the PF platoon leaders and ARVN lieutenants of the Delta took the lights on night ambush and used them as torches, to read a map under a poncho, to count their men on a dike, to find the path back. A 66,000-candela beam seen from the side is a marker visible for kilometres in the flat paddy¹⁹. On the night of 9–10 May a PF platoon of Can Duoc district was fired on from the next dike while its leader was checking his positions with his light, and lost two men before the ambush was set; three more platoons in Long An and Dinh Tuong were sprung the same way before the end of June. The province chiefs of Long An and Dinh Tuong banned the light on patrol in June, and the territorial NCO courses at the province centres added an hour on light discipline to the pistol block in July²⁰.

The negligent discharges came at the holster. The police, who drew last and were trained least, fired their new pistols into their own feet, their own floors and in four cases their own colleagues between May and October; across the RVNAF and the police about 100–120 more negligent discharges were recorded in 1964 than the old pistols would have produced, and about 8–10 more men died of them, at low confidence. On 22 August a constable of the Rach Gia station killed his corporal while holstering after a night check; the Directorate of Police doubled its course to eight days from October and copied the territorial course's safe-handling block word for word²⁰.

The gunships saw the lights too. On the night of 18–19 November an AC-130J over a post in Dinh Tuong held its fire for six minutes because the crew could not tell the post's weapon lights on its wall from the attackers' torches; the post held, and the 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship) and the IV Corps province chiefs agreed in December that the posts would mark their walls with strobes and fire and keep white light off the wall²⁴²⁰.

On the other side the COSVN security directorate read the lights by April from the first captured pistol, taken at Rach Kien on 22 March, and its circular of 4 June told the district security sections what the platoons were learning the hard way: hold fire until the lamp comes on, then fire at the lamp. For about four months, until a captured light was switched on in a hut in Hau Nghia, two district committees of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region believed the lamps saw through walls and kept their cadres out of the held villages altogether²⁰. By the end of the year the lights were used as they were meant to be, for two seconds at a door, a bend or a face, and the Front knew it.

#### The hunt for the committees: Can Giuoc, the Rach Cat and Hoc Mon
*9 Jun 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · Can Giuoc, Can Duoc and Rach Kien Districts, Long An Province; the Rung Sat edge of Nha Be and the Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province; Trung Lap, Hau Nghia Province · new · EVR05-3*
*Initiative: both*

The hunt began with a killing. On the night of 9 June 1964 armed propaganda teams of the Can Giuoc district committee, with the Long An provincial security section and a company of the 506th Local Force Battalion, entered three hamlets of Phuoc Lai, read names from the claimants' list posted in the village office, and shot four title holders and the land committee's secretary. By 14 July 37 title holders and 9 land committee members were dead in eleven hamlets, and several hundred families had brought their titles back to the district office; the RF companies in their mud forts along Route 5 did not move at night, and no reaction force came. Teams stopped posting names in hamlets not held at night, and a COSVN directive captured in August told the committees to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper" and make examples only where they began to inform⁴.

Three new instruments went after the men who had done it, and they did the same thing three ways: they found a committee from a file and followed it out of the villages. The task force's III Corps task group under Major Thomas R. Garvin, with Captain Nguyen Huu Phuoc of the Special Branch as his deputy, opened Operation DRY SEASON at Tan An on 20 July with a Special Forces detachment, a SEAL platoon and the Long An PRU. For two months it found almost nothing; of 19 operations in August and September, 14 found the target gone, and the American planners' attempt to raid on unconfirmed targets night after night was abandoned when the magistrate released 11 of 17 detainees. The turn came on 3 October, when a Rach Kien PF platoon ambushed a tax team and took a notebook of the committee's collections and a courier's route into the Nha Be nipa. On 5–6 October the SEALs went up the Soai Rap and took the security section's deputy chief, Ba Lien, who had commanded the Phuoc Lai killings. By 18 December 14 of the section's 23 members were dead, in prison or rallied, and no title holder was killed in Can Giuoc in 1965¹².

Delta arrived as the second instrument. MACV-JSOC stood up on 15 August under Brigadier General Harold M. Vance, a headquarters of 280 that could command its own units, declare a restricted area over a hamlet for a night and claim a target, and compel nothing outside MACV. Colonel James E. Teague's 1,310 men of the 1st SFOD-Delta came in from Pope between 28 August and 12 September, and rotated one squadron in four through rest at Tan Son Nhut, which is why the unit's sustained rate was never its first month's. Of 31 raids by the end of December, nine reached a cadre of district rank; the cordon by a Ranger company was tried twice in November and both times the house was empty, because the battalion had been told the night before, and it was dropped. Sergeant First Class Donald R. Keel was the unit's first dead, shot through the throat at a doorway in Can Giuoc on 2 December. From DRY SEASON's papers came NIGHT HERON: two men of G Squadron lay in the nipa 240 metres from a sampan mooring on the Rach Cat for seven nights, and at 0240 on 15 March 1965 two assault teams took six of the eleven members of the Long An province committee there, with the finance section's ledgers for 1964²⁵. Long An's collections in three districts were about a third of the previous year's by December. A week earlier, on 7 March at Trung Lap, a PF squad with no radio had walked up a hamlet path toward a raid inside a restricted area its post had never been told of, and Delta's team lay in a ditch until it passed; from 15 March an area was passed down to the platoon on the ground or not declared, which cancelled eleven raids in the year²⁶.

The third instrument was the district centre. Hoc Mon by February 1965 had 11 posts, a district chief from the list, and a centre under Captain Ngo Van Tam with a chart of the district committee's 31 names, 19 confirmed on three sources; the committee had moved to the hamlets along the Saigon River and governed the posted villages by messenger. Operation STILL WATER used no regular troops: two Police Field Force companies and the Gia Dinh PRU made the arrests, the RF companies of Hoc Mon and Go Vap held the cordons at night with their carbines and BARs, and every arrest went to the kit and then to a magistrate²¹. On the night of 26–27 March a courier of nineteen who had rallied for her brother's release from Con Son led the PRU to the house in Tan Thoi Nhi where the security section met, with a steel box of reports that named nine of the committee's informants in the posted villages, three of them PF soldiers. By 30 June 63 had been arrested and 17 released, and 9 of the committee's 14 members were dead, in prison or rallied; the rest went north to Cu Chi, from where the Front governed Hoc Mon until 1968¹⁰. The Commission's audit found 4 of the 63 arrests unsupported and sent them to the Special Court.

None of the three could reach the committees that moved, and each was rebuilt within a season by cadres sent from the region. What did not come back was the committees' ease: none of them again met where a boat or a patrol could reach from a posted hamlet. The Front learned that leaving a village no longer put its leaders out of reach, three years before the historical Phoenix tried to show it.

*Initiative: enemy*

Three attacks showed one thing three ways: the enemy chose the night and the target, and what held was whatever was already inside the wire. At 0230 on 6 July 1964 about 800–900 VC and PAVN attacked the CIDG camp at Nam Dong, held by Captain Roger H. C. #### The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution
*2–11 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · changed from history · EV000-3*
*Initiative: both*

After the OPLAN 34A raids on Hon Me and Hon Ngu, three P-4 torpedo boats attacked USS *Maddox* on its DESOTO patrol in daylight on 2 August 1964; four F-8s from *Ticonderoga* hit the retiring boats, and Johnson chose a warning. At Admiral Sharp's request I moved the Global Hawk orbit into the Gulf on 3 August. On the night of 4 August *Maddox* and *Turner Joy* fired for two hours in thunderstorms at contacts they took for a second attack. Major Harold Kessling had the watch in the exploitation cell. His radar log, on the set that had tracked the three boats of 2 August from their base, showed the destroyers, a cluster of junks thirty miles west, rain cells, and no fast small craft within forty miles at any time between 2000 and 2330. Herrick cabled that the action left "many doubts"³². The report reached Washington in the late morning, while McNamara and Sharp were still trying to learn what had happened, and McNamara told the President that afternoon that a radar which had watched the whole engagement contradicted the ships. Johnson held the strike order and at about 1900 cancelled it. Pierce Arrow was not flown, Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Everett Alvarez was not taken prisoner that day¹⁷.

The resolution was sent anyway, on the real attack of 2 August alone. The debate ran three days longer than history's, and Congress passed it on 10 August, 414–0 and 85–3, in the historical words¹⁷. It was again the only legislative authority for the war, but it rested on an attack that had happened, and the radar log of 4 August sat in the files at Tan Son Nhut and Honolulu as a record of a false alarm, not as the concealed proof of a pretext.

#### Saigon's autumn of 1964: the Vung Tau Charter, the September coup and Hop Tac
*16 Aug 1964 – mid-1965 · Saigon, Hue and Da Nang; the Hop Tac ring of Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces · changed from history · EVCP02-1*
*Initiative: neither*

Khanh tried to use the Tonkin crisis to make himself president under the Vung Tau Charter of 16 August 1964; students and Thich Tri Quang's Buddhists answered with riots, the charter was repealed, and a triumvirate of Khanh, Minh and Khiem followed. Before dawn on 13 September Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc seized key points in Saigon with about ten battalions. In this war they had a programme: Phat's broadcast promised to "suspend the land decree pending review", dissolve the "foreign-controlled" Aid Compliance Board and restore the corps commanders' authority over the provinces, which meant abolishing the Selection Board. The Special Branch had reported battalions of the 7th Division readying at My Tho without orders on 11 September, the Saigon police stayed in their stations on Cuong's order, and Taylor told the plotters within hours that Washington would not recognise them⁸. Ky's jets and Thi's support saved Khanh, and the coup collapsed on 14 September without a death³³. Its failure attached the reforms to the side that won: the Young Turks around Ky and Thi took up the land decree and the Board as their own causes⁴.

Hop Tac, the oil spot applied to a ring around the capital, opened in September and had its battalions pulled away by every coup alert; by mid-1965 it was moribund, as historically³⁴. Here it carried the titling teams into Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, where about 38,000 hectares were titled, and about 160 police posts were settled in the inner districts with chiefs from the list; the hamlets around the posts held at night through the coups even when the battalions left⁸. It could not save the programme. Pacification needed a stable government and a force that stayed in the hamlets at night, and in 1964 the Republic had only the beginning of the second.

#### The battle of Binh Gia
*28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · changed from history · EV000-9*
*Initiative: enemy*

The 271st and 272nd Regiments, the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, about 1,800 men, took the Catholic refugee village of Binh Gia on 28 December 1964 and waited for the relief. #### Operation HAMMER FALL: the opening strike on the North
*2–9 Mar 1965 · Phuc Yen airfield, Hanoi (Bach Mai, the Citadel, Yen Phu, Duc Giang and the Paul Doumer bridge), Haiphong port and Thuong Ly, Nam Dinh, the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa, Vinh; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; Yankee Station; Korat and Takhli, Thailand · new · EV013-1*
*Initiative: allied*

The strike was a year in the making, and it began in a Quonset hut. On 3 March 1964 Major Edwin T. Sorrell, a geodesist, opened a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut to turn the U-2S's GPS-true imagery into points a GBU-31 could fly to, each measured to a few metres and filed with its photographs. Its first discovery was that every map in the country was wrong: the 1:50,000 sheets were drawn on the Indian 1960 datum, and in the panhandle's mountains some crossings lay 300 metres or more from where the map put them. Sorrell's memorandum of 14 May 1964 said what the cell would enforce for a decade: "No coordinate taken from a map is to be given to a guided weapon, and no coordinate given to a guided weapon is to be plotted on a map without conversion"⁴⁷. By June 1965 the cell held about 1,400 folders on the panhandle and the North below the 20th parallel, at the cost of a quarter to a third of the U-2S's hours⁴⁸. The B-52Js of the 3960th Strategic Wing trained on Guam from the spring of 1964 against coordinates the cell sent them, and for sixteen months struck nothing; the aircraft were ready and the authority was not.

After the false alarm of 4 August the cell was given the North above the 20th parallel, and Brigadier General Walter E. Pruitt built a Combined Targeting Cell around it with Captain Harlan J. Reese for the Navy. To the Joint Chiefs' 94 targets it added the air defence command at Bach Mai, the Ministry of Defence in the Citadel, the transmitters, the radars, the power plants and the bulk fuel, about 760 aim points in all by February 1965. On the night of 2–3 March the crachin sat at 800–1,500 feet and nothing but the bombers could strike. Twenty-four B-52Js left Andersen in cells of three and released from 36,000–40,000 feet between 2340 and 0115. The first cells put GBU-38s on Phuc Yen's runways and revetments and wrecked 22–26 of the 921st Fighter Regiment's 36 MiG-17Fs; then the radars; then the penetrators on Bach Mai, the Citadel and Me Tri; then the Yen Phu and Haiphong power stations, the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly, two spans of the Paul Doumer bridge, and the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa. The last target was Haiphong's port, its one strike: about forty cranes destroyed and none of the fourteen ships alongside struck, and Hanoi gave the International Control Commission a figure of 214 port workers killed⁴³.

No B-52J was lost or hit; the guns fired blind into cloud at aircraft five miles above them. When the cloud lifted on 4 and 7 March the carriers and the F-105Ds from Thailand struck Kep, the rail yards and Thai Nguyen and lost seven aircraft to the guns, and two more cells restruck the Doumer bridge and the Haiphong power plant on 6 March, the first orders of the restrike watch. By 9 March the North had lost about 70–80 percent of its generating capacity, most of its bulk fuel, its great bridges, its only jet base and most of its fighters, its warning net and the cranes of its only deepwater port; its air defence command worked from provincial switchboards for three weeks. No member of the Politburo was hurt, because General Van Tien Dung's staff had worked from Hoa Lac since February, and the North's civilian dead were 800–1,500 by this command's estimate⁴³. The trucks of Group 559, the porters in the panhandle and the regiments in the South noticed nothing at all.

#### The air war of 1965: the rebuilt sky, the Thanh Hoa Bridge, the missiles and the first Arc Light
*10 Mar – 27 Jul 1965 · Hanoi, Moscow and Beijing; Pingxiang and the airfields of Mengzi and Ningming; Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong) and the Hanoi region; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · changed from history · EVCP02-2*
*Initiative: both*

The answer to HAMMER FALL was decided in three capitals, each for its own reasons. On 25–27 March the Politburo put the North on a war footing, dispersed every factory, depot and fuel store it could move, raised a militia of the whole people for air defence and repair, and, reading the strike as the preparation for a landing, held the 308th and 312th Divisions and two regiments of the 320th at home through the summer. Moscow doubled the SA-2 package and sent diesels, pumps and trucks; Beijing sent railway, engineer and anti-aircraft troops earlier and in greater numbers than historically⁴³.

Beijing's own decision, taken for its own reasons, slowed Moscow's. In April it refused the Soviets an air corridor and two Yunnan airfields, as it did historically, and made every Soviet shipment cross China by rail at Chinese pace. The refusal had nothing to do with the strike; it came from the quarrel over the leadership of the communist world and Mao's suspicion of a Soviet presence on his frontier. The first SA-2 battalions reached Hanoi through Pingxiang three to five weeks late, with a fifth of their equipment held at the border, and the VPAF's surviving MiG-17s re-formed at Mengzi and Ningming, to fight from Chinese fields that autumn⁴³.

The Ham Rong bridge, which history's 79-aircraft package under Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner failed to drop on 3 April, had been in the river since March; Risner's wing flew instead against the pontoons upstream, and the U-2S's dawn pass found each new pontoon for a strike within a day or two⁴⁹. The first SA-2 site was imaged on 22 March and the first Fan Song heard on 18 April; each site was struck within 24–48 hours of its first image, and the North answered by preparing three or four sites for every battalion, history's answer brought forward a year. The first American aircraft lost to an SA-2 fell on 22 August rather than 24 July. Over the five months about 60–75 American aircraft were lost over the North, against perhaps 85–100 in history's same months, nearly all to guns at low level⁵⁰. By the end of July the North had 2,500–3,000 guns, four SA-2 battalions rotating among about twenty sites, its fighters in China, its fuel in drums, its power in about 700 village diesels, its bridges in pontoons by night, and 300,000–400,000 people repairing and carrying full time, a year before history's figure reached that size⁴³.

The first Arc Light, on 18 June, was flown by thirty B-52Js from Andersen without tankers, so the collision that killed eight crewmen historically did not happen. Two aircraft put twenty GBU-31s each on four structures the U-2S had imaged near Ben Cat and found huts empty for days, and the rest found what history found⁴⁷. The press called it a sledgehammer swung at flies, now accurate to a few metres. It was the find-and-fix problem in its purest form, and the J changed none of it. By July the B-52's war had two halves: over the North a bomber that had destroyed in a week what history's fighter-bombers spent years on, and over the South a bomber that found nothing under the canopy⁴³.

#### How the phase ends
By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. The Viet Cong have regiments and divisions around Saigon and on the coast, PAVN regiments are in the Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though no battalion of the general reserve has been wiped out. Thieu and Ky have just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Hanoi the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength, and in October its regiments will go after Plei Me.

Inside the held belt the summer is not history's. About 330,000 hectares are titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most districts the government holds, and there the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's⁴. About 1,100 village posts stand, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, about 140 district centres are open behind the posts, and the Front's committees have begun to leave the villages around them, gaining the safety of the contested edge at the cost of the villages' daily obedience¹⁰. Outside the belt nothing has changed except the promise.

The army that enters Phase II is the one 1957 built and 1964 began to reform. Its privates and territorials are trained, its NCOs schooled for their grades and its companies led by the educated classes, at the price of 3,300–5,500 of its best sergeants kept as instructors in the years it could least spare them¹⁵. The board has filled about 70 of some 190 battalion commands and relieved its first nine commanders; the rest, and every general, are the generals' choices, and a third of the battalions are still under captains¹¹. The general reserve came back from Binh Gia and Dong Xoai with more of its men than on any course before this one, and the Front enters Phase II with 10,000–30,000 of the Republic's carbines and the academy's leader's manual¹⁸. The Directorate's plants run at about half capacity under a domestic share four months old, the coast's Route 1 is open after the worst flood in memory, and the Front has an industrial working class in Bien Hoa to organise¹⁴.

The command can see the enemy's rear and not his regiments under the canopy or his cadres in the hamlets. The sea route is closed and Sihanoukville open. The gunships have pushed the Front's attacks into the rain, onto posts beyond their net and into the mortar and the mine, and they changed the nights at Nam Dong and Dong Xoai and not the days²². The task force, Delta and the district centres have each broken a committee from one file and seen each rebuilt from the region. The combined command is a channel rather than a command, its orders passing through the prime minister's office and its allies' through Seoul's countersignature and Canberra's casualty estimate. The North has lost its fixed economy and replaced it with drums, diesels, pontoons and a fighter force living in China, and holds three divisions at home against a landing that is not coming⁴³. The flow south is untouched, and the American decision of July is history's.

### Mechanism

The pistol enters the war in IRON GATE and works through the posts. Three events carry it. IRON GATE now issues about 55,000–60,000 Glocks with the carbines and through the summer, about 40,000 old pistols coming in and about 9,000 of them going, over this headquarters' objection, to village and hamlet chiefs and the Combat Youth's leaders. The first posts carry them from the summer, and the night check of memorandum 002 becomes a check done with a light on the face and the booklet in the other hand. The first season, EV016-1, is the pistol's own lesson: the light as a torch on ambush, the negligent discharges at the holster, the gunship that could not tell the post's lights from the enemy's.

The constraint at the post did not move. The record's settled position is that small arms change how the fights the enemy accepts go and never which fights he accepts, and at a police post the pistol is weaker still: the posts fell to company assaults and the constables died on the roads to rifle fire at 50–150 metres, where it does not reach. The 85 posts lost and the 410 constables killed by July 1965 stand as written, and what absorbs the difference is plain: the pistol only decided how the last man in a breached post died. What moved was the Front's armoury: about 280–320 of the constables' Glocks by July 1965 and about 1,000–1,200 pistols of all sources by the end of the year, at low confidence.

On the ground the effect is not the same everywhere. In the Saigon belt, at Can Duoc and Rach Kien, the pistols reached the posts with the platoons and the night check worked from August; it was also where the light-as-torch ambushes happened. In the Delta the upper provinces got the night check and Rach Gia got the discharges; Chuong Thien, where the posts were the Front's, got nothing. On the coast of I and II Corps the posts held less often, so the pistols there were more often lost than used. In the Highlands there was no effect: the CIDG camps, which did the fighting, are outside the provision.

The soldier's family is untouched: the pistol does not change where a PF soldier's family lives or what his pay buys. Rice and the piaster are untouched too: the pistol costs the Republic nothing, imported with the carbines, and moves no rice. The Saigon black-market price of a Glock with a light, about a month of a captain's pay by 1965, is the one place it touches money, and it pays a few armourers and officers to sell²⁰.

### Enemy response and initiative

The COSVN security directorate read the pistol from the first captured example, taken at Rach Kien on 22 March, and its circular of 4 June gave the right answer: hold fire until the lamp comes on, then fire at it. That cost the Front nothing. Two committees of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region misread the lights for four months as night-vision that saw through walls, and kept their cadres out of the held villages; the error cost them some control of those villages until a captured light was switched on in Hau Nghia. The Dinh Tuong security section's decision in October to take the Long Dinh post's Glocks from the local-force company that had captured them, so that the pistols went to the men who killed officials rather than the men who fought posts, was the Front putting the weapon where it would matter most.

### Causal chains

**C016-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV016-1) The posts' platoon leaders and post commanders put their pistols' lights on the walls during night attacks in 1964 → on the night of 18–19 November an AC-130J over a Dinh Tuong post holds its fire for six minutes because its crew cannot tell the post's lights from the attackers' torches → the 14th Air Commando Wing and the IV Corps province chiefs agree in December that posts mark with strobes and fire and keep white light off their walls → from 1965 the Front's mortar crews, which had ranged the posts at night from 300 metres on the lights the posts showed, lose their aiming points, and the posts that held by mortar in 1965 are hit less accurately than the posts that had not changed their habits.

### Downstream

The Glocks taken from the posts in 1964–65 go to the security sections and turn up in the killing of district chiefs in 1966. The police's doubled course of October 1964 is the one they keep. The light discipline learned in 1964 is the habit the posts carry through the war. Nothing in Phase I's end vector moves: the pistol's effect on the American dead of 1965 is a handful of men, inside the range the record already carries, and its effect on the posts and the night is a trace inside ranges set by the carbine, the radio, the gunship and the courses.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

### Chronicle

*End of phase against the historical state: POP.night_control 71–76 (history 67.2; medium) · POP.night_control.I_corps 0 to +3 against history (low) · POP.night_control.II_corps 0 to +3 against history (low) · POP.night_control.III_corps +8 to +14 against history (medium) · POP.night_control.IV_corps +6 to +14 against history (medium) · VCI.strength -45% to -30% against history (medium) · ENEMY.engagement_control 61–66 (history 66; low) · TRAIL.tonnage_arriving -12% to -1% against history (low) · FIND.main_force 0 to +3 against history (low) · FIND.vci +1.65 to +3.3 against history (medium) · ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit +2 to +7 against history (low) · ARVN.effectiveness.fire_coordination -6 to -4 against history (low) · ARVN.self_sufficiency.fire_support -5 to +2 against history (low) · US.casualties 8,435–9,060 (history 9,378; low) · US.political_tolerance.public 42–44 (history 44; low).*

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US strength rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967¹. The big operations win their battles and do not change the war: the Ia Drang, Masher/White Wing, Hastings, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City each kill men the reports exaggerate and are followed by the enemy's return, because he decides when to fight and withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. In Saigon the junta survives the Buddhist uprising and wins an election with a third of the vote; pacification is reorganised under CORDS in May 1967; and in Hanoi the militants around Le Duan plan a general offensive for Tet.

Beneath the main-force war runs the one the provisions changed most, the war for the held belt. The land programme leaves the phase with about 820,000 hectares titled to about 640,000 families⁴. With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages, chiefs who stay, district centres everywhere and Delta's raids, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it, and the committees go to govern from the base areas and from across the border by courier. The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its legal cadres into the towns, and where the method is pushed into villages the government does not hold at night it becomes the historical Phoenix, three years early. None of this alters the main-force war, fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what that war is fought over.

The enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch, the gunships and Delta's road teams count the Trail's trucks from December 1965, and the B-52J cuts every bridge it can measure from April 1966; the enemy answers with fords and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before. Sihanoukville is measured by March 1967 as the source of most of the arms for III and IV Corps. The northern campaign is the theater's own: the restrike watch keeps the Red River crossings closed more days than not while the cell's monthly kills fall from about 140 to 35, there is no Christmas pause and no POL strike, and this command reports in October 1966 that the North's war in the South needs none of what is being destroyed⁴³.

The armies are better in the fights the enemy grants and get no more of them. Every infantryman carries a modern carbine and every squad a belt-fed gun; the Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle. The ARVN's battalions are filled from the board's list, its sergeants schooled and its privates trained, and the enemy does what he does against every better unit: he declines to fight it and lays his ambush for the one beside it. He moves his war in the lowlands south of Da Nang to the mine, which no course teaches against, and the devaluation of 1966 sends trained privates home to families their pay no longer feeds. The radio puts every post on the district net, and the Front answers by ambushing the company that answers the call, until the districts learn to answer with guns and gunships rather than trucks.

#### ◆ The war on the district chiefs
*Aug 1965 – Jun 1966 · The Delta, the provinces around Saigon and the coastal Centre · new · EV002-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

By the summer of 1965 the Front's provincial committees reported that the list-appointed chiefs had stayed through three governments, and COSVN concluded in August that the chiefs who kept their Popular Forces in the hamlets at night were its most dangerous enemies and were to be killed. Between August 1965 and June 1966 nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs died, sixteen of them from the list. On 14 November Major Tran Van Sang of Mo Cay, whom the Kien Hoa committee called "the most harmful of the puppet district chiefs in the province", was killed by a command-detonated mine on the road to Ben Tre⁸.

Four of the twenty-one were not mined but shot in their own houses at night, and the pistols that killed them were the Republic's. On the night of 2–3 March 1966 three men of the Dinh Tuong province security section crossed the canal behind the district chief's house at Cai Be, where Captain Le Quang Hien had slept since his appointment in 1964 with two PF guards on the gate and none at the back. Two of the three carried Glocks taken from the Long Dinh post in October 1964, and one of the two still had a light that worked, on cells taken from a district supply point in January. The leader switched it on in the bedroom doorway for the time it took to see which of the two shapes was the captain and which his wife, and fired four rounds; the second man killed the guard who came round the house at the shots, and the three were across the canal before the PF on the gate understood that the shooting was inside. The three other chiefs killed this way, at Duc Hue, Can Duoc and Mo Duc, were shot with pistols too, two of them with Glocks²⁰.

The Republic's best pistol in its enemy's hands changed the manner of these four deaths and not their number. The rate was set by COSVN's decision and by what the security sections could learn of a chief's house and his habits, not by their weapons: a section that could reach Hien's bedroom door could have killed him with a K-54, and the Front never killed chiefs faster than it could find them. The killings did not empty the districts, and that was the reform's quiet gain: a dead chief's deputy acted at once, the Board sent a ranked list for the vacancy within about three weeks, and Mo Cay had a new chief on 6 December and Cai Be on 24 March. The chiefs learned to travel with escorts and vary their roads, and after Cai Be the province chiefs put a guard inside every chief's house at night with orders to keep his own light off until he fired; the Special Branch traced five of the twenty-one attacks within the year, Cai Be among them through the serial of a Glock taken from a courier in May, and the Front never killed chiefs at that rate again⁸²⁰.

#### The restrike watch on the Red River
*1 Aug 1965 – 31 Dec 1966 · The Paul Doumer, Long Bien and Chuong Duong crossings at Hanoi; the Canal des Rapides and Bac Giang bridges on the northeast railway; the ferries at Phu Ly and Ninh Binh; the MACV Combined Targeting Cell, Tan Son Nhut · new · EV013-3*
*Initiative: both*

From August 1965 the Combined Targeting Cell ran the northern campaign on one list of about 300 targets and a daily repair list, and the Red River crossings at Hanoi were its pattern. The North did not rebuild the Paul Doumer bridge under the watch; by June the Hanoi bridge company and the Chinese 1st Railway Engineer Division had a pontoon bridge 3 km downstream, floated by night and broken up by day, and a rail ferry at Chuong Duong. The U-2S's dawn pass found each crossing in use and each repair, the cell's midday list ordered a J cell for the night or an F-105D package for the morning, and between August 1965 and December 1966 the crossings were struck 61 times and impassable to rail on about 290 of 517 days⁴³. The pontoons were what the J could not solve, because they were targets only for the hours they were assembled, and the North kept three sets for every one in the river.

What the watch measured was the North spending labour to keep moving: about 2,000 men a night and a Chinese engineer regiment to carry 40–60 percent of the trains of February 1965. The cell's monthly count of targets destroyed rose to about 140 in the summer of 1965 and fell to about 35 by December 1966, as the North stopped owning anything else worth a GBU-31. In October 1966 J-2 sent Washington, beside those figures, the Gulf watch's count of coastal traffic south of Thanh Hoa and the CIA's estimate of the North's imports; neither had fallen. Westmoreland's covering note said that the campaign was destroying everything in the North that could be destroyed, and that the North's war in the South needed none of it⁴³.

#### The committees driven out: FOLLOW THROUGH, NIGHT LANTERN and the empty beds
*2 Aug 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · Hoc Mon, Cu Chi and Trang Bang; Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, Long An; Cai Lay, Cai Be and Giao Duc, Dinh Tuong; Memot, Cambodia; the towns of the Delta · new · EVR05-4*
*Initiative: both*

Three operations in the held belt, by the task force, the province and Delta, did the same thing in three places, and together they are the story of what happened to the Front's district government before Tet: it was driven out of the villages it governed and went on governing them from further away. The first followed the Hoc Mon committee to Cu Chi. On 2 August 1965 the task force's III Corps task group, now about 420 strong under Major Garvin and Lieutenant Colonel Phuoc, opened FOLLOW THROUGH against the committee's survivors and the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's finance section in the hamlets along the Saigon River. For two months it took only couriers, each of whom knew only the next courier's name. On 14 October a cashier of twenty-three was arrested at a Hoc Mon market stall with 41,000 piasters in a rice sack and rallied against a named offer that her mother would not be detained. On the night of 19–20 October two SEAL platoons and the Gia Dinh PRU took the section's deputy chief in a tunnel under a family altar in Phu Hoa Dong, with the ledger for nine months of 1965, while a 25th Division company tasked against its division's plan held the land side. The ledger named the collectors of four districts: 64 arrests followed by March 1966, and 17 killed in raids, four of whom the Commission's audit could not show to be cadres. The committee's secretary, Nguyen Van Tu, went to the Ho Bo woods in January as the US 25th Division arrived at Cu Chi, and the region's collections from the posted villages of Hoc Mon and Go Vap in 1966 were about an eighth of 1964's¹².

The second was Long An's own. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings, about 400 families' titles were still held at the district office. Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, the province chief, and Major Le Cong Phuong, the district chief, both from the list, built NIGHT LANTERN in the 14 villages between Route 5 and the Vam Co around the settled post, the census and the folder, with eight RF companies, 26 PF platoons raised in the villages, two Police Field Force companies, 140 constables, a gunship on call at night, and Lan's rule that no post was settled until its platoon slept in the hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour. The posts went in from 15 November. On 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, where the killings had begun; the platoon and the constables held for three hours under the gunship until an RF company came down the canal, and the 506th left 38 dead. On 4 February a night check found a young man whose card did not match his booklet, and within a week his courier route had led to the chief of the district committee's security section, whose notebook the district centre matched against the census, 17 of 22 courier stations to their households. On 24–25 February the PRU took the committee's deputy secretary and finance chief at the Rung Sat's edge, where the committee had moved believing itself beyond the posts; the deputy secretary rallied and drew the chart of the province committee above him. By 30 April all 14 posts held, 212 had been arrested and 71 released, 64 had rallied to the police posts, the committee's first-quarter collections were a third of 1965's, and the 400 titles were handed to their families in their hamlets⁸. Captain Whitcomb, at the end of his eighteen months, reported that the committee's arrest and not a battalion's sweep had broken the tax, and that the families had asked him, not the district chief, whether the arrests would last¹⁰.

Delta was the third. By 1966 it had found its rate: 38 raids a month in 1966 and 44 in 1967, 171 and then 126 listed cadre of district rank or above taken, 61 of them province or region committee members, and nine finance sections with their ledgers, four raids in five in III and IV Corps, because that is where the files were. It paid in the currency it could not print: fourteen operators killed, four missing and fifty-one wounded by the end of 1967, and a quarter of its assault troops selected after the war began. In September 1967 the National Fusion Centre gave the Can Tho squadron twenty-six addresses in upper Dinh Tuong, the best file in the Delta. Twenty-three raids between 4 September and 14 November took two people. The other twenty-one found a bed that had been slept in and a family that said, truthfully, that the man had not been there for months. The courier taken on 19 September told why. The province committee had lived since March at Memot, across the Cambodian border, and came into the province for four or five nights every second month under a COSVN circular of April 1967 in three sentences: senior cadre do not sleep twice in a hamlet; committees meet in the base areas or across the border; couriers carry nothing written²⁵.

The raid yield fell by two-fifths, and so did the Special Branch's arrests in the held belt, to about 40 percent of their 1966 peak, with the district centres' neutralizations of district cadre falling from about 140 a quarter to about 50¹⁰. Komer proposed monthly arrest targets in February 1967; this headquarters answered on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working, Khoa and Hieu opposed the targets on the record, and no centre was given one in 1967. The measures the enemy could not fake agreed. The committees of the held belt were running their districts by courier from the Ho Bo woods, the Rung Sat and Memot at two months' remove, with collections at a sixth to an eighth of 1964's, and the falling yield was the measure of that success²⁵. The Front did not pay in people. It paid in walking time, which against the best three hundred operators of the American army was the cheapest answer any provision drew from it. And the legal cadres who left the villages settled in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the edges of Saigon among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000⁸.

#### The big-unit war: Starlite, A Shau, Hastings, Long Tan, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City
*18 Aug 1965 – 14 May 1967 · The Van Tuong peninsula and the DMZ in I Corps; the A Shau valley; Phuoc Tuy; War Zone C and the Iron Triangle in III Corps · changed from history · EVCP01-4*
*Initiative: both*

The big-unit battles of 1965–67 were each won on the ground, each followed by the enemy's return, and none changed the war. On 9–10 March 1966 the 95B Regiment overran the A Shau camp under cloud, and the valley became a PAVN base for the rest of the war⁵⁴. In Hastings in July the 3rd Marine Division and Ngo Quang Truong's 1st Division drove the 324B Division back across the DMZ, and drew the Marines north, away from the coastal hamlets⁵⁵. On 18 August D Company, 6th Royal Australian Regiment held the Long Tan rubber for three hours against the 275th Regiment with the guns of Nui Dat behind it, losing 18 killed⁵⁶. Attleboro grew from a brigade's shakedown into a corps battle against the 9th Division at a cost of 155 American dead⁵⁷. In 1967 Cedar Falls emptied Ben Suc and the Iron Triangle, which was a staging area again a year later, and Junction City sent two divisions and three brigades into War Zone C, where the enemy attacked American firebases five times at night or at dawn, at Prek Klok, Ap Bau Bang, Suoi Tre and Ap Gu, and lost heavily each time; the Americans lost 282 killed, COSVN moved, and the 9th Division withdrew into Cambodia⁵⁸⁵⁹.

The American battalions here, and the ARVN 1st Division at Hastings, carried the M4A1 and the M249; the Australians did not. In Junction City the Ghostriders of the 4th Squadron were over the night attacks, as they were over every allied position attacked at night in III Corps from 1964: at Suoi Tre and Ap Gu they lit the treelines and fired on the assault waves forming beyond the wire, and at Prek Klok they found the mortar positions⁶⁰. I do not adjust the historical returns for the rifles or the gunships. In each of these battles the fight was decided at the wire and by who attacked whom, where and when, and the enemy's losses were never counted closely enough for the difference to show³¹. What they proved here is what they proved historically: the enemy lost heavily when he attacked an American position, and he decided for himself whether to do so.

The board's second season changed the army's battalions faster than anything since 1955. #### The Da Nhim line
*3 Oct 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The 230 kV line from the Da Nhim station at Don Duong, Tuyen Duc Province, through Di Linh in Lam Dong and Long Khanh to the Thu Duc and Bien Hoa substations · new · EV008-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

The Bien Hoa melt shop drew about half its power down 250 kilometres of line from the Da Nhim hydro station, and on the night of 3 October 1965 sappers of the Front's Region 6 dropped two of its towers east of Di Linh; the region had chosen the line because it was the one target whose loss was felt in Saigon and which could be reached without fighting anybody. The Bien Hoa shops built replacement towers in sets of four a week, and a dropped tower was back in four to ten days rather than the six to eight weeks of one shipped from Japan. The power company paid the Koho villages along the line a wage to watch it and gave each a hamlet radio, the first government wage or radio those hamlets had had. The Front dropped about forty towers in 1966 and twenty-five in 1967, and the steelworks lost a fifth and then a tenth of its hours, rolling 180,000 and 220,000 tonnes against a rated 200,000–300,000; the arsenal, on the Thu Duc station, lost nothing¹⁴. The Republic got from the line, without meaning to, thirty Koho villages that took its wage, carried its radios and told the sector when the Front came through.

#### Plei Me and the Ia Drang
*19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · changed from history · EV000-23*
*Initiative: enemy*

On 19 October 1965 Major General Chu Huy Man's B3 Front attacked the camp at Plei Me and ambushed the relief by day; the 33rd Regiment's night assaults on the camp were met by Ghostriders from Nha Trang within about forty minutes, and its third, on the southern wall, was broken in the open. The PAVN withdrew toward the Chu Pong, and the newly arrived 1st Cavalry Division followed. On 14 November Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore's 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry landed at X-Ray, in the 66th Regiment's assembly area, and held for three days with massed artillery, tactical air and the first tactical B-52 strikes⁶¹. The troopers carried M4A1s with two M249s in each squad; two gunships held the night of 14–15 November over the perimeter, and the 7th Battalion, 66th Regiment, found forming on the slope at 0400, came in thinner at dawn²².

On 17 November Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade's 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, marching in column through tall grass to Albany, was ambushed by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd, who grabbed the column by the belt where artillery and air could not reach⁶¹. The head of the column formed a perimeter in the trees within minutes and held all afternoon. Behind it the companies broke into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, except two whose captains, of the educated classes of 1959–62, had put flank security into the grass on their own authority when the column halted; their men were found in perimeters rather than knots¹¹. Through the night the troopers in the grass put tourniquets on each other and on themselves, as the basic course had taught them, while the gunship overhead lit the field and fired on the PAVN search parties wherever the strobes in American helmets showed them more than 100 metres from any trooper²².

The campaign's American dead were about 165–170 against 237 historically. The squad guns account for about 30 of the difference, the companies that kept their security about 15, the tourniquets about 10–15 and the gunships about 10–20, most of them at Albany, which remained the worst American loss of a single day so far. Moore's count of 634 PAVN dead and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record, with the same caution⁶¹. Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success and drew the lesson that confirmed their strategy. The B3 Front's study added what the historical one did not need: the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and the aircraft "that sees in the dark" made the night assault as costly as the day, so the belt-grabbing attack must be made at closer range, under cloud or in the forest, or not at all²². McNamara advised the President at the end of November that even doubling US forces might produce only a stalemate at a higher level.

#### The II Corps list and its abandonment
*15 Nov 1965 – 2 Apr 1966 · Tuy An and Song Cau Districts, Phu Yen Province; Phu My and Phu Cat Districts, Binh Dinh Province; Qui Nhon · new · EV012-5*
*Initiative: both*

The II Corps task group formed at Qui Nhon in November 1965 on the thinnest ground in the country: the coast of Binh Dinh and Phu Yen had been the Viet Minh's for nine years and the Front's since 1960, and the Special Branch held files on hamlets it had never entered at night¹². Its commander, Lieutenant Colonel Harold J. Beck, adopted in December a high-value target list of 64 members of the two province committees, to be captured or, if they resisted, killed, with a Korean special-forces team among his raid elements.

Military Region 5 had its own plans for the coast, made for its own reasons. In October its Party Committee refused the B3 Front's request for a regiment for the Highlands and kept the 2nd Regiment and the 3rd Division's regiments in the An Lao, Kim Son and Suoi Ca valleys through the dry season, to guard the coastal rice its forces lived on and because its leaders held the coast, not the plateau, to be the decisive ground¹². That rivalry kept the province committees close to the regiments that protected them.

The list produced 11 operations, 9 detainees and 14 dead, three of them on the list. On 19–20 January 1966 the Korean team and the Phu Yen PRU killed four men running from a house in Hoa Thanh on a rallier's word; the Commission found that they were a family who had sheltered a cadre once, two years before, and that the rallier had a land dispute with them. The magistrate referred two Koreans for homicide and Chae Myung-shin refused to surrender them, and within a week the province committee's leaflet named the four men and the Korean unit in every market from Tuy Hoa to Song Cau. On 2 April the list was ended in II Corps and the task group returned to warrant arrests and named offers¹². It cost the task force its credibility on the coast for two years and gave the Front its first story about it, a year before Duc Thanh and believed where Duc Thanh was not.

#### The panhandle watch: STEADY COUNT, NIGHT LEDGER and the road teams
*6 Dec 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The Laotian panhandle from the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes to Tchepone and the Ban Laboy ford; Ubon, Thailand; the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut · new · EVR05-5*
*Initiative: allied*

Three instruments watched the Trail from December 1965, and their counts told one story. On 6 December, the day Tiger Hound opened, the Global Hawk orbit moved to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the panhandle, out of reach of the guns, and its radar watched Route 23 from Mu Gia, Routes 911 and 912 from Ban Karai and the fords down to Tchepone through the morning cloud on the passes¹⁶. The same night the sixteen Ghostriders of the 17th Air Commando Squadron, based at Ubon on a Thai condition that nothing be said of it, flew their first armed reconnaissance on its tracks, in an operation named NIGHT LEDGER because every engagement was filmed and counted against the radar²². From October two to four Delta teams of five were on the ground, under Ambassador Sullivan's limits on depth and target, moving at night on goggles and measuring what the radar could not see under the trees²⁵.

The strike force reached about one track in six within forty minutes; the gunships, flying to the tracks themselves, engaged about one in three within twenty. In the first dry season the pilots claimed about 560 trucks and the cell counted about 210, and the gunships' video counted 1,100–1,500 against crews' claims of 2,600¹⁷. Southbound movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 at the March peak. On 12 April 1966 the B-52Js of HARD CROSSING cut the crossings at the foot of the pass, and about twenty hours later the orbit watched the first trucks cross again on a ford reopened beside the broken bridges²⁶. The drivers learned the orbit's changeover hours and trellised the open stretches, Group 559 moved two battalions of 37mm guns to the crossings a year early, the convoys spread to intervals of five to ten minutes, and by the dry season of 1966–67 a third of the movements ran under canopy. On 2 March 1967 Ghostrider 22 was brought down by an unplotted 57mm battery at the Ban Laboy ford, with six of its nine crewmen lost²². Delta's teams completed 71 of 94 missions uncompromised and measured 84 truck parks, 31 gun positions and 19 storage points to a few metres, a third of them under canopy²⁵. On 11–14 July 1967 Captain Robert L. Ainsley's team of five was lost whole near the Ban Laboy ford to a security battalion that had learned to hunt the watchers.

MACV J-2 estimated from the tracks that 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle at the dry season's peak, more than the forces in the South needed from the North¹⁷. In 1966–67 the gunships counted 2,000–2,600 trucks destroyed or disabled, and the net loss to throughput rose by two to four points; Delta's cueing added one to four points more, inside the same width; the trucks were replaced from Soviet and Chinese deliveries within the year²². It was the first time any headquarters had counted the Trail rather than estimating it, and the count said that the bombing raised the toll on the transit without lowering what arrived.

#### The air war over the North, 1966–67: no pause, no fuel, the buffer's edge, Bolo and Muscle Shoals
*24 Dec 1965 – Dec 1967 · Hanoi and Haiphong; the northeast railway and the frontier at Pingxiang; the DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand; Washington · changed from history · EVCP02-4*
*Initiative: both*

The northern campaign of 1966–67 was measured at every step and changed nothing in the South. The 37-day pause of Christmas 1965 did not happen, nor the May halt and MAYFLOWER before it; Johnson sent his envoys to forty capitals with the fourteen points and words alone, and Hanoi answered with its four points as historically. About 2,800 sorties flew over the North in the 37 days, and the season of free movement history gave the North cost it two to three weeks of the 1966 flow, made up by April⁴³. Fulbright said in the February hearings that a peace offensive under bombs was a pretence, and the charge cost more in the press than in votes.

The petroleum strikes of 29 June 1966 were not flown, because the tank farms had burned in March 1965 and the North's fuel had gone into drums under the trees; J-2 told CINCPAC in September 1965 that fuel was no longer a target system, and McNamara's doubts hardened a year before history's. The Stennis hearings of 1967 were never held, because there were no restrictions to blame and the campaign's failure was this command's report, not the Pentagon's secret⁴³.

The buffer was tested once. On 12 May 1966 Captain James R. Ainsley's F-105D, hit by 57mm fire over Lang Son, ran north and he ejected inside the buffer; the Chinese gun crews of the 61st and 63rd Anti-Aircraft Divisions held him at Pingxiang for eleven days, and Beijing claimed he had been shot down over China. The President considered widening the buffer to history's 25–30 miles for three days and did not, because Rusk judged that China would not fight for a railway in Vietnam; no aircraft was to cross the line in pursuit, and the cell kept its strikes 18 miles from the frontier⁴³.

The MiG war was smaller and more dispersed than history's. The MiG-21s of 1966 lived at Mengzi and Ningming and flew into the North a sortie at a time. In Operation Bolo on 2 January 1967 four rose to Robin Olds's F-4Cs from Noi Bai and three were shot down; the VPAF reached about 120 MiGs by the end of 1967, fighting from China⁴³. The barrier began from STEADY COUNT's tracks, and Muscle Shoals laid its sensors where the radar's tracks ended, from 25 November 1967⁶²¹⁷.

#### ◆ The tunnel teams of Cu Chi and the Ho Bo woods
*8 Jan 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Cu Chi District, Hau Nghia Province; the Ho Bo and Boi Loi woods, the Filhol rubber and the Iron Triangle, Binh Duong Province · new · EV016-2*
*Initiative: allied*

The tunnels of Cu Chi were cleared in this war by the same small men who cleared them in history, and they went down with a light on the muzzle. The American tunnel teams began, as historically, in Operation Crimp in the Ho Bo woods in January 1966, where the Australian 3 Field Troop had gone down before them. From that month the 25th Infantry Division at Cu Chi and the 1st Infantry Division kept tunnel squads of volunteers, men about 165 centimetres or shorter, each going down with a Glock 21, an M7 bayonet, a probe and a wire, and a second man at the entrance with a field telephone and a line¹⁹²⁰.

What changed underground was the left hand. On 19 April 1966 Specialist Four Ramon Ortiz of the 25th Division's 65th Engineer Battalion went into a shaft under a haystack in Phu Hoa Dong with his light off, listened at the first bend for a minute and a half, and switched on for two seconds. The beam put the end of the gallery four metres off and a man in it with a rifle, and Ortiz fired three rounds before the man's rifle came up; he could do it because his free left hand was flat on the floor and not holding a torch. Behind the man were a document box and a second gallery with three wounded fighters of the Cu Chi district unit, who surrendered to the light. Ortiz's sergeant counted the gallery's contents by the same light: 140 kilograms of documents and a printing press²⁰.

The pistol did not change the rest of the tunnel war. The blast of a.45 underground deafened its firer as it had deafened history's men, the grenade on a tripwire at the second bend killed in the same way, and the Front answered within a season by defending fewer first bends and trapping more of them, and by leaving through the far exit when a team went down¹⁹. Through Cedar Falls in January 1967 and into the clearing of the Ho Bo woods that summer, the teams went down about as often as history's and cleared about as much tunnel a day. They lost about a third fewer men underground in the two years, about 6–9 lives against history's toll, and brought out more prisoners alive from the first galleries in 1966 and fewer in 1967, when the first galleries were empty and wired²⁰. That is the whole of the change, at low confidence: a few more men alive at the end of a rope, and a few more of the enemy taken rather than killed in the dark.

#### Operation GOLDEN SHEAF
*10 Jan – 30 Jun 1966 · Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien Provinces, along the Xa No canal from Can Tho to Vi Thanh · new · EV001-6*
*Initiative: allied*

GOLDEN SHEAF was the first operation of the war planned around the land programme rather than an enemy unit: the 21st Division, two Ranger battalions, about 40 RF companies, 24 titling teams and 6 muster teams carried the titling line west along the Xa No canal into about 60 villages of Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien in the dry season, with gunships over the posts at night. A regiment cleared a block of villages by day; RF companies stayed in the hamlets overnight; the titling teams followed ten days behind, posting parcel numbers rather than names; and the muster teams raised a PF platoon in each titled hamlet from the families who had just received their land⁴. A regiment moved on only when the last block was rated able to hold at night.

The enemy declined battle and withdrew toward the U Minh, leaving mines on the dikes. On 22–23 March two companies attacked an RF company and a new PF platoon at kilometre 28; they held four hours under a gunship and left 31 bodies. The units reported about 690 enemy dead; the muster teams' audit, the first ever made of a division's body count, accepted 268 by body and weapon. By 30 June about 46,000 hectares were titled to about 38,000 families in 112 hamlets, 74 of them held at night by their own forces and 38 by day only, and the operation stopped about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh at the line the territorials could hold, not at the limit of the titling teams, who sat idle in June⁴. In the held hamlets the Front's collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn villages that had never sold rice at the government market at Phung Hiep sold there.

#### The frontier battles of 1966–67: Masher/White Wing, the Hill Fights, Con Thien and Dak To
*24 Jan 1966 – 23 Nov 1967 · The Bong Son plain and the An Lao valley, Binh Dinh Province; the hills northwest of Khe Sanh and Con Thien, Quang Tri Province; Dak To, Kontum Province · changed from history · EVCP02-3*
*Initiative: both*

Four frontier battles were each won on the ground, and none changed who chose the next. In **Masher/White Wing**, 24 January – 6 March 1966, the 1st Cavalry Division with the ARVN and the Koreans swept the Bong Son plain against the PAVN 3rd Division, lost 228 killed, and displaced more than 27,000 civilians; the division slipped away and was back within a week⁶³. Here the parcels of the families who fled were held for the absent tiller under an instruction of February 1966, and families who came back from the camps in 1967 found their land in their own names, which took from the Front its best sentence in Binh Dinh⁴. In the **Hill Fights** at Khe Sanh, 24 April – 11 May 1967, the 3rd Marines took Hills 861 and 881 from the 325C Division; their M4A1s jammed at about the M14's rate, the marksmen killed machine gunners in their apertures at 400–500 metres, and I put Marine losses at about 125 killed against 155, with no inquiry into the rifle¹⁸.

At **Con Thien** the Marketplace ambush of 2 July killed 86, and nothing in the B-52J changed a hedgerow fight at a few dozen metres⁶⁴. From September the PAVN besieged the hill by fire from north of the Ben Hai. Here a second Global Hawk orbit imaged the ground every night from 4 September, the cell measured the gun positions before dawn, and from 11 September cells of Js with GBU-38s came every two to three hours. The gunners halved their rate to survive, the heaviest day came at about 600 rounds rather than 1,200, the 812th's trenches were struck and abandoned, and the siege lifted about three weeks early with about a third fewer Marines killed in September and October and perhaps half again as many PAVN dead⁴⁷. Westmoreland learned from it that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle. At **Dak To**, 3–23 November, the 4th Division, the 173rd Airborne and the ARVN fought the PAVN 1st Division through three weeks of bunkers for 361 American dead; with Loc Ninh, Song Be and Con Thien it drew about half the American battalions to the frontiers by January 1968, which was what Hanoi wanted⁶⁵.

#### The mines of Dien Ban
*1 Mar 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Dien Ban, Dai Loc and Duy Xuyen Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon; Son Tinh and Binh Son Districts, Quang Ngai Province · new · EV009-2*
*Initiative: enemy*

The enemy's answer to the trained soldier was to kill him without fighting him. In late 1965 the Quang Da Special Zone concluded that its local companies were losing firefights with units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds and did not bunch when hit, and its directive for 1966 made mines and booby traps every lowland guerrilla platoon's principal work, by monthly quota, from unexploded American ordnance, stolen grenades and Go Vap dry cells. The 1st Battalion, 26th Marines met the result in Dien Ban every day for a year. Its patrols moved as they had been taught, dispersed and at intervals, through hedgerows laid with bounding mines and rigged 105mm shells, and between September 1966 and August 1967 it lost about 38 killed and 290 wounded, two-thirds of the dead and four-fifths of the wounded to devices. Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey wrote that his Marines "do everything right in a firefight and are given none". The course gave them the tourniquet, and the surgeon at Hoi An reckoned a dozen men alive who would have died on the dike. The hospital also recorded about 300 civilian mine casualties in the two districts in 1966–67, a third more than the year before; the Marines came to treat the people who knew where the mines were as accomplices, and the battalion's bond with the PF platoons, which Major Carl E. #### The academy at Bien Hoa and the staff sergeant's waiver

#### The Buddhist uprising
*10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · changed from history · EV000-26*
*Initiative: neither*

After Honolulu, Ky moved against Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps and dismissed him on 10 March 1966; the Struggle Movement of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers under Thich Tri Quang took Da Nang and Hue, and Ky's first landing at Da Nang was blocked. On 15 May he airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang, with III MAF caught between two factions of its ally; Thi went into exile, the US Information Service library in Hue was burned, and Hue was retaken by 19 June, with about 150 dead⁶⁶. The uprising ended the Buddhists as an organised force, confirmed Thieu and Ky, and bought elections for a Constituent Assembly in September.

Here the police that Ky used was the one the decree of 1964 had built. On 12 March the Directory removed Director General Cuong unlawfully and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, and the Mission protested and did not pull the lever, because the Directory was the government it had just embraced. In Hue the Special Branch gave Loan the census of the pagoda quarter and the streets round the university, and about 1,300 were detained by July, about 1,000 of them later released by magistrates whose review the Mission made the price of its police aid. The repression was more efficient than history's and cost more: the first American stories of "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, and several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders went to the Front with what they knew of the census⁸. In the 1st Division four battalions under the board's commanders stayed on operations west of Hue and refused orders from both sides to march on the cities, which thinned the Struggle's armed support without speeding Ky's recapture. The board's list for the division was then headed by Ngo Quang Truong, whom Ky took, and the Military Security Service opened its first file on "the board's officers as a political factor" in July¹¹.

#### The Regional Force companies go to the corps
*4 Apr – 30 Oct 1966 · Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu Provinces, IV Corps, and the edge of the U Minh forest · new · EV004-2*
*Initiative: allied*

By 1966 the rearmed Regional Forces were good enough that the corps wanted them. On 4 April IV Corps formed 34 of the best RF companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu into six mobile groups for a season on the U Minh's edge. Lieutenant Colonel Lam Van Thien, province chief of Chuong Thien, protested that they were the reaction force for forty-one PF outposts and nine police posts, and was told that the outposts had their own carbines and radios now. The groups fought eleven operations well, and in the largest, near Thoi Binh in July, killed about 40 of a U Minh company for nine of their own. In their absence, between May and August, 23 PF outposts and six police posts in the three provinces were overrun or abandoned and about 300 weapons lost, against seven outposts in the same months of 1965; the Front had only to watch the boats carry the companies away¹⁸. Nineteen of the lost posts had called the district within ten minutes, and Thien laid the district's logs beside his returns: each call, its time and its answer, no company to send and a battery at Vi Thanh that could reach nine posts of forty-one. The companies went home in October. The case became, in 1967, the rule that RF companies belonged to the province chief and could be borrowed only with his consent and for thirty days, and Thien's request for guns the districts could call became the territorial artillery platoons of 1970¹⁴.

#### Operation HARD CROSSING and the estuary field
*12 Apr 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Mu Gia pass and Route 15; Ban Karai pass and Route 912; the Xe Bang Fai crossings and the Ban Laboy ford; the estuaries of the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang; Route Package 1 · new · EV005-2*
*Initiative: allied*

On 12 April 1966, the day history first sent B-52s against the Mu Gia pass, Washington authorized the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the panhandle and Route Package 1. On the first night eight Js put 160 GBU-31s on eleven bridges, causeways and ferries at the foot of Mu Gia and on the Xe Bang Fai, under cloud that made no difference, and every one was down by dawn. For six weeks each repair was seen, re-measured and cut the next night; by May the counts at the watch's entry gates were a third of March's, and in Saigon and Honolulu it was read for a few weeks as the Trail closing. It was not. Group 559's engineers gave up building anything the J could find, and at every cut crossing laid two, three and then five alternatives: fords paved a foot under the water, causeways floated at dusk and sunk at dawn, Soviet pontoons hidden under the bank. At Ban Laboy the cell counted one ford in April and nine by October. HARD CROSSING destroyed about 210 crossings for 1,900 GBU-31 kits and no bombers, taxed the flow by 10–15 percent at its peak and 5 percent by October, and by the end of October the trucks south of Tchepone were 15 percent above March⁴⁷. It had built the enemy a road net with no single point of failure, and he had drawn the right lesson in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so he would own nothing it could measure.

On 26 February 1967 Washington allowed the North's rivers to be mined, and four Js laid the first field of Quickstrikes in the Song Ca and Song Giang estuaries, set for steel hulls⁴⁸. Steel traffic south of Vinh fell to a fifth in a month and stopped by June, reseeded every three to four weeks without loss. The cargo moved to junks, sampans and trucks, and by the autumn truck movements below Vinh were a quarter higher with the tonnage into the panhandle about the same; the North spent 15,000–25,000 more porters on it, and asked Moscow for minesweepers and got trucks, which was what it needed⁴⁷. How much less arrived in the South because of it, nobody in Washington asked. My own answer is very little.

#### Operation OPEN ROAD
*3–21 May 1966 · Route 13 from Chon Thanh to An Loc, the Xa Cam rubber and the Tau O crossing, Binh Long Province; Bau Bang, Binh Duong Province · new · EV007-3*
*Initiative: enemy*

The ambush that was never sprung on Route 13 is the clearest case in this war of what the reforms did and why no report showed it. In April 1966 the VC 9th Division ordered Lieutenant Colonel Dang Huu Thoi's 272nd Regiment, about 1,500 men, to lay an ambush in the Xa Cam rubber south of An Loc for the next ARVN convoy, on the ground every regiment on that road had chosen, an L of two battalions along the eastern side and the Tau O crossing to stop the head of the column. The convoy of 62 trucks left Chon Thanh at 0600 on 4 May under Major Bui Quang Trieu's 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, given him from the board's list, and Trieu had written the order on the pattern the career course taught: two companies on foot in the rubber 300 to 400 metres each side of the road a kilometre ahead, PF guides from Tan Khai, mortars and a battery registered on the Tau O and four points in the rubber, and no truck moving until the flanks reported each kilometre clear¹¹. It was slow, and the division commander told him so twice by radio.

At 0830 the 272nd's scouts saw the flanking companies coming through the rubber toward the eastern arm of the L, at a walk. The Front's rule was to fight only where victory was certain. Thoi's political officer argued that the men had lain nine days in position and expected a fight. At 0910 Thoi withdrew the regiment east without firing, and the convoy reached An Loc at 1700¹¹. The division recorded OPEN ROAD as completed without contact, and MACV's summary did not mention it.

On 21 May the 272nd ambushed instead an RF convoy near Bau Bang, two companies riding fourteen trucks with no one in the trees. The territorial course held where it could: the men left the trucks on the side away from the fire, fought from the ditch in pairs, reported on the sector net, and the rear company put its BARs, not squad guns the territorials never drew, into the treeline within two minutes²¹⁵. The RF lost about 32 dead rather than 40. A private's training does not reach the order that puts him on a truck in the Xa Cam rubber. A notebook of the 272nd's operations officer, taken near Lai Khe in January 1967, recorded both days: "the puppet battalion now walks beside the road and not on it", the regiment should "seek its targets among the units that still ride", and the territorials "now fight from the ditch", so an ambush must kill in the first volley or not be laid¹¹. The better battalion lost fewer men because it was attacked less, and an attack that is not made leaves nothing to count; the phase's single estimate of what that was worth is in its ending.

#### The Delta corps
*1 Jul 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province; Dinh Tuong and Kien Hoa Provinces; Dong Tam base, Dinh Tuong Province · new · EV012-6*
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 July 1966 the IV Corps combined centre passed to Vietnamese command, with Major General Dang Van Quang as commander and an American brigadier as his deputy, the first American in the war to serve as deputy to a Vietnamese commander in a chain of command. Quang was the palace's choice and among the most corrupt generals in the army, and the combined command could only command through him; when the palace replaced him in November with the honest and cautious Major General Nguyen Van Manh, the Delta's combined orders went out on time and were executed slowly. The handover had begun, at the speed of the generals the palace chose.

#### The Commission's cases of 1966–67: the lot numbers, the quartermaster and the Phu Cat lot
*12 Jul 1966 – 20 Dec 1967 · Cao Lanh and the Plain of Reeds; the 9th Division supply point at Sa Dec; Can Tho; a firing position south of Phu Cat; the Bien Hoa arsenal; Cholon; the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · new · EVR05-6*
*Initiative: allied*

Three cases in eighteen months showed the Commission's reach, its limit and what the war's own paper could prove. On 12 July 1966 a 9th Division company overran a VC firing position near Cao Lanh, and its adviser, Captain Robert Halloran, noticed that the 900 spent 5.56mm cases behind it all bore one headstamp lot, which his own battalion had drawn in May. Since 1964 the Muster Service had recorded every unit's lots against its muster roll. The Commission matched lots from eleven firing positions and two caches against the Sa Dec supply point's ledgers and found about 1.4 million rounds issued to units that had never fired them, sold by sampan through two Cholon dealers to a Front purchasing agent at about 18 piasters a round; the point's commander, his clerk, three sergeants and the dealers were convicted in March 1967¹⁸. The leak moved to whole crates bought in the field, and the Front learned to pay more for Chinese 7.62mm, which could not be traced. Khoa told the Board it was the first case in which the war itself had supplied the evidence.

On 6 March 1967 the Commission's own judicial police, with no American present, arrested Brigadier General Phan Duy Khiem, IV Corps' deputy for logistics, with eleven officers. With ghost pay gone from the parade, the graft had moved to rations and fuel: the corps drew rice for 9,000 men it did not have and sold 2.1 million litres of fuel issued to RF companies with no vehicles, about 310 million piasters in two years. When Khiem's patron sought a military court, the Deputy Chief of Mission told Thieu that IV Corps' counterpart funds would be suspended, and Thieu, preparing his candidacy, let the case stand. On 18 April the Special Court sentenced Khiem to eight years. The Commission's reach now ran to the rank below the Directory and no higher, on documentary evidence and with the American lever behind it, and there were not enough clean officers to replace the convicted⁴.

On 14 August 1967 a 22nd Division howitzer burst its tube south of Phu Cat on a Bien Hoa round, killing four of its crew. A Directorate inspector had accepted that lot and three others without the proof-firing the specification required, and had been paid for the time he saved the line; he was convicted in December, and about 40,000 rounds were recalled. The gunners' verdict took longer. Through most of a year II Corps' artillery refused domestic 105mm rounds where it could draw American, and the domestic share of bodies fell from a third to a tenth¹⁴. The officers the courses had made restored it, with every lot proof-fired at Long Thanh under an officer's signature and the 22nd Division's artillery commander invited to watch the first ten; Binh Dinh's gunners went back to Bien Hoa's rounds in the summer of 1968. An arsenal without honest acceptance is worth less than nothing, because a soldier who does not trust his round will not fire it.

The relief at Dien Ban was the first time the enemy attacked an American battalion in the week it changed over. #### The monsoon nights of Binh Dinh and the first Vietnamese gunships
*15 Oct 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Hoai Nhon, Phu My and Tam Quan Districts, Binh Dinh Province; Bien Hoa air base and the Air Training Centre at Nha Trang; Duc Hue District, Hau Nghia Province · new · EV015-4*
*Initiative: both*

By October 1966 the posts within the gunship net were seldom assaulted on clear nights, and the Front's Military Region 5 drew the conclusion its Delta regions had drawn two years earlier: the 2nd Regiment and the Binh Dinh battalions would attack the coast's posts on the nights the north-east monsoon brought the cloud down. On 26–27 October, under a 1,200-foot ceiling, three companies attacked three posts on Route 1 within an hour; the Ghostrider orbited above the cloud under the wing's rule of 1964, dropped two GBU-39s on map coordinates the district read in the rain, and missed by 400 metres, and two posts fell. Through November and December the Front attacked on 18 of the 30 low-cloud nights and on 2 of the 31 clear ones, and took eleven posts and about 300 weapons; the Korean posts, each with a 105mm battery in range, held²². Major General Vinh Loc's figures made two cases, for district guns in reach of every post and for a gunship that could see through cloud.

The second arrived on 1 January 1967: forty-eight AC-130U Spooky IIs with radar that could fire on offsets from a post in any weather, handed to a VNAF that had no one to fly them. Lieutenant Colonel Pham Quang Khiem formed the 81st Gunship Wing, the crews converted in the United States, and the radar technicians were the hardest to find, because the courses were in English and the VNAF had perhaps eighty men who could take them. Mixed American and Vietnamese crews did not work, and sorties became all-Vietnamese. On the night of 19–20 August 1967 the RF post at Hiep Hoa on the Vam Co Dong called in Vietnamese that it was under attack; the 817th Squadron's Spooky II was overhead at 0012, its fire-control officer, Captain Le Minh Tuan, spoke to the post's commander directly, and the 40mm and 105mm broke the assault in the cane through cloud in twenty minutes, for one man lost. It was the first time a Vietnamese post had been defended at night from the air by Vietnamese, and Khiem played the post commander's call on the wing's loudspeakers the next morning. The second squadron was sent to Nha Trang for the coast on Vinh Loc's figures. The A-1 squadrons paid for the wing with a fifth of their avionics technicians and two to three points of readiness²².

#### Operation STEADY HAND

#### The Mo Duc raid and the Phoenix story
*11 Feb – 30 Jun 1967 · Duc Thanh village, Mo Duc District, Quang Ngai Province; Saigon; Hanoi; Washington · new · EV006-4*
*Initiative: both*

The raid at Duc Thanh was what the method became where the night was not held. With little left to find in the held belt, the corps and CORDS wanted the same results from the contested provinces, and Quang Ngai's province chief opened centres in four contested districts against Hieu's advice, on files built from ralliers and the S2's informants. At 0200 on 11 February 1967 a PRU team of 24 went into the house of a Duc Thanh schoolteacher named on two sources, both ralliers from the same hamlet, as the Mo Duc committee's propaganda chief. A man in the house fired a pistol, the team fired back, and the teacher, his wife, his father and two of his children died¹⁰. The propaganda chief was another man, arrested in May; the teacher had been named by a rallier whose family quarrelled with his over a well.

Hanoi had decided in January, for reasons of its own reading of Moscow, the American calendar and the world's opinion, to fight while negotiating, and its propaganda wanted material that would carry abroad. Liberation Radio broadcast the deaths on 19 February as the work of "the American Phoenix murder teams", and by 10 March two American national newspapers had the story, with the programme's name, the CIA's hand in its design and the PRUs' American pay, three years before history's Phoenix stories. The Commission published its finding on 6 April: two ralliers from one feud counted as independent, a warrant signed unread, no capture plan. The team leader was convicted of manslaughter, the judge removed, and raid files withdrawn from fifteen contested districts of I and II Corps until each village had a post and a platoon. On 30 June the national committee published the first audited count, about 14,500 neutralized on warrant since 1965, 800 of them killed, with one claim in ten unsupported in the held belt and one in four in the contested belt¹⁰. It was the first figure on the attack on the infrastructure the newspapers did not have to correct, and it did not undo Duc Thanh. The word Phoenix was in the American vocabulary, and it meant what the Front had said it meant.

#### The list, the devaluation and the 25th Division
*4 Mar – 31 Oct 1967 · Duc Hoa, Hau Nghia Province, and the 25th Division's districts in Hau Nghia and Long An; the Joint General Staff and the Officer Selection Board, Saigon · new · EV007-4*

The 25th Division was the ARVN's worst, and its soldiers' families had paid for the devaluation of June 1966. #### The night of the relief at My Phuoc Tay
*9–10 Mar 1967 · The PF post at Ap My Loi and the canal road to Cai Lay district town, My Phuoc Tay village, Cai Lay District, Dinh Tuong Province · new · EV008-4*
*Initiative: enemy*

By 1967 every PF post in the held belt of Dinh Tuong had a PRC-25 on the district net and a fresh battery every Thursday, and the Front had stopped assaulting them; that spring the provincial unit decided to use the radio against the men it called¹⁴. At 2220 on 9 March a company of the 261st Battalion attacked the post at Long Dinh, 25 kilometres east, and the province's Ghostrider went there, as the call told it to; it was the first diversion the Front made to draw the gunship off²². At 2235 a mortar section put twelve rounds into the post at Ap My Loi, six kilometres north of Cai Lay. Sergeant Le Van Muoi, schooled at Nha Trang and the Dinh Tuong centre, called the district at 2237 with the mortar and an assault forming to the west; at 2241 his listening post on the canal bank reported men moving south in the cane toward the road, and he added that as his third line¹⁵. The district chief, Major Nguyen Thanh Liem, read the first two lines and sent the 247th RF Company north in four trucks at 2250, because the rule said a post that called would have its reaction within the hour.

At 2304 a mine under a culvert three kilometres south of the post lifted the lead truck; its firing circuit was the Republic's own field wire and a pack of Go Vap dry cells. Two companies of the 514th Local Force Battalion fired into the other trucks from the cane for eight minutes and were gone before the battery at Cai Lay had a target. The 247th lost 31 dead and about 40 weapons; the post lost one man wounded¹⁴. The gunship reached the canal road at 2318 and caught one company crossing open paddy, and I put the 514th's dead at about 20–30²².

Liem wrote four rules that spring, and they spread through IV Corps. A post's first call would be for fire, from guns with its concentrations plotted, answered within four minutes. A relief would move by water or at first light, never at night on the one road the Front could predict. A post's report of movement on the relief route stopped the relief until the route was fired on or cleared. And no relief would move by road at night unless a gunship flew the road ahead of it. On 14 June the 247th went north again under a post's call with a Ghostrider two kilometres ahead; its turret found about fifty men in the cane at the same culvert, and the 514th left 26 dead and two recoilless rifles there²². Attacks on posts within the guns' reach fell by a third, and the Front turned to the posts beyond the guns' 11 kilometres and to batteries run down at the end of the week. The rules had a cost the returns did not show: a platoon that could call down the guns had less reason to leave its wire, the PF's night patrols in Cai Lay fell by a third, and the hamlet outside the post was walked at night more often by the Front's tax collectors¹⁴. Ap My Loi held every attack made on it for the rest of the war. Whether the hamlet was the government's after dark, its radio log cannot say.

On 9 May 1967 the President put American pacification, civil and military, under MACV as CORDS, with Robert W. #### Hanoi decides on a general offensive
*6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · changed from history · EV000-37*
*Initiative: enemy*

In the spring of 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive; Thanh died in Hanoi on 6 July, and the plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho, with Giap modifying it reluctantly. From 27 July the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair" arrested hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals, removing the opposition and much of Giap's staff. The premise was that the ARVN was finished and the Saigon government unpopular, and that an offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising; in October the Politburo chose Tet, and the border battles of the autumn drew the Americans away from the cities⁷⁰.

It was taken on history's dates by history's men for history's reasons. The provisions added an argument and weakened the premise. COSVN's reports recorded that the Delta's tax was falling, recruitment in the titled districts harder each season and the "land trick" unexposed, and Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait; COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 said that the government was not unpopular there, and was overruled⁴. A study group of Giap's combat research department had concluded that spring that the puppet infantry and territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat; its three senior officers were arrested in December for whose staff they were on, and the study went into the files with them¹⁸. The offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than history's, against an ARVN that had been better armed than its attackers for four years.

#### ◆ The night check at Tan Thoi Nhi
*14–15 Aug 1967 · Tan Thoi Nhi village, Hoc Mon District, Gia Dinh Province; the Hoc Mon district station and its centre · new · EV016-3*
*Initiative: allied*

The night check embodied what the pistol was for, and this night is the one the Hoc Mon station kept in its file. At 2240 on 14 August 1967 Sergeant Truong Van Lam of the Tan Thoi Nhi post took two constables and a PF fire team down the lane behind the market with the census booklets for eleven households. At the seventh house the door opened on a man with something in his right hand. In history Lam would have had a revolver in one hand and a torch in the other, or no torch at all; the station's report of 1965 counted three farmers shot in Hoc Mon that year by constables who saw a shape and an object at a door at night. Lam's light was on his pistol and his pistol was low. He lit the man for two seconds and saw a sickle, an old man and a frightened face, and the census booklet in the other constable's hand said the old man lived there²⁰.

At the tenth house a younger man whom the booklet did not list went for the back of the hut when the light came on, and stopped when it found him with his hands empty against the wall. He was a courier of the Hoc Mon district committee's finance section, which had governed from the Ho Bo woods since 1966; he was taken alive, talked at the district centre within the week, and gave the centre the route by which the section's collectors entered three villages of the district¹²²⁰. A courier is not a district cadre, and the section was not broken by him. But the old man with the sickle was alive in the morning and the courier was in a cell rather than a ditch, and both of those things were known in Tan Thoi Nhi by noon.

Across the held belt that is the honest measure of the pistol: about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year by 1967 from night shootings by soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadre a year taken alive or killed rather than escaping in the dark, both judgments on rates and at low confidence. The station logs of Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc and Cai Lay show the night checks' yield of strangers falling by about half from 1966 to 1967 as the cadres stopped sleeping in the held villages, which is the check's success and reads as its decline²⁰.

#### The constitution and the presidential election
*3 Sep 1967 · Republic of Vietnam · historical · EV000-38*
The Constituent Assembly elected in September 1966 produced a constitution, promulgated on 1 April 1967. In the presidential election of 3 September 1967, held with the Senate election, Thieu ran for president with Ky as his running mate, after the generals forced Ky to stand aside. Eleven tickets ran. Thieu and Ky won 34.83 percent, 1,649,561 votes; the runner-up, Truong Dinh Dzu, a lawyer campaigning on negotiations with the NLF, won 17.26 percent, 817,120 votes. The reported turnout was 83.17 percent of 5,853,251 registered voters. There were credible reports of soldiers showing voters how to mark their ballots, and of a secret military committee that would govern behind the elected government; on 10 August 57 members of the US House had condemned the conduct of the campaign. Dzu was later imprisoned. Thieu was inaugurated on 31 October and the lower house was elected on 22 October⁷¹.

The election gave the Republic of Vietnam an elected government for the first time since 1963 and, under Thieu, the stability that the previous four years had lacked. It did not give it legitimacy in the countryside: two-thirds of the voters chose someone else, and a civilian peace candidate had come second.

#### How the phase ends
At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 men in Vietnam and has lost 9,378 killed in the year¹. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on the North⁷², the Trail carries more than ever, and MACV and the CIA dispute whether the enemy numbers 300,000 or 430,000, a dispute over whether the guerrillas and the infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all. Westmoreland tells the National Press Club on 21 November that "the end begins to come into view"⁷⁰. McNamara is leaving. Saigon has an elected government under Thieu, and in Hanoi the Politburo has set Tet for the General Offensive and General Uprising. Each side is certain the other is weaker than it is.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts, and its committees govern from the Ho Bo woods, the Rung Sat and Memot by courier, with collections at a sixth to an eighth of 1964's; its legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less⁸. About 820,000 hectares are titled, rent has ended wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general⁴. Phung Hoang has an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, a fifth of them committee members, and the word Phoenix has been a press story since March¹⁰. The infrastructure is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, which is still most of the contested countryside.

The enemy's supply is measured, the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count and the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what his forces in the South need¹⁷. The bombers have learned that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot, and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle. Over the North the theater has had the campaign it asked for and the result it predicted, about 40–90 fewer American aircraft lost than historically, and no hearings on restrictions that were never drawn⁴³.

The armies enter 1968 better in the fights they are given. The American army has had no rifle crisis and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights and in the nights after them; its battalions rotate as units with staggered reliefs that cost 15,000–25,000 spaces in theater, and about 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps¹⁰. The ARVN has about four in five battalions under officers the board chose, its NCOs schooled and its privates trained, and the drill sergeants returning 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year⁵. **About two-fifths to a half of history's ARVN battalions broken in 1966–67 were not broken in this war. The judgment is low-confidence, and the true figure is more likely near two-fifths than a half**, because the officer reforms, the basic course and the NCO schooling each claimed a share from overlapping cases and none checked its cases against the others'²⁶. None of it shows in a return. The enemy declined the fights that would have shown it, attacked less what kept its routine, and moved his war to the mine in the lowlands and to the unit that still rode. The corps and the generals are what they were, and the palace has a file on the board's officers while COSVN has had every list the board published.

The factories produce what the domestic share buys, short of power and, after Phu Cat, short of their gunners' trust. Every post in the held belt has a radio, and where the guns reach, the first call is for fire, and the post holds itself better than the hamlet around it¹⁴. The gunships have taken the clear night over the posts of the held belt; the coast's monsoon nights are still the Front's, and the first Vietnamese gunship squadron is flying. The combined command has one corps under a Vietnamese commander and three under American deputies, moving at the speed of the generals the palace chooses¹². The population of the South, the object of the war, is still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

### Mechanism

Phase II shows the pistol's two real uses and its one real cost, each in a dated event. The tunnel teams of Cu Chi and the Ho Bo woods go down with the light on the muzzle and the left hand free, and Specialist Ortiz at Phu Hoa Dong on 19 April 1966 fires first at four metres because he can see. They lose about a third fewer men underground in two years, about 6–9 lives, at low confidence, and they take more prisoners alive from the first galleries in 1966 and fewer in 1967, when the Front stops defending them.

The strongest positive effect is shown at Tan Thoi Nhi on 14–15 August 1967, where a police sergeant lights an old man with a sickle and does not fire, and lights a courier against a wall and takes him alive. Across the held belt that is about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year by 1967 from night shootings by soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadres a year taken alive or killed rather than escaping in the dark²⁰. The end vector carries the second figure inside the range for identifying the infrastructure, now about 1,650–3,300 more listed cadres a year than history's, and the American dead of 1967 at about 8,435–9,060 against history's 9,378, the pistol's part being about 10–15 (P2 end vector).

The cost is shown at Cai Be on 2–3 March 1966: a Glock from the Long Dinh post, its light working on stolen cells, picks the district chief from his wife in his bedroom doorway. Four of the twenty-one chiefs killed in 1965–66 die this way. The number does not change, and what absorbs the difference is named in the event: COSVN's decision and the sections' knowledge of a chief's house set the rate, not their pistols.

The Mo Duc raid of 11 February 1967, where a PRU team shot a schoolteacher's family after a man in the house fired a pistol, stands as written: the PRUs are armed by the CIA and are outside the provision, which is why the raids that killed the most in the contested belt are not touched. The removed M1911A1 and revolvers are named in no other event of any phase. In the Delta, Phase II's effect is in Dinh Tuong (Cai Be) and the upper provinces; in the Saigon belt it is at Hoc Mon and Cu Chi; on the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps there is none worth stating, because the night check there ran in few villages; in the Highlands, none.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front answered the tunnel teams by leaving the first galleries empty and wired and the held villages by sleeping outside them, both cheap habits. The night checks' yield of strangers fell by about half from 1966 to 1967 in Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc and Cai Lay, the check's success reading as decline.

One decision in Phase II was the enemy's own. In May 1966 the Party committee of Military Region 2, which ran the upper Delta for COSVN, ordered its province security sections to cut their killing of officials by about half for the rest of the year and to put their men into tax collection. Nothing this command did drove it. The region's finances had collapsed with the price of rice after the devaluation of June 1966, and the committee's own reports said its local forces were going unfed²⁰. The order was obeyed slowly and with argument: the Dinh Tuong section, flush with captured pistols and proud of Cai Be, killed two more village chiefs in June before it complied. The effect was that the second half of 1966 in the upper Delta saw fewer officials killed than COSVN's August directive of 1965 had demanded, for a reason that was all rice and no war.

**Chain review.** C004-1, written by memorandum 004, ran: the PF platoons beside the police posts draw modern carbines → posts with their platoon hold and the Front stops assaulting them → the Front turns to the constables on the road, the CIDG camps and the militia with the handed-down Garands. ### Downstream

Phase III inherits a police and a territorial force that use their lights for two seconds, a Front that holds about a thousand of the Republic's pistols and has learned to fire at lamps, and tunnel complexes whose first galleries are empty. None of that changes Tet. Phase IV's handover passes the pistols to the RVNAF and the police with nothing to Vietnamise but the 123A cells, which come from America and keep coming until 1975. The values Phase II moves are small enough to be absorbed in each later phase's ranges. Phase II's chapter now stands at about 16,000 words, above the 15,000-word limit, and is flagged for the consolidation run at the next checkpoint.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

Tet was decided by whether the district towns and the cities held against battalions, and a pistol defends neither. Loan's pistol at the Saigon street on 1 February is a Glock with a light rather than his revolver, and the photograph's effect on the American public is history's. Phase II's moved values do not alter this phase: the ten to fifteen American dead the pistol saved in 1967 and the hundred-odd cadres taken alive are inside ranges far wider than they are, and neither touches the offensive's timing, which the files never held.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

The pistol needs nothing transferred: three-quarters of it was Vietnamese from 1964. Its one dependence, the American 123A cell, is met throughout these years, and the night check and the warrant raid run as in Phase II in a held belt that is wider. The decay of American discipline in 1969–71 is a matter of morale and the draft, which a sidearm does not touch.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The Easter Offensive was a conventional war of tanks, artillery and sieges at An Loc, Kontum and Quang Tri. Nothing in it turned on a sidearm. The territorial war behind the front, where the pistol works, was quieter in these months because the Front's local forces were spent supporting the offensive, so the pistol had even less to do.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The end was decided in Washington, in the palace and by the PAVN's corps, not at a door. The pistol's only exposure here is the cell supply: the 1974 appropriation keeps the 123A cells coming at a reduced rate, the lights go dark in the posts of the north first, and the pistol stays a.45 with glowing sights. It cannot affect the critical path of March 1975 or the date of the fall.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, this provision has changed four things, each small, and none of them the war's course.

First, the sidearm. In history the American and Vietnamese armies carried worn M1911A1s from the Second World War, never enough of them, and the police carried six-shot revolvers; Americans bought their own pistols. Here every holder carries a Glock 21 with night sights and a light from 1964, about 60,000–65,000 by the end of 1964 and about 235,000–245,000 at the peak.

Second, lives. About 10–15 fewer American dead in 1967 and perhaps 60–90 over the war, most of them tunnel clearers and men in the last metres of overrun positions, at low confidence. About 150–200 fewer South Vietnamese civilians shot at night by their own soldiers and police each year from 1967. Against these, about 8–10 more men killed by negligent discharges in 1964²⁰.

Third, the infrastructure. About 100–150 more listed cadres a year taken alive or killed at their doors rather than escaping in the dark, by 1967²⁰. The Front's apparatus was driven out of the held villages by the police, the centres and the raids of memoranda 002, 006, 012 and 014; the pistol helped at the door and nowhere else.

Fourth, the enemy's armoury. About 1,000–1,200 of the Republic's pistols in the Front's hands by the end of 1965, at low confidence, and four of the district chiefs killed in 1965–66 shot with them²⁰.

The margin is modest and stated as such. No battle, post, offensive or month has changed.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The cumulative force makes rung (b) reachable and no higher, and this memorandum does not move it. The Republic survives while American combat forces are present, and in this war it survives past 30 April 1975 because of decisions taken in Washington and Saigon, not because of anything that holds a door. Rung (c) is not reached because the insurgency is not contained in every corps region and Saigon does not stand with American air power available at the end, and the distance between (c) and (d) is the whole war, as it has been in every memorandum.

**The date of the fall.** The Republic's fall is expected between February and May 1976, as the record has it. The mechanisms pulling it earlier are the PAVN's five corps assembled north of Saigon and the Republic's loss of half its Highland force in 1975; those pulling it later are the appropriation near the 1974 level, the Vietnamese gunships over the Delta's posts, and III and IV Corps held intact. The appropriation dominates, because it decided whether Thieu stood in March 1975. The pistol pulls in neither direction: it cannot touch a corps, a vote or the rains.

**The running sum of American lives.** The latest entry, POSCP04-1, puts the American lives saved by the provisions at about 4,100–9,500, a judgment on the record's rates. This memorandum adds about 60–90 over the war, on a rate of about 10–15 a year in 1966–69 and fewer before and after²⁰. The sum is about 4,150–9,600, about 7–16 percent of the war's American dead, and it still does not move tolerance.

**The countryside.** By region: the Saigon belt (Hoc Mon, Cu Chi) and the upper Delta (Dinh Tuong) carry the whole effect; the coast of I and II Corps and the Highlands none worth stating. The pistol moves no rice and no piaster beyond a black-market price; the one dated event in this memorandum that turns on rice is the enemy's own, Military Region 2's order of May 1966 to put its security sections into tax collection.

**Unjudged dependencies.** The memorandum's evidence does not bear on ARVN night operations as a share of ambushes and patrols, on outpost defence or on the RVNAF's maintenance at a scale that would justify a judgment in units; the pistol changes how a night check or a breached post ends, not how often a unit goes out after dark. ## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- Every American, Vietnamese soldier and policeman who carries a sidearm carries a better one, with twice the rounds, sights that glow and a light on the gun; for the constable, who had six rounds and a torch, it is a large improvement in his own safety at a door at night.
- Fewer people are shot by mistake at night: about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year from 1967, and about 100–150 more listed cadres a year taken alive at their doors rather than lost in the dark. In a held village that is a government that can be trusted after dark a little more.
- The tunnel teams of Cu Chi and the Ho Bo woods go down able to see, and lose about a third fewer men underground in 1966–67; the American dead fall by about 10–15 in 1967 and perhaps 60–90 over the war.
- The Front holds about 1,000–1,200 of the Republic's best pistols by the end of 1965, and its killers use some of them on district chiefs and village officials.
- About 100–120 more negligent discharges and 8–10 more dead in 1964, mostly police, and four platoons ambushed on their own lights before the habit was trained out.

### What has not changed

- Who holds the hamlets after dark: the pistol adds nothing to night security and works only where it already exists.
- How many posts fell and how many constables died in 1964–65, and how many district chiefs the Front killed in 1965–66: the rates were set by assaults at rifle range and by COSVN's decisions, not by sidearms.
- The main-force war, Tet, the Easter Offensive and the end of the war, none of which turned on a pistol; the date of the fall stays between February and May 1976.
- The police's long arms (E05), and the weapons of the CIDG, the PRUs, the militia and the People's Self-Defense Force, which the provision does not reach.
- The end state, rung (b).

### Trajectory Log entry

Memorandum 016 replaced every pistol and revolver in American and Vietnamese hands, and gave every policeman in the Republic, a Glock 21 with glowing sights and a light on its rail, from 1964. It reached the bottom of the war and nowhere else. The constable's night check became one he could do and believe: at Tan Thoi Nhi in August 1967 a police sergeant lit an old man with a sickle and did not fire, and lit a courier against a wall and took him alive. The tunnel teams of Cu Chi went down able to see and lost a third fewer men. The first season cost ambushes sprung on lights used as torches and police discharges at the holster, and every post the Front overran gave its killers the Republic's best pistols, one of which shot the district chief of Cai Be in his bedroom. About 150–200 fewer civilians were shot at night each year, and perhaps 60–90 Americans lived. No post, battle or date moved, and the end state stays at rung (b).
