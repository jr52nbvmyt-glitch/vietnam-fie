# Study: US and ARVN small arms and individual weapons against the enemy's and against history (`studies/us-and-arvn-small-arms-and-individual-weapons-ag.md`)
*Written ST02 · reflects the record after memo 015 · last folded — · tags: ARVN.effectiveness, RFPF.effectiveness, ARVN.self_sufficiency, ENEMY.engagement_control, FIX.capability, US.casualties, VC.main_force, VC.local_force, INTL.communist_aid*

## Summary — the state of the area now

In this war the American and South Vietnamese infantryman carried, from the first week of January 1964, better individual weapons than his enemy, and better than history's soldier carried at any point in the war. Every rifleman of the US forces, the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers, the Vietnamese Marines and the Regional and Popular Forces carried the M4A1 carbine with the improved upper receiver and the Aimpoint CompM5 red-dot sight, firing M855A1; every regular US and ARVN squad carried a belt-fed M249 in place of its BAR or automatic rifles, and a marksman with the suppressed M110A2 and a 1–6x scope¹. Against them the Front and the PAVN carried what history gave them: the Type 56 and AK-47 assault rifles, the SKS, the RPD light machine gun, the B40 and later the B41 rocket launcher, the K-50 sub-machine gun in the hands of guerrillas and couriers, and, in the local forces and guerrilla platoons, whatever they had captured or bought². The record's verdict on this, stated in memorandum 004 and never reversed, is that the small arms changed how the fights the enemy accepted went and never which fights he accepted. Everything in this study is a working-out of that sentence over twelve years.

The margin over history was large, real and concentrated. It was largest for the territorials. Historically the RF and PF fought the five years in which the countryside was lost with M1 carbines, Garands and BARs against the AK-47, and drew the M16 only in 1969¹. In this war the Popular Forces platoon beside a village police post at Long Huu in Kien Hoa met a local-force company's assault in November 1964 with fire that did not pause to reload, and the assault broke on the bund. It was large for the ARVN's regular infantry, which historically waited until 1968 for its M16s, M60s and M79s while the enemy's infiltrators arrived with automatic weapons it could not match³. And it was a real but modest improvement for the Americans, whose historical M16 fired the same calibre and whose 1967 reliability crisis was fixable and fixed⁴. In this war the Marines on Hill 881 South did not die beside jammed rifles, and no congressional subcommittee investigated the rifle.

Its weight on the war, measured honestly, is smaller than its weight in any firefight. The record's judgment is that the small arms saved about 1,000–2,500 American and about 10,000–25,000 South Vietnamese lives over the war. Those are judgments built on rates, not counts, and the record repeats them only as such. The battles of 1965 that history records as defeats were still defeats. Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai were lost at a smaller price, and no battalion of the general reserve was wiped out; they were lost because the enemy chose the field and the corps fed its reliefs in piecemeal. The enemy answered the rifle within a season in every way the record names. He stopped assaulting rearmed posts and attacked those the rearmament did not reach: the constables, the CIDG camps, the hamlet militia and the self-defence groups carrying the Garands the army handed in. He moved his war from the rifle assault to the mortar, the sapper and above all the mine. He drew between 10,000 and 30,000 of the new carbines from overrun posts, deserters and ambushed battalions by the end of 1965, and fired the Republic's own 5.56mm in them⁵.

The historical army caught up from 1968, which closes the largest part of the margin in the second half of the war. By mid-1968 every historical ARVN infantry battalion carried the M16, the M60, the M79 and the LAW, and the territorials followed in 1969³. From then on the remaining difference was the squad gun, the marksman and four years' practice. The last chapter of the area is the one memorandum 004 itself named as its counter-directional finding. Ten years of fighting with the belt-fed gun and abundant American ammunition built an army that fought by volume. The ration of 1974 then cut the squad gun to about 200 rounds a month, which an M249 fires in two minutes. In this war the Republic's own arsenal at Bien Hoa loaded about two-fifths of its rifle cartridges by 1974, and the larger fiscal 1975 appropriation let the Joint General Staff double that ration in March 1974. Neither touched the 130mm guns that decided the artillery duel, and the weapons that were better than the PAVN's in every fight of 1974 mattered less in 1974 than in any year since 1964.

Set against the enemy, then, this war's infantry was better armed at every range that mattered, as follows:

- **At 10–50 metres**, in the meeting engagement and the ambush where most contacts were fought, the carbine with a red dot outshot the AK-47 in the first seconds¹.
- **At 300–800 metres**, in the paddy, on the coastal plain and from firebase perimeters, the marksman killed the enemy's machine gunners, spotters and leaders at ranges neither the AK nor the historical M16 could answer: at Ba Gia, on the 881s and at Thuong Duc.
- **In the squad**, the belt-fed gun gave a base of fire against the enemy's RPD that the BAR, reloading every twenty rounds, never had.

The enemy kept every advantage that was not a rifle. He chose where and when to fight. He kept the B40 and B41 against bunkers and vehicles, the mortar against the post, the mine on the path and, from 1972, the 130mm gun beyond the range of anything the Republic could fire. The area's value to the war is real and bounded. It made the Republic's soldiers harder to kill and harder to overrun where they stood and fought. It did not find the enemy, did not make a platoon go out at night, did not reach the police, the CIDG or the militia, and did not move the end state, which the record puts at rung (b), a reprieve of about a year, for reasons decided far above the squad.

## P1 · Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The rearmament arrived on the campaign's first working day. MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez* berthed at the Khanh Hoi wharves in Saigon on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines and their CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249s, 320 M110A2s and about 380 tonnes of ammunition. MACV called the issue Operation IRON GATE for its first rule: no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate in exchange. Its second rule was the zeroing range. Every company drew its carbines by name against its muster roll, fired each one at 25 metres until the dot sat on the point of aim, and handed in its Garands, M1 and M2 carbines, BARs and Thompsons before it left. Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn of the new-equipment training team set the course at five days for an infantry company and three for a Popular Forces platoon, and refused to shorten it when the corps commanders asked⁵.

The order of issue followed the danger. The 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions and the territorials of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac provinces drew first; the general reserve's Airborne, Marine and Ranger battalions next; I and II Corps from April. Khanh's coup of 30 January cost the issue nine days and about a fifth of the first half-year's 2.4 million training man-days, in units recalled to the province towns before they had zeroed. By 30 June about 310,000 carbines were out, about 180,000 of them with the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles in the regular infantry, and the three-week marksmen's course at Quang Trung had graduated its first 1,100⁵. The rearmament did not stand alone. The recruits who carried it had, since 1957, met the M4A1 and its sight on the ranges of a ten-week course in their fourth week, and the men the battalions would make gunners had met the M249 there too⁶. The effect was visible in an afternoon. A PF platoon at Can Duoc that had hit a 100-metre target about once in four shots with its Garands put most of its rounds into it by the third morning.

About 290,000 old weapons came back through the gate. A third of them went, over MACV's objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia, who were not covered by the rearmament⁵. That decision mattered more than anyone thought in 1964. It drew the line along which the enemy would fight the rest of the territorial war.

The enemy of 1964 was itself mid-rearmament. Historically the Front's main-force regiments were being re-equipped in 1964–65 with the 7.62x39mm family of Chinese and Soviet weapons, and the infiltrators from the North brought the AK-47, the RPD and the B40 that the ARVN could not match until 1968³². In this war that re-equipment arrived partly by sea, and TIDE TABLE saw it. The trawler run ashore at Vung Ro on 19 February 1964 had Chinese Type 56 carbines and assault rifles, B40 launchers, 82mm rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm in its after hold⁷. The Front's Long An committee called the government's new carbines "a trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty" in February and ordered its companies to capture one for study. The first was taken at Rach Kien on 22 March.

At the post wall the change was decisive in the first year. Of the posts in the held belt that had their PF platoon and a reaction force within an hour, about nine in ten held with the rifles against about four in five on the old weapons. With the six-week territorial course behind the rifles, the record puts the figure at about nineteen in twenty. The night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu is the area's founding case. A company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140. The post's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and its platoon had zeroed them in March. The first assault, which on the Front's experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause. Corporal Pham Van Tho had kept a third of the ammunition for the second assault, as his course had taught him, and the second was not pressed⁵⁸.

The Front learned faster than MACV expected. From December 1964 its district companies stopped assaulting posts with their platoons. They went for the posts settled without them, the constables on the roads, the CIDG camps and the militia with the handed-in Garands. Against posts that held they used the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts⁵. The rifles moved the enemy's targets before they moved anything else, and that is the shape of the area for the rest of the war.

In the main-force battles the squad weapons changed the losses and not the results. At Binh Gia on 31 December 1964 the 4th Marine Battalion was ambushed in the rubber at 20–40 metres by the 271st Regiment, armed with Type 56 and SKS rifles, RPDs and recoilless rifles, and lost its commander in the first minutes as history's did. Its squad guns, three to a platoon, put a base of fire into the trees that BARs could not have sustained. The companies closed into two perimeters within a quarter of an hour and fought out at dusk with most of their wounded. The battalion lost about 60–70 killed rather than 122, and about 50–60 once the sergeants' part is counted. At Ba Gia in May 1965 three companies of the 1st Battalion, 51st Regiment held a ridge with a squad gun in every squad and a marksman in most; the 3rd Marines held a moat line through the night with nine M249s under illumination, their marksmen killing the 40th Battalion's machine-gun teams at 300–400 metres. At Dong Xoai in June the 7th Airborne Battalion's three surviving companies turned their squad guns into the rubber and about 270 men fought their way out where history's 150 did, and about 290 once the sergeants' part is counted⁵⁹. Each of the three is still a defeat. Each also shows the area's first cost. At Ba Gia about 230 individual weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s with their sights, and the 2nd Division's local battalions were firing 5.56mm taken from the dead within the month¹⁰.

Where the rearmament did not reach, nothing changed. The CIDG at Nam Dong and Dong Xoai carried the weapons they had always carried and broke as they had broken historically, and the Rangers at Song Be, with M4A1s and M249s since February 1964, took their losses in the first hour to mortars, recoilless rifles and sappers inside the wire. By the end of 1965 the Front held between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's new carbines, the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, each needing the Republic's own ammunition⁵.

## P2 · Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is the period in which the margin over history was widest, and it is widest for the Americans in one particular. Historically the M16 reached American units in 1965–66 without chromed chambers, cleaning kits or instructions, on a powder that fouled it, and the stoppages that followed led to a House subcommittee's hearings from May to August 1967 and to the fixes of the M16A1⁴. In this war every American battalion that landed carried the M4A1 with its 2026 upper, and the subject did not exist. On the Hill Fights at Khe Sanh, 24 April–11 May 1967, the armourers of the 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 3rd Marines recorded stoppages at about the M14's rate. The marksmen on the saddle between the 881s killed machine gunners in the bunker apertures at 400–500 metres, and the record puts Marine losses at about 125 killed and 400 wounded against 155 and 425 historically⁵. The bunkers fell, as historically, to 2,000 lb bombs, 8-inch howitzers, the LAW and the flamethrower. No rifle breaks a bunker (P004a negative specification).

The squad gun was built for the Ia Drang and helped less there than it should have. At X-Ray, 14–16 November 1965, the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry had two M249s in each squad and an M110A2 in most, where historically it had the M16 and the M60 at platoon; the perimeter held as it held historically, with American losses at about 70 killed against 79. At Albany on 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry was ambushed in column in tall grass. The head of the column, with Alpha Company and the command group, formed a perimeter in the trees within minutes. The companies strung out behind were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, and the record's judgment is exact: "a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends". The B3 Front's after-action study added a sentence history's did not need: the Americans now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", so the belt-grabbing assault must be made from closer still, at night, or not at all⁵. That is the enemy reading the weapon correctly, and choosing.

The American big-unit battles of the phase are not adjusted for the rifles. Starlite, Hastings, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City went as history's did, because who attacked whom, where and when decided their losses far more than the squad weapons did¹¹. The Australians at Long Tan did not carry the M4A1; the provision did not cover them. The record's position is that a difference in rifles was never going to show in counts this coarse, and this study agrees with it.

For the ARVN the phase showed what the squad weapons did in the hands of trained men under schooled sergeants. On 16 November 1966 at Hau My in Cai Be, the 3rd Battalion, 11th Regiment's lead company walked into the 263rd Main Force Battalion's L-shaped ambush on a canal dike, with an RPD on each arm and a 57mm recoilless rifle at the corner, at 40–60 metres. Captain Nguyen Huu Tam and both his forward platoon leaders were hit in two minutes. Sergeant First Class Tran Van Loc's squads went off the dike on the side away from the long arm, each squad leader putting his M249 on the tree line while the riflemen crawled, and the 105mm battery at Cai Be fired eleven minutes after the ambush opened⁹. The company lost 14 killed of about 120 and brought out every weapon; on the historical course the record expects 40–60 killed and the squad guns and radio left on the dike. That is the squad gun at its best: a base of fire that holds an enemy while the rest of the system arrives.

At Bau Bang on 21 May 1966 it was the territorials. Two RF companies riding trucks on Route 13 met the 272nd Regiment's ambush, got off on the side away from the fire, returned it in pairs from the ditch, and the rear company put its two M249s into the tree line in the first two minutes⁸. The RF lost about 32 dead rather than about 40. The 272nd's notebook of January 1967 drew the conclusion that its ambushes "must kill in the first volley or not be laid". That event, and STILL WATER's RF companies "with M4A1 carbines and their M249s" in March 1965, put squad guns in Regional Force hands, which memorandum 004 says they never received; the study raises this below (Q-1). The broader point does not depend on it. The weapons made the ambush dearer and did not make the convoy walk its flanks.

The rearmed territorials were good enough to be borrowed, and that is the phase's perverse effect. On 4 April 1966 IV Corps formed 34 RF companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu into six mobile groups for the U Minh. They fought well; near Thoi Binh in July the RF marksmen and squad fire killed about 40 of a U Minh 10th Battalion company crossing a canal for nine of their own dead⁵. Behind them, 23 PF outposts and six police posts fell between May and August, against seven outposts in the same months of 1965, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s. The Front's U Minh command "had not needed to plan anything". The rule of 1967, that RF companies belonged to the province chief, came out of it.

The phase also produced the area's one clean case of the weapons as evidence. On 12 July 1966 a 9th Division company picked up about 900 bright 5.56mm cases behind a VC firing position near Cao Lanh, all of one lot, a lot Captain Robert Halloran's own battalion had drawn in May⁵. Every crate under IRON GATE carried its lot, and the Muster Service recorded lots issued against muster rolls. The Commission traced about 1.4 million rounds from the Sa Dec ammunition supply point to two Cholon dealers and a Front purchasing agent at about 18 piasters a round, and the Special Court convicted seven men in March 1967. The leak moved rather than stopped: the dealers bought whole crates from units in the field, and the Front's agents paid more for untraceable Chinese 7.62mm than for the Republic's 5.56mm.

The enemy's own decisions on the area in this phase were his own. The Quang Da Special Zone's directive for 1966, captured near Dien Ban in 1967, concluded that its companies lost more than they gained in firefights with units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds. It ordered every lowland guerrilla platoon to make mines and booby traps its principal work⁸. In Dien Ban the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines lost about two-thirds of its dead and four-fifths of its wounded to devices in its first year, against about two-fifths before the directive. The rifles had won the firefight, and the enemy stopped offering it. In Hanoi, in the spring of 1967, a study group of the PAVN General Staff's combat research department concluded that the ARVN and its territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat. Its three senior officers were arrested in December in the Revisionist Anti-Party Affair for reasons unconnected with the study, and the offensive was planned on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve⁵.

## P3 · Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

Tet is where the margin over history was tested at scale, and where it showed most plainly in the district towns. Historically the first wave penetrated nearly all of the 64–72 district towns attacked; in this war the attackers penetrated about two-thirds. The towns they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, in pairs, with ammunition kept for the second rush, as at Long Huu four years before. At Cai Lay on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st. It had its own carbines and one squad gun lent by the 7th Division, and at 0600 it still had about a third of its basic load. The battalion had planned to be through the town by 0400⁵⁸.

The record divides the ARVN's saving in the first wave among the provisions. It puts the ARVN's killed at about 2,150 against 2,788 historically, and credits the rifles with about 290 of the difference, the officers with about 200 and the course with about 150⁵¹²⁸. More of those torn by a B40 fragment or a burst of AK-47 fire were alive at dawn, because the man beside them had a tourniquet. None of this touched Saigon, Hue or the Highlands, where the attackers were main-force units and the fighting was decided by numbers, walls and weather.

At Hue the squad weapons made the city cheaper to retake and no faster. The Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound on the first night with M4A1s and four squad guns, a small change because the Hac Bao was among the few units historically rearmed by then. Across the river the Marines had two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman who could put rounds into an upper-floor window at 200 metres from cover. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and public health building between 4 and 6 February. What took the blocks was what took them historically: 106mm recoilless rifles, Ontos, gas and men going through walls. The rifles alone would have made the dead about 410 ARVN and 215 American against 458 and 250 historically; with the combined command's shorter battle the record puts them at about 370 and 200⁵¹³.

The general mobilization of June 1968 was where history's rearmament ran out of rifles, and where this war's did not. Historically the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the RF and PF raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70³. In this war about 260,000 M4A1s were already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June, and the new RF companies and PF platoons drew them from their first month⁵. The old weapons went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force. Its members had a few days of instruction from the district chiefs, as historically, because no course reached them.

The enemy's third offensive went exactly there. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee, planning the August offensive after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, ordered its subregions to avoid the RF and PF posts that had held in February and May and to strike the new self-defence groups. From 17–18 August through September the local-force companies attacked about 30 groups in Duc Hue and Trang Bang and overran about two-thirds. Across Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong about 600–900 members were killed and about 1,500–2,000 of their weapons taken, almost all of them Garands and carbines of 1950 that the RVNAF had handed in since 1964⁸. The line the IRON GATE depots drew in 1964, between the rearmed and the handed-down, was the line along which the enemy fought in 1968. Where a PF platoon came out to the group's flare, as at Rach Kien on 2–3 September, the attack broke at the hamlet gate. The Can Duoc district chief then sent his PF sergeants to teach the groups the first ten minutes, and Long An's province chief made it the rule on 24 October; in November and December the Front overran four of eleven groups attacked in Long An, against about two in three in Hau Nghia in the autumn⁹.

The phase ends with the gap closing from history's side. As the record puts it, the RVNAF "is armed throughout with weapons the historical army was still waiting for, a gap now closing from the historical side as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969" (P3, How the phase ends)⁵. The PAVN, by 1968, was carrying the AK-47 throughout its infantry, and the guerrilla and local forces were moving from the SKS and captured weapons toward the AK as the North's supply allowed². In this war they also carried the M4A1s they had taken, and fired whatever 5.56mm the leak and the battlefield gave them.

## P4 · Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV is the phase in which the area stops being a difference, and the record says so plainly. Memorandum 004's null for the phase reads that "the rearmament does not bite in Phase IV because the historical army catches up with it here"¹. Historically the Improvement and Modernization programme of 1969 re-equipped the RVNAF with the M16 for the regulars and then the territorials, the M60, the M79 and the LAW, with the RF and PF rearmament expected complete by the end of 1969 and about 350 mobile advisory teams sent to help the territorials use it³. In this war the RVNAF had held its modern rifles since 1964, and the 1969 rearmament had nothing to replace. The chapter's account of Nixon's first months, EV000-56, still describes the historical re-equipment unchanged; the study raises the inconsistency below (Q-2).

What remained of the margin in 1969–71 was what memorandum 004 named: the squad gun, the marksman and practice. The historical ARVN squad of 1969 had the M16 and an M79 and the platoon had its M60. This war's ARVN squad had the M4A1 with a sight, a belt-fed gun of its own and a marksman, and men who had trained on all three for five years. The American squad, by 1969, differed from history's mainly in the squad gun and the marksman, since the historical M16A1 of 1969 was a reliable rifle⁴.

The incursion into Cambodia in May 1970 and Lam Son 719 in February–March 1971 were decided by the frontier constraints, by command failures at the landing zones, and by Washington's timetable; the record holds no event in which a squad weapon changed either, and this study finds none (memo 004 §4.P4)¹. The area's real activity in the phase was below the battles. In 1970–71 the territorial post's call passed from American to Vietnamese guns, the district artillery platoons that began with Lam Van Thien's request after Chuong Thien in 1966. From 1969 the VNAF's AC-130U wing answered the posts the Ghostriders had answered. That is why the study holds the territorial posts a few points above history in 1971 when the rifle no longer is. The margin had moved from the man on the wall to what he could call.

The enemy's rifles in the phase were, on the record, his historical rifles. By 1970 the communist inventory was mostly standardized on the 7.62x39mm family even at the village guerrilla level. The Front's local forces were rebuilding from the losses of 1968 with northern fillers and with the North's weapons. The record does not say how many of the M4A1s taken in 1964–68 were still in use, or whether the leak of 5.56mm that EV004-3 drove from depot to field went on; the study raises that gap (Q-4). One effect of the phase can be stated with some confidence. The rifles the Front had captured from 1964 to 1968 had cost the Republic posts and men, but they did not become the Front's standard weapon. A guerrilla platoon with Republic carbines depended on Republic ammunition. A local force that could draw 7.62x39mm from the North, as it could by 1970, had less reason to keep them²¹⁴.

The phase is also the last in which the American soldier carried the 2026 kit in numbers. American infantry strength fell from the 1968–69 peak through 1971, and with it the share of the war's small-arms fights that were American. The record's American losses for 1971 are unjudged, and the study leaves them so for the reasons given under Judgments. Nothing in the record shows a small-arms difference in the American battles of the drawdown that history's M16A1 would not also have produced. The marksman and the squad gun are the remaining difference, and the record gives them no event in the phase.

## P5 · Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The Easter Offensive was decided by armour, artillery, B-52s and the quality of corps command, and the area touches none of them. Memorandum 004's null for the phase is the record's judgment and this study endorses it. Quang Tri fell because its defence collapsed at the top. An Loc and Kontum held because the bombers held them. A belt-fed gun in each squad at An Loc made every assault on the perimeter dearer, and the PAVN answered with 130mm guns, which no rifle reaches¹. By 1972 the historical ARVN carried the M16 and the M60 and the gap left was too narrow to change any battle's result.

The individual weapon that mattered in 1972 was not a rifle. At An Loc on 13 April the 9th Division's first armoured assault was stopped in the streets by defenders firing M72 LAWs at close range, as historically, and tank-killing teams were formed from that day (P5, EV000-76). The LAW was historical equipment for both armies by 1972 and nothing in the record changes it. In this war the defenders of An Loc were also fed by the B-52J and the coordinate cell, which made the siege the PAVN's worst battle. The squads on the perimeter held a position that was being defended chiefly from the air.

On the enemy's side, 1972 was the year the PAVN brought the war's heaviest individual weapons into the South with its divisions: the B41 against bunkers and vehicles, the SA-7 against aircraft, and the 12.7mm and 14.5mm machine guns in numbers. The SA-7 matters to this area at one remove. It drove the VNAF's slow aircraft above the altitudes at which they could support infantry in contact, which is where the squad's own weapons had to carry more of the fight. In this war the record bounds the VNAF's gunships by the same missile.

For the territorials the year is barely in the record. The posts of the Delta and the Saigon belt fought the Front's local forces as they had in 1971, with the call for fire and the gunships behind them. The record holds no territorial event in 1972 that measures them, and the study carries its 1971 range for the posts unchanged at low confidence rather than invent a movement. That is a genuine gap in the area's record, not a quiet year. The territorial war under the Easter Offensive is unrecorded in this war as it is thinly recorded in history's.

The American soldier left the area in 1972. The last American ground combat units departed in August 1972, and with them the American share of the small-arms war. History's American dead by hostile action in 1972 were about 300 (R03 anchor)¹⁵. The record does not adjust the figure, and no small-arms difference would show in it.

## P6 · Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

Phase VI is where memorandum 004's counter-directional finding comes due, and where the Republic's own arsenal partly answers it. The ARVN had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical army. When the ammunition and fuel supplies were cut on 25 January 1974, the Joint General Staff's directive of February limited each squad to its basic load for a month. The squad gun went first, at about 200 rounds a gun a month, which an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes. Battalion commanders who had learned their trade breaking contacts with the squad gun's volume found that the ration let them do it about twice a month. The infantry, rather than fight with aimed rifle fire as its fathers had fought with the Garand, avoided contacts it could not break with volume. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had its own line, were rationed harder still, and by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms for want of ammunition⁵. The divisions hoarded against the ration, and by the end of 1974 the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Divisions held about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm outside the corps depots.

In this war the ration was not the whole story. Since 1965 the Aid Compliance Board had made the RVNAF buy a domestic share, and by 1974 the Bien Hoa arsenal made about two-fifths of the RVNAF's 5.56mm and 7.62mm cartridges, every one filled with imported propellant bought out of the appropriation. A dollar of propellant and primers loaded at Bien Hoa bought about one and a half times the rounds of a dollar of finished American ammunition. From March 1974 the Joint General Staff raised the squad gun's monthly ration to about 400 rounds¹⁶. The fiscal 1975 appropriation of about $950 million against history's $700 million then put its margin into aviation fuel, diesel and heavy rounds¹⁷. Whether Bien Hoa was loading the M855A1 of the provision or a simpler ball round, the record does not say; the study raises it (Q-5).

The weapons were better than the PAVN's in every fight of 1974, and it mattered less than at any time since 1964. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the 304th Division's 66th Regiment. Their marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and their squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night⁵. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed¹⁸; the record judges that the domestic rounds, the radios and the larger appropriation together saved perhaps 2,500–6,500 of them¹⁶¹⁷. The small arms are inside those judgments and not separable from them.

The territorial war of 1973–74 is where the area's margin, by then a margin of radios and gunships rather than rifles, did most. In Chuong Thien in the spring of 1974 about 46 of 64 attacked canal posts held, answered at night by the VNAF's gunships¹⁹. The post's carbine was by then equal to history's M16; what held it was the call and the answer.

The last campaign took the area's material with it in the north and left it in the south. In this war no withdrawal order came on 13 March 1975, and I Corps was held for five weeks. The 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin came apart between 22 and 28 March as history's did, because its families lived in its path, and its squad guns, marksmen's rifles and hoard went to the PAVN 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade. The 1st Division's hoard at Hue, about 3 million rounds of linked 5.56mm, was destroyed in place on 17 April, when Truong withdrew Hue's garrison by sea and over the Hai Van. At Pleiku the 320A's assault battalions came at the southern perimeter on 2 and 5 April across open ground. The 44th Regiment's and the 4th Ranger Group's squads broke them with the squad guns fed and the artillery firing at the 1973 allowance, the defence's best fight of the year, and it did not change the siege. About 11,000 of 25,000 regulars and Rangers reached the coast down Route 19 with their weapons. The squad weapons of II Corps and of the 1st and 2nd Divisions were in the PAVN's hands by the end of the spring with the hoards of Quang Ngai and the Highlands; the hoards of III and IV Corps were still the Republic's (P6, How the phase ends)⁵¹⁷. Xuan Loc, historically the gate of the Ho Chi Minh Campaign, was not attacked. The 18th Division held it through the rains with its squads, its marksmen and the ammunition the Long Binh depot could send, which was more than history's April could find.

At the end of 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south. It has the Bien Hoa arsenal loading its rifle and squad-gun cartridges, and an infantry that has carried better individual weapons than its enemy for twelve years. Five PAVN corps are assembled against it, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976. The area's last word is the record's first: the rifle acts only in a contact, and the enemy still chooses most contacts.

## Judgments

**Headline facets judged in the chapters (amend staged in this run).**

- **ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit, P1: +1 to +5 points (low).** Unit: % of company-and-below contacts the ARVN initiates. From December 1964 the Front's district companies stopped assaulting posts held by rearmed and trained platoons. That made fewer of the small fights the enemy's to open. The main-force ambush remained his, and the range is small because this facet counts who opens the fight, which the rifle does not decide.
- **ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit, P2: +2 to +7 points (low).** OPEN ROAD: the 272nd Regiment withdrew from an ambush it would have sprung on a battalion walking its flanks. STEADY HAND: a 7th Division company took a fight back from an ambush. POS010-1: posts and battalions that kept the routine were attacked less. Against these the mine, which the enemy moved to in Dien Ban, is a contact nobody initiates. The P3 value of +3 to +8, judged by an earlier work, is not re-judged and is consistent with this range.
- **ARVN.effectiveness, P1 and P2:** left unjudged on its range. The study adds the binding facet, **combined_arms**, as DIRR01-3 requires once a facet is judged. The battles of 1965 and the losses of 1966–67 were decided by how the corps fed reliefs and whether infantry, armour and guns arrived together. The squads' gains do not move the parent unless that facet moves, and this study does not judge combined arms, an area beyond small arms.
- **US.casualties, P1 (1965): 1,250–1,335 against history's 1,369 (low).** This is a sum of rates, not a measurement. The rates are those the record already uses: the carbine and squad gun, the courses, the tourniquet and the gunships. Most of the saving is shown at the Ia Drang, where the campaign's American dead fall from 237 to about 165–170. A further 2–6% applies elsewhere in a year in which most American dead fell in the second half²⁰.

**Headline facets left unjudged, and why.**

- **ARVN.effectiveness.small_unit, P4–P6.** From 1969 the historical army carried the same class of rifle. The record holds no small-unit event in 1969–72 that measures initiation, and the P6 events show an army avoiding contacts it could not break under the ration. That would cut against the earlier gain, but the record does not quantify it. Left for a checkpoint.
- **ARVN.effectiveness.night_ops, every phase.** The red dot is visible in the dark and the target is not, and the force had no night-vision devices (P004a negative specification). Nothing in the record measures the share of ARVN ambushes and patrols conducted at night. The one territorial indicator runs the other way: PF night patrols in Cai Lay fell by about a third in 1967 once posts could call for fire. That is a territorial figure and not the ARVN's.
- **ARVN.effectiveness.combined_arms, ARVN.effectiveness.anti_armor, ARVN.effectiveness.urban, ARVN.effectiveness.fire_coordination and ARVN.effectiveness.mobile_defence.** These are outside the area. The only small-arms evidence on them is Hue (urban: the marksmen shortened nothing, EV000-44) and An Loc (anti-armour: the historical LAW), and neither supports a range on the facet.
- **ARVN.self_sufficiency and its facets.** The area bears on logistics and maintenance through the ammunition dependence and the Bien Hoa cartridge line. Those values were judged by other works in P3 and P6 and are not re-judged. ARVN.self_sufficiency.maintenance and ARVN.self_sufficiency.replacement are left to a study of sustainment, because the record holds no small-arms readiness or replacement figure.
- **ENEMY.engagement_control, P1, P4, P5.** The record's settled position is that small arms do not change which fights the enemy accepts. This study's area therefore gives no evidence on the variable beyond what memoranda 005, 012 and 015 judged.
- **US.casualties, P4 and P5.** History's figures are 1,380 (1971) and 300 (1972). The record holds no event in the drawdown that shows the provisions' rates operating, and from 1969 the M16A1 closed most of the small-arms difference. A range would be a rate applied without evidence.

**Study-held values judged.**

- **RFPF.effectiveness and RFPF.effectiveness.outpost_defence, P1–P6.** Both are in % of attacked outposts that hold, as a difference from history's national figure, which the record does not hold. P1 +4 to +10 rests on Long Huu and the held belt's nineteen in twenty. It is kept small nationally because the gain was concentrated in posts with their platoon, and the Front moved to the rest. P2 +6 to +14 adds the radios, the gunship and the Dinh Tuong rule of 1967, and the Chuong Thien losses of 1966 bound it. P3 is the same, on the district towns at Tet. P4 and P5 at +3 to +9 reflect the historical army's 1969 rearmament closing the rifle gap³, with the radio and VNAF gunships holding the remainder. P6 at +4 to +11 rests on Chuong Thien in 1974. All are low confidence. The parent carries the same range because its unit is the same as the facet's.

**Study-held values left unjudged, and why.**

- **RFPF.effectiveness.night_patrol.** The only indicator is Cai Lay's fall of about a third in 1967, in one district, and it is a perverse effect of the radio rather than the rifle.
- **RFPF.effectiveness.reaction.** This turns on the gunship and the district gun, and it belongs to a study of fire support. The record's only push is memorandum 015's.
- **FIX.capability.** The record shows the squad weapons changing loss rates once in contact (Binh Gia, Ba Gia, Hau My), not the share of located main-force units engaged before they break contact, which is this variable's unit. POS003-1 and POS004-1 forbid reading one as the other.
- **VC.main_force and VC.local_force.** Both are in thousands. The record holds the Front's captured carbines and memorandum 004's push on the local force. It holds no strength figure that a small-arms effect could be read from.
- **INTL.communist_aid.** The area touches it only through the Type 56 cargoes of 1964, with no figure.

## Questions for the record

- **Q-1** · ERRATUM · EV007-3 (Bau Bang, 21 May 1966: the rear RF company "put its two M249s into the treeline") and EV006-2 (STILL WATER, March 1965: "the Regional Forces companies of Hoc Mon and Go Vap, with M4A1 carbines and their M249s") give Regional Force companies squad guns. Memorandum 004 states in P004c's negative specification and its card that "the Regional and Popular Forces do not receive it" and abandoned the squad gun to the outposts as EC004-10. EV000-42 keeps the rule: the 311th RF Company had "one squad gun lent by the 7th Division". Either the two events are corrected to carbines and BARs (or lent guns), or the record says when and how RF companies drew M249s.
- **Q-2** · CHECKPOINT · EV000-56 (Nixon takes office; Vietnamization, P4) is marked HIST and still says the Improvement and Modernization programme re-equipped the RVNAF with "M16 rifles for the regulars and then the territorial forces, M60 machine guns". POS004-2 says that in this war the 1968–69 rearmament does not occur, and the RVNAF has carried the M4A1 and M249 since 1964. The event should be HIST-ALT, saying what the programme replaced in this war (if anything) and where its small-arms money went.
- **Q-3** · CHECKPOINT · POS004-3 says the divisional hoards of 5.56mm "are lost in the collapse of March 1975". After memorandum 011 the record no longer has that collapse. The Hue hoard was destroyed in place on 17 April, the hoards of Quang Ngai and the Highlands were taken by the PAVN in March and April, and those of III and IV Corps remain the Republic's (P6, How the phase ends; EV000-96). The position needs a REV entry that states the hoards' fate as the chapters now have it.
- **Q-4** · CHECKPOINT · The record holds no ledger of the Republic's small arms after 1966. F004-7 puts 10,000–30,000 M4A1s in the Front's hands by the end of 1965, and EV004-3 traces 1.4 million rounds of leaked 5.56mm in 1965–66. Nothing after that says how many carbines and sights were lost each year, whether the Front's local forces kept them once the North's 7.62x39mm reached the village level, or what the weapons-captured ratio was. That is the historical anchor G-10 already lists as missing, and it is the evidence VC.local_force would need. A checkpoint should say whether the record will carry it.
- **Q-5** · CHECKPOINT · EV000-89 has Bien Hoa loading about two-fifths of the RVNAF's 5.56mm and 7.62mm by 1974. P004a's card says the M855A1, optics, batteries and parts all come from the United States and cannot be made or repaired at depth in the Republic. The record does not say whether Bien Hoa loaded M855A1, with its steel penetrator, or a simpler ball round, or what that meant for the carbine's performance at range in 1974–75. Nor does it say whether the CompM5's batteries and the TANGO6T scopes were kept up after 1973. A checkpoint should reconcile the arsenal's output with the provision's dependence.
Checked and found consistent: the American historical baseline (M16 to US units 1965–66, the 1967 subcommittee, the M16A1; EVCP02-3 against⁴). The ARVN's historical rearmament dates (EV000-51 and memorandum 004 against³; the record's "territorials in 1969" and the source's "complete by end 1969" agree). The squad-gun counts (two per Army squad at the Ia Drang, two or three per Marine squad at Hue, one per ARVN squad, against P004c). The marksman ranges in the events (300–800 m) against P004b's practical range.
