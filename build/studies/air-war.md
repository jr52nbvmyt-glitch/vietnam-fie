*Written ST01 · reflects the record after memo 013 · last folded CP03 · tags: AIR.north_effect, AIR.north_defences, AIR.losses, VNAF.capability, STRIKE.responsiveness, TRAIL.capacity, TRAIL.tonnage_arriving, SANCT.laos, SANCT.cambodia, ARVN.effectiveness.fire_coordination, ARVN.self_sufficiency.fire_support, POP.civilian_harm · RE-RUN ADVISED: R03 retired the −10..+10 scale: every study value was on it, and judging them in natural units, with the since-entries of memoranda 012 and 013, is a new study rather than a fold (CP03)*

## Summary — the state of the area now

The air war in this record is history's air war with two new instruments in it, and its shape is set by one fact: American air power became far better at destroying what it could measure, and the enemy, within a season each time, stopped owning anything that could be measured. The two instruments arrived on the war's first day. The first was eyes: two U-2S Dragon Ladies and four RQ-4B Global Hawks, working under the standing condition that gives them GPS and satellite links (E01), so that from January 1964 the enemy's roads, passes, fuel dumps, missile sites and assembly areas were watched in any weather and counted every night. The second was a hand: every B-52 replaced one for one by the re-engined B-52J with the AN/APQ-188 radar, carrying the GBU-31 and GBU-38 Joint Direct Attack Munitions and the Quickstrike family of influence mines, and fed from March 1964 by a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut under Major Edwin T. Sorrell that turned imagery into aimpoints true to a few metres.

Between them they changed four things, and each change was real. First, the war's interdiction debate became honest five years early: the panhandle watch counted the Trail's trucks from December 1965, the Sihanoukville count settled in March 1967 an argument history settled only with captured ledgers in 1970, and from 1969 every Commando Hunt claim travelled to Washington beside a radar count that never let the claim that more trucks had died than existed survive a season. Second, a siege the enemy dug became the enemy's worst battle: Con Thien lifted about three weeks early in 1967, Khe Sanh about two weeks early in 1968 with perhaps 3,500–4,000 PAVN dead to July against about 2,469 acknowledged, and in 1972 the J's cells broke the penetration of An Loc on 12 May and the assault on Kontum short of the wire. Third, the air war over the North, when Washington finally allowed it in 1972, chose from folders seven years in the making: the Thanh Hoa bridge fell on the first night of Freedom Train, and Linebacker II cost about six bombers rather than fifteen and did not hit Bach Mai or Kham Thien Street¹. Fourth, a handful of single events went the other way from history because a radar watched them: Pierce Arrow was never flown after the false alarm of 4 August 1964, and Neak Luong was never bombed in August 1973.

None of that reached the three things that decided the war, and the record says so as plainly as it records the gains. The enemy's flow south was taxed, never stopped. HARD CROSSING cut every bridge in the panhandle in 1966 and taxed the flow by 10–15 percent for a few weeks; by the autumn Group 559 had built a braided net of fords, causeways and pontoons with no single point of failure, and from then on the panhandle was harder to cut than history's ever was. The study judges tonnage arriving in the South a trace below history's at most in every phase from 1966 to 1972, and the Trail's capacity one to three points above history's from 1966 to the end, which is the measured price of the J's precision against a labour-rich enemy. The main force under canopy was found no better than history found it, and the air war's precision added nothing against it. And none of the instruments belonged to the Republic. The U-2S, the Global Hawks, the cell, the folders and the bombers all went home in 1973; the Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell and there was nothing in the VNAF for it to send coordinates to. The ARVN had learned, at Lolo and Sophia, An Loc and Kontum, to hold a besieged position and wait for the bombs, and in 1974 it waited at Thuong Duc for a siege box that no longer existed. At Phuoc Long the province chief's plan asked for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan" by the call signs of 1972.

So the margin over history is large inside the air war and small outside it. Over the North and on the Trail it is a better-informed, more precise and less costly version of the same campaign against the same rules, with the same result. In the South it is a set of besieged positions held more cheaply while the Americans were there and an army more dependent on them when they left, which the study judges as the Republic's own fire support standing up to three points below history's at the end of 1972 (P5). The one place the air war's balance turns in the Republic's favour is late and indirect: in 1974–75 a larger appropriation kept a third more of the VNAF flying, and its transports and helicopters carried the relief of Phu Loc, the counter-attack at Phuoc An and three weeks of the siege of Pleiku. At the end of 1975 the Republic still has an air force, which history's did not, and it still cannot break a siege with it. The gap register's sentence on air power stands at its true weight in both directions: decisive against the measured siege and the fixed crossing, nothing against a regiment under canopy or an attack on a post at night, and none of it Vietnamese.

## P1 · Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The air war of Phase I was an air war of watching and waiting, and its most consequential sortie was one that was not flown. The two U-2S and four Global Hawks flew from January 1964. On 3 March 1964 Sorrell opened the coordinate cell in a Quonset hut beside Major Harold Kessling's exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut, with eleven officers and twenty-six enlisted analysts, and its first discovery was that every map in the country was wrong: the Army Map Service's 1:50,000 sheets sat on the Indian 1960 datum, several hundred metres off the satellites' truth at Tan Son Nhut and 300 metres or more in the panhandle's mountains. His memorandum of 14 May 1964, that no coordinate taken from a map was to be given to a guided weapon, became the cell's law for a decade. On Guam the 3960th Strategic Wing took delivery of its first B-52Js with GBU-31 and GBU-38 kits in storage that spring and trained on ranges in the Marianas. By June 1965 the cell held about 1,400 folders, every road and rail bridge between the 20th parallel and the DMZ, the ferries on the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes and the first truck parks in Laos, and no folder had been struck¹. A quarter to a third of the U-2S's hours went to re-imaging known targets rather than searching for new ones.

The decisive night was 4 August 1964. At Admiral Sharp's request a Global Hawk had moved to the Gulf of Tonkin the day before, and when USS *Maddox* and USS *Turner Joy* fired for two hours at radar contacts in thunderstorms, Kessling's log showed the destroyers, a cluster of fishing junks 30 nautical miles west, rain cells, and no fast craft within 40 nautical miles. McNamara told the President so in the early afternoon; Johnson held the strike order and cancelled it at about 1900. Pierce Arrow's 64 sorties against the torpedo-boat bases and the Vinh oil storage were not flown, Lieutenant (jg) Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Lieutenant (jg) Everett Alvarez was not taken prisoner that day. The Southeast Asia Resolution passed anyway on 10 August, on the real attack of 2 August, 414–0 and 85–3². The air war's legal foundation was the same; its first pretext was gone.

The rest of the phase ran on history's dates for history's reasons. Barrel Roll began over northern and eastern Laos on 14 December 1964 and Steel Tiger over the panhandle on 3 April 1965, under the ambassador's control of targets and the fiction of Laotian neutrality, against a Trail that was still largely porters and bicycles³. After Pleiku on 7 February 1965, Flaming Dart struck Dong Hoi, Chanh Hoa and Chap Le; Rolling Thunder opened on 2 March with 104 USAF aircraft at Xom Bang and 19 VNAF A-1s at Quang Khe, the only VNAF strike the record names before 1974, and to 24 December 1965 flew 54,139 sorties and lost 180 aircraft⁴⁵.

Washington refused every request to use the new bombers over the North. On 3 April 1965 Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner's 79-aircraft package failed to drop the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa; MiG-17s of the 921st Fighter Regiment shot down two F-105s the next day⁶. The folder for Ham Rong had been complete since the previous August, and Sharp forwarded on 6 April a request for four Js to release forty GBU-31(V)3/B penetrators from twelve miles out; it was refused on 13 April, and again in June, because the B-52 was a strategic force whose use over the North would be read in Moscow and Peking as escalation¹. The SA-2 sites were seen coming: change detection on 22 March, the first Fan Song emissions on 18 April, eleven prepared sites mapped by 24 July. McNamara declined to strike them, for fear of killing Soviet technicians, as he had historically. When an SA-2 shot down Captain Roscoe Fobair's F-4C on 24 July, the retaliation of 27 July was re-targeted from an empty site to the occupied one on the U-2S's pass of the day before, destroyed two launchers and the Fan Song van, and lost two F-105Ds².

The first Arc Light, on 18 June 1965, was thirty B-52Js from Andersen with no tankers, so the mid-air collision that killed eight crewmen historically did not happen; twenty-eight aircraft struck a suspected base near Ben Cat, two of them with twenty GBU-31s each on four huts the U-2S had imaged, and found them empty for days¹. It was the find-and-fix problem in its purest form, and the precision changed none of it. The enemy's one blow at the air war on the ground fell at Bien Hoa on 1 November 1964, where a mortar round holed the U-2S in maintenance and cut the panhandle's daily deep look to twice a week until 20 December². At the end of July 1965 the North's air defence was an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles, every part of it on a map in Saigon and a weapon able to strike every part of it waiting on Guam.

**Memorandum 013 (folded at CP03).** The folders this study describes as unstruck until 1972 were struck in one week. Operation HAMMER FALL, 2–9 March 1965, took the North's power, bulk fuel, the Doumer and Ham Rong bridges, Phuc Yen and most of the 921st Regiment's MiG-17s, the warning and fire-control radars and Haiphong's cranes, for no bombers and seven tactical aircraft lost. The SA-2 sites were struck as they were built, and the first missile kill came on 22 August rather than 24 July. About 15–40 fewer aircraft were lost over the North in March–July 1965. The North answered as a society, not as an economy: drums, village diesels, pontoons and a fighter force based in China.

## P2 · Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is where the air war learned its two lessons, and it learned them in the order that would cost most. The first was learned over the Trail. On 6 December 1965, the day Tiger Hound opened, the Global Hawk orbit moved to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the panhandle west of Tchepone, and STEADY COUNT began: tracks of six or more vehicles passed from the exploitation cell to the 2nd Air Division, from April 1966 the Seventh Air Force, and the airborne command post, which sent A-1Es of the 602nd Air Commando Squadron, B-57Bs, F-100Ds, F-105Ds and from February AC-47 gunships. Of about 1,900 tracks in five months, aircraft reached about one in six within forty minutes, attacked one in ten and destroyed or damaged trucks in one in twenty; the pilots claimed about 560 trucks and the cell counted about 210; nine US aircraft were lost, most to 37mm guns Group 559 moved to the passes from January. Southbound movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 in March, and J-2 estimated 70–90 tons a day reaching the South at the peak, more than the forces there required from the North². By February the drivers had learned the orbit's changeover hours and began to trellis the open stretches. It was the first count of the Trail anyone had made, and it said that the bombing raised the toll without lowering what arrived.

On 12 April 1966 Washington authorised the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the panhandle and in Route Package 1 below the 18th parallel, and Operation HARD CROSSING began: eight Js of the 4133rd Bombardment Wing (Provisional) put 160 GBU-31s on eleven targets that night, and every one was down by dawn through low cloud. For six weeks the crossings stayed down; the Mu Gia bypass was cut for the fourth time in sixteen days on 28 April, the counts at the entry gates fell through May to about a third of March's, and the tonnage south of Tchepone to about half. It was read for a few weeks as the Trail closing. Phan Trong Tue's engineers read it correctly in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so Group 559 would own nothing it could measure. Fords paved a foot under water, bamboo causeways floated at dusk and sunk at dawn, Soviet pontoon sections hidden under the bank by day: at Ban Laboy the cell counted one ford in April, four in July and nine by October. By September the trucks were back to March's level and by October about 15 percent above it. About 210 bridges, ferries and causeways died for about 1,900 kits and no bombers; the tax on the flow was 10–15 percent at its May peak and about 5 percent by October, and what HARD CROSSING built for the enemy was a road net more redundant than history's ever became¹. The chapter's Mu Gia sentence in STEADY COUNT, written before memorandum 005, still has the trucks moving through the craters twenty hours after the first B-52 strike (Q-3).

The second instrument against the panhandle was the sea. On 26 February 1967 Washington allowed the aerial mining of the North's rivers south of the 20th parallel, and four Js from U-Tapao laid 80 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes in the Song Ca and the Song Giang at Quang Khe in a single pass each. The steel lighters stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa by June; the cargo moved to junks, sampans and Route 1A and Route 15, the radar's count of trucks below Vinh rose 25–30 percent while the tonnage into the panhandle stayed about the same, and the North spent perhaps 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers on Route Package 1¹. The study takes the record's own answer to how much less arrived because of it: very little.

Over the North the phase was history's, measured earlier. The U-2S showed in May and June 1966 that the North's fuel was already in drums under the trees, and J-2 told CINCPAC on 14 June that the bulk farms were no longer the system; the POL strikes of 29 June flew regardless, and on 1 August J-2 reported that they had not measurably reduced movement south, three months before the intelligence community said so historically. Robin Olds's Bolo destroyed seven MiG-21s on 2 January 1967, and the VPAF grew from about 100 interceptors to 180 MiGs by 1968. The barrier's sensors were laid where the radar's tracks ended, and Task Force Alpha at Nakhon Phanom took the Global Hawk's tracks by teletype from its first day². Rolling Thunder had dropped about 864,000 tons by 31 December 1967⁴.

The second lesson was learned in the South, at Con Thien. From September 1967 the PAVN besieged the hill by fire from 130mm and 152mm guns north of the Ben Hai, with the 90th and 812th Regiments of the 324B Division entrenched to cover them⁷. The Global Hawk's second orbit imaged the ground every night from 4 September, and from 11 September cells of three Js with thirty to forty GBU-38s each came every two to three hours; the bombardment of 25 September came at about 600 rounds rather than 1,200, the 812th's trenches that reached 900 metres on 13–14 September were struck by morning, and the siege lifted about three weeks early with perhaps a third fewer Marines killed in September and October¹. Westmoreland took from the hill the lesson that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle. It was true, and it would cost the coastal hamlets of Thua Thien a brigade in January.

The enemy's initiative in the area was the engineering answer of 1966, taken by Group 559 on its own reading in one season and never reversed. The ARVN's place in the air war of the phase is absent: the record shows no VNAF sortie between 1965 and 1974, and the posts of the held belt learned from 1967 to call for fire from district guns rather than men, which is the Republic's only fire support the provisions improved.

**Memoranda 012 and 013 (folded at CP03).** The restrike watch kept the Red River crossings at Hanoi closed to rail on about 290 of 517 days in 1965–66. Its count of targets destroyed fell from about 140 a month to about 35 as the North stopped owning anything worth a GBU-31. The POL strikes of June 1966 were never flown, Bolo killed three MiG-21s rather than seven, and the VPAF fought from Chinese airfields. About 40–90 fewer aircraft were lost over the North in 1966–67. In the South, memorandum 012's combined fire-support centres routed American air, Arc Light and the J's boxes to ARVN units through one switchboard. That made American fire more available to the ARVN and no more its own. The study's central judgment stands and is strengthened: the flow south was taxed, never stopped, and the bombing of the North did not reach it at all.

## P3 · Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

The air war of 1968 won the year's most complete victory on the ground and watched the year decided elsewhere. From mid-December 1967 the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos, about 20–25 a night rising to about 80 by mid-January; the U-2S's signals payload fixed the 325C Division's headquarters northwest of Hill 881 North on 2 January and the 304th's near Co Roc on 11 January. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration the command had ever held, and Westmoreland, after Con Thien, read it on 13 January as the battle the enemy would give him; the radar plots went every evening to the White House and its terrain model. The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that attacked the cities on 30–31 January moved into them among the Tet crowds, where no radar could tell them from anyone². A brigade of the 101st Airborne Division was drawn from the coastal districts of Thua Thien with the 1st Cavalry Division, and was not there when the Front's local forces returned to them¹.

Operation Niagara had two halves. The historical half, about 60 B-52s a day on the valleys, the parks and Route 9 in Laos, fell as a carpet half as dense because the J carries about half the D's unguided bombs, about two-thirds of the historical tonnage of unguided bombs. The new half was a Global Hawk over the plateau every night imaging the ground within three kilometres of the perimeter each hour, a detachment of the cell moved to Da Nang, and from 26 January cells of three Js every ninety minutes with GBU-38s on positions measured that night; from 15 February they were cleared to bomb 400 metres from the Marines and the 37th Ranger Battalion. The 304th Division dug toward the eastern perimeter on 17, 22 and 27 February, and each time its trenches were destroyed at 500–800 metres; on 29 February its regiment formed in the open to assault the Rangers and was broken before the wire. The 130mm and 152mm guns rolled out of the caves at Co Roc across the Laotian border were struck within hours of each mission and by mid-March fired only at night. Lang Vei had forty minutes' warning of the twelve PT-76s of the 203rd Armored Regiment on 7 February and fell all the same². The air bridge, about 14,356 tons, lost about a third fewer helicopters because the flak around the approaches was struck each night⁸. Major General Tran Quy Hai pulled the 325C back in mid-March and the 304th before the end of the month, about two weeks early; the study takes the record's judgment of about 180 Marines killed against more than 274, and perhaps 3,500–4,000 PAVN dead to July against 2,469 acknowledged¹.

The halt ended the northern campaign on 1 November on history's terms. Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder flew 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine attack sorties and lost 506 USAF, 397 Navy and 19 Marine aircraft over or near the North, and the record carries those figures unaltered⁴ (Q-1). The Gulf watch, flying over international waters, kept counting: within a week trucks moved by day in columns of forty and fifty, the ferries at Quang Khe ran in daylight, and in December southbound movements on Route 15 toward Mu Gia were about three times October's². The count went to Washington twice a week and changed nothing about the halt, which had been traded for the talks.

It changed the next campaign. Commando Hunt opened on 15 November with Igloo White, the AC-130s of the 16th Special Operations Squadron and, in this war, the Global Hawk as a third sensor and the J as a fourth instrument. The interdiction points at Mu Gia, Ban Karai and Ban Laboy were cut with GBU-31s on measured coordinates rather than cratered by boxes, the Xe Bang Fai and Xe Kong were sown with Quickstrikes set for the steel pontoons, and the tankers freed by the J's range let the F-4s fly about a quarter more sorties a night. The points did not close: at Ban Laboy the cell now counted fourteen fords and two underwater causeways. The phase's claims ran to 7,322 trucks; the radar counted about 2,600–3,200 destroyed or disabled and a cut of about a sixth in tonnage reaching the South, the J's crossings adding perhaps a fiftieth part of the flow⁹²¹. From May 1969 the two figures travelled to Washington together.

The PAVN's own reading of the year, taken by the front command and not provoked by anything new in it, was the clearest enemy initiative of the area: it would not again lay siege to a position the Americans could measure. That was the one way of choosing its battles it had lost, and it chose all the others. The ARVN took a different lesson from Khe Sanh, which it watched rather than fought, and the VNAF appears nowhere in the year's record.

**Memoranda 012 and 013 (folded at CP03).** There was no partial halt, and the summer of 1968 over Hanoi cost about 30–40 aircraft that history's campaign never risked. Rolling Thunder ended on 1 November having lost about 810–880 aircraft rather than 922. At Hue a single fire centre in the Citadel took every call for naval gunfire, artillery and aircraft, and the battle ended on 27 February rather than 2 March.

## P4 · Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV is the air war turned onto the sanctuaries, and in each sanctuary the precision had a season of targets and then none. On 18 March 1969 forty-eight Js diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, about 1,600 tons in the boxes rather than 2,400, and twelve aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s onto storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload. Menu flew about 3,800 sorties to 26 May 1970; here it dropped about 75,000 tons rather than 108,823, a fifth of the sorties carrying JDAM¹⁰. About 300 measured structures died in the first six weeks of Breakfast and Lunch; by June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, searched the base areas for spies and beacons, found neither, and owned nothing in the open again¹. COSVN itself, a few hundred people under the canopy, was never located. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969 and the wiretaps followed; in this war they are never exposed, because there is no Watergate¹¹. The study leaves Cambodia's value to the enemy to the study of maritime interdiction, which holds Sihanoukville (see Judgments); the air war's part in it was a season of targets.

The radar's second ledger made Commando Hunt the honest campaign history's never was, and the campaign failed regardless. Phase V, 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks, which Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; the radar counted about 4,500–5,500 destroyed or disabled and a net loss to the throughput of about 8–10 percent. The DIA model that historically reached zero trucks fourteen times in 1969 never reached the Secretary of Defense, because the radar showed every night the trucks it said did not exist. By 1970 the cell's folders held about 4,000 crossings, a third in use on any night; the Quickstrike fields on the Xe Kong and the Se San kept the pontoons and barges off the rivers, so the fuel drums floated down on bamboo rafts; and the one target that could not move, the pipeline from Vinh toward the A Shau, had its pumping stations cut about eighty times between October 1970 and the end of 1971 and bypassed within two to four days each time by flexible pipe around the crater. The study takes the record's judgment that the J raised the net loss by one or two points at the season's peak¹. The Trail's corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles, the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads, and the count of southbound trucks rose to about 150–200 a night at the 1971 peak⁹². It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving, and in this war this headquarters wrote that sentence in 1969 from its own counts. The record's comparison of the radar's 8–10 percent with the historical estimate of about 2 percent does not say how much of the difference is effect and how much measurement (Q-2).

Lam Son 719 showed what the air war could and could not do for the ARVN once the advisers stayed behind. J-2's estimate of 4 February 1971, from the U-2S's signals and the panhandle watch, put 35,000–40,000 men within reach of Tchepone, about twice the planners' historical figure, and the operation went ahead on its date². Sorrell's detachment at Quang Tri, a dozen Americans who could not cross the border, passed measured positions to the Js; the ARVN on the hills passed nothing, because they had no means to give a coordinate a bomber could use. At Lolo and Sophia, where the PAVN 2nd Division's regiments dug in from late February, the trenches were struck each night at 400–700 metres and the bases held until evacuated; at Ranger North, Ranger South and Fire Support Base 31, where the 308th and 320th Divisions came straight at the wire in daylight, the bases fell much as they had historically, and on 25 February six of about twenty tanks the radar tracked forming up were destroyed before they reached the hill, which fell that afternoon. In the retreat the Js' GBU-38s destroyed about 110 dug-in gun positions around the landing zones, and the study takes the record's judgment of about a sixth fewer of the historical 108 helicopters lost¹²¹. Hanoi admitted 2,163 killed; the record does not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the landing zones and the command.

The lesson I Corps drew was the one that would cost it. The bases it had held were the bases where the bombs came every two hours, and it began to build its plans for the defence of the North around them. The Republic's only countervailing fire-support gain of the phase was in the Delta and in the plants: at Long My from February 1970 the subsector advisers handed the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets, and by the end of 1971 a third of the 105mm rounds were domestic. The VNAF grew on Vietnamization's historical schedule; nothing in the force reached it.

## P5 · Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

1972 was the air war's year in this record, the year the instruments of 1964 were finally allowed to do everything they had been built for, and it confirmed exactly what the earlier phases had shown. The armour of the Nguyen Hue offensive was seen before it crossed: from 12 March the Gulf watch tracked tracked-vehicle columns moving south at night on Route 1A and Route 101 from Vinh Linh, and by 25 March J-2 counted about 90–120 armoured vehicles in assembly areas within 20 kilometres of the DMZ; MACV warned I Corps on 27 March. Lieutenant General Lam expected the blow from the west, and the warning changed the 3rd Division's rotation only in part. When the 304th and 308th Divisions came across on 30 March under cloud that grounded the fighter-bombers, the Bs bombed by radar on the crossings of the Ben Hai and the roads south of Gio Linh and destroyed or disabled about 20–30 armoured vehicles before they reached the firebases². The firebases fell all the same, Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh surrendered Camp Carroll on 2 April, and Quang Tri fell on 1–2 May. The radar had seen the tanks; it had not made the 3rd Division steady.

Over the North the folders of seven years were opened. On the night of 10 April twelve Js put 240 GBU-31s on the Ham Rong bridge, the Thanh Hoa rail yards and the tanks at Vinh, in cloud, and the Dragon's Jaw fell into the Song Ma seven years and seven days after the first request to drop it was refused. The Paul Doumer bridge was dropped on 10–11 May by F-4s with laser-guided bombs as historically, because the restricted zone around Hanoi stayed closed to B-52s until December. On the night of Pocket Money the Js laid the secondary fields, about 900 Quickstrikes in the approaches to Hon Gai, Cam Pha, Thanh Hoa, Vinh and Dong Hoi and the waterways south of Haiphong, about 200 of them Quickstrike-ERs released more than forty miles out; from June the Gulf watch saw the trucks massing at Dong Dang and Lao Cai to replace the sea. Linebacker flew 41,653 missions and lost 134 aircraft, 104 in combat; the record judges, loosely, that the electronic order of battle and the J's work on fixed targets saved perhaps 15–25 of those losses, and that traffic below Vinh in July stood at about 35 percent of March against about 40 percent historically, because the bridges fell in the first week rather than the first month¹³²¹.

In the South the siege box did what it had done at Con Thien and Khe Sanh, and did it for the ARVN. At An Loc from 15 April a Global Hawk was given the town every night and Sorrell's detachment at Long Binh measured gun positions, bunkers, tank harbours and trenches; from 16 April cells of Js came every two hours with GBU-38s, the nearest 400 metres from Brigadier General Le Van Hung's lines, and the town's shelling came at perhaps two-thirds of history's weight. The assault of 11 May was on the radar from the evening of the 10th, and through the morning of 12 May the cell measured the buildings the 9th Division had taken in the northern town and put GBU-38s on them 300–400 metres from the ARVN line, one aimpoint to a bomb; the penetration was destroyed by noon. At Kontum the three weeks after Tan Canh were the B3 Front's to lose: John Paul Vann had a detachment of the cell flown to the airfield on 28 April, it measured about 600 positions in the arc north and west of the city by 13 May, and about a third of the guns the PAVN brought were destroyed before the assault; the 320th Division's 48th Regiment was broken short of the wire on 14 May and Colonel Ly Tong Ba declared the city secure on 4 June, five days early¹. Tan Canh itself fell in a day to tanks already inside the wire, and nothing in the J reached a fight of that kind.

Linebacker II ended the year. The 729 completed sorties were J sorties, a third of them carrying twenty GBU-31s each on measured aimpoints in the rail yards at Kinh No, Lang Dang and Yen Vien, the power plants and the SAM sites; the rest carried the J's unguided load, about 11,000 tons rather than 15,237. The North fired 266 SA-2s¹⁴. The first three nights' routes were SAC's, as historically, and the missile crews learned them; the record judges about six Js lost rather than fifteen B-52s, from the later electronic-warfare suite, the higher release and the destruction of the sites at Kinh No and Phuc Yen on the fourth night rather than the eighth, with about thirteen men killed or missing and twelve captured against 33 and 33. Neither Bach Mai hospital nor Kham Thien Street was hit, and the record judges the civilian dead at about 900–1,200 against Hanoi's count of 1,624¹. The terms were October's.

The PAVN's 1972 losses stand in the record at about 7,000–10,000 above history's, concentrated in the 5th, 7th and 9th Divisions and the 320th and 2nd, most of the difference made by the siege box¹⁵. The VNAF, fourth largest air force in the world after Enhance Plus delivered 234 F-5As and A-37s, 32 C-130s and 277 UH-1Hs¹⁶, is absent from the record's account of every siege. The ARVN ended the year having learned more completely than history's that it held besieged towns with American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts, and the agreement about to be signed took both away.

## P6 · Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The air war's last phase in this record is the war of its absence, and the absence was felt more sharply than history's because more had been taken away. Until 15 August 1973 the Js went on bombing Cambodia in support of Lon Nol; on 6 August, where historically a B-52 whose navigator failed to set a beacon offset killed about 137 people in Neak Luong, the J released on its own GPS into its box in the forest east of the river. Case–Church passed in the same words and by the same margins, because the vote was not about the President. The Js went home to the alert pads at Andersen and U-Tapao and stayed on alert there each dry season as the visible instrument of the President's promise to Thieu¹¹. Sorrell's cell, folded into the Defense Attaché Office in March, was disbanded in September, and its folders for every firebase and district town in the South went to Clark and then to the Defense Mapping Agency. The Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell of its own; there was nothing for it to send coordinates to, because no aircraft the VNAF flew could carry a guided bomb¹.

The North's air defences moved south with its corps, and its roads were rebuilt on the historical schedule. Resolution 21 of October 1973 set the rebuilding, and the strategic road east of the Truong Son and the pipeline into the South were built as history built them; the study judges the Trail's capacity still one to three points above history's, because they were laid onto a panhandle net that the J had taught to have no single point of failure. The chapter judges tonnage arriving at the end of 1974 about history's, and at the end of 1975 two to five points above it, because the road had a second dry season's offensive to carry.

The ARVN fought 1974 with plans written for an air force that no longer came. At Thuong Duc the 79th Ranger Battalion dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, while the 304th Division's regiment dug to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling, and the town fell on 7 August. The Airborne's counter-attacks on Hill 1062 were planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia under the Js, and went forward in the expectation that the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it, and the record judges the Airborne's losses on the heights perhaps a tenth higher than history's¹. Historically the VNAF placed 224 aircraft in storage in 1974 and cut its flying hours by about half¹⁷. In this war a fiscal 1975 appropriation of about $950 million, stretched by the domestic plants, put a third fewer aircraft on the ground by December; at Phu Loc from 28 August the 1st Division's counter-attack had VNAF UH-1Hs for lift and resupply on most days instead of some, and the hills were retaken in the third week of November rather than on 10 December¹¹.

Phuoc Long was the air war's epitaph. The province chief's plan for Phuoc Binh, drafted in 1973 by officers who had served at An Loc, was to hold for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege, and it named the bomber cells by Hollingsworth's call signs of 1972. On 13 December 1974 he asked III Corps for the B-52 support provided for in the plan; III Corps forwarded it to the Joint General Staff, which forwarded it to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it. On 18 December Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers, in those words and with more conviction than history's, because his officers had seen at An Loc and Kontum what the bombers could do¹. The attack itself was the enemy's own initiative and had an author: Lieutenant General Tran Van Tra argued it in Hanoi in November as a test of the American response, against a Politburo more cautious than history's, and won. On 7 January 1975 the President asked Congress for authority to use American air power in the event of a major offensive; the request was dead within the month¹¹. Hanoi read it rightly: the President wanted to return, and the Congress would not let him.

In 1975 the VNAF flew the Republic's last fights on fuel the appropriation had bought. The counter-attack from Phuoc An toward Ban Me Thuot on 15–18 March went in lifted by VNAF CH-47s and UH-1Hs and was broken by the PAVN 10th Division as history's was. At Pleiku the C-130As and C-123Ks flew from Nha Trang into Cu Hanh by day, about 25–35 sorties a day, until the 968th's 130mm guns closed the runway on 9 April, and the 6th Air Division's A-37s flew from Pleiku itself until that day. The siege lasted three weeks, the town fought it without bombers, and about 11,000 of 25,000 regulars and Rangers reached An Khe and Qui Nhon. The supplementals that kept the VNAF flying, about $150 million in March and $175 million in May, bought fuel and rounds and nothing that could carry a guided weapon¹¹. At the end of 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the bases at Bien Hoa, Tan Son Nhut and the Delta, and so it still has an air force, which history's had not by the end of April. It is an air force that can lift, resupply and strike with unguided bombs, and it cannot break a siege. The Js are on alert at Andersen; the statute keeps them there.

**Tagged and left unjudged.** *SANCT.cambodia* is tagged because Menu and the Sihanoukville count touch it, and left unjudged here: its value to the enemy turns on the Sihanoukville route, which the study of maritime interdiction in this run covers, and one copy of the value should sit there. *POP.civilian_harm* is tagged because the air war's part in it is real in every phase (Arc Light's carpet half as dense, Menu's 75,000 tons rather than 108,823, Linebacker II's 900–1,200 dead against 1,624, Neak Luong spared), and left unjudged because the variable is civilian harm from all sides' operations, and its decisive differences in this record lie outside the air war: My Lai removed, the Highlands column of 1975 losing about 9,000 civilians against history's 155,000.
