## Summary — the state of the area now

The air war in this record is history's air war with five new instruments in it, and its shape is set by one fact. American air power became far better at destroying what it could measure, and the enemy, within a season each time, stopped owning anything that could be measured. Three of the instruments were eyes and a hand that arrived on the war's first day: two U-2S Dragon Ladies and four RQ-4B Global Hawks to watch the enemy's roads, passes and dumps in any weather, and the re-engined B-52J replacing every B-52 one for one, with GPS-guided GBU-31 and GBU-38 bombs and Quickstrike mines and a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut to aim them (P005a–P005d). The fourth was an authority: from 2 March 1965 the northern campaign was run from Saigon on the theater's own list, with no zones around Hanoi or Haiphong and no pauses, and it opened with HAMMER FALL, a week that took the North's power, bulk fuel, great bridges, jet base and warning net. The fifth was a night gun. History's AC-47, AC-119 and AC-130 fleets never existed: from January 1964 sixty-four AC-130J Ghostriders of the US Air Force flew over the South's posts and the Trail, and from 1967 forty-eight AC-130U Spooky IIs of the Vietnamese Air Force took over the posts and kept them after the Americans left.

Each instrument changed something real, and the study weighs each at full weight. The interdiction debate became honest five years early: the radar counted the Trail's trucks from December 1965 and travelled to Washington beside every claim. The measured siege became the enemy's worst battle: Con Thien lifted about three weeks early in 1967, Khe Sanh about two weeks early in 1968 with perhaps 3,500–4,000 PAVN dead to July against 2,469 acknowledged, and in 1972 the J's cells broke the penetration of An Loc on 12 May and the assault on Kontum short of the wire. The North's 1964 economy was wrecked in a week, three years before history's campaign reached it. Rolling Thunder lost about 810–880 aircraft rather than 922, and Linebacker II about six bombers rather than fifteen¹². The night attack on a post, the Front's best tactic of the village war, lost a quarter to two-fifths of its effect in 1964–72, and in 1974 the Republic's own gunships held about 46 of 64 attacked canal posts in Chuong Thien³.

None of that reached the three things that decided the war, and the record says so as plainly as it records the gains. The flow south was taxed and never stopped: the study judges the Trail's capacity about 4–7 percent above history's from 1966, at low confidence, because a labour-rich enemy answered every precise cut with a redundant net. The bombing of the North, unrestricted from 1965, destroyed everything in the North that could be destroyed and the North's war in the South needed none of it. The main force under canopy was found no better than history found it. And almost none of the instruments belonged to the Republic. The cell, the bombers, the satellites' coordinates and the Ghostriders went home in 1972–73; the ARVN had learned to hold a besieged town and wait for bombs that no longer came, and at Phuoc Long in December 1974 the province chief asked for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan".

The margin over history is therefore large inside the air war and small outside it. The one instrument the Republic owned changes the end of the war at the level of the posts, not of the divisions. At the end of 1975 the VNAF still exists, which history's did not after April, and it can lift, resupply, strike with unguided bombs and hold a post at night through cloud. It cannot fly over a PAVN division with its own air defence, and it cannot break a siege.

## Primer — the air war explained

#### What the air war was

The air war in Vietnam was not one campaign but five, fought at once by different services under different rules, and a reader who keeps them apart will understand the rest of this study. The first was the bombing of North Vietnam, from Flaming Dart in February 1965 through Rolling Thunder (March 1965 – October 1968) to Linebacker and Linebacker II in 1972: a campaign of fighter-bombers from Thailand and carriers in the Gulf of Tonkin, against bridges, rail yards, fuel, power and the North's air defences, run in history under Washington's weekly approval of targets. The second was the interdiction of the Ho Chi Minh Trail in the Laotian panhandle, under the operations called Steel Tiger, Tiger Hound and from 1968 Commando Hunt: armed reconnaissance by night against trucks, cuts at the passes and fords, and from 1967 the Igloo White sensor fields, all under the American ambassador in Vientiane's control of targets. The third was the war in the South: close air support for troops in contact, called in by forward air controllers in light aircraft; preplanned strikes; and Arc Light, the B-52 boxes that carpeted a block of jungle a mile wide. The fourth was mobility, the reason the American army in Vietnam could fight as it did: the tactical airlift of C-130s, C-123s and C-7s into short strips, and the helicopter, which carried the infantry into battle, resupplied it and took its wounded out. The fifth was the Vietnamese Air Force's own war, which grew from a few squadrons of propeller aircraft in 1964 into, by the ceasefire of January 1973, about 2,075 aircraft of 25 types in 65 squadrons⁴.

#### Who flew what

Each service fought its own air war and kept its own chain. The **US Air Force** flew from bases in South Vietnam (Bien Hoa, Tan Son Nhut, Da Nang, Phan Rang, Cam Ranh Bay, Tuy Hoa, Phu Cat, Pleiku, Binh Thuy) and Thailand (Takhli, Korat, Ubon, Udorn, Nakhon Phanom, U-Tapao), under the 2nd Air Division and from April 1966 the Seventh Air Force. Its B-52s belonged to Strategic Air Command and flew from Guam, Okinawa and U-Tapao in Thailand⁵, and their targets were approved through a separate chain. The **US Navy** flew from the attack carriers of Task Force 77 at Yankee Station in the Gulf of Tonkin and, in 1965–66, Dixie Station off the South; it kept two or three carriers on the line before August 1966, often three or four after, three on a monthly average in 1968, two in 1969–71 and five or six in 1972, each air wing of 70 to 100 aircraft in two fighter and three attack squadrons⁶. The **US Marine Corps** flew its own wing, the 1st Marine Aircraft Wing, from Da Nang, Chu Lai, Marble Mountain, Phu Bai and Ky Ha, in support of its own divisions in I Corps⁷. The **US Army** flew more aircraft than any other service in the theatre, almost all of them helicopters, in the airmobile divisions, the 1st Aviation Brigade and the aviation companies of every division⁸⁹. The **Vietnamese Air Force** flew from the same South Vietnamese bases under its own air divisions, with American advisers and, until 1973, American maintenance support⁴. Alongside them flew the **Royal Australian Air Force** (Canberras, Caribous and Iroquois), the **Royal Lao Air Force** with its T-28s, and on the other side the **Vietnam People's Air Force** with Soviet MiGs and the North's air defence command of guns, missiles and radars¹⁰¹¹¹²¹³¹⁴.

#### How the war in the air was fought

Over the North the problem was a defended country with a small war economy. A Rolling Thunder strike was a package flown from Thailand or a carrier along routes the North's radars learned, bombing in clear weather by eye, against targets released a few at a time. The defence was layered: the guns at low level, the SA-2 from about 3,000 feet upward, and the MiGs coming from behind under ground control. The attacker could fly low and meet the guns or high and meet the missiles, and the jammers and Wild Weasels existed to make the second choice survivable. The North answered bombing by dispersal, repair and import: fuel moved into drums, bridges into pontoons and fords, and the trucks, guns and missiles came by rail from China and by sea into Haiphong.

On the Trail the problem was finding. The Trail was a net of roads, paths, fords and rivers under forest, worked by Group 559's engineers, porters, drivers and gunners, and it carried what the war in the South needed, which was small: a few tens of tons a day in the mid-1960s. Aircraft could kill trucks they could see, which meant at night on open road, and in history the gunships killed most of them; but the Trail's capacity was elastic with labour, and the measure that mattered was tonnage arriving in the South, not trucks destroyed.

In the South the problem was the enemy's choice of battle. Most main-force fights began when the enemy chose, at close range, so air power's work in the South was mostly the defence of units in contact and of posts and bases under attack, and the bombing of base areas the enemy had left. An airstrike in the South was called by a ground unit, passed through a direct air support centre, given a forward air controller who found the target and marked it, and flown by whatever was on alert; in history the fastest answer at night was a gunship on airborne alert¹⁵. The helicopter changed what infantry could do: it put a battalion into a clearing in minutes, took its wounded to a surgeon within an hour, and let commanders move artillery to hilltops. Nearly 12,000 helicopters served in the war, more than 7,000 of them Hueys, and over 5,000 were lost¹⁷.

#### The enemy's air defence

The North's air defence is the reason the air war over the North was expensive. In August 1964 the VPAF had about 36 combat aircraft; it had 206 in May 1972. The 921st Fighter Regiment was formed in 1964 with MiG-17s, the 923rd about a year later, the 925th in February 1969 and the 927th in February 1972¹⁴. The North's guns numbered 7,119 on 24 October 1967, and on 20 March 1968 there were 4,802 guns of 37–57mm and 993 of 85–100mm on 1,158 sites. The SA-2s fired 194 missiles in 1965, 1,096 in 1966, 3,202 in 1967 and 4,244 in 1972, and the share of shots that downed an aircraft fell from 5.7 percent to about 1 percent as jamming and tactics improved¹⁶. The guns, not the missiles or the MiGs, did most of the killing.

## P1 · Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The air war of Phase I opened two years early in the South and eight months early over the North, and its most consequential sortie was one that was not flown. In the South the war in the air began on the night of 8–9 January 1964, when the 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship) flew its first sortie with 64 AC-130J Ghostriders under Farm Gate's rules: a Vietnamese observer aboard, support of the RVNAF only, fire only on the province chief's clearance. Through the spring the wing flew 400–600 sorties a month and answered 120–180 calls from posts under attack. The clearance took a median 35 minutes while posts were overrun beneath circling guns, so in July clearance within 300 metres of a post was delegated to the district chief and the wait fell to about 12 minutes³. The price of that rule was paid later, in the hamlets beside the posts. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee answered in March with an order of its own, the area's first enemy initiative: no assault on a post with a radio on a clear night within reach of the airfields. By June such assaults had nearly stopped in Long An and Dinh Tuong.

Above the South, the eyes were at work. The two U-2S and four Global Hawks flew from January 1964, and on 3 March Major Edwin T. Sorrell opened the coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut, whose first discovery was that every map in the country was wrong by up to 300 metres in the mountains². On 4 August a Global Hawk over the Gulf of Tonkin saw the destroyers, a cluster of fishing junks, rain cells and no fast craft within 40 nautical miles, and Pierce Arrow's 64 sorties were not flown; the Southeast Asia Resolution passed anyway on the real attack of 2 August²⁹.

The opening blow on the North was HAMMER FALL, 2–9 March 1965, and it was the largest single change this record makes to the air war. On the night of 2–3 March, under crachin at 800–1,500 feet, twenty-four B-52Js left Andersen in cells of three. Their GBU-38s wrecked 22–26 of the 921st Fighter Regiment's 36 MiG-17Fs at Phuc Yen; their penetrators struck Bach Mai, the Citadel and the radars; the power stations, the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly, two spans of the Paul Doumer bridge, the Ham Rong bridge and about forty of Haiphong's cranes followed. No J was lost; the carriers and the F-105Ds lost seven aircraft when the cloud lifted. By 9 March the North had lost about 70–80 percent of its generating capacity, most of its bulk fuel, its great bridges, its only jet base and its warning net¹. The trucks of Group 559, the porters in the panhandle and the regiments in the South noticed nothing at all.

The North answered as a society, not as an economy, and the answer was decided in three capitals. Hanoi dispersed everything it could move and put its fuel in drums and its power in about 700 village diesels. Moscow doubled the SA-2 package. Beijing, for its own reasons in the quarrel over the communist world's leadership, refused the Soviets an air corridor and made every shipment cross China by rail at Chinese pace, so that the first SA-2 battalions arrived three to five weeks late and the surviving MiGs re-formed at Mengzi and Ningming in China. Each SA-2 site was struck within 24–48 hours of its first image, and the first American aircraft lost to a missile fell on 22 August rather than 24 July. About 60–75 American aircraft were lost over the North in March–July, against perhaps 85–100 in history's same months¹. The first Arc Light, on 18 June, was thirty Js from Andersen without tankers, so the collision that killed eight crewmen historically did not happen; two of them put GBU-31s on four huts near Ben Cat that had been empty for days².

At 2310 on 14 December 1964 Major Thomas R. Gaither was orbiting Ghostrider 63 above the cloud over Binh Dinh when the post at Tam Quan called that a company was on its wire. Below him there was nothing but grey at 1,800 feet; the turrets saw the top of the overcast and the glow of a flare inside it. He took the aircraft down through the cloud, and at 1,500 feet the post came out of the rain in front of him, the southern wall lit by the defenders' own fire, the attackers bunched in the paddy beyond it. He put the 105mm on them. Then the tracers came up from two places on the dikes, close and from the side, and thirty rounds of 12.7mm went through the left wing and the tanks in six minutes. He broke the assault on the southern wall, climbed back through the cloud on three engines, and brought the aircraft home to Qui Nhon. At 0400 the attack came again in the rain, with nothing overhead, and the post fell with nineteen of its thirty men³.

The aircraft that defended the posts of the Delta on clear nights did not see through cloud, and the provincial units of the coast spent the winter learning that faster than the wing did; on 16 December it forbade attacks below 3,500 feet. Phase I left the air war with the North's economy wrecked and irrelevant, the posts covered on clear nights, and the coast's monsoon nights the Front's.

## P2 · Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is where the air war learned its two lessons, and it learned them in the order that would cost most. The first was learned on the Trail. On 6 December 1965, the day Tiger Hound opened, the Global Hawk orbit moved to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the panhandle, and the same night sixteen Ghostriders of the 17th Air Commando Squadron at Ubon flew their first armed reconnaissance on its tracks, in an operation called NIGHT LEDGER because every engagement was filmed and counted against the radar. The strike force reached about one track in six within forty minutes; the gunships engaged about one in three within twenty. In the first dry season the pilots claimed about 560 trucks and the cell counted about 210, and the gunships' video counted 1,100–1,500 against their crews' claims of 2,600³. Southbound movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night to about 120 at the March peak, and J-2 estimated that 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle, more than the forces there needed²⁹.

On 12 April 1966 the Js of HARD CROSSING put 160 GBU-31s on eleven crossings in one night, and every one was down by dawn through low cloud; about twenty hours later the Global Hawk watched the first trucks cross again on a ford the engineers had reopened beside the broken bridges³⁰. For six weeks the flow fell, to about half at Tchepone. Phan Trong Tue's engineers read it correctly in a season, and their answer was the enemy's own initiative of the phase: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so Group 559 would own nothing it could measure. At Ban Laboy the cell counted one ford in April, four in July and nine by October. By October the trucks were about 15 percent above March's level. About 210 bridges, ferries and causeways died for about 1,900 kits, the tax on the flow was 10–15 percent at its May peak and about 5 percent by October, and what HARD CROSSING built was a road net more redundant than history's ever became. From February 1967 four Js laid Quickstrikes in the mouths of the Song Ca and Song Giang, and the cargo moved from the lighters to the roads, the radar's count of trucks below Vinh rising 25–30 percent while the tonnage into the panhandle stayed about the same². The drivers learned the orbit's changeover hours, Group 559 moved two battalions of 37mm guns to the crossings a year early, and on 2 March 1967 Ghostrider 22 was brought down by an unplotted 57mm battery at the Ban Laboy ford with six of its nine crew³. Delta's teams on the ground completed 71 of 94 missions and measured 84 truck parks, 31 gun positions and 19 storage points to a few metres, a third of them under canopy; a whole team was lost near the same ford in July 1967³¹.

Over the North the phase was a watch on repairs. The restrike watch kept the Red River crossings at Hanoi closed to rail on about 290 of 517 days in 1965–66, and its count of targets destroyed fell from about 140 a month to about 35 as the North stopped owning anything else worth a GBU-31. There was no Christmas pause, no petroleum strike in June 1966 because the fuel had burned in March 1965, and no Stennis hearings, because there were no restrictions to blame. The MiGs lived in China; in Operation Bolo on 2 January 1967 four rose to Robin Olds's F-4Cs and three were shot down, against seven in history's battle¹¹⁶. About 40–90 fewer aircraft were lost over the North in 1966–67.

The second lesson was learned in the South, at Con Thien. From September 1967 cells of three Js came every two to three hours with GBU-38s on trenches the Global Hawk imaged each night, and the siege lifted about three weeks early². Westmoreland took from the hill the lesson that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle. Over the posts, the Ghostriders answered on clear nights, and the Front moved its night attacks into the cloud: in November and December 1966 it attacked the coast of Binh Dinh on 18 of the 30 low-cloud nights and 2 of the 31 clear ones, and took eleven posts. On 1 January 1967 forty-eight AC-130U Spooky IIs were handed to a VNAF that had no one to fly them, and Lieutenant Colonel Pham Quang Khiem formed the 81st Gunship Wing³.

At midnight on 19–20 August 1967 Captain Le Minh Tuan was in the fire-control station of the 817th Squadron's Spooky II over Duc Hue when the RF post at Hiep Hoa came up on the net in Vietnamese. The cloud was down over the Vam Co Dong and the cane. Nobody in the aircraft could see the post. The radar could: the post was a bright return on the scope, and the men in the cane were where the post's commander said they were, on the north side, between the canal and the wire. Tuan did not need an American to repeat the post's words to him, or an adviser to relay his to the post. He talked to the post's commander directly, laid the offset, and the 40mm and the 105mm fired through the cloud at a place none of them could see. Twenty minutes later the post reported the assault broken, for one man lost. The next morning Khiem played the post commander's call over the loudspeakers at Bien Hoa³.

That was the first time a Vietnamese post had been defended at night from the air by Vietnamese, and it was the beginning of the one instrument of the air war the Republic would own. The A-1 squadrons paid for it with a fifth of their avionics technicians and two to three points of readiness.

## P3 · Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

The air war of 1968 won the year's most complete victory on the ground and watched the year decided elsewhere. From mid-December 1967 the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos, and the U-2S's signals payload fixed the 325C Division's headquarters on 2 January and the 304th's near Co Roc on 11 January. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration the command had ever held, and Westmoreland read it as the battle the enemy would give him. The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that attacked the cities on 30–31 January moved into them among the Tet crowds, where no radar could tell them from anyone²⁹.

Operation Niagara had two halves. The historical half, about 60 B-52s a day on the valleys, the parks and Route 9 in Laos, fell as a carpet half as dense, because the J carries about half the Big Belly's unguided bombs. The new half was a Global Hawk over the plateau every night imaging the ground within three kilometres of the perimeter each hour, and from 26 January cells of three Js every ninety minutes with GBU-38s on positions measured that night; from 15 February they were cleared to bomb 400 metres from the Marines and the 37th Ranger Battalion. The guns at Co Roc across the border were struck within hours of each mission and by mid-March fired only at night. Lang Vei had forty minutes' warning of twelve PT-76s on 7 February and fell all the same. The air bridge delivered about 14,356 tons and lost about a third fewer helicopters, because the flak around the approaches was struck each night³²².

On the morning of 1 March 1968 Major General Tran Quy Hai read the reports from the eastern trenches at his front command post in the hills west of the base. Three times his 304th Division had dug toward the Rangers' sector, on 17, 22 and 27 February, and three times the trenches had been destroyed at 500–800 metres from the wire, each one on the night it was opened. He had learned to expect the B-52s; his men had sat out the carpets in the bottoms of the trenches since January. These were different. They came every hour and a half, a few bombs at a time, each on a trench head, a bunker or a gun that had been dug the night before. On the night of 29 February the regiment that was to assault the Rangers had formed in the open, beyond the trenches it could not keep, and it had been broken before it reached the wire. Hai could not see how the Americans knew where his men had dug, only that they knew by morning. In mid-March he pulled the 325C back from the hills, and before the end of the month the 304th from the eastern trenches, about two weeks before history's withdrawal².

The record takes about 180 Marines killed against more than 274, and perhaps 3,500–4,000 PAVN dead to July against 2,469 acknowledged, as judgments built on the historical figures². The PAVN's own reading of the year, taken by the front command and not provoked by anything new in it, was the clearest enemy initiative of the area: it would not again lay siege to a position the Americans could measure. That was the one way of choosing its battles it had lost, and it chose all the others.

Over the North there was no partial halt. From April to October the campaign went on over the whole North, about 70 American aircraft were lost there, about 40 of them over the gun belts of Hanoi and Haiphong where history's campaign of those months sent no aircraft, and by October the North held almost nothing above the 19th parallel that a GBU-31 could kill and that it could not rebuild in a night. Rolling Thunder ended on 1 November on history's terms, having lost about 810–880 aircraft rather than 922¹. The Gulf watch kept counting: within a week trucks moved by day in columns of forty and fifty, and in December southbound movements on Route 15 were about three times October's²⁹. The count changed nothing about the halt, which had been traded for the talks.

It changed the next campaign. Commando Hunt opened on 15 November with Igloo White, the Ghostriders and the Js; at Ban Laboy the cell now counted fourteen fords and two underwater causeways, and the radar counted about 2,600–3,200 trucks destroyed or disabled and a cut of about a sixth in tonnage reaching the South²⁹. At Hue a single fire centre in the Citadel took every call for naval gunfire, artillery and aircraft, and the battle ended on 27 February rather than 2 March. The VNAF appears nowhere in the year's record except as a wing still training.

## P4 · Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV is the air war turned onto the sanctuaries and then handed, in part, to the Vietnamese. On 18 March 1969 forty-eight Js diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, about 1,600 tons in the boxes rather than 2,400, twelve of them putting 240 GBU-31s on storage sheds, a vehicle park and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload. Menu dropped about 75,000 tons rather than 108,823. About 300 measured structures died in the first six weeks; by June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and owned nothing in the open again². Delta's 61 foot missions in Cambodia in 1969 counted 94 cache sites and 11 field hospitals under rubber the radar could not see, and the folders are part of why the incursion of 1970 found the caches in days; COSVN had gone to Kratie on 19 March 1970 regardless³¹.

The radar's second ledger made Commando Hunt the honest campaign history's never was, and the campaign failed regardless. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks, which the Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; the radar counted about 4,500–5,500 destroyed or disabled. The Ghostriders of the 17th Squadron, five years on the panhandle, counted about 3,000–3,800 on their video at about 2.5–3.5 trucks a sortie and raised the net loss to the throughput at the season's peak to about 10–13 percent, against guns that had been on the Trail a year longer than history's. Two were lost in that dry season, one to a 57mm battery near Ban Bak and one to an SA-2 fired from across the border in February 1971, with eleven of their eighteen crewmen³. The pipeline from Vinh toward the A Shau had its pumping stations cut about eighty times and was bypassed within two to four days each time². The corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles and the count of southbound trucks rose to about 150–200 a night at the 1971 peak³³²⁹. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving, and in this war the headquarters wrote that sentence from its own counts.

Lam Son 719 showed what the air war could and could not do for the ARVN once the advisers stayed behind. At Lolo and Sophia, where the PAVN 2nd Division dug in, the trenches were struck each night at 400–700 metres and the bases held until evacuated; at Ranger North, Ranger South and Fire Support Base 31, where the 308th and 320th Divisions came straight at the wire in daylight, the bases fell much as they had historically. The ARVN on the hills passed no coordinates, because they had no means to give one a bomber could use. The record takes about a sixth fewer of the historical 108 helicopters lost³⁴². I Corps drew the lesson that would cost it: the bases it had held were the bases where the bombs came every two hours.

The night net changed hands. The 4th Squadron left Bien Hoa in October 1970 and the 14th left Nha Trang in December 1971, while the 16th at Da Nang and the 17th at Ubon stayed into 1972. The 81st Gunship Wing flew about 700–850 sorties a month at its best, against the Ghostriders' 1,300–1,500 at their peak, at about 55 percent of its aircraft operational; it covered about two-thirds of the posts the Ghostriders had covered, and in the north-east monsoon of 1969–70 the 819th answered about 60 percent of the posts' calls in Binh Dinh and Phu Yen on cloudy nights, where history's coast had almost nothing³. The posts beyond its reach, the islands of An Xuyen and the Highland posts of Kontum and Darlac, were attacked more often as the Americans left. The wing lost four aircraft in the three years, and about one crewman in six left it in 1971 for the airlines.

At Bien Hoa on the morning of 15 August 1970 Lieutenant Colonel Pham Quang Khiem had the report in front of him: Spooky 34, orbiting at 7,000 feet over a convoy on Route 7 near Snuol the night before, had been hit by a 37mm battery and gone down in the rubber east of the town. An ARVN Ranger company had found four of its thirteen men. He knew the nine names. He knew what the aircraft was for, too, because he had played the Hiep Hoa post's call on the loudspeakers three years before: it was for the posts, under cloud, in Vietnamese, and over Route 7 there were no posts, only a road the PAVN had filled with 12.7mm and 37mm guns since June. On 2 September he asked the Joint General Staff to withdraw the squadron to the Delta, where the posts needed it and the guns were few, and on 30 September it came home³.

The gunship was a weapon of the South's posts, and it died over defended roads. The Republic's other fire-support gain of the phase was on the ground: at Long My from February 1970 the subsector advisers handed the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets.

## P5 · Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

1972 was the air war's year in this record, the year the instruments of 1964 were finally allowed to do everything they had been built for, and it confirmed exactly what the earlier phases had shown. The armour of the Nguyen Hue offensive was seen before it crossed: from 12 March the Gulf watch tracked columns moving south at night from Vinh Linh, J-2 counted about 90–120 armoured vehicles within 20 kilometres of the DMZ by 25 March, and MACV warned I Corps on 27 March²⁹. When the 304th and 308th Divisions came across on 30 March under cloud, the Js bombed by radar on the crossings of the Ben Hai and destroyed or disabled about 20–30 armoured vehicles. The firebases fell all the same and Quang Tri fell on 1–2 May. The radar had seen the tanks; it had not made the 3rd Division steady.

Over the North, the bridges fell in the first week. On the night of 10 April twelve Js put 240 GBU-31s on the Ham Rong bridge, the Thanh Hoa rail yards and the tanks at Vinh, in cloud. On the night of Pocket Money the Js laid about 900 Quickstrikes in the approaches to the northern ports and the waterways south of Haiphong, about 200 of them extended-range mines released more than forty miles out. Linebacker flew 41,653 missions and lost 134 aircraft; the record judges that the electronic order of battle and the J's work on fixed targets saved perhaps 15–25 of those losses, and that traffic below Vinh in July stood at about 35 percent of March's against about 40 percent historically²²²⁹².

In the South the siege box did what it had done at Con Thien and Khe Sanh, and did it for the ARVN. At An Loc from 16 April cells of Js came every two hours with GBU-38s, the nearest 400 metres from the defenders' lines, and the town's shelling came at perhaps two-thirds of history's weight. History's AC-119s and AC-130Es did not exist; the night shift was flown by the 17th Squadron's Ghostriders from Ubon, the last American gunships in the theatre, and the 817th's Spooky IIs from Bien Hoa, 50–200 metres from the defenders, until the SA-7 drove both above 10,000 feet in the last days of April³⁰. At Kontum a detachment of the coordinate cell measured about 600 positions north and west of the city, about a third of the PAVN's guns were destroyed before the assault, and the 320th Division's 48th Regiment was broken short of the wire on 14 May².

On the morning of 12 May 1972 Brigadier General Le Van Hung was in his command bunker in the southern half of An Loc, and the northern half of the town was the 9th Division's. The assault had come at dawn on the 11th, after a night when the radar had shown the tanks and trucks moving up through the rubber, and it had broken into the town. In history's war the bombers could not have touched it there: the carpets fell a kilometre from friendly troops, and the enemy was closer than that. Here the American detachment at Long Binh measured the buildings and bunkers the attackers had taken, one by one, and passed them to the bomber cells. Through the morning Hung heard the 500-pound bombs come down 300–400 metres from his line, one on each building, never a carpet, and his men did not have to leave their positions to be safe from them. By noon the penetration was gone².

The ARVN ended the year having learned more completely than history's that it held besieged towns with American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts. The PAVN's 1972 losses stand about 7,000–10,000 above history's, most of the difference made by the siege box³⁵. Linebacker II ended the year: the 729 sorties were J sorties, a third of them carrying twenty GBU-31s each, the SA-2 sites at Kinh No and Phuc Yen were destroyed on the fourth night rather than the eighth, about six Js were lost rather than fifteen B-52s, and neither Bach Mai hospital nor Kham Thien Street was hit². The terms were October's. The VNAF, fourth largest air force in the world after Enhance Plus³⁶, is absent from the decision of every siege, and the agreement about to be signed took the instruments that decided them away.

## P6 · Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The air war's last phase in this record is the war of its absence, and the absence was felt more sharply than history's because more had been taken away. Until 15 August 1973 the Js bombed Cambodia in support of Lon Nol, and on 6 August, where historically a B-52 whose navigator failed to set a beacon offset killed about 137 people in Neak Luong, the J released on its own GPS into its box in the forest. Case–Church passed in the same words. Sorrell's cell was disbanded in September and its folders went to Clark; the Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell, and there was nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to².

The one instrument that stayed was the gunship wing. In 1973 the 81st Gunship Wing, about 36–40 Spooky IIs after its losses, flew about 600–700 sorties a month on contractor teams paid by the appropriation; in the war of the flags it answered about two calls in three in Kien Hoa, Vinh Binh and Dinh Tuong, and the posts history lost in those nights were about a third fewer³. The posts the Americans had covered and the Vietnamese did not, about a third of them, were lost at nearly history's rate. In 1974 a fiscal 1975 appropriation of about $950 million, stretched by the domestic plants, put about a third fewer VNAF aircraft on the ground by December²⁷, and the Joint General Staff put the wing's Binh Thuy detachment first on the fuel list.

The Military Region 9 Party Committee planned its rice campaign of 1974 in Chuong Thien on its own reckoning, before Hanoi's directives reached it, and on the reading that the VNAF was grounded for want of fuel. That was the enemy's initiative in the area in this phase, and it was wrong about the gunships. From 15 March the battalions attacked the canal posts of Kien Hung and Long My one or two a night, and the Spooky IIs answered about two calls in three, through cloud when the cloud came in May, on the district towns' beacons. Of 64 posts attacked, about 46 held³. The harvest behind them went to the government's buyers, and the region asked COSVN for a division and was refused.

Elsewhere the ARVN fought 1974 with plans written for an air force that no longer came. At Thuong Duc the 79th Ranger Battalion dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, while the 304th Division dug to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling; the first Spooky II over the trenches went down with all thirteen of its crew to the division's own guns and SA-7s³. The Airborne's counter-attacks on Hill 1062 were planned in the expectation that the crest would be broken from the air first, and the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it². At Phu Loc the 1st Division's counter-attack had VNAF UH-1Hs for lift and resupply on most days instead of some, and the hills were retaken in the third week of November rather than on 10 December²⁷.

On 13 December 1974 the province chief of Phuoc Long sat in his headquarters at Phuoc Binh with the plan his staff had drafted in 1973, men who had served at An Loc. The plan said the town would hold for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege, and it named the bomber cells by the call signs General Hollingsworth's men had used in 1972. The PAVN 4th Corps had attacked the province the day before with two divisions and fourteen T-54s. He did what the plan told him to do. He asked III Corps for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan". III Corps sent the request to the Joint General Staff, the Joint General Staff sent it to the Defense Attaché Office, and the Defense Attaché Office had no means to answer it. He did not hear back. The 130mm guns silenced his two 155mm and four 105mm howitzers in the first week, and the last post fell on 6 January 1975³⁷².

The attack had an author: Lieutenant General Tran Van Tra argued it in Hanoi in November as a test of the American response and won. On 18 December Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers, in those words and with more conviction than history's. In 1975 the VNAF flew the Republic's last fights on fuel the appropriation had bought: the counter-attack from Phuoc An toward Ban Me Thuot on 15–18 March went in lifted by VNAF CH-47s and UH-1Hs and was broken as history's was; at Pleiku the C-130As and C-123Ks flew 25–35 sorties a day into Cu Hanh until the 968th's guns closed the runway on 9 April, and the 819th made the 320A's assaults about a fifth dearer and lost two aircraft in a night to radar-laid guns²⁷³. At the end of 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and still has an air force, which history's did not after April. It can lift, resupply, strike with unguided bombs and hold a post at night through cloud, and it cannot break a siege. The record traces the wing's fuel and not its spares, and CP04 judged that it flew 1974–75 partly by cannibalising grounded aircraft³⁰.
