## 1. The state of the war

### 1964–65: the held belt, the night net and the first raids

The war this headquarters has written begins on 1 January 1964 with three decrees signed in one session: land, police and Phung Hoang. Through 1964 the posts went into the villages of the Delta and the Saigon belt one at a time. The district centres opened behind them, the first at Phu Tan in An Giang on 16 March 1964. The platoons beside the posts carried their own PRC-10s from April. Over the same posts flew the Ghostriders, the AC-130J gunships of Farm Gate, from January 1964. When Farm Gate's clearance was found to take half an hour, the Joint General Staff gave the district chief authority to clear fire within 300 metres of a post. From December 1964 the wing did not go below 3,500 feet, after Tam Quan. Those two rules decided where the night war went for the next eleven years.

MACV-JSOC was formed on 15 August 1964. Delta arrived in three lifts between 28 August and 12 September, 1,310 officers and men. It mounted 31 raids in the Saigon belt and the upper Delta between 11 October and 31 December. Nine reached a listed cadre and nineteen found the house empty. Its first finished operation, NIGHT HERON, took six of the eleven men of the Long An province committee at a sampan mooring in March 1965, with the finance section's ledgers. The near-miss at Trung Lap on 7 March 1965 showed what the new headquarters was for: a restricted area passed to every Vietnamese headquarters but not to a platoon 1,400 metres away.

Above the North, the B-52Js burned the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly on the first night of March 1965. TIDE TABLE closed the sea route, and the arms went to Sihanoukville instead. The general reserve was broken at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and came back from both with more of its men than on the historical course. The battalions are noticeably steadier than history's. The enemy still chooses where they fight.

### 1966–67: the infrastructure broken in the held belt, the main-force war unchanged

The phase is two wars. In the held belt the Front's resident infrastructure was driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967. The arrest count fell because there was little left to arrest, and this headquarters said so to Komer in writing on 3 April 1967. Delta mounted about 1,000 raids in thirty months. It took 61 province and region committee members in 1966–67 at a price of fourteen operators killed and four missing in Laos. In September 1967 the committees of the upper Delta were found to have gone to Memot in Cambodia. They visited their districts by courier at two months' remove, under COSVN's three-sentence rule of April. The Mo Duc raid of 11 February 1967 killed a schoolteacher and four of his family on a file built from one feud. The word Phoenix was in the American press by 10 March, three years early. The audited count of 30 June, about 14,500 neutralized since January 1965, was the first figure on the programme the newspapers never had to correct.

In the main-force war nothing turned. Operation HARD CROSSING cut every crossing in the panhandle from 12 April 1966. By the end of October the trucks south of Tchepone were about 15 percent above the March level, on a road net with no single point of failure. The estuary field of 26 February 1967 stopped the steel lighters south of Vinh. It did not stop the junks and sampans. The Sihanoukville count of March 1967 settled an argument three years early and changed no rule. The North's fuel was no longer a target after March 1965, and McNamara's doubts hardened a year early.

The ground war shows the reforms in what did not happen. On 4 May 1966 the 272nd Regiment declined to spring its ambush in the Xa Cam rubber on a battalion that walked its flanks. It ambushed instead an RF convoy at Bau Bang that rode. At Hau My on 16 November a company lost its officers on a dike and held its base of fire under a schooled platoon sergeant. The enemy's answer was to kill without fighting. South of Da Nang, the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines lost about two-thirds of its dead to mines and booby traps in its first year. On the II Corps coast the Front attacked on monsoon nights, when the Ghostriders could not go down. Eleven posts fell in Hoai Nhon, Phu My and Tam Quan in two months of 1966. The VNAF's Spooky IIs arrived on 1 January 1967. The 817th flew its first all-Vietnamese sortie over Hiep Hoa on 19–20 August.

### 1968: Tet against a broken infrastructure

The General Offensive came on its historical date against a Delta whose committees governed from Memot and a Saigon belt whose committees governed from the Ho Bo woods. Delta was used for nine days as urban assault infantry in Saigon and took 14 command posts for nine dead. Its raid rate in the Delta then fell to about 25 a month, in the window when the Front's provincial cadres were most exposed by their own Tet losses. Commando Hunt opened on 15 November with the Ghostriders, not Spectres, over the Trail and the radar as its third sensor.

### 1969–71: the inheritance

Phase IV is the phase the American lever lapses. Resolution 9 of July 1969 conceded the land issue in the titled hamlets and told the Delta's committees to govern villages with posts by visiting cadres. Menu's precision had one season of targets before COSVN's rear services owned nothing in the open. The Cambodian incursion went in on a map five years in the making against a COSVN that had moved to Kratie on 19 March 1970. Lam Son 719 went into Laos on 8 February 1971 without the Delta teams that had watched the 559th Group for five years, because Cooper–Church took them out on 19 January. The Son Tay raid was cancelled on 2 November 1970 when two men of G Squadron reported the camp empty.

In Saigon the palace took the board's general-officer list, the Directorate, the national file and the Commission between January 1970 and September 1971. It broke the NCO promotion gate by the decree of 1 November 1970, which the training command half restored. Below the palace the work went on. The Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap depot closed the loop the handover would otherwise have left open. At Long My the subsector advisers handed the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets. The 81st Gunship Wing ended 1971 holding the night over about two-thirds of the posts the Ghostriders had held, in Vietnamese.

### 1972–75: the reprieve

The Easter Offensive's armour was struck through cloud by B-52s before it crossed the DMZ. The siege box broke the assault on An Loc on 12 May. The ARVN held its besieged towns more cheaply than history's and learned more completely that it held them with American bombs. In the ration of 1974 the Joint General Staff put the gunship wing's Binh Thuy detachment first on the fuel list. Military Region 9's rice campaign, planned on the reading that the VNAF was grounded, met the gunships every night. In 1975 the 819th flew over the siege of Pleiku from Nha Trang every night from 21 March, and the 821st met at Thuong Duc the anti-aircraft limit the dossier had named. The end state stands at rung (b), with the fall expected between February and May 1976.

### What the cumulative force has and has not done

Fifteen memoranda have given the Republic a held belt that the Front governs only by courier, an army better led and trained at every level below division, a police and a file that worked to a warrant until the palace took them, and a night that belongs to the government wherever a gun or a gunship can reach a post. They have not changed who chooses the battle, what comes down the Trail, or the politics of the palace. Memoranda 014 and 015 fit that pattern. Delta broke the Front's provincial government in the upper Delta and pushed it into Cambodia. It did not reach COSVN and could not stay in Laos. The gunships took the clear nights from the Front. The enemy moved to the cloudy nights, the hamlet's edge and the mine. The war is longer by about a year and ends the same way.

## 3. Interaction sweep

**ARVN.self_sufficiency.** P015b's two values, 2–6 points more of the RVNAF's fire support its own by 1971 and 3–8 points by 1974, depend on the wing's maintenance. The record carries the fuel side of the 1974 ration. It does not carry the spares. The AC-130U's radar, fire control and engines are not in the Bien Hoa rebuild works, which make cartridges, batteries, wire and shell bodies. Nothing in Phases IV to VI says where the wing's spares came from after 1973. My judgment is that they came from the aid appropriation alone, as the A-1s' and the F-5s' did. The wing flew in 1974–75 by cannibalising grounded aircraft, so the two to three points of operational rate that the A-1 squadrons lost to the wing in 1967 were lost again in 1974, this time by the wing². That does not change any event in the record, because the Binh Thuy detachment, the 819th over Pleiku and the 821st at Thuong Duc all fly in it. It does lower the confidence of P015b's Phase VI value. P010b's maintenance sergeants and P015b interact only through the technicians, and EV015-5 and EV015-6 carry that.

**US.casualties.** P014a adds 5–25 American dead in 1967 and P015a takes 80–400 away. Both mechanisms are told: Delta's dead in EV014-3, the gunships over Plei Me, X-Ray and Albany in Phase II. The record does not add them, and it should. The net is 55–395 fewer, or about 0.6 to 4 percent of the 9,378 of 1967³. That is too small to move the political tolerance of the war, which is how C011-1 and the end state treat it. P004a, P009a and P010a each claim "modest" savings in the first minutes of contact. Their sum is never stated anywhere in the record. That belongs to calibration.

**LOG.helicopter_hours.** P014a's lift dependence is met in the record by Delta's own E Squadron, so Delta does not compete with the task force's raid cycle or with Dustoff. The interaction that is missing is the one Phase IV implies: the Vietnamese special unit of 1969 flew on the VNAF's 219th Helicopter Squadron (P3), which also flew the territorial forces' medevac. The record is silent on the competition. I judge it marginal, because the 219th's night sorties were few.

**GVN.admin_capacity.** Memoranda 014 and 015 do not touch it. The pairs that matter are P002b with P007c and P010b: the province chiefs come from the officer pool and must release territorial NCOs to the courses. Both are told (EV002-6, EV010-6, E03). One interaction is new in memorandum 015 and is already in the record: the district chief's authority to clear fire within 300 metres of a post made the merit list's district chiefs the arbiters of civilian harm at night. That is a load on administrative judgment, not on capacity, and it is recorded.

**SUBVERT.defection.** The record does not carry the interaction between Delta's raids and the targeted offers to named cadres. Both work from the same file and the same names. A raid on a named cadre removes the offer, and an offer to a cadre warns him. The record shows cadres moving out of reach under the three-sentence rule, not rallying. I judge that from 1966 the raids lowered defection among the senior cadre of the Saigon belt and the upper Delta, because a cadre who expects a raid moves to Memot, where no constable's door is open². P012b's claim that a cadre with a reason to fear the raid rallies first holds for district and village cadres. They had nowhere to go. It does not hold for province committees, which did. Nothing in the chapters needs to change. The rallier counts are not broken out by level.

## 5. Calibration

**⁴ about a third fewer ARVN battalions rendered ineffective in 1966–67.** It rests on cases like OPEN ROAD and on a notebook taken in January 1967. The chapter says it cannot be measured. Under A13 it is low reliability, honestly labelled. It moved by a traceable mechanism: an ambush not sprung.

**⁵ and⁶, the stacked fractions.** P2's *How the phase ends* claims about one in eight fewer battalions broken beyond the officer reforms (009). It then claims one in six to one in five fewer beyond the officer reforms and the basic course (010). Each is stated as an increment on the last, so the record does carry their compounding. Taken together with⁴, the claim is that about half the historical count of broken battalions in 1966–67 did not happen. Each step rests on cases, and none was checked against the others' cases. This is assertion stacked on assertion. I recommend Part 2 state the combined figure once and give it low confidence.

**⁷, Phung Hoang's audited count.** About 14,500 by 30 June 1967, about 18,000 by the end of 1967 and about 63,000 by the end of 1971. The series is internally consistent. It rests on the Commission's audit and moves by a traceable mechanism. It is the most reliable headline figure in the record.

**⁸, US.casualties from P015a, 80–400 fewer dead in 1967.** The mechanism is told at Plei Me and the Ia Drang. The range is wide and its confidence is low, correctly. P014a's 5–25 more, also low, is consistent with Delta's fourteen dead in thirty months plus the losses on cross-border reconnaissance.

**⁹, COSVN's size and date.** The chapters and memorandum 014 differed. Phase IV said "a few hundred people" moving "from about 19 April", and memorandum 014 says about 2,400 people at Kratie from 19 March. The record should carry the memorandum's figures, which rest on¹⁰. Both are fixed in the staged chapter.

**¹¹, the Delta's posts.** "Attacked about a third less often" when they can call (P2). This rests on district logs and is traceable.

**Figures that differ between chapters and memoranda.** Beyond COSVN, the differences are those fixed in §2: the RVNAF's rifle in 1969, the AC-130 over the Trail in 1968, the age of MACV-JSOC in 1965, and the radio at Trung Lap. EV000-76 is the one left for an erratum.

## 6. The experience of the war

**A Popular Forces platoon leader, Ap My Loi post, Cai Lay district, Dinh Tuong, the night of 11 December 1967.** The claim tested: on clear nights the district's RF move by road again, and fire first relaxes. From the post the change is visible as sound. He hears the gunship's engines over the canal road an hour before the RF company's trucks. He no longer tells the relief to wait for daylight. On the night the cloud comes down he hears nothing, and he tells them to wait. The claim holds, and it holds only on the clear nights, as the record says.

**A hamlet chief, a hamlet on Route 1 north of Bong Son, Hoai Nhon district, Binh Dinh, 27 October 1966.** The claim tested: the gunship had taken the clear nights from the Front by 1966. He watched the post on the hill fall in the rain while an aircraft he could hear and not see dropped two bombs on the wrong field. To him the gunship is something that comes when it does not matter. The change is not visible from the coast in the monsoon, and that confirms the claim's limit: the gain is in the Delta and the Saigon belt, and the coast's night belongs to the weather until the 819th.

**A district committee member of the Front, Cai Be district, at Memot, Cambodia, October 1967.** The claim tested: the district administration runs by courier at two months' remove. He has been in his district six nights since April. His couriers bring him tax receipts eight weeks old and the names of three collectors who were taken while he was gone. He knows the district is still the Front's at night in the hamlets the government holds only by day, and that he governs it as a guest. The claim is visible, and it is sharper from here than in the record: the collections fall because the man who sets them is not there to argue them.

**A porter of the 559th Group, the Ban Laboy ford on Route 912, Laos, the night of 30 October 1966.** The claim tested: HARD CROSSING built the enemy a road net with no single point of failure. He has carried stone for the paved ford under the water for six weeks. There are nine fords now. He has never seen a bomber, and the trucks have not stopped once in his time. From where he stands the bombing is labour, not loss. The claim is visible, and so is its price, which is his nights.

**An American district adviser, Long My district, Chuong Thien, May 1971.** The claim tested: the call for fire passed to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets before the Americans left. He is the last adviser in the district and has not keyed his own set for a fire mission since February. The PF post commanders call the district gun in Vietnamese, and the district gun answers. He writes this in his last report and is not sure it will survive the aid cut. The claim is visible. His doubt is the record's doubt about 1974.

**An official of the Directorate of Defence Industry, Saigon, September 1971.** The claim tested: the Directorate went to the palace "keeping its output and losing its honesty". He signs the same monthly returns for cartridges and batteries as last year. The figures are right. What has changed is who reads them first and which contracts go to whose relatives. From the ministry the output and the honesty look like different ledgers, and only the second has changed hands. The claim holds as written.
