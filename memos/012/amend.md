## EV012-1 · The combined command stands up, and Khanh's coup tests it · 1 Jan – 31 Mar 1964 · Saigon; the Joint General Staff compound and the MACV headquarters at Tan Son Nhut; the four corps headquarters at Da Nang, Pleiku, Bien Hoa and Can Tho · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

The Combined Operations Command, Vietnam, was proclaimed in the same session of 1 January 1964 as the land, police and Phung Hoang decrees. It was the one decree of the session that the Military Revolutionary Council signed with visible reluctance. General Paul D. Harkins became combined commander. Lieutenant General Tran Van Don, the council's chief of the Joint General Staff, named Major General Le Van Kim as Vietnamese deputy. Kim was the general of the council most trusted in the American Mission and least trusted by the corps commanders [X-012]. The Korean and Australian deputies existed on paper. Seoul sent a brigadier general with a liaison staff of eleven. Canberra appointed the commander of the Australian Army Training Team, Colonel F. P. Serong, as its interim deputy, with a staff of four [H] [X-012]. In January 1964 their contingents were a Korean field hospital and Taekwondo instructors not yet arrived, and about 30 Australian advisers in the field [M012·S4] [M012·S5].

The staff was built in a requisitioned school building on the edge of Tan Son Nhut. By the end of January it held about 260 officers: 140 American from the MACV J2, J3 and J5, 110 Vietnamese drawn from the JGS and the corps staffs, and a handful of Koreans and Australians [X-012]. The first combined operations order, for a sweep of the Plain of Reeds by the ARVN 7th Division with the 9th Division blocking across the IV Corps boundary, was drafted in Vietnamese and English on 22 January. It was never issued.

At dawn on 30 January Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took the government (EV000-1). He was a corps commander under the combined commander's operational control, and he used the command's own network. The combined centre at Da Nang, still only a communications room and nine officers, passed I Corps traffic to Saigon on the night of the coup, and the American duty officer had no reason to stop it [X-012]. Kim was arrested with the Dalat generals that morning. For nine days the combined command had no Vietnamese deputy and no Vietnamese orders. The corps commanders took theirs from Khanh, and Harkins issued none, because there was nobody on the Vietnamese side to countersign them.

What saved the command was the same lever that saved the decrees. On 2 February the President's note to Khanh carried the Mission's condition on the decrees (EV000-1). Harkins and Ambassador Lodge added the combined command to it on 4 February, in a meeting at which Khanh asked how an American general could command his army. Lodge answered that the command had not been designed to command a coup and would not be used against one, and that the aid that paid the RVNAF's salaries was conditional on the command's continuing [X-012]. Khanh needed the Americans more than he resented the arrangement, and he made a counter-offer. The combined command would continue, the Vietnamese deputy would be his man, Major General Nguyen Van Thieu, and Thieu would be made Khanh's own chief of staff as well, so that every combined order passed through Khanh's office before it reached a corps [X-012]. Harkins accepted on 9 February. The first combined order went out on 14 February, three weeks late and countersigned by a deputy whose first loyalty was to the man who had just used the command's radios to take the state.

By the end of March the command had issued eleven operations orders and had learned two things that no study had predicted [X-012]. The first was that operational control meant exactly what the junta of the day wished it to mean. Under Minh it had been a concession, and under Khanh it was a channel through the prime minister's office. The second was that the Vietnamese staff officers were better than their American colleagues had expected and their orders slower. A combined order took about four days to clear Khanh's office in February and about thirty hours by late March, as Thieu learned which orders Khanh wished to see (F012-11) [X-012]. On 26 March the combined staff sent the ARVN 21st Division across the IV Corps boundary into the Ca Mau forests on a combined order in 26 hours, the first time in the war an ARVN division had moved across a corps boundary on a foreign commander's order. It found a base camp and no regiment.

## EV012-2 · Operation DRY SEASON: the Can Giuoc security section · 20 Jul – 18 Dec 1964 · Can Giuoc, Can Duoc and Rach Kien Districts, Long An Province; the Rung Sat edge of Nha Be, Gia Dinh Province · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

The task force's first campaign was aimed at the men who had killed the title holders of Phuoc Lai (EV001-3). The Long An provincial security section and the Can Giuoc district committee's armed propaganda teams had killed 37 title holders and 9 land committee members between 9 June and 14 July, and the province had suspended titling in the two districts [X-001]. On 20 July the task force's III Corps task group, then about 180 strong, opened Operation DRY SEASON from a compound at Tan An under an American Special Forces major, Thomas R. Garvin, with a Special Branch captain, Nguyen Huu Phuoc, as his deputy [X-012]. Its raid element was one Special Forces detachment, a SEAL platoon of SEAL Team One on loan from the Rung Sat, and the Long An Provincial Reconnaissance Unit of about 70 men. Its lift was three UH-1Bs and two UH-1Ds of the 120th Aviation Company, kept on call at Tan Son Nhut.

The first two months produced almost nothing. The task group had the Long An Special Branch's files, six months old and built on hamlets the police held by day only. The district centre at Can Giuoc did not yet exist. Of 19 operations in August and September, 14 found the target gone, 3 took a man the magistrate at Tan An released within 72 hours, and 2 took real cadres who knew nothing of the security section [X-012]. The 2026 cycle was attempted and abandoned in the same weeks. The task group's American planners ran raids two and three nights running on targets the exploitation cell had not confirmed, and the magistrate released 11 of 17 detainees in September (EC012-12) [X-012].

The turn came from a ledger. On 3 October a Rach Kien PF platoon ambushed a tax team on the canal at Long Huu and killed two men. One of them carried a notebook listing the district committee's monthly collections by hamlet, with a courier's route to the security section's base in the nipa swamps on the Nha Be edge of the Rung Sat [X-012]. The exploitation cell had the notebook translated by morning. On the night of 5–6 October the SEAL platoon went up the Soai Rap in two STAB boats and a PBR borrowed from the Vietnamese Navy's river assault group. It inserted at the mouth of the Rach Ong Chuong and walked into the courier station at 0340 [X-012]. It took four men alive and a fifth was killed reaching for a K-50. One of the four was the security section's deputy chief, Ba Lien, who had commanded the Phuoc Lai killings. He was handed to Phuoc at Tan An at 0710 and before a magistrate on 7 October [X-012].

What Ba Lien told the interrogators, and what his papers told the exploitation cell, opened the section. Over the following ten weeks the task group mounted 41 operations in Can Giuoc, Can Duoc, Rach Kien and the Nha Be edge. They took 58 people on warrant, of whom the magistrates released 19, and killed 9, two of them men whom the Commission's audit in January could not show to be cadres [X-012]. Of the security section's 23 members, 14 were dead, in prison or had rallied by 18 December. Its chief had gone to the Rung Sat proper, where the task group could not follow without a battalion [X-012]. One PRU member and one SEAL were killed, the SEAL by a booby-trapped bunker at Ap Dong Thanh on 2 November.

Titling resumed in the eight Can Giuoc hamlets with PF posts in February 1965, two months earlier than on the course of memorandum 001 (EV001-3) [X-012]. The other hamlets waited. The section was rebuilt by the spring of 1965 from cadres sent down from the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region, but the new men did not know the hamlets, and no title holder was killed in Can Giuoc in 1965 [X-012]. DRY SEASON was a small operation that showed two things: the task force was worth nothing in its first two months, and it was worth a security section once it held one notebook.

## EV012-3 · The red cards: Seoul, Canberra and the combined command · 8 Jan – 30 Jul 1965 · Seoul; Canberra; Saigon; Bien Hoa air base; Di An, Bien Hoa Province · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

The Korean and Australian governments' consent to combined command, stipulated by this headquarters' instructions, was tested when their troops arrived. In Seoul the National Assembly debated the dispatch of the Dove Force of engineers and a security battalion in January 1965 [M012·S4]. The opposition asked whether Korean soldiers would be commanded by Americans abroad as they had refused to be commanded at home in matters of domestic order. President Park's defence minister, Kim Sung-eun, answered that they would be under the combined commander's operational control "for the tasks agreed between the governments and no others" [X-012]. That was the red card, stated in public. The Dove Force reached Di An in March under a Korean brigadier, and it took combined orders for its construction tasks without dispute, because it had no others to take [M012·S4].

The Australians had already given their consent at a price. When 1st Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment arrived at Bien Hoa in June 1965 and joined the 173rd Airborne Brigade, Canberra's instructions to Brigadier O. D. Jackson, commanding Australian Army Force Vietnam, set three conditions on the combined command [H] [X-012]. The battalion would not be broken up below company level. It would not be employed in the Delta. And any operation expected to cost it more than a stated number of casualties would be referred to Canberra [X-012]. The third condition was the one the combined staff had not expected. It meant that a combined order to the Australian battalion carried a casualty estimate, and that the estimate went to a capital. On 28 June, when the 173rd went into War Zone D, the combined staff's estimate for the battalion's part was sent to Canberra overnight and approved by 0600 [X-012]. Nothing in the operation required the delay. The precedent did.

The Koreans' combat consent was the larger question, and it was settled on paper in July. The working arrangement that historically gave the Koreans MACV's support without American command (6 September 1965) was replaced here by a combined agreement of 23 July [M012·S2] [X-012]. It placed the Korean field forces under the combined commander's operational control within an agreed area of responsibility on the II Corps coast, gave the Korean commander a right to refer any order outside that area to Seoul, and required a Korean officer to countersign any combined order to a Korean formation. Lieutenant General Chae Myung-shin signed it in Saigon. He told the combined commander, General William C. Westmoreland, that he would carry out every order he countersigned and countersign only the ones he would carry out [X-012].

That sentence defined the Korean contingent for the rest of the war. It was operational control in name and a Korean veto in practice. The combined staff learned to write orders for the Korean divisions that the Korean commander had already agreed to. It was, I judge, a better arrangement than the historical one for exactly one reason: the Koreans' orders and the ARVN's now came from the same plan, so that the seam between the Korean area and the ARVN 22nd Division's was planned by one staff instead of negotiated by two (F012-9) [X-012].

## EV012-4 · Operation FOLLOW THROUGH · 2 Aug 1965 – 30 Jun 1966 · Cu Chi and Trang Bang Districts, Hau Nghia Province; Hoc Mon District, Gia Dinh Province; the Ho Bo and Boi Loi woods and the Filhol rubber, Binh Duong Province · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

FOLLOW THROUGH was the task force's answer to the district committee that STILL WATER had driven out of Hoc Mon. Five of the Hoc Mon committee's 14 members had escaped STILL WATER and governed the district's posted villages from Cu Chi by courier (EV006-2) [X-006]. With them in the Cu Chi hamlets along the Saigon River sat the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's finance and economy section, which collected taxes from the market gardens of Hoc Mon and Go Vap and the rubber workers of the Filhol plantation [X-012]. On 2 August 1965 the III Corps task group opened the operation from Hoc Mon district town, with Major Garvin commanding and Lieutenant Colonel Phuoc of the Special Branch as his deputy. The task group was now about 420 strong, with a Special Forces detachment, two SEAL platoons, the Gia Dinh and Hau Nghia PRUs and four UH-1Ds and two UH-1B gunships of its own [X-012].

The operation ran on the cycle the task force had learned in Long An: an arrest or a rallier, exploitation the same morning, the follow-on that night (EC012-7). Its first two months took eleven of the region finance section's couriers and none of its members, because every courier carried only the next courier's name [X-012]. The break was a woman again, as it had been in STILL WATER. On 14 October a 23-year-old cashier of the finance section, arrested at a market stall in Hoc Mon with 41,000 piasters in a rice sack, rallied on the 16th against a named offer that her mother would not be detained (EC012-10). She described the section's three meeting houses in An Nhon Tay and Phu Hoa Dong [X-012].

The raid on Phu Hoa Dong on the night of 19–20 October was the task force's first contested-belt operation at full scale. Two SEAL platoons and the Gia Dinh PRU inserted by helicopter at 0230 into a paddy east of the hamlet. A Regional Forces company of Cu Chi district held a cordon on the river side, and a company of the ARVN 25th Division's 49th Regiment closed the land side. The III Corps combined centre had tasked that company at 1500 the previous afternoon on the task group's request, against the division's own plan for the night (F012-6) [X-012]. The team took the section's deputy chief and two cadres alive in a tunnel entrance under a family altar, with the section's ledger for the first nine months of 1965. One SEAL was killed by a grenade thrown from a second tunnel mouth. The PRU killed three men in the paddy who had fired on the cordon. One of them was the deputy chief's 16-year-old son, carrying a K-44 rifle [X-012].

The ledger was the most valuable document the task force had yet taken. It recorded the region's tax collections hamlet by hamlet across Hoc Mon, Go Vap, Cu Chi and Trang Bang, and it named the collectors. Between November 1965 and March 1966 the task group made 64 arrests on warrants drawn from it, of which the magistrates released 21. It killed 17 people in raids, of whom the Commission's audit of May 1966 could show 13 to be cadres and could not show 4 [X-012]. Of the Hoc Mon committee's five surviving members, three were taken and one rallied. The fifth, the committee's secretary, Nguyen Van Tu, left Cu Chi for the Ho Bo woods in January 1966 with the finance section's remaining members, as the American 25th Infantry Division arrived at Cu Chi and began clearing the plantation edge [X-012] [H]. The task group could not follow him there. The woods were the 165th VC Regiment's base area and a main-force problem (F012-8).

What FOLLOW THROUGH achieved shows in the Front's own accounts. The region finance section's collections from the posted villages of Hoc Mon and Go Vap in the first half of 1966 were about one-eighth of the first half of 1964's, against about one-fifth on the course of memorandum 006. Its collections from Cu Chi's contested hamlets fell by about two-fifths, where on that course they had grown [X-012]. Nobody governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi after March 1966. The Front governed it from the Ho Bo woods, eighteen kilometres further out, by couriers who now took two nights rather than one, and it never re-established a tax collector who lived in a Hoc Mon hamlet before 1968 (F012-27) [X-012]. The operation cost the task force two SEALs, four PRU members and a Regional Forces soldier killed. It cost the Front the region's tax administration west of Saigon for about a year. It cost the families of the four men the audit could not clear a reason to hate the government, and the census told the Front which families they were (F012-16).

## EV012-5 · The II Corps list and its abandonment · 15 Nov 1965 – 2 Apr 1966 · Tuy An and Song Cau Districts, Phu Yen Province; Phu My and Phu Cat Districts, Binh Dinh Province; Qui Nhon · NEW · 012
*Initiative: both*

The II Corps task group was formed at Qui Nhon in November 1965, as the ROK Capital Division settled into its area around Qui Nhon and Phu Cat [M012·S4]. It worked on thinner ground than any other. The coastal districts of Binh Dinh and Phu Yen had been the Viet Minh's Interzone V for nine years and the Front's since 1960, and the Special Branch held files on hamlets it had never entered at night [X-012]. The district centres opened there in 1965 had little but ralliers' accounts. The task group's commander, an American lieutenant colonel, Harold J. Beck, adopted in December what the 2026 task forces had called a high-value target list: 64 named members of the Phu Yen and Binh Dinh province committees, to be captured or, if they resisted, killed [X-012]. Its raid elements were a Special Forces detachment, a Korean special-forces team of the 1st Airborne Special Forces Group attached to the Capital Division, and the two provinces' PRUs.

Military Region 5 had its own plans for the coast, made for its own reasons. Its Party Committee had decided in October 1965 to hold the 2nd VC Regiment and the 3rd PAVN Division's regiments in the An Lao, Kim Son and Suoi Ca valleys through the dry season of 1966. One reason was to protect the rice harvest of the coastal plain, on which its own forces lived. The other was that it refused the B3 Front's request to send a regiment to the Highlands. The region's leaders held that the coast, not the plateau, was the decisive ground of their war [X-012]. That rivalry between two of Hanoi's commands kept a division on the Binh Dinh coast through the winter, and it kept the province committees close to the regiments that protected them.

<!-- initiative -->
The list was worked from January to March 1966. It produced 11 operations, 9 detainees and 14 dead [X-012]. Three of the dead were on the list. On the night of 19–20 January the Korean team and the Phu Yen PRU raided a house in Hoa Thanh village, Tuy An District, on a rallier's word that the Phu Yen committee's security chief slept there. They killed four men who ran from the house and found no papers. The Commission's inspectors, who reached Tuy An on 3 February, found that the four were a family who had sheltered a cadre once, two years before, and that the rallier had a land dispute with them [X-012]. Under the 1964 police statute a killing without a warrant was a matter for the magistrate. On 16 February the Phu Yen magistrate referred two members of the Korean team for homicide, and the Korean commander refused to surrender them [X-012]. Chae Myung-shin's position was that his soldiers answered to Korean military law and nobody else's. The Phu Yen province committee's position was a leaflet, in every market from Tuy Hoa to Song Cau within the week, naming the four men and the Korean unit.

On 2 April the combined commander and the task force commander ended the list in II Corps. The Korean team was withdrawn from the task force at Seoul's request, the American team was kept, and the II Corps task group returned to warrant arrests in the held towns of the coast and named offers in the contested districts (EC012-11) [X-012]. The episode cost the task force its credibility in Phu Yen for two years and gave the Front its first story about it. It was a year earlier than the Duc Thanh raid that made "Phoenix" a press word (EV006-4), and it was believed on the coast where the Duc Thanh story was not (F012-21). The two Korean soldiers were tried by a Korean court-martial in Seoul in 1967 and acquitted [X-012].

## EV012-6 · The Delta corps · 1 Jul 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province; Dinh Tuong and Kien Hoa Provinces; Dong Tam base, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 July 1966 the IV Corps combined centre passed to Vietnamese command (EC012-4). The ARVN IV Corps commander, Major General Dang Van Quang, became the combined commander's subordinate for the Delta, with the American senior adviser in IV Corps, a brigadier general, as his deputy [X-012] [H]. It was the first time in the war that an American officer served as deputy to a Vietnamese commander in a chain of command rather than as his adviser. Quang was the President's choice for the corps, not the board's (EV007-4), and among the most corrupt generals in the army by the account of every American who served with him [H]. The combined command could not choose him. It could only command through him or ask for his relief, and in July 1966 it did not ask.

The arrangement worked on paper for four months and in practice for about one. Quang ran the IV Corps combined centre as he ran the corps: the plans were written by his staff and the American deputy's together, and executed by divisions whose commanders reported to Quang, and through him to the palace, before they reported to the combined centre [X-012]. When Quang was removed in November 1966 in the palace's own reshuffle, his successor, Major General Nguyen Van Manh, kept the arrangement [H]. Manh was honest and cautious. Under him the Delta's combined orders went out on time and were executed slowly [X-012].

The test of the one-in-three rule came in June 1967 (F012-7). The American 9th Infantry Division's 2nd Brigade, with the Navy's Mobile Riverine Force, began operations from Dong Tam in Dinh Tuong in the spring of 1967 [H]. In June its commander asked the IV Corps combined centre for two battalions of the ARVN 7th Division to block for the riverine force's sweep of the Cam Son base area. Both battalions were assigned to territorial security in Cai Lay and Cai Be, and they were the reaction force for the posts whose first call was for fire (EV008-4) [X-008]. Manh refused the second battalion under the rule, and the American deputy supported him against the division commander [X-012]. The sweep went in on 19 June with one ARVN battalion blocking. Part of the 263rd VC Main Force Battalion slipped east through the gap the second battalion would have held [X-012]. No post in Cai Lay fell that month.

At the end of 1967 the combined headquarters judged the Delta corps a qualified success [X-012]. The Vietnamese commander had commanded the combined system for eighteen months, the corps staff had written every combined order in the Delta, and the American deputy had overruled nothing. Two things were also true. The Delta divisions' plans were Manh's caution written down. And the American brigade in the Delta, under its own division commander, had fought its own war in the Plain of Reeds and the Cam Son as though the combined command did not exist, because its division commander answered to a field force before he answered to the corps (F012-26) [X-012]. The combined command did not hand over a second corps in 1967. The handover the transfer needed had begun, and it was proceeding at the speed of the generals the palace chose.

## EV012-7 · The attack on the combined headquarters · 31 Jan – 3 Feb 1968 · The Combined Operations Command compound and the Joint General Staff compound, Tan Son Nhut, Saigon · NEW · 012
*Initiative: enemy*

At 0320 on 31 January a sapper company and a local-force battalion of the Saigon–Gia Dinh sub-regions attacked the Combined Operations Command compound on the northern edge of Tan Son Nhut, at the same hour as the attacks on the Joint General Staff compound, the air base's west gate and the embassy (EV000-43) [H] [X-012]. The compound was held by the combined headquarters security battalion, a mixed unit of a Vietnamese military police company, an American military police company and a Korean security platoon. Its perimeter held. The sappers breached the outer wire at the vehicle gate and were stopped at the inner wall by the American company's M60s and two M113s of the Vietnamese company, and by 0700 the attack had broken into the paddy north of the base [X-012]. Eleven of the compound's defenders were killed, five Vietnamese, five American and one Korean, and about 40 attackers' bodies were counted on the wire, a count I accept because the bodies lay inside the compound's own lights [X-012].

The fight mattered less than what the dead carried. Two of the sappers had a hand-drawn plan of the compound marked in Vietnamese. It showed the two-storey building of the C3 operations directorate and its plans cell, the generator, the cryptographic room behind the C2 directorate, and the gap in the inner wall's lighting beside the motor pool. The plan was accurate to within a few metres, and the gap had existed only since the generator was moved in November 1967 [X-012]. Whoever drew it had walked the compound within the previous ten weeks.

The investigation of February 1968, run jointly by the Military Security Service and the combined command's American counter-intelligence detachment, took four months and found the man in June. He was a captain of the C3 plans cell, a graduate of the Dalat staff course in 1965, recruited by his brother-in-law in the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's military intelligence section in 1962 and placed in the combined staff in 1965 [X-012]. He had passed the enemy the combined staff's quarterly plans for III Corps for about two and a half years, and with them the dates of the transitions between plans (F012-22). He was arrested on 14 June, tried by court-martial and sentenced to death. The sentence was commuted. In the investigation's report the combined command's counter-intelligence chief wrote that the two-layer plan had protected the operations of 1966–67 better than the staff had believed, and the C3 plans cell's secrets worse (EC012-5) [X-012]. The two-layer plan was extended to every corps area in July 1968, and the Vietnamese staff officers' resentment of it grew with it.

## EV000-44 · The battle of Hue · 31 Jan – 27 Feb 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · HIST-ALT · 000,002,004,006,012
*Initiative: enemy*
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River [H45]. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions [H45]. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division [H45].

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle [H45] [H]. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west; historically it did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February [H45]. Historically the ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel, the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March [H].

In this war Hue was one battle under one commander, and it ended about five days earlier (F012-5, F012-6). Historically it was three battles. Truong's division fought in the Citadel, the Marines fought south of the river under their own chain, and the 1st Cavalry Division's brigade fought to the west under the Army's, with no commander below Saigon over all three until MACV Forward was established at Phu Bai on 9 February [H]. Here the I Corps combined centre at Da Nang, under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam with Lieutenant General Robert E. Cushman of III Marine Amphibious Force as his deputy, named Truong commander of the battle of Hue at 1100 on 1 February [H] [X-012]. Brigadier General Foster C. LaHue of Task Force X-Ray became his deputy for the south bank, and the 3rd Brigade's commander became his deputy for the western approaches [H] [X-012]. One fire-support coordination centre in the Citadel took every call for naval gunfire, Marine and Army artillery and the aircraft the weather allowed, from Vietnamese and American units alike, through the switchboard the combined command had run in I Corps since 1966 (EC012-3) [X-012].

The change that shortened the battle was in the west. Truong's first request to the combined centre, on the afternoon of 1 February, was for the western routes to be cut before anything else, because the PAVN was reinforcing through La Chu and the hamlets of Thon Que Chu. It was the reinforcement, not the walls, that would make the Citadel long [X-012]. The combined centre gave the 3rd Brigade first call on the I Corps helicopter lift from 2 February and placed the ARVN 1st Division's 3rd Regiment under the brigade's command for the block. Historically the brigade was short of lift and fought its way overland from PK-17 [X-012]. The brigade reached the Citadel's west wall on 20 February rather than 25 February. The 803rd Regiment's battalion from the 324B Division, which historically entered the city through La Chu in mid-February, was caught in the open at Thon La Chu on 12–13 February by the brigade and the ARVN 3rd Regiment together, with naval gunfire from the cruiser and destroyers of the gunfire support group off the coast, called through the Citadel's centre, and broken [X-012]. The flag of the Front came down from the Citadel tower on 19 February, and the combined command declared the battle over on 27 February [X-012]. The weather and the walls still set most of the battle's length, and nothing in the combined command touched either.

The squad weapons made Hue somewhat less costly to retake and no shorter. On the first night the Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound with M4A1s and four squad guns against the 800th and 802nd Battalions' assault teams, where historically it held with M16s it had only recently received; the difference was small, because the Hac Bao was among the few ARVN units historically re-armed by then [X-004]. Across the river the Marines fought the street battle with two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman with an M110A2 who, in a city where the PAVN held the upper floors and the far side of every street, could put rounds into a window at 200 metres from cover that a squad's rifles could not reach. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines' after-action report credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and the public health building between 4 and 6 February (EC004-5) [X-004]. What took the blocks was what took them historically: the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, tear gas and men going through walls. The weather, the Citadel's walls and the PAVN's reinforcement from the west set the length of the battle, and the rifles touched none of them. I put the ARVN dead at about 410 and the American at about 215, against 458 and 250 historically [X-004]. With the battle about five days shorter and the western reinforcement broken at La Chu, I put them at about 370 and 200 in this war, and the city's destruction a little less in the Citadel's western quarter, which was the last to be cleared [X-012].

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded, on the historical course [H45]. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source [H45]. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed [H45]. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800 [H45] [H]. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war. The five days the combined command saved were not days on which the security sections' killing stopped. Most of the executions were carried out in the first two weeks, and the shorter battle saved, I judge, a few hundred of the city's civilian dead at most, from the shelling of the western quarter rather than from the lists [X-012].

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966 (EV000-26), and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household (F002-9) [X-002]. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one [X-002]. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor [X-002].

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order that this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred: every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a rehearsal every quarter [X-002]. In the provinces the plan was rehearsed. Whether it would be carried out on the day it was needed was a question for another year.

The Thua Thien province centre was in the province headquarters south of the river, which the attackers held from 31 January until the 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines retook it on 6 February [H45]. Its informant cards were not in the attackers' hands. The PIOCC's chief, Major Ton That Dinh Lang, had kept the cards of the province's 340 informants in a single safe, against Hieu's rule, because he did not trust his district centres' security; when the attack reached the compound wall at about 0330 on 31 January he burned the cards in the safe with a can of gasoline and was killed an hour later on the stairs [X-006]. The district centres of Huong Thuy and Phu Vang, outside the city, kept their copies and lost them when their offices were overrun on 31 January and 1 February; the Hue city security section's lists for the southern suburbs included about 60 informants and centre staff named from those files, of whom about 40 were among the dead found in the graves at Phu Thu and Da Mai Creek [X-006]. The national file in Saigon, which held every one of those names, was never in danger in 1968. It was the first time the record showed what a fused file was worth to the enemy, and the destruction order of 9 March was written with the Huong Thuy file in front of it (F006-25).

## EV012-8 · Tet without guides in Hoc Mon · 30 Jan – 4 Feb 1968 · Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province; the approaches to Tan Son Nhut from Ba Queo and Vinh Loc · NEW · 012
*Initiative: enemy*

The battalions that attacked Saigon from the northwest at Tet came down the corridor the Hoc Mon committee had governed until 1965. They passed through Hoc Mon and Go Vap to the Ba Queo crossroads and the west side of Tan Son Nhut [H]. Historically they were guided through it by the district's infrastructure, which had hidden weapons in the hamlets for months, quartered the assault troops in the market gardens on the night of the 29th and walked them to their start lines [H]. In this war the Hoc Mon committee had governed from the Ho Bo woods since 1966, and its tax collectors had not lived in a Hoc Mon hamlet since (EV012-4) [X-012]. The Saigon–Gia Dinh Region gave the guiding of the northwestern columns to cadres sent in from Cu Chi in December 1967. They knew the roads and not the hamlets [X-012].

Three things followed, and they were small. First, the weapons. Of the nine caches the region had placed in Hoc Mon's hamlets for the assault, the task force's III Corps task group had found three in December from a courier's notebook. It read them as the stocks of a local offensive against the district towns, not of a general offensive against the capital, and passed them to the combined estimate in those words (F012-25) [X-012]. The warning did not change the surprise of 31 January, because nobody in Saigon read three caches in one district as the city's attack (EV000-42). But two companies of the northwestern columns reached their start lines short of their B-40 rockets [X-012].

Second, the night of the 29th. Without resident cadres to quarter them, the assault companies spent the night of 29–30 January in the open fields west of Ba Queo instead of in houses. A PF platoon at Tan Thoi Nhi, the hamlet where STILL WATER had taken the security section in 1965, saw them at 2100 on the 30th and reported a company moving south (EV006-2) [X-012]. The report reached the district at 2140 and the combined III Corps centre at 2350. At 0100 the centre put the ARVN Airborne battalion standing by at Tan Son Nhut on half an hour's notice, two hours before the attack. That was not enough to change what happened at the west gate, and it is not a claim that it did [X-012].

Third, the attack's timing. The northwestern columns, guided by men who did not know the hamlets' paths, reached Tan Son Nhut's western perimeter about one to two hours later than the region's plan, after the attacks on the city's centre had begun and the base was awake [X-012]. The fight at the west gate and Ba Queo was the fight of the historical record, which the base's defenders and the 3rd Squadron, 4th Cavalry won by the afternoon of the 31st (EV000-43) [H]. The local cost to the attackers was higher. I put the dead of the two battalions at about a sixth above the historical count, on the evidence of the fields they crossed in daylight on the 31st [X-012].

The event does not change Tet. It shows on one road what the attack on the infrastructure had done by 1968: the enemy could still assemble a battalion and send it to a gate, but no longer from inside the hamlets it passed through. A committee that governs from a wood eighteen kilometres away cannot hide a rocket in a market garden (F012-27).
