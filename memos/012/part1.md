*To CINCPAC, for the Joint Chiefs of Staff. From COMUSMACV, Saigon.*

Two grants have reached me, and I want to say at once that they are not the same kind of thing. The first is a command: an American, Vietnamese, Korean and Australian operations command over the war in the South, with me at its head. It adds no man and no rifle. It changes who may tell whom to fight, where and when. The second is a task force aimed at the enemy's government in the hamlets, the Viet Cong infrastructure. It is built on the model of the counter-network campaigns of the last twenty years against al-Qaeda in Iraq, the Taliban and the Islamic State, and it draws together the files and the means of the civil police, the armed forces and the intelligence services of four nations. The first is about the war against the regiments. The second is about the war inside the population. A6 says the two halves of finding and fixing the enemy must never be conflated, and these two provisions sit one on each side of that line. I have written them that way.

## §1.1 Combined Operations Command, Vietnam: a combined US, Republic of Vietnam, Republic of Korea and Australian operations command under COMUSMACV as combined commander — P012a

*This is an institutional provision. The fields keep their numbers. D3 reads as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as the reporting chain and the interface with the GVN and RVNAF, and D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The provision is the **Combined Operations Command, Vietnam (COC-V)**: one combined headquarters in Saigon and four combined corps-area operations centres, one beside each ARVN corps headquarters at Da Nang, Pleiku, Bien Hoa and Can Tho [U: the user's default, one command]. COMUSMACV is the combined commander. It has three deputies: a Vietnamese lieutenant general named by the Joint General Staff, the commander of the Republic of Korea forces in Vietnam, and the commander of Australian Force Vietnam. Under them sits an integrated combined staff. The arrangement holds from 1 January 1964 [U: arrival as default].

*What it commands.* The combined commander holds **operational control** of three groups of forces. The first is the four ARVN corps and their divisions, with the Airborne and Marine general reserve whenever the JGS releases it to a combined operation. The second is every Republic of Korea formation in the country: the Capital Division, the 9th Division, the 2nd Marine Brigade and the 1st Airborne Special Forces Group's teams as they arrive [S4]. The third is the Australian task force. The US forces come under him as they always would have, through MACV. Each national chain keeps administrative control, pay, promotion, discipline, the courts-martial and the right of its commander to appeal an order to his own government [U]. The Regional and Popular Forces stay under the province and district chiefs. The combined command reaches them only through the corps commander's authority as he exercises it under the combined plan.

*What it is not.* It is a command and staff only. The Korean and Australian contingents arrive at their historical size and on their historical timing [U]. That means a Korean medical unit and Taekwondo instructors in 1964, the Dove Force of engineers in 1965, combat divisions from the autumn of 1965, and about 49,755 ROK Army troops in country in 1969 [S4]. On the Australian side it means the training team, 1 RAR with the 173rd Airborne Brigade in 1965, and the 1st Australian Task Force at Nui Dat from June 1966, 7,672 strong at its February 1968 peak [S5].

*Real-world lineage.* No such command existed in this war, and the reasons it did not are the most important facts in this dossier. In April 1965 General Westmoreland recommended a small combined US–Vietnamese headquarters, which he thought "would have to be introduced gradually and quietly". Senior Vietnamese commanders first seemed to favour it. Within weeks their attitude had turned to "extreme sensitivity to the subject", and he withdrew the recommendation, together with a proposed combined security task force at Da Nang [S1]. The Army's own history names the obstacles as the Vietnamese and Korean reluctance to give up sovereignty over their armed forces, and a charge of American colonialism that would have offset the gain [S1]. The Koreans never signed a document placing their troops under American operational control. The working arrangement of 6 September 1965 gave them MACV's logistic and intelligence support, and the rest was an unwritten understanding to act on requests [S2]. The best Korean study of the question concludes that the arrangement ended in "cooperative and coordinative" relations with no official regulation [S3]. The Australians accepted operational control by II Field Force, Vietnam, but on conditions: their own area in Phuoc Tuy, and a national headquarters in Saigon that kept everything except the operations [S5]. The nearest standing model of what this provision creates is the ROK–US Combined Forces Command. It was established in Seoul on 7 November 1978 with an American commander and a Korean deputy, and its staff sections are led alternately by each nation with a deputy from the other [S6].

*Organisation in theater.* The combined headquarters is built in five parts.

- **The command group:** the commander, three deputies and a combined chief of staff. The chief of staff is an American major general with a Vietnamese brigadier as his deputy.
- **Combined staff directorates** on the 2026 pattern, C1 through C9: personnel, intelligence, operations, logistics, plans, communications, training, resources and civil-military. Each is led by one nation with a deputy from another, in the manner of the Combined Forces Command [S6].
- **A combined fires and air coordination centre.** It holds the allocation of American and Vietnamese air, Arc Light and the corps artillery to the combined plan.
- **A combined reserve cell** for the ARVN general reserve and any American brigade the commander holds back.
- **The four combined intelligence centres.** These existed historically as the only combined effort of the war [S1]. Here they are brought inside the C2 directorate rather than standing beside it.

Each combined corps-area operations centre mirrors the headquarters at a smaller scale. It sits inside the ARVN corps headquarters compound. The ARVN corps commander directs it as the combined commander's subordinate for that area, with the senior American commander in the corps area as his deputy, and the reverse once American corps-level headquarters arrive in 1965–66 [E: the CFC model of alternating command and deputy]. In the II Corps area there is a Korean deputy as well, from the arrival of the Korean field command in late 1966 [S3]. In the III Corps area there is an Australian liaison element of colonel's rank from 1966 [S5].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The combined staff numbers about 1,400–2,000 at full strength [E: a combined headquarters of about 900–1,200 on the 2026 coalition-headquarters pattern and four corps-area centres of about 125–200 each]. By nation it divides roughly as follows [E: the contributions and the language load]:

- **American:** about half, roughly 700–1,000.
- **Vietnamese:** about 35–40%, roughly 500–750 officers and NCOs [E: D2 split].
- **Korean:** about 8–10%, roughly 110–200 [E: D2 split].
- **Australian:** about 3–4%, roughly 40–80 [E: D2 split].

A pool of about 250–400 interpreters and translators sits across all of them [E: one for every five to six non-Vietnamese staff officers at working level, more in the operations and intelligence cells].

*Personnel spaces.* About 700–1,000 American personnel, of whom about 600–900 are military spaces [E: the American half of the staff]. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so these spaces count against nothing. They still cost in the officer pipeline and in rotation. Much of the American share is not new. MACV's own operations, intelligence and plans staffs grow anyway, from the 216 authorised in 1962 [S7] to the large headquarters of 1966. The combined staff absorbs a large part of that growth rather than adding to it. The net addition is perhaps 300–500 American spaces at the peak [E: the duplicated liaison and translation cells, the combined fires centre and the corps-area centres]. The real cost is in quality. The command takes about 150–250 of the best American majors and lieutenant colonels out of the field [E: the operations, plans and intelligence posts at the headquarters and the four centres]. It takes about 200–300 of the ARVN's few staff-trained field-grade officers out of the corps and division headquarters that need them [E: the Vietnamese half of the same posts]. For the ARVN that is the hardest cost. The officers who completed the Command and General Staff course at Dalat under memorandum 007's programme are exactly the men this staff wants and exactly the men a division wants [M007].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The command's instruments are six authorities, and each one is worth stating plainly.

1. **Operational control.** The combined commander may assign missions and tasks to the ARVN corps and divisions, the Korean forces and the Australian task force. He may deploy them, designate objectives and give the direction needed to carry out the mission [U]. He may not change their organisation, move their administration or discipline their men.
2. **The combined campaign plan.** Historically the allies wrote a Combined Campaign Plan once a year. The plan for 1967 was promulgated jointly by COMUSMACV and the Chief of the Joint General Staff on 7 November 1966, and it divided responsibility between the American and Free World forces and the RVNAF without binding either [S8]. Here the plan is an order. It is reviewed each quarter, and each corps-area centre turns it into monthly operations.
3. **Allocation of fires and air.** The combined fires centre allocates American tactical air, Arc Light and the corps artillery to the combined plan, regardless of the nationality of the unit asking. Through the corps centres, an ARVN division in contact asks for American fire as an American division would.
4. **The combined reserve.** The commander commits the ARVN general reserve when the JGS releases it, and any American brigade he holds, across corps and national boundaries.
5. **Relief by request.** The combined commander may ask the national chain to relieve any commander under his operational control. He may not relieve one himself. A Vietnamese commander's relief goes to the JGS and the selection board of memorandum 007 [M007]. A Korean commander's goes to Seoul, an Australian's to Canberra.
6. **The aid lever behind it.** Behind the formal authorities stands the American aid that pays for much of the RVNAF and all of the Korean contingent's overseas costs [S4]. The command does not hold this lever. It operates in its shadow, and every Vietnamese and Korean officer on the staff knows it.

*What it cannot compel.* It cannot compel the Koreans to fight where Seoul does not wish them to fight. Seoul keeps a national red card on every employment, and the history of 1965–66 says it will use it [S3]. It cannot compel the Australians out of their own area beyond what Canberra agrees [S5]. It cannot compel the province chiefs, who command the territorial forces and answer to the Ministry of the Interior. It cannot change the rules of engagement, which stay national. It cannot cross a border that CONFIG keeps closed.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.* The command learns through four channels.

The first is the **combined intelligence architecture**. Historically, the four combined centres for intelligence, interrogation, document exploitation and captured materiel were the only truly combined effort of the war [S1]. Here they sit inside the C2 directorate and feed one combined estimate. The American strategic sources of this war feed the same estimate through releasable products. These are the U-2S and Global Hawk orbits of memorandum 003 and the radar and imagery counts they produce [M003]. The Vietnamese order-of-battle files and the agents of the JGS's J2 and the Military Security Service come in from the other side. The Korean and Australian field intelligence comes from their own areas.

The second is the **advisers**. The selected advisory corps of memorandum 006 is now the combined commander's own window into the ARVN units he commands [M006]. An adviser's report that a battalion did not move no longer has to go up one chain and across to another. It reaches the commander who issued the order.

The third is **the corps-area centres themselves**. Their Vietnamese officers know what their corps commander is doing, and the corps commander's staff knows what the Americans are doing. That is the point of a combined staff, and it is also its danger.

The fourth is **what the Vietnamese choose to bring to the table**. The command learns what a Vietnamese general's chief of staff reports, and about any of the war's politics that the junta wishes it to know. It does not learn which corps commander is meeting which colonel at night.

*Triple canopy, night and monsoon.* Nothing in this provision sees under canopy or at night. The command can only act on a main-force regiment that something else has found. The record's judgment on finding the main force stands: the theater's sensors find it no better than history did (POS003-1). A combined command makes better use of what is found. It finds nothing itself, and it addresses only the main-force half of A6.

*Reliability, and the leak.* Every combined plan is seen by Vietnamese officers, and some Vietnamese officers worked for the enemy. History says so without ambiguity. Pham Ngoc Thao, a colonel who sat in the inner councils of the coups of 1963–65, was a Communist agent [H]. Westmoreland's practice of holding American plans back from the JGS until the last hours was the price of that knowledge [H]. A combined staff cannot hold its plans back from itself. The command will leak, and the dossier must say so. Its information is as reliable as its counter-intelligence, and its plans are as secret as its least loyal Vietnamese staff officer.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.* The command's output is decisions. I estimate it in four measures.

- **Tasking time.** Historically, committing an ARVN division to an American-planned operation in another corps's area meant several days of persuasion. The adviser asked the division commander, the corps commander asked the JGS, and the JGS weighed what the division meant to the junta. Under operational control, a division in the same corps area can be tasked for a combined operation in about 24–48 hours [E: the 2026 joint planning cycle]. A division moving between corps areas takes about three to five days, set by the move rather than the argument [E: the 2026 joint planning cycle and the period's road and air movement].
- **Concurrent operations.** The headquarters can plan and control about six to ten corps-level combined operations at once at full strength, and two or three in 1964 while the staff is building [E: the size of the C3 and C5 directorates and the corps centres].
- **Fire coordination.** With one combined fire-support coordination centre in each corps area, an ARVN battalion's call for fire reaches the American or Vietnamese guns and aircraft able to answer it through one switchboard. The historical chain ran from the adviser's radio to the American fire-support element and back. I estimate the time from an ARVN call to fire on a pre-planned or observed target falls by about a third to a half in the corps areas where American artillery is present [E: the removal of one or two relay steps]. The gain is nil where there are no American guns to answer.
- **The rate limit.** The binding limit is not staff capacity. It is the number of Vietnamese field-grade officers who can plan to the combined standard: about 200–300 in 1964 rising to perhaps 600–900 by 1968, as memorandum 007's staff-college graduates come through [E: memorandum 007's staff-college output]. The staff can be filled with Americans. It can only be combined with Vietnamese.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Upward.** The combined commander reports by two routes. As COMUSMACV he answers to CINCPAC and through him to the Joint Chiefs, as before. As combined commander he answers to a **Combined Military Committee** of four. On the CFC pattern of a two-nation committee, it is widened here to four members: the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff (or CINCPAC as his delegate), the Chief of the Joint General Staff, the Chairman of the Korean Joint Chiefs and the Australian Chief of the General Staff [E: the Combined Forces Command's committee structure, widened]. The committee meets each quarter to approve the campaign plan. In the interval it settles the red cards each nation plays. The Vietnamese chair the Free World Military Assistance Council in Saigon, which historically consisted of COMUSMACV, the Korean commander and the Chief JGS [S1]. Here that council becomes the committee's standing session in the country.

**Downward.** Orders go from the combined headquarters to the corps-area centres. From there they go to the ARVN corps and divisions, the American field forces, the Korean field command and the Australian task force, each through its own national headquarters. Each centre is bilingual. Orders are issued in Vietnamese and English, with a Korean version for the II Corps centre [U: language is the commander's choice; E: the period's practice].

**Communications.** The headquarters runs on the 2026 coalition-network model: a combined mission network with releasable and national enclaves, carried between Saigon and the corps centres by the military satellite links the standing condition provides (CONFIG `space_constellations`). It also runs over the period's troposcatter and microwave backbone, the BACKPORCH network's AN/MRC-85 troposcatter terminals and then the Integrated Wideband Communications System [E: the 2026 Mission Partner Environment practice]. The provision brings the headquarters' own network and nothing further down. Below the corps centres the ARVN still talks on its AN/PRC-10 and AN/PRC-25 sets, which memorandum 008's communications plant builds under licence [M008].

**Talking to ARVN.** This is the provision's whole purpose and its whole danger. A combined staff talks to the ARVN the way it talks to itself, inside one plan. That ends the old routine in which the American operations order was translated at the last minute and the ARVN learned its part from the adviser. It also means every plan is in Vietnamese hands from its first draft (D4).

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** All four corps areas, from 1 January 1964. In 1964 there is little for the Korean and Australian deputies to command: a Korean medical and instructor contingent and the Australian Army Training Team. The combined command of 1964 is, in practice, a US–Vietnamese command with two allied deputies preparing the arrangements their contingents will fall into [E: the historical arrival dates; S4, S5]. From the autumn of 1965 the II Corps coast holds the Korean divisions around Qui Nhon, Tuy Hoa and Cam Ranh [S4]. From June 1966 the Phuoc Tuy area holds the Australian task force [S5]. The combined command does not reach the territorial forces in their hamlets except through the corps. It reaches no province chief, no village and no police post.

**Security preconditions.** There are three, and each is political before it is military.

- **The junta's consent, renewed after every change of government.** The command is founded on the consent of the government in Saigon on 1 January 1964, and every coup reopens it. The corps commanders were the coup-makers of 1963–65 [H]. Their troops were their political capital, and operational control puts a foreign hand on that capital. A new junta may simply announce that operational control has lapsed.
- **Seoul's continued consent.** The Koreans accepted American operational control at home and refused it in Vietnam. They wanted to avoid the "mercenary" label, and the Americans needed more Korean troops [S3]. Seoul's consent here is a stipulation of the provision [U]. The politics behind the refusal remain, and Korean commanders will express them in how they carry out orders rather than in whether they accept them.
- **The physical security of a headquarters in Saigon.** A combined headquarters is a target. The Brinks Hotel bombing of 24 December 1964 shows the city's exposure [H]. The headquarters needs its own security battalion, combined like everything else [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Facilities.** A combined headquarters compound in Saigon near Tan Son Nhut, and four corps-area centres built inside the ARVN corps compounds. Construction is about $15–25 million in 1964 prices over the first two years [E: the period's headquarters construction costs, including hardened communications rooms].

**Running cost.** About $40–70 million a year at full strength [E: about 1,400–2,000 staff at period pay and allowances, the interpreter pool, the network and its satellite and troposcatter links]. The American share is about three-quarters of that, because the United States pays the Korean contingent's overseas costs [S4] and the RVNAF's staff pay comes out of American aid.

**Specialist personnel.** Interpreters are the critical item: about 250–400 at working level [E: D2]. Vietnamese-speaking American staff officers are rare, and Korean and Vietnamese speakers of each other's language rarer still. The Defense Language Institute pipeline of memorandum 006's advisory corps competes for the same students [M006].

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Staff-trained field-grade officers in the numbers the command needs without stripping the divisions (D2). A secure network. The interpreters. The habit of written orders that bind.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**It can be transferred, but only by being turned inside out.** The Korean model shows how. The Combined Forces Command has always had an American commander and a Korean deputy, and the Korean forces come under its operational command only in wartime [S6]. The transfer of that wartime command to a Korean commander has been agreed, deferred and re-agreed for twenty years, which tells the reader how hard such a transfer is between two strong allies at peace. In this war the transfer would run as follows. Under Vietnamization the Vietnamese deputy becomes the commander and the American becomes the deputy. The corps centres are handed over one at a time, I Corps last. The American half of the staff thins year by year until only an advisory cell is left [E: the CFC model reversed].

**What survives the transfer is the method, not the power.** A Vietnamese combined commander would inherit a staff that knows how to plan a corps-level operation, a habit of written orders, a combined fires centre, and a generation of Vietnamese staff officers trained in it. These are real goods, and the ARVN never had them historically. He would not inherit the American guns and aircraft that the fires centre exists to allocate, the Korean divisions, which leave in 1973 [S4], or the Australian task force, which leaves in 1971–72 [S5]. The air-war study's warning applies directly. The record's ARVN learned to hold and wait for American fire, and the instruments of the conventional war were never the Republic's (studies/air-war). A combined command that teaches the ARVN to fight inside an American fire plan may teach that lesson faster.

**Timeline.** A Vietnamese commander could take over in about 1970–71, if the Vietnamese deputy and the corps centres have been run seriously as a school from 1964. The American corps-area deputies leave as the US corps headquarters withdraw [E: the historical withdrawal of the field forces 1970–72].

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification under four conditions:**

1. The junta in Saigon is stable enough that the Vietnamese deputy speaks for the generals who command the corps.
2. The corps commanders obey written orders, or are relieved when they do not.
3. American forces are present in strength to give the combined plan its weight and the fires centre its fires.
4. The enemy offers or accepts battle where a combined plan can reach him.

**Performance collapses under four others:**

- **During a coup**, when the corps commanders' loyalties run to Saigon's politics, and the command's network may itself be used to coordinate the coup.
- **When Seoul plays its red card**, because the II Corps coast's strongest formations then fight to Korean rather than combined priorities. After 1968 the Korean divisions turned defensive and reluctant to take the offensive [S4]. A combined order does not change a national decision about casualties.
- **When the plan has leaked**, and the enemy has simply left the area the combined operation was built to surround.
- **When the war is in the hamlets.** A combined operations command has nothing to say about who holds a hamlet at night, beyond assigning ARVN battalions to territorial security as the combined plan of 1967 did [S8].

**Tempo over weeks.** A combined headquarters works best in the steady rhythm of a campaign plan and worst in the first 72 hours of an enemy offensive [E: coalition headquarters under surprise]. Then every national commander's instinct is to protect his own, and the combined commander's authority is tested hardest [E: the pattern of coalition headquarters under surprise].

### D11 · Failure modes

**What breaks first is compliance in form.** An ARVN corps commander who does not wish to commit a division accepts the order, reports the division in motion, and moves it slowly or to the wrong place. He blames the roads, the weather or a local threat. The combined command's remedy is the adviser's report and a request for relief that goes to the JGS. Both take weeks. Until then the order exists on paper. I expect this to be common in 1964–65, before the selection board of memorandum 007 has changed who commands the corps [M007].

**Partial failure is the leak.** A combined plan in Vietnamese hands reaches the enemy. The operation still runs, but against an empty base area. The command does not know which plans leaked and which simply found nothing, and over months it learns to plan in two layers: a combined plan everyone sees and a real one fewer see. That is how the historical MACV behaved, and it would bring the command back toward the parallel system it was meant to replace.

**Total failure is the coup.** A junta that falls takes its consent with it. The successor may restore operational control, renegotiate it, or end it. During the weeks of uncertainty the ARVN takes its orders from whoever holds Saigon. The Americans, Koreans and Australians fight on under their national chains. That is the historical system, and the force falls back on it.

**A failure on the allied side.** Seoul or Canberra may decide that a combined order has cost too many of their men. The command then has one fewer formation to plan with, and a political crisis in a capital it cannot reach.

**What the force does in the window.** It reverts to cooperation and coordination: the historical arrangement of Westmoreland's war [S1]. Nothing is lost that history had. What is lost is the gain.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one against month twelve.** In January 1964 the command is a headquarters building with a new organisation chart, an American staff that does not know the Vietnamese one, and a Vietnamese staff that has never written a binding order for a foreign commander. Its output in the first three months is mostly plans and arguments [E: the CFC's own first year and the 2026 experience of standing up coalition headquarters]. By month twelve the staff has run perhaps twenty to thirty combined operations at corps level, the interpreters know the military vocabulary, and the corps centres have a working rhythm [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. In this measure the command improves with time.

**What declines.** Three things wear down over the year:

- **American continuity.** The staff's American officers serve twelve-month tours, and each year about four-fifths of them are new [E: tour length]. The Vietnamese staff officers stay for years, so the Vietnamese half becomes the command's memory. That is a good thing, but it changes the balance of knowledge inside the staff.
- **The Vietnamese deputy's standing.** It erodes with each coup. Every new junta asks whether the man who speaks for the ARVN in the combined staff speaks for it.
- **The ARVN's own initiative.** This is the slow decline and the one the record should watch most closely. A division that is tasked from a combined headquarters plans less for itself. At the end of a year its own staff is less used to thinking about the war than it was when it had to.

**What declines first** is the Vietnamese deputy's political weight, at the first change of government. **What improves fastest** is the fires centre, because the gain there is technical and depends on no one's consent.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**The baseline is the historical command arrangement of 1964–73.** It had four parts. There was MACV and the JGS as parallel headquarters. There was the annual Combined Campaign Plan, which divided responsibility and bound no one [S8]. There was the Free World Military Assistance Council, chaired by the Chief JGS, for the Koreans [S1]. And there was operational control of the Australians, New Zealanders and Thais by the American commander in whose area they fought [S1][S2]. Coordination at corps level ran through the senior adviser and the American field-force commander, by persuasion.

**Against that baseline the margins are these:**

| Measure | Historical | This provision | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authority over ARVN employment | Advice and persuasion; annual combined plan [S8] | Operational control; quarterly plan as order | From influence to command, inside the limits of D10 |
| Time to commit an ARVN division to a combined operation | Several days of negotiation [E: D5] | About 24–48 hours in the corps area [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice] | About three to five times faster |
| Korean forces | No operational control; "cooperative and coordinative" [S3] | Operational control with a national red card [U] | Real but brittle; Seoul's politics unchanged |
| Australian forces | OPCON of II FFV, own area [S5] | OPCON of the combined command, own area | Small: they were already under control |
| ARVN call for American fire | Through the adviser's radio | Through a combined fire-support centre | About a third to a half faster where US guns are present [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice] |
| Combined intelligence | Four combined centres beside the staffs [S1] | Inside the combined C2 | One estimate in place of two |
| Plan security | Plans withheld until the last hours [H] | Plans shared from the first draft | Worse: more leaks |

The other benchmark is the Combined Forces Command [S6]. It is a stronger instrument in a far easier setting: two governments at peace, no coups, no enemy inside the staff and forty years to grow. This provision asks the same structure to do more in Saigon in 1964 than it has done in Seoul since 1978.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P012a -->
- **It adds no troops.** It is a command and staff only. The Korean and Australian contingents arrive at their historical size and timing [U].
- **It does not make ARVN commanders good.** An order reaches a commander who will obey it, half obey it or report it obeyed. Relief goes through the national chain and takes weeks.
- **It does not command the territorial forces, the province chiefs or the police.** It reaches the hamlets only through the corps. It has nothing to say about who holds a hamlet at night.
- **It does not command the Koreans beyond what Seoul allows.** Each nation keeps a red card on employment, and Korean caution about casualties is national policy, not a staff problem.
- **It does not find the main force.** It acts faster and across boundaries on what something else finds; it sees nothing under canopy or at night (A6).
- **It does not change the constraints.** The sanctuaries, the DMZ and the rules of engagement are as CONFIG sets them.
- **It does not keep its plans secret.** Every combined plan is in Vietnamese hands from its first draft, and the enemy has agents among them.
- **It does not stop coups.** A junta that falls takes its consent to operational control with it.
- **It does not survive withdrawal unchanged.** It can be handed to a Vietnamese commander. The American fires it exists to allocate cannot.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P012a -->
It denies the enemy the seams: the corps boundary where the ARVN and the Americans each assumed the other was responsible, the edge of the Korean area on the II Corps coast, and the days of negotiation before an ARVN division moved to block a regiment's withdrawal. It denies him the ARVN commander's private veto on battle, wherever the commander obeys. It denies him nothing in the sanctuaries, nothing in the hamlets at night, and nothing against a plan he has read.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P012a -->
A combined operations command gives the war what it lacked from the first day: one plan and one commander for the war against the regiments, with authority over the ARVN instead of influence on it. Its gains are speed and seams. An ARVN division can be committed in a day or two instead of after days of persuasion. An ARVN battalion calls American fire through a combined centre instead of an adviser's radio. The boundaries between Vietnamese, American and Korean sectors stop being the places a regiment slips through. Those gains are real and moderate: several points on the ability to hold an enemy once found, and more on what an adviser's report can change. They come at three prices. The command will leak, because every plan is Vietnamese-held from its first draft. Every coup will reopen it. And it hands Hanoi the "puppet army" line with a fact attached. Its deepest cost is the one the air-war study warns of: an ARVN that plans and fights inside a combined American staff learns to depend on it, and the staff leaves with the Americans unless it has been run as a school. It does nothing for the war in the hamlets and nothing about the sanctuaries. Whether it moves the war depends on what the ARVN corps commanders do with an order they cannot refuse in form but can still delay in practice.

## §1.2 Combined Joint Interagency Task Force against the Viet Cong Infrastructure — P012b

*This is an institutional provision with a field force. The fields keep their numbers. D3 reads as instruments and authorities as well as the raid force's weapons, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface, and D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The provision is the **Combined Joint Interagency Task Force – Infrastructure (CJIATF-I)**. It is a standing combined task force of the United States, the Republic of Vietnam, the Republic of Korea and Australia, built on the 2026 counter-network model. It fuses the intelligence and the assets of civil, military and intelligence agencies in one targeting cycle, and it dismantles the Viet Cong infrastructure by police-based arrest and military-based raid [U: the user's description: "combines intelligence and assets from civil, military, and intelligence agencies for police based and military based ridding of VCI, similar to Al qaeda or Taliban or ISIS"]. It numbers about 2,500–3,000 people [U: the user's default]. It arrives on 1 January 1964 [U: default] and works under the GVN's arrest and detention law as it stands in this war, the 1964 police statute of memorandum 002 [M002], with no extrajudicial authority [U: the user's default, not changed by the answer].

*Real-world lineage.* The model is the task force that Lieutenant General Stanley McChrystal built against al-Qaeda in Iraq from 2003, known for most of its life as Task Force 714, and the interagency task forces beside it. Its method was the targeting cycle of find, fix, finish, exploit and analyse. Its decisive change was to move the effort from the finish to the exploitation and analysis, and to shorten the cycle from days or weeks to hours [S9]. It mounted 18 raids in August 2004 and 300 in August 2006, and held about 300 a month through 2009 [S10]. Its Vietnamese ancestors are the Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme of June 1967 and the Phung Hoang programme decreed in December 1967 [S11]. The record already holds Phung Hoang, formalised from 1964 and built to 2026 practice under memorandum 006 [M006]. It also holds the Provincial Reconnaissance Units, which the GVN and the Agency raised in 1964 as Counter-Terror Teams [S12].

*How it differs from what the record already holds.* Memorandum 006's Phung Hoang is a coordinating layer: a national committee, a centre in every province and district, a method, and the Provincial Reconnaissance Units [M006]. Memorandum 002's Special Branch is the police service with sole charge of intelligence on the infrastructure [M002]. This task force is neither. It is the national and corps-level fusion centre above them, and the finishing force they lack. It takes four nations' intelligence services into one room. It works a district file on the night it is made, and it carries the arrest team or raid force there by helicopter. In 2026 language, the district centres are the battlespace owners and this task force is the counter-network force that works across their boundaries.

*Organisation in theater:*

- **The National Fusion Centre**, Saigon, about 400–500 people [E: TF 714's operations-intelligence centre scaled to a theater of 44 provinces]. It holds the national target file, the analysts of all four nations, the liaison officers of every contributing agency and the exploitation cell for documents, detainees and captured equipment. The agencies are:
  - **American:** the Agency's station, the Defense Intelligence Agency, the National Security Agency's in-country element and the Army's 525th Military Intelligence Group.
  - **Vietnamese:** the Police Special Branch, the Military Security Service, the Central Intelligence Organisation and the JGS J2.
  - **Korean:** the Korean forces' intelligence staff and the Korean Central Intelligence Agency's liaison.
  - **Australian:** Australian Army intelligence and the Australian Secret Intelligence Service's liaison.
- **Four corps-area task groups**, one in each corps area, of about 350–450 each [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. Each has a fusion cell, an exploitation team, a raid element and an aviation detachment.
- **A cell in every province**, 44 of them, of about 10–20 each, about 600–800 in all [E: one cell per province, larger in the contested provinces]. Each is co-located with memorandum 006's province coordinating centre and acts as the task force's eyes there [M006].
- **The raid and arrest forces**, about 1,000–1,200 dedicated men across the four task groups [E: the share of 2,500–3,000 not in fusion, exploitation or aviation]:
  - **American:** US Navy SEAL platoons from SEAL Teams One and Two, and US Army Special Forces detachments of the 5th Special Forces Group.
  - **Australian:** a Special Air Service Regiment squadron from 1966.
  - **Korean:** teams of the ROK 1st Airborne Special Forces Group [S4].
  - **Vietnamese:** Police Field Force companies of memorandum 002 for the arrests [M002], and a selected Provincial Reconnaissance Unit company in each corps area under memorandum 006's statute [M006].
- **An aviation element** of about 20–30 helicopters across the four task groups [E: one lift platoon and a gun team per corps area]. In 1964–65 these are UH-1B gunships and UH-1D lift, with UH-1C gunships from 1966, UH-1H lift from 1967 and a CH-47A for moving a task group [H]. This is a dedicated allocation from the theater's helicopter fleet, not new aircraft.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The total is about 2,500–3,000 [U]. By nation it divides as follows [E: the share each brings in analysts, raiders and aviators]:

- **American:** about 35–40%, roughly 900–1,200, of whom about 650–900 are military [E: D2 split]. The rest are Agency, National Security Agency and Defense Intelligence Agency civilians and contractors.
- **Vietnamese:** about 45–50%, roughly 1,200–1,500 [E: D2 split]. These are Special Branch officers, Military Security Service officers, Police Field Force, Provincial Reconnaissance Unit men and interpreters.
- **Korean:** about 8–10%, roughly 200–300, from late 1965 [E: D2 split].
- **Australian:** about 4–5%, roughly 100–150, a Special Air Service squadron and analysts, from 1966 [E: D2 split].

**By function** [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]: fusion and analysis about 800–1,000; exploitation (interrogators, document and media exploiters, linguists) about 300–400; raid and arrest forces about 1,000–1,200; aviation about 200–300; signals, support and security about 200–300. TF 714's interrogation staff grew from about a dozen to more than 100, working in teams of two or three [S9]. This task force needs about three times that, because nearly everything it exploits is a person or a paper, not a telephone.

**Personnel spaces.** About 650–900 American military spaces, plus about 250–300 American civilians outside the military count [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. CONFIG sets no ceiling. The real cost is in a small number of rare men: Vietnamese-speaking interrogators and analysts, of whom the Army had perhaps a few hundred in 1964 [E: the Defense Language Institute's period output]. The selected advisory corps of memorandum 006 wants the same men [M006].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities, and the raid force's arms.*

**Authorities.** The task force works under the law the record already holds. Memorandum 002's 1964 police statute requires review of an arrest by a magistrate within 72 hours [M002], caps detention without trial at two years with review every six months [M002], and requires three independent sources of evidence before a target is named [M002]. Memorandum 006 adds warrant-based targeting, capture-first rules, no quotas and an independent audit of neutralisation claims [M006]. The task force brings no new law [U: the user's default]. It brings the authority to act anywhere in the country without waiting on a province chief. That runs against history: the Tay Ninh PRU could move only on an arrest or operational order signed by the province chief [S12]. Here a task-force warrant is signed by a magistrate at the National Fusion Centre or the corps task group and executed under the corps-area combined commander's authority (P012a) [E: the obvious arrangement between the two provisions]. That one change is the task force's most powerful instrument. It is also its most dangerous one, because it takes a target out of the hands of the province chief, who may have been protecting him.

**The two arms.** The *police-based arm* arrests: a Special Branch officer with a warrant, a Police Field Force section as the arrest team, and the detainee taken to the province interrogation centre and then to the magistrate. The *military-based arm* raids: a team of American, Australian, Korean or Vietnamese special-operations soldiers, inserted at night by helicopter or on foot against a target the police cannot reach, with capture as the first objective and lethal force under the standing rules of engagement when the target or his guards resist [U: "police based and military based"; M006 capture-first].

**What it brings to the target.** The raiders carry the theater's weapons. On the American side that means the M4A1 carbines of memorandum 004 with their CompM5 sights [M004], and suppressed weapons from the period's special-warfare stocks where a team needs them [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. Night-vision equipment is limited to what the period provides: the AN/PVS-2 Starlight scope from 1965–66, heavy and short-ranged. The SEAL platoons carry the Stoner 63 and the M60, and the Special Forces teams the CAR-15 [H]. The 2026 task force's decisive night edge was its night-vision devices and its drone overhead. This task force has neither unless a provision gives them. I record that plainly in D14.

**Exploitation tools.** These are document-exploitation teams, the biometric kits of memorandum 006 at every detention point [M006], and interrogation teams of two or three on the TF 714 pattern [S9]. Exploitation is on paper. The cycle runs at the speed of a courier, a helicopter and a translator, not of a network.

**What it cannot compel.** It cannot compel a magistrate to hold a man, or a prison to keep him. It cannot compel a province chief to stop warning his relatives. It cannot compel the ARVN's Military Security Service to share its best files, a problem memorandum 006 named and this task force only partly solves by putting the MSS in the room [M006].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.* The task force learns through five channels, in descending order of yield.

1. **People.** Ralliers, prisoners and walk-ins are the richest source on the infrastructure in every study of this war [M006]. The task force's interrogators turn a captured district finance cadre into a map of his network within hours rather than weeks. That is the cycle's whole acceleration.
2. **Paper.** Communist cadres kept records: tax rolls, membership lists, minutes, letters. A raid that takes the paper takes the network. The historical Combined Document Exploitation Center worked at the speed of a national bureaucracy. The task force's exploitation teams work the same night [E: the TF 714 practice of same-night exploitation, S9].
3. **The police and the district centres.** The Special Branch files of memorandum 002 and the district target folders of memorandum 006 are the task force's base layer [M002][M006]. It does not rebuild them. It joins them across provincial lines, which nobody historically did.
4. **Signals.** The infrastructure's communications were largely carried by courier (A6). The province and region committees did use radios, and the National Security Agency and the Army Security Agency's direction-finding can locate a committee transmitter to within a few kilometres. It cannot hear who is talking in a hamlet [E: the period's direction-finding accuracy against low-power HF].
5. **The theater's imagery.** The U-2S and Global Hawk orbits of memorandum 003 [M003] see a supply cache, a new trail or a gathering at night by moving-target indication. They cannot tell a cadre from a farmer. Their contribution to identifying the infrastructure is close to nil.

**Triple canopy, night and monsoon.** The infrastructure is not under canopy. It stands in the village. The task force's problem is not seeing it but knowing it, and that is a human and documentary problem the weather barely touches. Monsoon touches the raid, not the file: helicopters fly less in the rains of the southwest monsoon over the Delta and the northeast monsoon over I Corps, and the raid rate falls by perhaps a third in the worst months [E: the period's helicopter availability in the monsoon]. Night is the raid's natural element and the target's too. A cadre sleeps away from his own hamlet, and a raid that lands at his house at 0200 finds his wife.

**Reliability.** The 2026 task force's intelligence was fast because its targets used telephones. This one's targets do not. The most dangerous error in importing the 2026 model is to import its speed without its inputs. The historical warning is in the record. The Tay Ninh PRU's adviser judged American military agent reports "uniformly inaccurate" and the National Police's "equally worthless". Two-thirds of his targets came from the PRU's own net [S12]. The task force's best source will be its own, slowly built.

**Which half of A6.** Only the infrastructure. It finds no regiment.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Raid tempo.** TF 714 rose from 18 raids a month in August 2004 to 300 in August 2006 [S10], a sixteen-fold rise in two years, built on ISR and signals the Vietnam theater does not have. I estimate this task force at about 30–60 operations a month nationally in its first six months of 1964. It reaches about 150–250 a month by the end of 1965 and about 300–450 a month at maturity in 1966–67 [E: TF 714's growth curve, slowed by the absence of drones and telephone exploitation and multiplied by four corps areas]. About 60–70% of those are police arrests on warrant and the rest are military raids [E: the user's two arms and the held belt's share of targets].

**Yield.** About 1–2 detainees per operation, of whom about half are on the target list and half are found with them [E: the period's capture yields and TF 714's practice]. That gives about 300–700 infrastructure members taken a month at maturity, of whom perhaps 20–35% are at district level or above [E: the task force's targeting priority]. For comparison, the historical Phung Hoang programme neutralised 81,740 between 1968 and 1972, about 1,360 a month, of whom 26,369 were killed, and about 87% of the killed fell in conventional military operations rather than targeted ones [S11]. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units, never more than about 4,500 men, accounted for 14% of the infrastructure members killed [S12]. The Tay Ninh PRU's 92 men killed 31 and captured 64 in ten months [S12]. **This task force's numbers will be smaller than history's totals and better in kind.** That is the right outcome, and it must not be measured as a shortfall.

**Cycle time.** From a detainee's arrival at a corps exploitation cell to a follow-on target worked up: about 6–24 hours, against weeks historically [E: the TF 714 practice, S9, slowed by paper and translation]. From target to raid in the held belt: hours. In the contested belt: one to three nights, the time to put a team into a hamlet the GVN does not hold [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

**The rate limit.** It is not the raid force. There are three, in order:

1. **Interrogators and translators**, about 300–400 in all at full strength (D2).
2. **The prisons and the magistrates.** Memorandum 006 found that once the infrastructure is known, the constraint becomes keeping the cadre: the prisons, the courts and the two-year cap (F006-7) [M006]. This task force raises the arrest rate and so loads that constraint harder than anything the record has yet done.
3. **Night security in the contested belt.** Without it a raid is a single visit and the cadre's replacement arrives the next week.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Reporting chain.** The task force commander is an American major general, with a Vietnamese police brigadier-general as deputy. That pairing reflects the police arm's lead in law and the American lead in exploitation and lift [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. He reports operationally to COMUSMACV as combined commander (P012a). He reports in policy to a four-nation steering board that sits beside memorandum 006's national Phung Hoang Committee: the American ambassador's deputy for pacification, the Director-General of the National Police, the Korean and Australian ambassadors' representatives, and the Agency's chief of station [E: the arrangement the interagency task forces used, S9]. Its targeting authority comes from the magistrates' warrants. Its operational authority comes from the combined commander.

**Interface with the GVN.** The task force is a guest in every province, and its welcome depends on the province chief. The merit-selected chiefs of memorandum 002 are better hosts than history's [M002]. Even a merit chief has a brother-in-law. The Special Branch is the task force's partner and its rival. Memorandum 002 gave the branch sole charge of intelligence on the infrastructure [M002], and this task force puts the Agency, the military services and three foreign governments into the branch's business. Part 2 must address that friction, not assume it away.

**Communications.** The National Fusion Centre runs on the same combined network as the combined command, with national enclaves and a releasable target file [E: D6 of P012a]. The corps task groups link to it by satellite and troposcatter. Field teams carry the period's AN/PRC-25, and long-range raid teams carry the AN/PRC-74 high-frequency set [E: the period's special-operations radios]. The 2026 operations-intelligence teleconference that linked up to 72 locations by 2007 [S9] has no equivalent below the corps task groups. A province cell reports by radio, teletype or courier.

**Talking to ARVN.** The task force talks to the ARVN mainly through the Military Security Service and the corps commanders' intelligence staffs. It needs ARVN battalions for cordons on the larger arrests in the contested belt, and it requests them through the corps-area combined centre (P012a).

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** The whole country, by design. In practice the task force's work is shaped by memorandum 002's three belts [M002]:

- **The held belt**, where police posts and Popular Forces hold the hamlets at night. Here the police arm arrests on warrant, the arrest sticks, and the task force's contribution is the upper layer: the district and province committee members whom the hamlet police seldom reach [M006].
- **The contested belt**, where neither side holds the night. Here the military arm raids. It reaches the target once, takes him or does not, and leaves. The arrest is real, but the replacement arrives because nobody stays.
- **The Front's villages.** Here the task force can only raid at long range, on thin files, at a high risk to its own men and to the innocent. The record's warning stands: raids on thin files kill the wrong men [M006].

**Security preconditions.** The police arm needs a held hamlet. The military arm needs lift, a file thick enough to justify the risk, and a reaction force within about 20–30 minutes' flight [E: the period's helicopter speeds and reaction practice]. Both need a task force whose targets have not been warned, which is a counter-intelligence precondition before it is a military one (D11).

**Terrain.** In the Delta the raid goes by helicopter or by boat. The SEAL platoons' riverine insertions in the Rung Sat and the Delta's canals, by PBR Mk II and the SEAL team assault boat, are the natural method [E: the SEALs' historical employment from 1966]. In the Highlands the infrastructure among the Montagnards is thin, and the task force's targets there are the province committees in their base areas. In I Corps the coastal hamlets of Quang Nam, Quang Ngai and Quang Tin are the hardest ground in the country. The Korean task group's area on the II Corps coast is the one where the task force's discipline is most at risk, because the Korean forces' record in the coastal hamlets of 1966–69 includes massacres the Korean government has never fully acknowledged [S4].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Running cost.** About $60–110 million a year at full strength in 1964 prices [E: about 2,500–3,000 people at mixed military and civilian cost, about 20–30 dedicated helicopters at the period's operating cost, the exploitation centres and the network]. The helicopters are the largest single item.

**Facilities.** The National Fusion Centre at Saigon, four corps task-group compounds with exploitation cells and secure holding areas, and 44 province cells [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. The holding areas are for days, not months. Detention belongs to the GVN.

**Specialist personnel.** Interrogators, linguists and document exploiters fluent in Vietnamese, and Korean–Vietnamese interpreters for the Korean task group [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. These are the scarcest people in the theater. Special-operations raiders: two SEAL platoons, about three to five Special Forces detachments of 12, one Australian Special Air Service squadron of about 70–100, and two ROK special-forces teams [E: the period's establishments; S4]. Helicopter crews: about 60–90 pilots for 20–30 aircraft [E: three pilots per aircraft].

**What RVNAF and the GVN cannot supply.** Lift at night, exploitation at speed, and an analytic culture that shares files. The thing the task force cannot supply either is prison space and courts. Every arrest is a man who must be held, tried or released. The GVN's prisons were historically overcrowded and porous, and a man released for a bribe is back in his hamlet within months [M006].

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**Half of it transfers.** The police-based arm is Vietnamese by nature. Its Special Branch officers, Police Field Force companies and magistrates are Vietnamese, and its method can be handed over in full by about 1970–71 [E: six to seven years of joint operation]. The fusion centre's Vietnamese analysts can learn the method and keep it.

**Half of it does not.** The Agency, the National Security Agency, the helicopters, the exploitation teams' speed and the American special-operations raiders leave with the Americans. The Korean and Australian elements leave with their contingents in 1971–73 [S4][S5]. What remains is the Phung Hoang structure of memorandum 006 with a stronger Special Branch, a national file and a generation of trained Vietnamese analysts [M006]. That is a real inheritance. It is not the task force.

**What happens to it if transferred.** The national target file becomes the Saigon government's property. Memorandum 006's card named the danger: a national file on every suspected cadre is the most valuable instrument a President can seize against his opposition [M006]. This task force's file is larger, better cross-referenced and joined across provinces. After transfer it must be protected by the same independent audit memorandum 006 built [M006], or it becomes the political police's file.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification under four conditions:** where night security holds; where the Special Branch and the district centres have built files thick enough to justify a warrant; where its own counter-intelligence is intact; and where the prisons and courts can hold what it takes.

**Performance collapses in four ways:**

- **In the Front's villages**, where it can only raid on thin files. There it becomes the historical Phoenix at its worst: a body count of men who may or may not have been cadres.
- **When the task force is penetrated** and its targets are warned. The raid then finds an empty house, and the source who named the target is killed.
- **When the detention system breaks.** Cadres released within months for a bribe or by an overloaded magistrate turn the arrest rate into a revolving door.
- **When quotas creep back.** Memorandum 006 forbids them [M006]. A task force measured by its throughput will be tempted to count instead of choose. Historical Phung Hoang chiefs given monthly quotas fabricated reports and made false arrests [S11].

**Tempo over weeks.** The 2026 task force's lesson is that the network breaks under sustained pressure, not under a single blow [S9]. The pressure must be continuous for months in one area, so that each replacement is taken before he has learned the network he inherited. Spread thin across the whole country, the same number of raids does much less. The task force should be concentrated on a few provinces at a time, in sequence [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

**What it never does on its own.** The 2026 evaluation is plain. TF 714's raids degraded al-Qaeda in Iraq to an "irreducible minimum", but were "never sufficient in and of themselves". The surge succeeded because of the Awakening, and the network regenerated as the Islamic State after the withdrawal of 2011 [S10]. The Vietnam equivalent of the Awakening is the hamlet that holds at night and the farmer who owns his land. The task force cannot provide either.

### D11 · Failure modes

**What breaks first is secrecy.** A task force of 2,500–3,000 drawn from four nations and a dozen agencies, with Vietnamese police and soldiers in every cell, will be penetrated within its first year [E: the record's finding that enemy penetration moves to wherever the files are, F006-5]. The first symptom is a run of empty houses. The second is the death of a source.

**A partial failure is the wrong man.** The raid takes the man the file named, and the file was wrong: a feud, a debt or a denunciation by a rival. In the held belt the magistrate's review within 72 hours catches some of these [M002]. In the contested belt there is no magistrate at the door, and a man who runs from a night raid may be shot. Each wrong man is a family the Front recruits from.

**Another partial failure is the turf war.** The Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the Agency each hold back their best sources from the shared file, so the fusion centre fuses what each is willing to lose. This is the failure the 2026 task forces overcame by personal leadership and constant liaison [S9]. In Saigon in 1964 it is overcome only partly.

**A total failure is capture.** A junta or a president uses the file against his rivals, or the task force's raid arm is turned on a political opposition. Its raiders, lift and warrants make it the most dangerous instrument in the country to anyone who seizes it.

**What the force does in the window.** If the task force is down, the district centres and the Special Branch of memorandums 002 and 006 continue at their own pace [M002][M006]. The war against the infrastructure loses its speed and its reach across provinces. It does not lose its base.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** About 30–60 operations a month, mostly in the held belt around Saigon and the Delta's market towns. The files are those memorandum 002's Special Branch and memorandum 006's first centres have built in a few months [M002][M006]. The interrogators are learning the infrastructure's vocabulary. The raiders are learning the hamlets [E: D5].

**Month twelve.** About 150–250 operations a month [E: D5]. The national file now joins about 20,000–40,000 named suspects across provinces [E: the district centres' throughput and the task force's own exploitation]. The interrogators know the system. The enemy has also learned: his district committees have moved out of the held belt, and he has begun to work on the task force's own Vietnamese staff [M006].

**What declines first is the source network.** Each raid that is compromised costs a source, and the task force's best sources are hard to replace. Next is the quality of the files in the contested belt, as the easy targets are taken and the remaining ones are the careful ones. Last is the restraint of the raid forces, which wears thin with each friend killed by a mine on the path to a hamlet.

**What improves** is the analysis. The national file grows denser each month, and each detainee is cross-checked against everything taken before. The enemy can change his people faster than he can change his history.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**The baseline is two things.** The first is the historical Phoenix of 1967–72. It had about 4,000–4,500 PRU men [S12] and province coordinating centres from 1968. It worked under quotas [S11]. It neutralised 81,740 people in five years, 26,369 of them killed, nearly all of those killed in ordinary military operations rather than targeted ones [S11]. The second is what the record already holds: memorandum 002's police and Special Branch and memorandum 006's 2026-practice Phung Hoang from 1964 [M002][M006].

The other benchmark is TF 714 itself [S9][S10]. Against that model this task force has the method and the organisation. It lacks the ISR, the signals and the telephone exploitation that made the model fast. It has more raid targets per analyst, because the infrastructure is bigger than al-Qaeda in Iraq ever was. And it has a harder detention problem, because a Vietnamese cadre can be held for two years at most [M002] and his replacement is trained in the North or the sanctuaries.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P012b -->
- **It does not find the main force.** It targets the infrastructure inside the population; it does nothing against a PAVN or VC regiment (A6).
- **It is not TF 714's speed.** It has the method and the organisation, not the drones, the signals or the telephones. Its exploitation runs on paper, interrogation and helicopters. Its cycle is hours to days, not minutes.
- **It brings no new law.** It works under the 1964 police statute: magistrate review within 72 hours, detention capped at two years, three sources per target [M002]. It has no extrajudicial authority.
- **It does not hold the hamlet.** A raid in the contested belt takes one cadre, and his replacement arrives next week unless someone stays.
- **It does not solve detention.** It raises the arrest rate into the prisons and courts that memorandum 006 found were already the binding constraint.
- **It does not replace the district centres or the Special Branch.** It sits on their files, and without them it has nothing to fuse.
- **It does not stop regeneration.** The infrastructure's cadres are replaced from the North, the sanctuaries and the cadre schools; replacement rate, not arrest rate, is the measure.
- **It does not protect itself from capture.** Its national file and its raid arm are the most dangerous instruments in the country in the hands of a president or a junta.
- **It does not make its partners share.** It puts the Special Branch, the MSS and the Agency in one room; it does not make them hand over their best sources.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P012b -->
It denies the infrastructure the province line. A district committee that moves across a provincial boundary has historically escaped one file for another, and here it moves into the same national file. It denies the upper layer its distance: the province and region committee members whom no hamlet policeman ever reached are now targets that a helicopter and a warrant can reach in a night. It denies a captured cadre's network the weeks it used to have to scatter before his papers were read. It denies nothing in the Front's villages, where it can only raid on thin files, and nothing against the main force.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P012b -->
This task force is the finishing arm the record's war against the infrastructure has lacked. Memorandum 002 gave the war a police that knows the hamlet. Memorandum 006 gave it a method and a centre in every district. This provision gives it a national file across four nations' agencies, a cycle measured in hours, and lift, raiders and arrest teams that can reach a province committee member the same night his name surfaces. Its strongest effect is on the upper layer of the infrastructure, the men who make the hamlet cells into a government, and in the held belt it should take them faster than the Front can train their successors. That is a real and substantial blow to the infrastructure. It is worth stating at full weight, because it is the half of the war the conventional force cannot touch. It is not a transformation, for three reasons the 2026 experience confirms. The record already names most of the infrastructure, so the task force's gain is in finishing rather than finding. Raids alone never defeat a network: they degrade it to an irreducible minimum that holds only where someone holds the ground. And the task force loads the prisons and courts, which were already the binding constraint. Its dangers are penetration, the wrong man in the contested belt, and capture by the palace. Where night security holds, it is the best instrument the war against the infrastructure has. Where it does not, it is the historical Phoenix with better equipment.
