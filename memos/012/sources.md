# Sources — Memorandum 012

One line per source, numbered in order of first use. Cite as [S1], [S2] … in the text.

[S1] George S. Eckhardt, *Command and Control, 1950–1969* (Vietnam Studies), Chapter III "The Buildup of U.S. Forces: July 1965 – July 1966" — US Army Center of Military History, https://webdoc.sub.gwdg.de/ebook/p/2005/CMH_2/www.army.mil/cmh-pg/books/vietnam/comm-control/ch03.htm — Westmoreland's April 1965 recommendation of a small combined US–Vietnamese headquarters "introduced gradually and quietly", the Vietnamese commanders' turn to "extreme sensitivity", its withdrawal; sovereignty and the charge of colonialism as the obstacles; the four combined intelligence centres as the only combined effort; ROK forces under their own control within limits set by the Free World Military Assistance Council (COMUSMACV, COMROKFV, Chief JGS in the chair); other Free World forces under US operational control from April 1965.
[S2] "Free World Military Forces" — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_World_Military_Forces — the working arrangement of 6 September 1965 (MACV logistic and intelligence support, no ROK signature to US operational control); Australia, New Zealand and Thailand under US operational control; peak strengths (ROK about 50,000, Australia 7,626, Thailand 11,568, New Zealand 552); the International Military Assistance Office of December 1964 and the Free World Military Assistance Office of October 1965.
[S3] Hosub Shim, *The Forgotten Army: The Republic of Korea Forces' Conduct in the Vietnam War, 1965–1973* — doctoral dissertation, University of Kansas, 2020, https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/798fa99b-e75e-4bb4-bc89-f45ecdcc18a1/content — no clear agreement on operational control of ROK forces; the late-1965 negotiation ending in "cooperative and coordinative" relations; the ROK field command of late 1966 working as an equal of I Field Force and ARVN II Corps; both governments' wish to avoid the "mercenary" label.
[S4] "South Korea in the Vietnam War" — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea_in_the_Vietnam_War — ROK units (Capital Division, 9th Division, 2nd Marine Brigade, 1st Airborne Special Forces Group teams); 49,755 ROK Army in 1969 and 37,438 at the end of 1972; about 320,000 served; US financing; the more passive posture after 1968; the massacre allegations of Quang Nam and Quang Ngai.
[S5] "1st Australian Task Force" — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_Australian_Task_Force — 1 ATF as an independent command under operational control of II Field Force, Vietnam, by the Wilton–Westmoreland arrangement; its own area in Phuoc Tuy; Nui Dat from 5 June 1966; Australian Force Vietnam as the national administrative headquarters; 7,672 Australians at the February 1968 peak; withdrawal 1970–72.
[S6] "ROK/US Combined Forces Command" — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ROK/US_Combined_Forces_Command — established 7 November 1978; an American commander and a Korean deputy commander; staff sections led alternately by nationality with a deputy of the other; Korean forces under its operational command only in wartime.
[S7] "Military Assistance Command, Vietnam" — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Assistance_Command,_Vietnam — MACV established 8 February 1962 with an initial authorized strength of 216; reorganized 15 May 1964 absorbing MAAG; the Joint General Staff as its counterpart; US strength about 16,000 rising to about 23,300 in 1964.
[S8] Joint Chiefs of Staff Historical Office, *The Joint Chiefs of Staff and the War in Vietnam, 1960–1968*, Part 3, Chapter 4 — https://www.jcs.mil/Portals/36/Documents/History/Vietnam/Vietnam_1960-1968_P003.pdf — the Combined Campaign Plan 1967 promulgated by COMUSMACV and the Chief JGS on 7 November 1966 with a non-rigid division of responsibility; 52,600 Free World soldiers, mostly Korean, on 1 January 1967 out of 1,171,800 allied troops.
[S9] Liam Collins, "Rapid and Radical Adaptation in Counterinsurgency: Task Force 714 in Iraq" — Modern War Institute at West Point, https://mwi.westpoint.edu/rapid-and-radical-adaptation-in-counterinsurgency-task-force-714-in-iraq/ — the F3EA cycle and the shift of effort from finish to exploit and analyse; the cycle shortened from days or weeks to hours; ISR growth from one Predator to dozens of platforms; interrogation staff from about a dozen to over 100 in teams of two or three; the daily operations-intelligence teleconference of up to 72 locations by 2007; more than 2,000 Iraqis sent to trial 2005–07.
[S10] Richard Shultz, "The Irreducible Minimum: An Evaluation of Counterterrorism Operations in Iraq" — PRISM 7:3, National Defense University Press, 15 May 2018, https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/1983604/the-irreducible-minimum-an-evaluation-of-counterterrorism-operations-in-iraq/ — 18 raids in August 2004 and 300 in August 2006, about 300 a month through 2009; the Awakening as the condition of the surge's success; the "irreducible minimum"; raids "never sufficient in and of themselves"; AQI's regeneration as ISI and ISIL after 2011.
[S11] "Phoenix Program" — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_program — ICEX of June 1967; the Phung Hoang decrees of December 1967 and July 1968; 81,740 neutralized 1968–72 of whom 26,369 killed; about 87% of the killed in conventional operations; quotas and false arrests; the 1971 hearings; Colby's and Herrington's accounts of the communists' view of the programme.
[S12] Andrew R. Finlayson, "The Tay Ninh Provincial Reconnaissance Unit and Its Role in the Phoenix Program, 1969–70" — *Studies in Intelligence* 51:2 (2007), CIA Center for the Study of Intelligence, https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/The-Tay-Ninh-PRU-Studies-51-2-2007.pdf — PRUs (Counter-Terror Teams) from 1964, never more than about 4,500 men in all 44 provinces; Tay Ninh's 92 men in five teams of 18; 31 VCI killed and 64 captured September 1969 – June 1970 for two PRU dead; about two-thirds of targets from the PRU's own net; PRUs 14% of VCI killed under Phoenix; agent reports "uniformly inaccurate"; interagency reluctance to share; the province chief's sole authority to sign an arrest order.
