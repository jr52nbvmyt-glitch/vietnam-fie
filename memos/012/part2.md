## §2 Part A — Integration

The two grants fill the two oldest gaps in this headquarters' organisation chart, and they fill them unequally. The combined operations command answers the gap register's sentence that nobody manages the war (G-13). It does so for the military war only, and only while the Vietnamese consent. The task force answers the problem the record has carried since memorandum 006: the infrastructure, driven out of the held belt, governs the held belt from just outside it (F006-4). One of these provisions sits on the main-force side of A6 and the other on the infrastructure side. They meet in one place, the corps-area combined centre, where the task force's raids draw on the combined command's lift, cordons and authority to act across a province line. That meeting is where most of this memorandum's compound effects live.

### Functional systems

**P012a joins FIX, STRIKE, ADVISE and SUSTAIN, and strengthens FIX most.** It holds no enemy itself. It shortens the time between finding a regiment and putting a blocking force across its line of withdrawal. It also removes the boundaries the enemy used to slip through: the seam between ARVN and American sectors, the edge of the Korean area on the II Corps coast, and the province lines that each corps defended as its own. In STRIKE it is a switchboard, not a gun. The combined fire-support centres send an ARVN battalion's call to whichever American, Vietnamese or Korean guns can answer it. In ADVISE it changes the most from history: the adviser's report becomes an input to a commander who can act on it. That is a change in kind, and the TNA's sixth position applies at once. Every gain in leverage is paid for in the Republic's claim to be its own government (POSTNA-6). In SUSTAIN it is a cost: about 1,400–2,000 staff and their interpreters [M012: D2 of P012a].

**P012b joins SUBVERT and FIND on the infrastructure side, and makes one thing newly viable in FIX: the finish against a district or province committee outside the held belt.** Before this provision, the record's attack on the infrastructure had a method (P006a), a police (P002a), a census and a land register (P001a). Its finishing force was a PRU of about 80 men per province and a Police Field Force company [M006][M002]. It could reach a committee that sat inside the posted villages. It could not reliably reach one that had moved to the contested edge and governed by courier, which is what the Hoc Mon committee did after STILL WATER. That committee governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi until 1968 (EV006-2) [X-006]. The task force's helicopters, raid teams and cross-province warrants make that edge reachable.

**What neither touches:** INTERDICT and the sanctuaries. Neither provision changes the Trail, Sihanoukville, the Cambodian base areas or the restricted zones. The combined command plans to the border and stops there, as CONFIG requires.

The compound that matters most is **P012b with P006a and P002a**. Memorandum 006 found that the district centres drove the district committees out of the held belt and that the committees went on governing from the contested edge by courier (F006-4). The task force exists to follow them there. It has three things the centres lacked: a file that crosses the province line, a helicopter and a raid team available the same night, and an interrogator who can turn a courier into the committee's meeting house before the committee hears she has been taken. The first year of this pairing should look like STILL WATER repeated across the Saigon belt and the Delta, but with the remnant chased into Cu Chi rather than left there.

**F012-1** (SUBVERT, FIND) The task force sits above the district centres as their finishing and cross-province arm. The centres name the committee and the task force reaches it wherever it has moved. Across a provincial boundary, a committee that history let escape one file for another now moves into the same national file. The integration is real only if the centres' files are thick: in 1964 most of them are a few months old, and the pairing matures from about mid-1965 [E: the centres' build-up of 100 districts by end 1964 and all by end 1965, M006].

**F012-2** (SUBVERT, SECURE) The task force's police arm runs through memorandum 002's Special Branch, and its military arm runs through four nations' special-operations troops. The statutory charge on the infrastructure belongs to the Branch [M002]. The task force therefore needs a rule, and I set it at the outset: every arrest on warrant is a Special Branch arrest, and the military arm raids only where a Branch officer certifies that the police arm cannot reach. That keeps the law in Vietnamese hands. It also gives the Branch a veto over raids, which a penetrated Branch will use to warn targets (F012-14).

**F012-3** (ADVISE, FIX) The combined staff's Vietnamese half comes from the officers the Dalat staff course has produced since 1957 [M007]. In 1964 those are perhaps 200–300 staff-trained field-grade officers [M012: D5 of P012a]. The combined command and the divisions draw on the same men, and so do the province list and the district centres (POSCP01-3). The command is staffed in 1964 by taking a staff-trained major from about one division in three [E: 200–300 men against about 10 divisions' operations and intelligence staffs and the corps staffs].

**F012-4** (ADVISE) The selected adviser of memorandum 006 now reports to a commander who can act on what he says [M006]. Under the historical system his report that a battalion would not move went up the American chain and stopped there. Here it reaches the combined commander, who can task the battalion's division and ask the board to relieve its commander. The adviser's influence becomes, at one remove, authority. This is the largest single change P012a makes, and it is the one POSTNA-6 prices most heavily.

**F012-5** (STRIKE, FIX) The combined fires centre makes the B-52J, the GBU-38 and the coordinate cell's aimpoints available to ARVN units through the same switchboard as to American ones [M005]. History lent American fire to the ARVN by request, through the adviser. This command allocates it by plan. The air-war study judges that the instruments of the conventional war were never the Republic's (studies/air-war). This makes them more available and no more owned.

**F012-6** (FIX, SUBVERT) The combined command and the task force meet at the corps-area centre. The task force asks for an ARVN battalion to cordon a large arrest in the contested belt, and the centre tasks one within a day instead of after a week of persuasion. In return, the task force's exploitation of captured infrastructure papers tells the combined estimate where a main-force regiment will be fed, guided and quartered. Rice caches, guides and hamlet billets are the infrastructure's work [E: the logistics relationship between the infrastructure and the main force]. This is the one place where the two halves of A6 touch, and it is real but narrow. The infrastructure knows where a regiment will eat. It does not know where the regiment will fight.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

**The chain is unchanged at its first link and shortened at its second.** The theater still finds the main force no better than history did under canopy, and the regiment still chooses its fights (POS003-1, G-7). What changes is the interval between a find and a block. Historically, a regiment located by radio direction-finding or a rallier in one corps area, moving toward the boundary of another, was free for the days it took two national chains to agree who would block it. Under the combined command the corps-area centre tasks the blocking force within 24–48 hours [M012: D5 of P012a], and across corps boundaries within three to five days.

That improvement matters at the boundaries and nowhere else. A regiment that withdraws toward Cambodia still reaches the sanctuary. A combined block on the near side of the border closes the route the regiment would have used inside the Republic. It does nothing about the line it can cross. A7 governs. The regiment withdraws into a base area this force may not enter, rebuilds there, and returns on its own timetable. The combined command makes the toll of the transit higher. It does not touch the arithmetic of regeneration.

**The chain's weak link moves to the order's execution.** A combined order to an ARVN division to block a valley reaches a division commander who may move fast, slowly or to the wrong place. In 1964–65 that is the binding constraint (F012-9). From 1966 the board's command lists change who commands the divisions [M007], and the constraint eases.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

**This chain was already strong in the record at identification, and P012b strengthens its last three links: fix, finish and exploit.** The chapters set FIND.vci at the top of the converted scale in 1965–68 (P2 and P3 end vectors). That conversion may overstate it (POSCP02-1 and DIRR01-2 require it to be re-judged). The honest reading is that the Special Branch and the district centres know most of the hamlet cadres in the held belt and many district committee members, and that the problem has moved to three places: reaching the committee that has left, keeping the cadre once taken (F006-7), and finding the senior cadre who never entered the held belt at all.

P012b acts on the first and third. It reaches the committee that left by helicopter and cross-province warrant. It finds the senior cadre through exploitation: a district finance cadre's papers name the province finance section, and its courier names the region. It does nothing for the second, and it makes the second worse by raising the arrest rate into prisons and courts that were already the binding constraint (F012-8).

**A6 requires me to state which half this is.** It is the infrastructure half entirely. The task force finds no regiment, and its exploitation of infrastructure papers tells the combined estimate where a regiment will eat, not where it will fight (F012-6).

### Dependence on SECURE

**Everything P012b achieves depends on night security, and P012a can damage night security while helping the war elsewhere.** The task force's police arm works only where the GVN holds the hamlet at night. Its military arm reaches further, but a raid in a hamlet nobody holds afterward is a visit (POSTNA-2, POS006-1). Its best yield is in the held belt's edge: the committees that left the posted villages and now sit one district away. Only resident security keeps a taken committee from being replaced. Concretely, that means Popular Forces in the posts of memorandum 002, a radio from memorandum 008, and a schooled leader from memoranda 009 and 010.

P012a's danger to SECURE is the corps commander's new instrument. A combined order can take an ARVN battalion off territorial security and send it to block a regiment. The 1967 combined plan made ARVN battalions available for territorial security [S8]. Under operational control the combined commander can move them the other way, and an American commander will be tempted to. The record has already seen what happens to districts when their Regional Force companies go to the corps (EV004-2). A combined plan that strips the held belt to fix the main force is the historical American war fought with Vietnamese battalions.

**F012-7** (SECURE, FIX) The combined command can take ARVN battalions off territorial security for main-force operations faster than any historical arrangement could, and an American combined commander measured on main-force results will be tempted to do so. The protection is a rule in the combined campaign plan. ARVN battalions assigned to support a province's territorial security are released only by the corps commander with the province chief's concurrence, and never more than one in three at a time [E: the rule this headquarters sets in the first combined plan, so that no province loses its whole regular screen at once]. Whether that rule holds is a question for the chronicle.

### The ADVISE cluster

**P012a is the largest change to the advisory relationship in the record, and it changes it in the direction POSTNA-6 warns about.** Every earlier provision in the ADVISE cluster deepened influence without adding authority: the selected adviser (P006c), the educated counterpart (P007b), the board (P007c) and the Aid Compliance Board (P001b). POS006-3 and F007-3 both say so in terms. This provision adds authority. It is operational control, not advice.

That is why it works. It gives the combined commander the power to task the ARVN and the adviser's report a commander who can act on it. It is also why it costs. The Republic's generals now take operational orders from an American, and the Front's propaganda has a fact to point at. The ADVISE cluster's long-run product, an RVNAF that can fight without the Americans, is served only if the combined staff is run as a school and the Vietnamese deputy commands in fact as well as in name from about 1969 [M012: D9 of P012a]. Run any other way, it is the most efficient route yet devised to an ARVN that waits for orders.

P012b feeds the cluster on the police and intelligence side. It trains a generation of Vietnamese analysts and interrogators in the 2026 method, which survives the Americans' departure. It starves the cluster on the military side, by taking the PRU's best men and the ARVN's best intelligence officers into a combined organisation that will leave with the Americans.

### Against the studies

The pack loads one study, the air war (studies/air-war). The pushes it shares with this memorandum are P012a's on fire coordination (*ARVN.effectiveness.fire_coordination* +2..+5) and the Republic's own fire support (*ARVN.self_sufficiency.fire_support* −4..0), and P012b's on civilian harm (0..+3).

**Fire coordination and self-sufficiency: consistent.** The study leaves *fire_coordination* unjudged in every phase, because judging it would oblige the study to name the binding facet of ARVN effectiveness (Q-5). It records the evidence: ARVN battalions in Lam Son 719 with no means to give a coordinate, and the officers of 1972 using American air with an educated staff's precision. P012a's push on fire coordination does not contradict that account. It adds a mechanism, a combined switchboard, that makes the ARVN's use of American fire faster and more routine from 1964. The study judges the Republic's own fire support at its lowest in Phase V, at −3..0, on the finding that An Loc and Kontum held on American coordinates (POS005-2). P012a's −4..0 on the same facet runs in the same direction and is consistent: a combined fires centre that the ARVN uses from 1964 teaches the dependence earlier and more thoroughly. The study's summary sentence describes the risk exactly. The ARVN learned to hold a besieged position and wait for the bombs, and in 1974 waited at Thuong Duc for a siege box that no longer existed (EV000-91). P012a does not change that conclusion. It makes it more likely, unless the combined fires centre is handed over with Vietnamese guns behind it.

**Civilian harm: consistent.** The study tags *POP.civilian_harm* and declines to judge it, because the variable covers harm from every side's operations and its decisive differences lie outside the air war (Q-6). P012b's push of 0..+3 lies entirely outside the air war, in night raids on thin files. Nothing in the study contradicts it. The study's question stands: no study or checkpoint yet holds this variable, and P012b adds to the list of provisions that push it and nobody judges it.

### Employment concepts

#### EC012-1 · The quarterly combined plan

This is P012a's backbone. Each quarter the combined staff writes a campaign plan, and the Combined Military Committee approves it. It assigns each corps area its tasks against the main force, its contribution to territorial security and its share of the combined reserve and fires. Each corps-area centre turns it into a monthly operations order. The cycle runs on a twelve-week drumbeat. The plan is drafted in weeks one to four, reviewed with the corps commanders in week five and approved by the committee in week six. Then come six weeks of execution and assessment, overlapping the next plan's draft [E: the 2026 joint planning cycle compressed to a quarter]. Its minimum viable package is the combined headquarters' C3 and C5 directorates, the four corps-area centres and the Vietnamese deputy with authority to speak for the JGS. Without the Vietnamese deputy's authority it degrades to the historical annual plan that bound nobody [S8].

#### EC012-2 · The cross-boundary block

This is the concept that closes the seams. A regiment located near a corps or national boundary is blocked by whichever force is closest, of whatever nation, under a single order from the corps-area centre, or from the headquarters if two corps are involved. The sequence runs as follows:

1. The corps-area centre receives a location from the combined intelligence centre, a rallier or a radio fix.
2. A planning cell of four to six officers produces a blocking order within six to twelve hours.
3. The blocking force, an ARVN battalion, an American battalion or a Korean battalion where Seoul has agreed, moves by road or helicopter within 24–48 hours.
4. The block holds for three to seven days, and then the force returns to its own area.

[E: the corps-area centre's capacity and the period's movement rates.] The cycle rate is about two to four cross-boundary blocks a month per corps area at full strength [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. The minimum package is one battalion, its lift, and fire from the corps-area fires centre. The concept depends on the 1st Cavalry Division's airmobility from late 1965 and on the theater's helicopter hours. Without lift it degrades to road moves the enemy watches.

#### EC012-3 · The combined fire switchboard

An ARVN battalion or a territorial post calls for fire on its own net. The call reaches the division's fire-support element and, through the corps-area fires centre, every gun and aircraft able to answer it, whatever their nationality. The centre runs around the clock with about 15–25 officers and NCOs per corps area [E: a 2026 fires cell scaled to the period]. It handles about 40–120 fire missions a day per corps area at the 1967–68 peak [E: the corps areas' historical artillery tempo]. The B-52J's close-support boxes and the coordinate cell's aimpoints come into the same switchboard from 1965 [M005]. The guns it calls are the ARVN's M101A1 105mm and M114A1 155mm howitzers, the American M102 and M109 batteries from 1965–66, the AC-47 gunships at night, and the tactical air that an O-1 Bird Dog forward air controller marks. Without American guns in the corps area the switchboard has only Vietnamese guns to call, and its value falls to what the ARVN's artillery and the VNAF's A-1H Skyraiders can deliver.

#### EC012-4 · The Vietnamese deputy's corps

From 1966, one corps area is commanded in the combined system by its ARVN commander, with the American field-force commander as his deputy. IV Corps in the Delta is the natural candidate, with few American troops until 1967 [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. It is the school for the eventual transfer [M012: D9 of P012a]. The cycle is a year: the ARVN commander runs his corps area's combined operations, the combined headquarters reviews them each quarter, and a second corps area follows when the first works. The concept depends on the selection board's command lists (P007c) putting a capable general in IV Corps. Without one it degrades to an American deputy running the corps in fact.

#### EC012-5 · The two-layer plan

This is the concept the leak produces (F012-15). The combined staff writes a combined plan that every Vietnamese, Korean and Australian staff officer sees. A smaller cell of the combined commander's choosing writes the real objectives and the timing, and releases them to the executing commanders at the last hours. It is the historical MACV's practice brought inside the combined staff [H]. The combined staff knows it exists, and the Vietnamese officers resent it. It is the price of the command's penetration. I record it as a concept the command will adopt by mid-1964, not one I would choose.

#### EC012-6 · The province campaign

This is P012b's backbone, and it rests on the 2026 lesson that a network breaks under sustained pressure rather than single blows [S9]. The task force concentrates on three to five provinces at a time, for four to six months each. It works each committee from the hamlet to the province level until the replacements arriving are untrained and the network's informants have stopped reporting. Then it moves to the next group of provinces and leaves the district centres to hold what was gained. In 1964–65 the order runs through the Saigon belt (Gia Dinh, Hau Nghia, Long An, Bien Hoa), then the upper Delta (Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa, Vinh Long), then the coastal provinces of II Corps [E: the held belt's extent, M002]. The minimum package per province is a task-group detachment of about 60–100 people, two lift helicopters and a gunship on call, and a district centre with at least six months of files [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. Without the district files it degrades to raids on rumour.

#### EC012-7 · The same-night follow-on

A detainee taken at 0200 reaches the corps task group's exploitation cell by 0400. By 1000 his papers and his first interview have produced one to three follow-on targets. The follow-on raid goes that night, before the network knows he talked [E: the 2026 practice of exploitation in hours, S9, slowed by paper and translation]. The cycle runs at about two to four follow-ons a week per corps task group in 1964, rising to one or two a night by 1966 [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. It is the 2026 model's core, and it works only where the exploitation cell has a Vietnamese-speaking interrogator, the detainee has something to give, and the target is within helicopter reach. In the Delta's riverine provinces the follow-on goes by PBR or SEAL team assault boat [E: the SEAL platoons' historical insertion means].

#### EC012-8 · The warrant arrest in the held belt

This is the task force's police arm on an ordinary night. A Special Branch officer holds a warrant signed by the corps task group's magistrate on three sources. A Police Field Force section makes the arrest, with the hamlet's Popular Forces platoon in the cordon. The detainee goes through the biometric kit [M006], then to the province interrogation centre and the magistrate within 72 hours [M002]. The cycle takes about 4–8 hours per arrest from the move to the handover. A province task-group detachment makes about one to three arrests a night at full tempo [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. This is the concept that should carry 60–70% of the task force's operations [M012: D5 of P012b].

#### EC012-9 · The raid into the contested belt

This is the military arm, against a target the police cannot reach. A raid team of 12–20 men inserts by helicopter or on foot at night. It is an American, Australian, Korean or Vietnamese special-operations team with a Special Branch officer attached. An ARVN or Regional Force company forms a cordon where the target is a committee with guards. A gunship stands on call and a reaction force is within 20–30 minutes' flight. The team aims to capture, and fights when fired on. The cycle is one raid per team every two to four nights, constrained by planning, rest and the helicopters [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. The minimum package is the team, two lift helicopters, a gunship and a Branch officer. Without the Branch officer the raid is a military operation without a warrant, and the rule (F012-2) forbids it.

#### EC012-10 · The named offer

This one is borrowed from memorandum 006's concept of the same purpose (EC006-2) and accelerated. A cadre the task force has fully mapped receives, through a relative or a rallier, an offer to rally before the raid. The offer names his network and his role, so he knows the task force knows. About one in five to one in three such offers is taken in the held belt's edge [E: the period's Chieu Hoi response to targeted appeals]. Each taker is worth more than any raid, because he brings the network's current state rather than its history.

#### EC012-11 · The joint VCI kill list (abandoned)

The 2026 model's high-value-target lists, in which a named man is killed if he cannot be captured, were tried by the military arm's American and Korean elements in the contested belt of the II Corps coast in late 1965 [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. They were abandoned within months for three reasons. The files there were thin, and the men killed included several whom the Commission's audit could not show to be cadres. Under the 1964 statute a kill without a warrant is a homicide case for the magistrate, and two Korean team members were referred. And the Front made a story of every death on the pattern it later used at Duc Thanh (EV006-4). The concept names the constraint the dossier stated: the task force has no extrajudicial authority [U], and a kill list is one.

#### EC012-12 · The drone-model cycle (abandoned)

The American planners of 1964 attempted to run the 2026 cycle at the 2026 tempo: dozens of raids a night against a target library, the cycle measured in minutes. It was abandoned by the summer of 1964. There were no telephones to exploit, no persistent full-motion video over the hamlet and no data network below the corps task groups [M012: D14 of P012b]. The tempo produced raids on targets the exploitation cells had not had time to confirm, and the magistrates released half the detainees within 72 hours [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. The concept names the constraint of the inputs: the method transfers, the speed does not.

#### EC012-13 · Task-force custody (abandoned)

The corps task groups first held detainees for up to two weeks of exploitation in their own compounds, outside the GVN's prisons. That broke the 72-hour rule. The Special Branch's own officers refused to sign the extensions, and the Commission opened a case in the first quarter of 1965 [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. The task groups returned to holding for 72 hours and handing over to the province interrogation centres. Custody is the GVN's, and the task force's exploitation must happen inside the 72 hours or through the GVN's own interrogators.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**The infrastructure's upper layer moves one step further out, and the main force's crossings move from the seams to the borders.**

**F012-8** (SUBVERT, SECURE) The district and province committees the task force can now reach move beyond helicopter-and-warrant range: into the base areas (War Zones C and D, the U Minh, the Do Xa and the An Lao), into Cambodia, and into the towns. The task force follows them into the towns, where the police arm works best. It cannot follow them into the base areas without a main-force operation, or across the border at all. The committee that governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi under memorandum 006 governs it from the Ho Bo woods or from a Cambodian sanctuary under this one [E: the next step of F006-4's displacement]. That is a longer courier line. The cost to the Front is real: slower orders, more couriers to intercept, tax collected by visiting teams rather than resident ones. But the committee survives. What the task force's intelligence loses is the committee's proximity. A committee in Cu Chi could be watched. One in the Ho Bo woods is a main-force problem.

**F012-9** (FIX) The combined command closes the boundaries between corps and nations inside the Republic, and the main force's escape routes move to the international borders. A PAVN regiment in Binh Dinh historically used the seam between the Korean Capital Division's area and the ARVN 22nd Division's to slip from the coast into the An Lao valley. It now finds the seam watched by one corps-area centre. It moves its withdrawal routes west, toward the Highlands and Laos. That is longer, harder on the regiment, and ends in a sanctuary this force may not enter. The effect is to push the main force's war closer to the borders, which concentrates it where the American firepower of memoranda 003 and 005 is most effective [M005]. It also makes the cross-border sanctuaries more valuable to the enemy than they already were.

**F012-10** (ADVISE) Displacement inside the RVNAF: the able staff officers move from the divisions to the combined staff. A division that loses its best operations officer to the combined command plans worse in 1964–65 [E: the thin pool, POSCP01-3]. That is a real cost in the divisions that need planning most, the Delta divisions fighting the main-force battles of 1964 (EV002-2).

### Bottleneck migration

**F012-11** (FIX, ADVISE) Against the main force, the binding constraint moves from agreement to execution. Historically the constraint was getting the ARVN to agree to a combined plan. Now it is getting the ARVN division commander to carry out a combined order at the speed it was given. From 1966 the constraint moves again, to the enemy's choice of battle, which no command arrangement touches (POSTNA-3). At that point the combined command's gain is spent, and the war against the main force is back where G-7 says it has always been.

**F012-12** (SUBVERT, SECURE) Against the infrastructure, the binding constraint moves decisively to detention and to replacement. Memorandum 006 found it had already moved from knowing the cadre to keeping him (F006-7). P012b raises the arrest rate by about 300–700 a month at maturity [M012: D5 of P012b], into a prison system the record has never enlarged. The two-year cap of the police statute [M002] means that the cadres taken in 1965 are released in 1967, often to the same districts. The task force will be taking some of them a second time by 1968. Behind the prisons stands the replacement rate. The Front's cadre schools in the North and the sanctuaries train district cadres at a rate this force cannot touch [E: the period's cadre training system]. The real measure of the task force is whether it takes cadres faster than they are replaced in the provinces where it concentrates. In the held belt's edge of 1965–67, I judge that it does. In the provinces where it does not concentrate, it does not.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F012-13** (SUSTAIN, ADVISE, FIND) Three things are consumed that the war cannot spare:

- **Helicopter hours.** The task force's 20–30 dedicated aircraft are about 5–8% of the theater's Army helicopters in 1965 and 1–2% at the 1968 peak [E: about 400 in mid-1965 and about 3,000–4,000 at the peak, against 20–30 for the task force]. In 1964–65 that is a real cut in the lift available to ARVN operations and medevac.
- **Vietnamese-speaking interrogators and analysts.** The task force needs about 300–400 of them [M012: D2 of P012b]. They are the same men the district centres, the coordinate cell's interpretation and the advisory corps need (F006-11).
- **Staff-trained field-grade officers, Vietnamese and American.** The combined command takes about 150–250 of the best American majors and lieutenant colonels and about 200–300 of the ARVN's staff-trained field-grade officers [M012: D2 of P012a].

What is not being done: in 1964–65 about one ARVN division in three runs with a weaker operations staff than history's. The district centres lose some of their best analysts to the task force's province cells. The ARVN loses perhaps a sixth of the lift it would have had in 1964 [E: 20–30 helicopters against about 150–250 in Army aviation units supporting the ARVN in 1964].

### Counterfactual erasure

**F012-14** (ADVISE, FIX) Three historical arrangements no longer happen. The first is the Free World Military Assistance Council as a forum for Korean independence: it becomes the Combined Military Committee's standing session, with the Koreans inside the command rather than beside it. The second is the parallel MACV–JGS planning of 1964–66, in which the American and Vietnamese staffs planned separate wars and met once a year to divide the country [S8]. The third is the "cooperative and coordinative" relations of the Korean divisions [S3]. The attention those arrangements consumed, perhaps a quarter of the senior staff's time on coordination in 1965–66 by Westmoreland's own account of the burden [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice], is freed for operations. It is consumed instead by the combined command's own politics: the red cards, the Vietnamese deputy's standing and the two-layer plan.

**F012-15** (SUBVERT) What erases on the infrastructure side is the historical gap of 1964–67. In history the targeted attack on the infrastructure barely existed before ICEX in June 1967 [S11]. The record had already filled that gap with the police and the district centres (F006-13). This provision adds the finishing arm from the first day. Erased with it is the period in which a district committee driven out of the held belt could reconstitute in the next district at leisure. The Front's district cadres in the Saigon belt and the upper Delta of 1965–67 are no longer the men history's Phoenix found waiting in 1968. Many are dead, in prison, rallied, or gone to the base areas.

### Perverse effect

**F012-16** (SECURE, SUBVERT) The task force's success in the held belt pulls it into the contested belt, where its method becomes a raid on a thin file. This is memorandum 006's perverse effect (F006-15) carried by helicopter and at four times the speed. Each wrong man taken or killed in the contested belt is a family the Front can name. The household record of the census and the land register (P001a, P002a) tells the Front, as well as the task force, exactly whose family it was. The effect is moderate. It is bounded by the Commission's audit and by the abandonment of the kill list (EC012-11). It is not bounded to zero.

**F012-17** (ADVISE, SECURE) A capable ARVN division, made more effective by the combined staff's planning, is tasked away from the province it secured. The more effective the division, the more the combined command wants it elsewhere. This is the dossier's warning (F012-7) seen as a perverse effect of success. The 25th Division's districts in Hau Nghia and Long An, which the board's list improved (EV007-4), are the case: a combined commander who sees a good division sees a reserve.

**F012-18** (SUBVERT, ADVISE) The task force's national file is larger, better cross-referenced and better joined across provinces than memorandum 006's. In this war memorandum 006's file was moved into the Central Intelligence Organization in 1970, consolidated for the 1971 election and captured intact on 30 April 1975 (POS006-4). Every improvement P012b makes to the file makes that capture worse. The palace's use of the file in 1970–71 is better informed, and the North's use of it in 1975 names more people. Per DIRMT01-5 the net must be stated both ways. The file's value against the infrastructure in 1965–70 is large. Its value to the palace in 1970–71 and to the enemy in 1975 is large too, and the second does not cancel the first because it falls on different people at different times. The people named in 1975 are the informants, ralliers and police who made the file. That is the moral and practical weight of this finding, and I record it at moderate-to-major.

### Anticipation

**F012-19** (FIX) The enemy declines battle near the boundaries the combined command has closed, and the combined command's metrics show nothing. A regiment that would have slipped through a seam in history does not try. It waits, withdraws early, or moves its war elsewhere. The combined command's gain appears as fewer contacts at the seams, which its own reporting will read as success or failure depending on who is reading. This is the anticipation effect at its purest, and A13 applies: an invisible improvement is not a nonexistent one, and an improved metric is not an improved war.

**F012-20** (SUBVERT, FIND) In the provinces where the task force has concentrated, its arrest count falls within six to nine months, because the network it was taking has been taken or has left. This is POS002-1 and F006-18 again. The count falls where the method works best. The task force must be measured by the Front's tax collections and its replacement rate in those provinces, not by its arrests. Memorandum 002's captured tax receipts from Hoc Mon, at about a fifth of 1964's collections by 1966, are the measure (EV006-2) [X-006].

### Enemy detection and learning

**F012-21** (SUBVERT) COSVN reads the task force within a season and draws three conclusions, two of them right. The first is right: the danger is the same-night follow-on, so cadres must be compartmented and a taken cadre's network must move within hours. The Front adopts a rule that any member taken alive is presumed to talk, and his contacts move by dawn [E: the period's Front security doctrine, accelerated]. The second is also right: the task force has Vietnamese staff, and the Front's security sections set out to penetrate it through the Special Branch and the PRUs. The third is wrong. COSVN reads the task force as an American assassination machine and builds its propaganda on that. The task force's own capture-first rules, the magistrates' releases and the Commission's audit then undercut the propaganda wherever the rules hold. In the Delta and the Saigon belt the Front's word on the task force is believed less than its word on the historical Phoenix. On the II Corps coast, where the Korean element works and the kill list was tried, it is believed more.

The doctrine propagates from COSVN to the region and province committees by courier in two to three months, and to the district cells in four to six [E: the period's courier times]. Hanoi's Ministry of Public Security applies the same lesson to its own agents in the GVN: compartment them, and never let one know another.

**F012-22** (FIX, ADVISE) Hanoi reads the combined command quickly and correctly as a political fact before a military one. Within weeks the Front's radio calls the ARVN an army commanded by an American general [E: the predictable line, given the "puppet" vocabulary of the period]. Hanoi's military staff learns the combined plan's rhythm, the twelve-week cycle and the monthly corps orders, from its agents in the combined staff within six months. It times its main-force operations to the plan's gaps: the weeks of transition between quarterly plans and the days after a coup when the Vietnamese deputy's authority is in doubt.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F012-23** (SUBVERT, FIX) On the infrastructure side the exchange favours the Republic in the held belt and is roughly even at its edge. Compartmenting cells and moving the network at dawn costs the Front speed, coordination and the tax take, because a network that moves every time a man is taken governs badly. Moving the district committees to the base areas costs the Front its proximity to the population it governs. Penetrating the task force is cheap. A few agents in the Special Branch and the PRUs cost the Front little, and each is worth an empty house and a dead source. That is the adaptation the Front can afford, and it is the one that hurts the task force most. The Commission's audit and the Branch's counter-intelligence are the defences, and they catch some (P001b, P002a).

On the main-force side the exchange is cheap for the enemy. Moving withdrawal routes to the borders costs the regiments a longer march. Penetrating the combined staff costs one or two agents and is worth a plan a quarter. The combined command's gain against the main force is worth what it costs only at the seams it closes. The enemy's adaptation costs him less than the provision costs this force.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F012-24** (ADVISE, SUSTAIN) The combined command reaches Washington by two routes, and both return. The first runs through Congress and the press. An American general commanding Vietnamese and Korean divisions is an American war in every sense the critics will use: the Senate Foreign Relations Committee asks in 1965 why the Republic cannot command its own army, and the question returns as pressure to Vietnamize sooner [E: the period's congressional politics]. The second runs through Seoul. The Korean contingent's place in a combined command makes it more visibly an American instrument, and the "mercenary" charge both governments wanted to avoid is sharpened [S3]. The National Assembly in Seoul debates the 1966 reinforcement with the combined command as an issue. I judge the reinforcement still passes, because the American payments and the guarantee on American troops in Korea are unchanged [S4]. That is a judgment, and the chronicle should test it.

The task force reaches Washington faster than memorandum 006's centres did, because an American-led raid force is a press story whatever its rules. POS006-5 records that "Phoenix" became a press scandal in March 1967 over the Duc Thanh raid. A task force with American and Korean raid teams makes a scandal likely a year earlier, in 1966. The Commission's audit answers each case within weeks, which is the most this force can do. Congressional tolerance for the police and intelligence programmes is a variable the critics can reach. Each raid scandal lowers it slightly, and the return path is pressure on the Public Safety and the Agency appropriations, not on the war's.

### Open channel

**F012-25** (FIND, SUBVERT) The task force produces something the theater has never had: a national, cross-referenced picture of the Front's political organisation, its finance, its recruitment and its courier lines, at the level of the region and the province. In history that picture existed only in fragments in the Combined Document Exploitation Center and in the Agency's station. Here it is a living file. Its first use is targeting. Its second, which the task force was not built for, is strategic. The national file shows by 1966 which provinces the Front is losing and which it is rebuilding, months before the Hamlet Evaluation System's scores or any main-force contact would show it [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. It is the only metric in the theater generated by the enemy's own administration (A13), and the combined command should plan against it.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F012-26 · Counter-directional** (ADVISE, FIX) The combined command makes the ARVN fight better in 1964–72 and weaker in 1973–75, and the second effect may exceed the first. A division commander tasked from a combined headquarters, supplied with American fire through a combined switchboard and judged by an American commander's plan, stops planning his own war. The combined staff becomes the ARVN's brain, and the staff's American half and its fires leave in 1972–73. The air-war study judges the Republic's own fire support at −3..0 in Phase V on exactly this mechanism, at the end of a war in which the ARVN had no combined command (studies/air-war). P012a pushes the same facet −4..0. The weight is major. It does not cancel the provision's gain in the main-force war of 1965–68, which is real (F012-27). But it is the reason this provision may make the 1975 collapse no later and possibly earlier, unless the transfer of EC012-4 begins by 1966 and the Vietnamese deputy commands two corps areas in fact by 1969. The headquarters intends to run it that way, and Part 3 will say whether it did.

**F012-27 · Strongest positive** (SUBVERT, SECURE, FIND) The Front's government of the held belt from its edge is broken in 1965–67. This is the compound of P012b with the district centres (P006a), the Special Branch (P002a) and the census and land register (P001a). Memorandum 006 drove the district committees out of the posted villages. This memorandum takes them where they went. The cross-province file, the same-night follow-on and the helicopter reach the committee in the next district. The committees of the Saigon belt and the upper Delta are taken faster than the Front can train their replacements in the provinces where the task force concentrates. By the end of 1967, I judge, the Front's district-level administration in the held belt and its edge is about a third to a half weaker than it is in the record without this provision in about 12–18 of the 44 provinces, on the province campaign's rate set against the cadre schools' replacement rate [X-012]. The tax take in those provinces falls toward the Hoc Mon figure of a fifth of 1964's. This is the half of the war the conventional force could never reach. Breaking the Front's administration at the district level is the precondition for every gain in hamlet security the record has made since memorandum 002, because a district committee that cannot govern cannot rebuild the hamlet cells that hamlet security keeps out. It is not a transformation, because the Front's villages and the base areas are untouched and the replacements keep coming. It is the largest single blow to the infrastructure in the record.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

MACV's headquarters becomes the American half of the combined headquarters. Its J2, J3 and J5 merge into the combined C2, C3 and C5, with Vietnamese deputies or chiefs. The senior adviser at each corps becomes the deputy, or later the chief, of the corps-area combined centre. His advisory function passes to a separate corps advisory group under him. The selected advisers of memorandum 006 report through the advisory chain as before, but their reports on an ARVN unit's compliance with combined orders go to the corps-area centre as well. This is the change F012-4 describes. The Free World Military Assistance Office becomes the combined headquarters' C9 coalition directorate.

### US conventional forces

From 1965 the American divisions and field forces fight under the same combined plan as the ARVN and the Koreans, and they block across boundaries on corps-area centre orders (EC012-2). The 1st Cavalry Division's airmobility becomes a combined asset. It is the force the corps-area centres most often call for a cross-boundary block. American commanders lose some autonomy over their own areas. In return they gain ARVN and Korean blocking forces available on a day's notice. American infantry battalions provide occasional cordons for the task force's larger contested-belt raids.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

Detachments of the 5th Special Forces Group provide part of the task force's raid elements, about three to five detachments of 12 [M012: D8 of P012b]. That is a small draw on a group of several thousand by 1966 [E: this headquarters' judgment from the period's practice]. The CIDG camps and their Montagnard strikers are unchanged, and they stay under Special Forces control. Their reports of infrastructure activity near the borders now reach the national file through the corps task groups. The FULRO question in the Highlands is not touched by either provision. Where it rises, the combined command's Vietnamese deputy and the American Special Forces commander will be on opposite sides of it inside one staff.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

The JGS remains the RVNAF's national headquarters for administration, pay, promotion and the general reserve. Its operational planning moves into the combined staff. The ARVN corps commanders become combined corps-area commanders under operational control. Their divisions take combined orders, lose some staff officers to the combined staff in 1964–65 (F012-10), and gain access to American and Korean fires through the switchboard (EC012-3). The board of memorandum 007 receives the combined commander's relief requests as evidence. ARVN battalions provide cordons for the task force's larger raids (F012-6). The Military Security Service sends officers into the task force's fusion centre, and it must share some files it held back under memorandum 006 [M006].

### RF/PF and territorial security

The territorial forces are not under the combined command. They remain under the province and district chiefs. Two changes reach them. First, the combined plan's one-in-three rule (F012-7) protects the ARVN battalions that support them from being stripped wholesale. Second, the task force's police arm uses the Popular Forces platoons of the held belt as its cordons for warrant arrests (EC012-8). That is a new task for the PF, at night, and it adds a few nights a month of work to platoons already stretched. The task force does not change who holds the hamlet at night. Its gains hold only where the PF do (POSTNA-2).

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. The air war over the North is an American operation under CINCPAC and Washington's target approval, outside the combined command's operational control. The combined command has a voice through COMUSMACV, as MACV had, and no more.

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged in its means and its rules. The combined command's planning of the border areas gives the ARVN's ground reconnaissance of the Laotian approaches a combined plan. It does not change what may be done across the border (CONFIG `laos_ground` MODELLED).

### Arc Light

The B-52J's in-country strikes come into the combined fires centre's allocation from 1965 [M005]. ARVN units can request Arc Light close-support boxes through the switchboard as American units do. The coordinate cell remains American. The B-52 strike-clearance chain now runs through the combined staff, which is a security risk the record already names: a penetrated clearance chain warns the target (P005a depends on INTEL.penetration).

### Airmobile formations

The 1st Cavalry Division and the later airmobile brigades become the combined command's preferred cross-boundary blocking force (EC012-2). The task force takes 20–30 helicopters from the theater's pool, a cut of about 5–8% in 1965 [M012: D2 of P012b] that the ARVN's lift feels most.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The SEAL platoons of SEAL Teams One and Two work for the task force's IV Corps and III Corps task groups in the Rung Sat and the Delta. They insert by PBR and SEAL team assault boat on the same-night follow-on (EC012-7). The coastal surveillance force and the river patrol force of 1965–66 come under the combined naval component's coordination. Their boardings and searches feed the national file through the corps task groups.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Memorandum 006's Phung Hoang committee, its PIOCCs and its DIOCCs are the task force's base layer, and the task force does not replace them (F012-1). Three things change. Their files are joined into a national file across province lines. Their targets outside the held belt are worked by the task force rather than by the province PRU alone. And their best analysts are drawn upward into the task force's province cells (F012-13). The province PRUs remain under the province chiefs. One selected PRU company in each corps area joins the task force's raid element. The independent audit of memorandum 006 extends to the task force's operations from 1965.

### Third-country forces

The Korean forces come under the combined commander's operational control when they arrive, with a national red card [U]. In practice the Capital Division and the 9th Division take combined orders for their own areas on the II Corps coast and refuse or slow some orders to fight outside them, as Seoul's politics require (F012-24). Korean special-forces teams join the task force's II Corps task group, which is the task group most at risk of abuse in the coastal hamlets [S4]. The Australian task force in Phuoc Tuy is under the combined command as it was under II Field Force [S5], and its Special Air Service squadron joins the task force's III Corps task group. Thai, Philippine and New Zealand contributions come under operational control on the Australian pattern [S2].

### Prior provisions

- **P002a** National Police Reform Programme: the Special Branch leads the task force's police arm and holds a veto over raids by certification (F012-2). Its 72-hour magistrate review is the task force's legal floor, and the task force pulls some of its best officers into the fusion cells.
- **P002b** Provincial Administration Reform: the merit chief hosts the task force's province cell. Cross-province warrants take some targets out of his hands, which protects the target from a weak chief and diminishes a strong one.
- **P006a** Phung Hoang Programme: the base layer under the task force (above).
- **P006c** Selected US Advisory Corps: the adviser's reports reach the combined commander (F012-4). The task force competes for the same Vietnamese-speaking officers.
- **P007b** RVNAF officer education: the Dalat staff college supplies the combined staff's Vietnamese officers (F012-3).
- **P007c** RVNAF Officer Selection and Promotion Reform: the board receives the combined commander's relief requests and becomes the instrument of his authority over ARVN commanders.
- **P001b** Anti-Corruption Programme: the Commission's audit extends to the task force's raids and the combined staff's procurement. The Aid Compliance Board's lever stands behind the combined command's authority.
- **P001a** Land-to-the-Tiller Programme: the land register helps the task force identify households, and it names the wrong man's family to the Front as well (F012-16).
- **P003a, P003b** U-2S and Global Hawk: their product feeds the combined estimate through releasable channels. Sharing with Vietnamese staff officers raises the leak risk the record already notes (P003a depends on INTEL.penetration).
- **P005a, P005c** B-52J and GBU-38: allocated through the combined fires centre to ARVN units by plan (F012-5).
- **P008b** RVN electronics manufacturing base: its licensed radios on ARVN and territorial nets reach the combined fire-support centre.
