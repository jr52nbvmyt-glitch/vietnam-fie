## §4.0 The course of the war

The war this headquarters inherited on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war that was being lost, in a state that had just killed its president. In this war that state began the year with more than history gave it. It had a land decree and a commission against corruption, a resident police with a Special Branch, district chiefs chosen by examination and a Phung Hoang committee over the files. It had an officer corps, an NCO corps and a rank and file schooled since 1957, an industrial base a generation above its own, and from Guam and the high air an American eye that could count the enemy's trucks and an American bomber that could hit what the eye measured. From this memorandum it also had two more things. The first was a combined operations command, American, Vietnamese, Korean and Australian, with the American commander holding operational control of the ARVN corps. The second was a combined task force built on the counter-network model of the wars of the 2000s, to find, fix and finish the Front's government in the hamlets.

The combined command was tested before it had issued an order. On 30 January 1964 the I Corps commander, Nguyen Khanh, took the government, and he did it over the command's own radios. The Vietnamese deputy was arrested with the Dalat generals that morning, and for nine days the command had nobody on the Vietnamese side to countersign its orders (EV012-1). What saved it was the lever that saved the decrees. Lodge and Harkins told Khanh that the aid paying his army's salaries was conditional on the command's continuing. Khanh, who needed the Americans more than he resented them, kept it, and put his own chief of staff, Nguyen Van Thieu, in the deputy's chair. Every combined order thereafter passed through the prime minister's office. That was the first lesson, and it held for the life of the command: operational control meant what the junta of the day allowed it to mean. It was more than history's coordination and less than command.

Within those limits the command did what it was built to do. On 26 March 1964 the ARVN 21st Division crossed the IV Corps boundary into the Ca Mau forests on a combined order issued 26 hours earlier, the first time an ARVN division had moved across a corps line on a foreign commander's order. It found a base camp and no regiment. That was also a lesson, and it was the one the rest of the war repeated. The combined command shortened the interval between finding a regiment and blocking it, and it closed the seams between national sectors through which regiments had slipped. It did not find regiments, and the regiments still chose their fights. The enemy answered the closed seams the way he answered everything that worked: he stopped using them and moved his war to the borders, where the sanctuaries were.

The task force began worse and finished better. Its first two months in Long An, in the summer of 1964, produced nothing. The Special Branch's files there were six months old and built on hamlets held only by day, and the American planners' attempt to run the 2026 cycle at the 2026 tempo, raid upon raid on unconfirmed targets, ended with the Tan An magistrate releasing eleven of seventeen detainees in September (EV012-2). The method needed what the 2026 task forces had had: telephones, a drone over the hamlet, a data network. It had paper. It learned to run on paper. On 3 October a Popular Forces platoon at Long Huu ambushed a tax team and took a notebook. Two nights later a SEAL platoon came up the Soai Rap and took the deputy chief of the Long An security section, the man who had commanded the killing of the Phuoc Lai title holders in June. By December the section that had killed 37 title holders was broken, and no title holder was killed in Can Giuoc in 1965.

That was the pattern from then on, and it is the strongest thing this memorandum adds to the war. The district centres of memorandum 006 had driven the Front's district committees out of the posted villages. The committees had gone to the contested edge and governed by courier (F006-4). The task force followed them there. Operation FOLLOW THROUGH, from August 1965 to June 1966, followed the Hoc Mon committee and the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's finance section into Cu Chi (EV012-4). It turned on a cashier taken at a market stall with 41,000 piasters in a rice sack, who rallied against a named offer and gave the section's meeting houses. A raid at Phu Hoa Dong on the night of 19 October took the section's ledger from a tunnel under a family altar. The ledger named the collectors, and the warrants it produced took them. By the first half of 1966 the region's collections from the posted villages of Hoc Mon and Go Vap were about an eighth of 1964's. Nobody governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi after March 1966. The Front governed it from the Ho Bo woods, eighteen kilometres further out, by couriers who took two nights instead of one.

The cost was written down as carefully as the gain, because the task force was built to be audited. At Phu Hoa Dong the PRU killed a cadre's 16-year-old son who fired on the cordon. Of the seventeen people the task group killed between November and March, the Commission could not show four to be cadres, and the census told the Front which families they belonged to (F012-16). On the II Corps coast it went worse. There the files were ralliers' words. The task group's American commander adopted a list of 64 names to be captured or killed, and on the night of 19–20 January 1966 a Korean team killed four men at Hoa Thanh in Tuy An who were a family with a land dispute with the rallier who named them (EV012-5). The Phu Yen magistrate referred two Korean soldiers for homicide under the 1964 statute, and the Korean commander refused to surrender them. The list was ended on 2 April. The Front's leaflet was in every market from Tuy Hoa to Song Cau within a week, a year before Duc Thanh made "Phoenix" a word in Washington. On the coast it was believed.

The combined command's year-by-year story is less dramatic and, in the end, more troubling. Seoul and Canberra consented to combined command on conditions. The Koreans countersigned every order to a Korean formation, and their commander, Chae Myung-shin, told Westmoreland that he would carry out every order he countersigned and countersign only those he would carry out (EV012-3). The Australians sent a casualty estimate to Canberra with every combined order. It was operational control in the Korean case in name only. Its real gain was that the seam between the Korean area and the ARVN 22nd Division's was now planned by one staff. In July 1966 the command handed the Delta's combined centre to the ARVN IV Corps commander, with an American brigadier as his deputy (EV012-6). That was the beginning of the transfer the provision's dossier said it needed. But the corps commanders were the President's choices, not the board's. The first was Dang Van Quang, among the most corrupt generals in the army. His successor, Nguyen Van Manh, was honest and cautious, and the Delta's combined orders were executed at Manh's speed. When the American 9th Division asked for two ARVN battalions to block for the riverine force in June 1967, Manh refused the second under the combined plan's rule that kept two in three on territorial security. The 263rd Battalion slipped through the gap, and no post in Cai Lay fell that month. The rule worked once and showed what it cost.

Tet showed both provisions at the edges of the war's great event rather than at its centre. At 0320 on 31 January 1968 a sapper company attacked the combined headquarters at Tan Son Nhut, and the compound held. On two dead sappers was a plan of the compound accurate to within a few metres, drawn by a captain of the C3 plans cell who had passed the enemy the III Corps quarterly plans for two and a half years (EV012-7). The combined staff had been penetrated as its dossier said it would be. The two-layer plan it had adopted in 1964, real objectives held back from the shared plan, had protected its operations better than its staff believed, and it was extended to every corps.

At Hue the combined command made one battle out of three (EV000-44). The I Corps combined centre named Ngo Quang Truong commander of the battle on 1 February, with the Marines' Task Force X-Ray and the 1st Cavalry Division's brigade as his deputies. At his request it gave the brigade first call on I Corps lift to cut the western routes before anything else. The brigade and the ARVN 3rd Regiment caught the 324B Division's reinforcing battalion in the open at La Chu on 12–13 February. The Citadel's flag came down on 19 February rather than the 24th, and the battle ended on 27 February rather than 2 March. The weather and the walls still set most of its length. The executions, nearly all in the first two weeks, were not shortened at all.

And on the northwestern road into Saigon, the columns that came through Hoc Mon came through hamlets with nobody living in them to quarter the assault companies or walk them to their start lines (EV012-8). They spent the night of the 29th in the fields, a Popular Forces platoon at Tan Thoi Nhi saw them, and they reached the air base's west gate an hour or two late. The fight at the gate was the fight of history, and it was won as history's was. The event did not change Tet. It showed on one road what the attack on the infrastructure had done by 1968: the enemy could still send a battalion to a gate, but no longer from inside the hamlets it passed through.

From 1969 the record carries neither provision in any event, and that silence is an analytical claim I defend in the phase nulls below. The task force's American half drew down with the American forces. Its national file was the file memorandum 006 built, made larger, and it went where that file went: into the Central Intelligence Organization in 1970 and into the President's service in 1971 (POS006-4). The combined command's American half went home with the American divisions. Whatever survived of it in Vietnamese hands survived as a staff habit, not an institution. The war's later course was decided by what neither provision reached: the American withdrawal and its politics, the PAVN's conventional offensives, and the orders, families and roads of March 1975.

The war therefore ends where memorandum 011 left it, and this memorandum does not move it. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, under Thieu, without American air power, with five PAVN corps assembled for an offensive on the capital in the first months of 1976 (EV011-3). In those provinces the Front's district government was broken earlier and harder than in any version of this war yet written, and the Special Branch's knowledge of what remains is ten years deep. That is why III and IV Corps are the part of the Republic that still stands, and it is not enough to make them stand without the Americans. The combined command made the ARVN better at fighting the American way while the Americans were there. The task force made the Front's hamlet government weakest in the provinces the Republic kept longest. Neither reached the PAVN's divisions in their sanctuaries, Hanoi's will, or the American Congress, and those decided the war.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

Three events carry this memorandum into Phase I, and each embodies a finding rather than a first-order use.

**The combined command and the coup (EV012-1).** The dossier predicted that the command would be founded on a junta's consent and reopened by every coup. It was reopened within a month. The coup's mechanism is the finding that mattered: a corps commander under the combined commander's operational control used the command's own network to take the state, because nobody had thought to treat the command's radios as a coup's instrument (F012-22, D11 of P012a). The command survived because the American lever was pulled at once, the same lever that protected the decrees of 1 January (POS001-2). It survived at a price: its orders now passed through the prime minister's office, so that operational control was exercised through a political filter from February 1964. By late March the time to clear a combined order had fallen from about four days to about thirty hours (F012-11) [X-012]. The binding constraint moved, as the finding predicted, from agreement to execution, and by the end of the phase to the enemy's choice of battle. The Ca Mau sweep of 26 March, a division across a corps boundary in 26 hours that found nothing, is the find-to-fix chain in one sentence. The fix was faster and the find was the same.

**The task force's first campaign (EV012-2).** DRY SEASON shows the dossier's warning and the finding of the drone-model cycle at work (EC012-12, D14 of P012b). For two months the method ran without its inputs, and the result was raids on nothing and detainees the magistrate released. The turn came from paper, a notebook taken by a Popular Forces ambush, and from the same-night follow-on (EC012-7). The security section that had killed the Phuoc Lai title holders was broken by December. The land programme's suspension in Can Giuoc ended two months earlier than it would have, and no title holder there was killed in 1965. This is the compound of P012b with the land programme (P001a) and the police (P002a): the task force protected what the land reform gave, in the place where the land reform had first learned that it changed nothing about who held the night (EV001-3, POS001-1).

**The allies' red cards (EV012-3).** The Korean and Australian consents arrived with conditions that made operational control real for the Australians and nominal for the Koreans. The finding that counted was not the Korean veto, which history would have predicted. It was the gain it left: the Korean and ARVN sectors on the II Corps coast came under one plan, and the seam between them was planned rather than negotiated (F012-9).

The phase's end vector moves on three variables. The infrastructure is a band weaker at the end of July 1965 than the converted range said, at −5..−3, on the evidence of Can Giuoc and Hoc Mon. Identification is re-judged down from the converted +5..+7 to +4..+7, because the files of 1964 were thin, as DRY SEASON's first two months showed. Political stability is re-judged from a converted nil to −1..+1, because the command neither caused nor prevented a coup but gave one a radio net.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's first response to the task force was the right one, and it came from the Long An provincial security section itself. After Ba Lien's arrest on 6 October, the section's chief took the remaining men into the Rung Sat proper, where the task group could not follow without a battalion (EV012-2). That is the displacement the Part 2 finding names: the upper layer moves beyond helicopter-and-warrant range (F012-8). The Saigon–Gia Dinh Region rebuilt the section by the spring of 1965 with cadres sent down from the region, men who knew the roads and not the hamlets. That cost the Front its local knowledge, the infrastructure's most valuable and least replaceable asset.

The enemy's response to the combined command in this phase was propaganda first and penetration second. Liberation Radio's line from February 1964 was that the ARVN was an army commanded by an American general, which was now true in a sense the Front could document (F012-22). The penetration took longer. The captain who drew the plan of the combined compound in 1967 was placed in the combined staff in 1965, recruited in 1962 by his brother-in-law in the region's military intelligence section (EV012-7). The Front's agents were in the RVNAF before the combined staff existed, and the staff drew its officers from the RVNAF.

### Causal chains

**C012-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV001-3) The Long An security section kills 37 title holders in Can Giuoc in June–July 1964 and the titling is suspended → the task force's first campaign is aimed at the section, finds nothing for two months on files built by day, and then breaks it from a notebook taken in a PF ambush (EV012-2) → the section is rebuilt from the region with cadres who know the roads but not the hamlets → when the land programme returns to Can Giuoc's posted hamlets in February 1965, nobody in the rebuilt section knows which families hold titles, and no title holder is killed in 1965: the task force's most durable effect in Long An is not the 14 men it took but the local knowledge the Front could not send down from the region with their replacements.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a combined command that is a channel through the prime minister's office, a Korean contingent under a combined plan with a national veto, an Australian contingent under conditions that send casualty estimates to Canberra, and a task force that has learned in Long An to run on paper rather than signals. It also inherits the Hoc Mon committee, driven by STILL WATER from its district and governing from Cu Chi, which the III Corps task group has by name (EV006-2). Phase III inherits the captain in the combined plans cell, placed in 1965. Phases IV to VI inherit a precedent: in this war, operational control of the ARVN by an American commander was established in the first week, with the American lever behind it, and the lever's lapse in 1970–71 (DIRMT01-5) is the date from which nothing protects it.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

**The strongest positive effect of this memorandum lands here, in FOLLOW THROUGH (EV012-4).** The finding is F012-27: the Front's district government of the held belt from its edge is broken in 1965–67, in the provinces where the task force concentrates. FOLLOW THROUGH shows the mechanism on the ground, link by link. The district centre and STILL WATER had driven the Hoc Mon committee into Cu Chi (EV006-2). The task force's cross-province file and its helicopters reached Cu Chi, where the district centre's warrants and the province PRU could not (F012-1). A cashier rallied against a named offer (EC012-10), a raid at Phu Hoa Dong took the region finance section's ledger, and the ledger's warrants took the collectors. The III Corps combined centre's tasking of an ARVN company for the cordon at 1500 on the afternoon before the raid is the compound pairing of the two provisions at work (F012-6). Under the historical arrangement that company belonged to the 25th Division's own plan for the night, and the task group would have had to ask the division commander, not order through a combined centre. The result is measured in the enemy's own ledger rather than in arrests (F012-20, A13). The region's collections from Hoc Mon's and Go Vap's posted villages fell to about an eighth of 1964's, and from Cu Chi's contested hamlets by about two-fifths, where on the course of memorandum 006 they had grown [X-012].

The phase's limit is shown in the same event. The committee's secretary and the finance section's survivors went to the Ho Bo woods as the American 25th Division arrived at Cu Chi (F012-8). The woods were the 165th Regiment's base area, and the task force could not follow. The Front governed Hoc Mon from eighteen kilometres further out. It governed it badly, but it governed it.

**The perverse effect lands on the coast (EV012-5).** The II Corps list is F012-16 at its worst and EC012-11's abandonment in a dated event. It is the task force's success in the held belt carried into a province where nobody held the night and the files were ralliers' words. The four dead at Hoa Thanh were named by a rallier with a land dispute. The Korean commander's refusal to surrender his soldiers to a Vietnamese magistrate put the Korean red card (EV012-3) and the 1964 statute's homicide rule (D3 of P012b) into direct conflict, and the red card won. The finding's weight was moderate in Part 2, and the event bears that out. The list was ended within three months and the American element kept to warrants. But the Front's story about the task force was born on the coast in 1966, and it was believed there.

**The transfer begins and stalls (EV012-6).** The Delta corps is the counter-directional finding's test and its partial answer (F012-26). The handover of the IV Corps combined centre to its ARVN commander in July 1966 is exactly what the finding said was needed by 1966 if the ARVN was not to become a body without a brain when the Americans left. It happened on time. It did not produce a Vietnamese command that planned its own war, because the corps commanders were the palace's choices (EV007-4), and the board that chose battalion and regimental commanders never reached the corps. The one-in-three rule held in June 1967 against an American request (F012-7). That was the rule's purpose. It also let part of the 263rd Battalion through, which was its price. The Delta in 1967 had a Vietnamese commander of the combined system who executed cautiously, and an American division that fought beside it as though the system did not exist.

The phase's end vector is re-judged on four entries. The infrastructure stands at −8..−6, medium, against the converted −7..−5. Identification is re-judged from the converted cap of +8..+10 to +6..+9, medium. The held belt is known house by house, but the coast is known from ralliers' words, as EV012-5 proves, and the committees in the base areas are names without locations. The enemy's control of engagement is re-judged from a converted nil to −1..0, low, for the seams the combined command closed and the battles the enemy declined at them (F012-19). The Republic's own fire support is widened to −2..+1, because the switchboard routes American fire to ARVN units by plan (EC012-3).

ARVN effectiveness and its fire-coordination facet are left as they stand. The combined switchboard bears on fire coordination, but judging that facet requires naming the binding facet of ARVN effectiveness in each phase. The air-war study declined that judgment and sent it to the next checkpoint (studies/air-war Q-5), and one memorandum's evidence on one facet does not settle seven.

### Enemy response and initiative

<!-- initiative -->
The enemy's own decision in this phase was taken by Military Region 5's Party Committee in October 1965, for its own reasons and against the wishes of another of Hanoi's commands (EV012-5). The region held the 2nd VC Regiment and the 3rd PAVN Division's regiments on the Binh Dinh coast through the dry season of 1966. It did so to protect the coastal rice harvest on which its forces lived, and because its leaders held, against the B3 Front's request for a regiment in the Highlands, that the coast was the decisive ground of their war. Nothing this headquarters did provoked it. It was a quarrel between two enemy commands over rice and doctrine. Its consequence for this memorandum was that the Phu Yen and Binh Dinh province committees stayed close to regiments that protected them, which is one reason the II Corps list found so little and killed the wrong men.

The enemy's reaction to the task force in the Saigon belt was the reaction Part 2 predicted (F012-21). The Saigon–Gia Dinh Region adopted the rule that any cadre taken alive was presumed to talk, and his contacts moved by dawn. The region finance section's couriers each carried only the next courier's name, which is why FOLLOW THROUGH's first two months took eleven couriers and no section members. The rule worked until a cashier with her mother's freedom at stake gave the meeting houses. The compartmenting cost the Front the coordination of its tax administration, and in the first half of 1966 the administration did not survive the cost (F012-23).

The enemy's reaction to the combined command was penetration, and it was the cheapest exchange in the war. One captain in the C3 plans cell, placed in 1965, passed the III Corps quarterly plans and the dates of the transitions between them until June 1968 (EV012-7, F012-22).

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits the precedent of the Delta corps and its limit: the transfer reached the corps commander the palace chose, and stopped there. Phase VI inherits, in III and IV Corps, an infrastructure whose district layer was broken in 1965–67 and never fully rebuilt inside the post line. That is part of why the Front's committees there lost about twice the historical share of their members in 1973 (EV000-84), and why those corps are the part of the Republic that stands at the end of 1975.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet is the war's great event, and this memorandum touches it in three places, none of them central.

**Hue (EV000-44)** is the combined command's clearest gain in a battle. Historically three forces fought three battles under three chains, with no commander below Saigon over all of them until MACV Forward was formed on 9 February. Here one commander, Truong, held the battle from 1 February, with a single fire centre in the Citadel and first call on I Corps lift for the brigade that had to cut the western routes. The finding is the compound of F012-5 and F012-6: one switchboard and one plan, across the boundary between a Vietnamese division, a Marine task force and an Army brigade. The battle ended five days earlier. The 324B Division's reinforcing battalion was broken at La Chu, and about 40 ARVN and 15 American lives were saved by my estimate [X-012]. The weather and the walls set the rest. The executions, nearly all in the first fortnight, were not shortened.

**The attack on the combined headquarters (EV012-7)** is the penetration finding made visible (F012-22, F012-23). The sappers' plan of the compound was drawn by a captain in the C3 plans cell, and his two and a half years of passing the III Corps quarterly plans were found only because the plan was on a dead man. The finding said penetrating the combined staff was the enemy's best and cheapest exchange, and the event confirms it. The two-layer plan (EC012-5) limited the damage, which is also what the finding predicted.

**Tet in Hoc Mon (EV012-8)** is the strongest positive effect showing two and a half years downstream (F012-27, C012-2). It is narrated at length because it embodies a second-order effect rather than a use: an operation against a tax administration in 1965 changed how an attack column crossed a district in 1968. It changed that, and not the outcome at the gate. The task force's December find of three caches, read as the stocks of a local offensive, is the open channel's strategic file at work and at its limit (F012-25). It saw the preparation and misread its scale, as every intelligence service in Saigon misread Tet.

The end vector is re-judged as in Phase II. The infrastructure stands at −8..−6 and identification at +6..+9, both medium, and the Republic's fire support at −2..+1.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's decision to guide the northwestern columns with cadres from Cu Chi was a forced adaptation, and it shows what the Front had lost: men who lived in the hamlets. The Region's choice to place nine caches in Hoc Mon in the autumn of 1967 regardless, in a district it no longer governed, was a decision taken for its own reasons. The General Offensive's plan required the northwestern columns to pass through Hoc Mon, and the plan was Hanoi's, not the region's (EV000-37). The region carried out a plan that assumed a hamlet government it no longer had. That is the enemy's misreading of its own position, and it cost two companies their rockets.

At Hue the PAVN's Hue City Front command did what it did historically, and it reinforced from the west because the west was open. Its reinforcement was caught at La Chu by a brigade that had been given the lift to get there. The enemy's decision was the same. What changed was the speed of the answer.

### Causal chains

The phase's chain is C012-2, whose terminal link lands here (EV012-8); it is placed in Phase II with its beginning.

**C012-4** [months] (begins in Phase II, EV012-6) The combined command adopts the two-layer plan in 1964 against the leak its dossier predicted, and the Vietnamese staff officers resent it → a captain of the C3 plans cell, placed by the region's military intelligence in 1965, passes the shared plans and their transition dates for two and a half years → the sappers who attack the combined compound at Tet carry his drawing, and the investigation finds him in June 1968 (EV012-7) → the investigation's report shows that the two-layer plan protected the real operations better than the staff believed, and the command extends it to every corps: the agent's discovery entrenches the arrangement that most insulted the Vietnamese officers on whom the transfer of the combined command depends (F012-26).

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits an infrastructure that lost its Tet cadres from a network already broken in the held belt, and a combined command with the two-layer plan in every corps, a Vietnamese staff that resents it, and an American half about to begin going home. Phase V inherits the I Corps combined centre's fire switchboard, which is how the ARVN of 1972 learned to use American air with an educated staff's precision (F007-23). The switchboard made that skill routine, and it made the ARVN's dependence routine too.

### Clock

The combined command's effect on American political tolerance in 1968 was nil to slightly negative, and the task force's was nil. Hue was shorter by five days and cost about 15 fewer American lives [X-012]. That is a trace against 14,592 American dead by hostile action in the year [H40], and it did not touch the decision of 31 March (EV000-45). ## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the cumulative force of twelve memoranda has changed the war's countryside deeply, its main-force war moderately and its end by about a year. This memorandum's share of that change is concentrated in three places.

**The infrastructure in the Saigon belt and the upper Delta, 1964–68.** History had no targeted attack on the infrastructure before ICEX in mid-1967, and Phoenix proper began in 1968 [M012·S11]. The record had already given the war a police and district centres from 1964. This memorandum gives it a finishing arm from the same date. Its measurable result is in the enemy's own accounts. The Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's collections from the posted villages of Hoc Mon and Go Vap fell to about an eighth of 1964's by the first half of 1966, against about a fifth on the course before this memorandum, and the Front governed Hoc Mon from the Ho Bo woods rather than from Cu Chi (EV012-4) [X-012]. The Can Giuoc security section that killed 37 title holders in 1964 was broken by December of that year, and no title holder was killed there in 1965 (EV012-2) [X-012]. I judge the Front's district-level administration in the held belt and its edge to be about a third to a half weaker at the end of 1967 than the record held without this memorandum, in about 12–18 of the 44 provinces (F012-27) [X-012]. That is a margin over history of about four years and a large fraction of the district layer, in the part of the country that held longest in 1975.

**One battle.** Hue ended on 27 February rather than 2 March, the flag came down on 19 February rather than the 24th, and about 40 ARVN and 15 American lives were saved by my estimate (EV000-44) [X-012]. The city's civilian dead are history's, less a few hundred from the shelling of the western quarter at most.

**The command of the war.** For the first time, the war had one commander for the military campaign in the South from 1964: an American, with a Vietnamese deputy who countersigned through the prime minister's office, a Korean veto and an Australian casualty referral. The gap register's thirteenth gap, no single manager of the war, is narrowed for the military war and untouched for pacification, which CORDS still took over on its historical date (EV000-35). The combined command closed seams between national sectors, made Hue one battle, and began a transfer in the Delta that stopped at the palace's choice of corps commander.

**What history kept.** The main force was found no better, chose its battles as before, and regenerated in sanctuaries no provision touched. The coastal lowlands of I and II Corps, where the files were ralliers' words, got a list, a killed family and a Korean court-martial, not a broken infrastructure (EV012-5). The Highlands and their peoples were reached by neither provision: the task force had no files on the Montagnard hamlets and no Montagnard-speaking interrogators, and FULRO's quarrel with Saigon was outside both provisions' reach. The war's end is memorandum 011's: the Republic stands at the end of 1975 in III and IV Corps and the southern coast, with its fall expected in the dry season of 1976.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

**The end state does not change.** The Republic of Vietnam survives 1975 in III and IV Corps and on the coast south of Nha Trang, under Thieu, without American air power, facing five PAVN corps. Its fall is expected between February and May 1976 (EV011-3). The rung is (b), dependent survival, by the test below, because Saigon stands past 30 April 1975. The outcome in its own terms is the one memorandum 011 named: a reprieve of about a year, which the record ends before it is spent.

**Why this memorandum does not move it.** The two provisions act on the war while the Americans are present. The combined command's power was American power, delegated through a Vietnamese deputy and exercised over corps commanders the palace chose, and its American half and its fires went home with the American forces. The task force's speed, lift and raid arm were American and allied, and they went home too. What stayed was a Vietnamese Special Branch with ten years of files and a district layer of the infrastructure broken in the provinces the Republic kept. That is real, and the record already shows it at work in 1973 (EV000-84). But the war's end was not decided in the hamlets of III and IV Corps. It was decided at Ban Me Thuot, on Route 7B, on the coast road out of Hue and in Congress (POSMT01-6, POS011-3, REV011-1). Neither provision reaches any of those. This is the record's oldest position, stated again: no provision that leaves the Saigon government's standing in the countryside unchanged moves the end state above rung (b) (POSTNA-1). The task force weakens the Front's government without strengthening Saigon's, and the combined command strengthens the ARVN under American command, which is the opposite of what the end state needs.

**Why the next rung is not reachable.** Rung (c) requires the GVN and RVNAF to hold the countryside in every corps region without American ground forces, and American air power available at the end. Neither holds. The coast of I and II Corps fell to PAVN divisions in 1972 and 1975, and Case–Church ended American air power in 1973. Nothing in this memorandum changes either. The task force's thinnest work was on the coast (EV012-5), and the combined command is an American command that ends with the American air.

**The date of the fall (DIRMT01-1).** The window stays February to May 1976. Two mechanisms in this memorandum pull it earlier. The first is the counter-directional finding: an ARVN that planned inside a combined staff for nine years has a weaker staff of its own in 1975 (F012-26). The record shows the Delta transfer stopping at the palace's corps commander (EV012-6). The second is the national file, a better instrument for the palace's purges of 1970–71 (F012-18). Two pull it later. The Front's infrastructure in III and IV Corps is weaker than in any earlier version of this war, so the PAVN's offensive on the capital will have fewer guides, caches and recruiting grounds in the provinces it must cross, as the Hoc Mon columns had at Tet (EV012-8). And the Special Branch's files are a better instrument against the Front's last-hour risings in the cities. I judge the two pairs roughly to cancel, because the first acts on the ARVN's command in a battle and the second on the enemy's rear, and the 1976 offensive will be decided by the first. The window does not move, and this is a conclusion of the test, not a carry.

**Running sums (DIRMT01-2).** American lives saved: the record's sum stands at about 3,700–7,800 (REV011-2). This memorandum adds about 50–300 in 1964–68, chiefly at Hue (about 15, EV000-44) and in the cross-boundary blocks of 1966–67, at a rate of perhaps a fraction of one percent of the American dead of those years [X-012]. The sum moves to about 3,750–8,100. It supports the lower half of a trace on American casualties and does not move tolerance. ARVN battalions not broken: this memorandum adds none that I can show in a dated event, and I do not claim any. The infrastructure is the variable this memorandum moves, and its sum is the phase vectors above. In 1965–68 the record now holds the Front's district layer at about a third to a half weaker in 12–18 provinces than before this memorandum, a product of overlapping judgments (P002a, P006a, P012b) whose upper bound I mark: the half is the upper bound, and the third is what I would defend [X-012]. The enemy's answer, which DIRCP02-3 requires me to name, was to move the district committees to the base areas and govern from further out (F012-8).

**The four regions (DIRMT01-8).**
- **The Delta:** a Vietnamese-commanded combined corps from July 1966 that executed cautiously and held the territorial battalions in their districts (EV012-6).
- **The coastal lowlands of I and II Corps:** a list, a killed family at Hoa Thanh and a story the Front still told in 1968 (EV012-5), and Hue as one battle (EV000-44).
- **The Highlands and their peoples:** nothing. The task force had no files and no language there, and the combined command's Highlands war was the American divisions' frontier war, unchanged.
- **The Saigon belt:** the strongest effect, from Can Giuoc in 1964 to Hoc Mon at Tet (EV012-2, EV012-4, EV012-8).

**The soldier's family (DIRMT01-9).** The combined command moved ARVN divisions across corps boundaries more readily than history did, beginning with the 21st Division's sweep into Ca Mau in March 1964 (EV012-1). An ARVN soldier's family lived beside his unit's base. A division moved across a corps line left its families behind or brought them into the new corps area's camps, and either way its desertion rose for the months of the move [E: the period's pattern of desertion on redeployment]. The one-in-three rule kept most territorial-security battalions in their provinces and their families' reach, which is the rule's least advertised value (F012-7).

**Rice and the piaster (DIRMT01-11).** The task force's work against the region finance section is a work on rice and money. The ledger of Phu Hoa Dong recorded the Front's collections in piasters and in rice, hamlet by hamlet, across Hoc Mon, Go Vap, Cu Chi and Trang Bang. Its collectors were taken in the winter of 1965–66, and the region's take from the market gardens and the rubber workers fell accordingly (EV012-4). The Front's rice in the Saigon belt in 1966–67 came increasingly from purchase in the towns and from Cambodia rather than from tax in the hamlets. That is a cost the Front paid in money it had to bring from the North [X-012]. The piaster and the soldier's pay are untouched by either provision.

**The enemy's authors (DIRMT01-10).** The decisions in this memorandum's events were taken by different bodies. On the coast it was Military Region 5's Party Committee, against the B3 Front (EV012-5). In Hoc Mon it was the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region, under COSVN, carrying out Hanoi's plan for Tet (EV012-8). The combined staff's agent belonged to the region's military intelligence section (EV012-7). Hanoi's Politburo appears in none of them except as the author of the Tet plan, which is as it should be: this memorandum's war is the war below the Politburo.

**Phase redraw (G2).** No redraw is proposed. Neither provision changes the shape of the phases.

**A question for the record.** The chapters of Phases IV to VI carry no event for the combined command's handover or dissolution, or for the task force's drawdown, and this memorandum has defended their absence as nulls. The next checkpoint should decide whether the combined command's end in 1972–73, when the last American corps-level headquarters left, needs an event of its own.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The Front's district government of the held belt was broken from its edge in 1965–67.** The task force reached the committees that memorandum 006's centres had driven into the contested edge. The Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's collections from Hoc Mon and Go Vap fell to about an eighth of 1964's, and the Front governed Hoc Mon from the Ho Bo woods (EV012-4). This is the largest single blow to the infrastructure in the record, about a third to a half of the district layer in 12–18 provinces beyond what the record held [X-012], and it is the half of the war the American divisions could never touch.
- **The Can Giuoc security section was broken in 1964,** and the land programme returned to Can Giuoc's posted hamlets two months early with no title holder killed in 1965 (EV012-2). The task force protected the land reform where it had first been shown helpless.
- **The war had one military commander from 1964.** Combined operational control closed seams between national sectors, made an ARVN division movable across a corps line in a day, and made Hue one battle that ended five days early with about 55 fewer allied dead (EV012-1, EV000-44).
- **The Korean and ARVN sectors on the II Corps coast were planned by one staff** instead of negotiated by two (EV012-3).
- **A Vietnamese general commanded a combined corps area from July 1966,** with an American as his deputy, the first time in the war (EV012-6).
- **At Tet the northwestern columns came through Hoc Mon with nobody to guide them** and reached Tan Son Nhut late (EV012-8). It was a small effect, and the proof that the infrastructure war of 1965 shaped the main-force war of 1968.

### What has not changed

- **The main force is found no better and still chooses its battles.** The combined command shortened the fix and closed seams. The enemy stopped using the seams and fought at the borders, and its sanctuaries were untouched (F012-9, F012-19).
- **The coast and the Highlands.** The task force failed on the II Corps coast, where its list killed a family and its Korean element was withdrawn (EV012-5). It never reached the Montagnard hamlets.
- **The combined command could not choose corps commanders.** The Delta transfer reached Dang Van Quang and Nguyen Van Manh, the palace's choices, and stopped (EV012-6). The ARVN's corps and divisions are no better led than they were.
- **The combined staff was penetrated,** as its dossier said it would be, for two and a half years (EV012-7).
- **The American lever lapses in 1970–71, and with it the protection of both provisions.** The task force's file goes where memorandum 006's went, into the President's service (POS006-4).
- **The end of the war is unchanged.** The Republic stands at the end of 1975 in III and IV Corps, without American air power, and its fall is expected in the dry season of 1976 (EV011-3). Neither provision reaches Hanoi's will, the sanctuaries, the PAVN's divisions, the orders of March 1975 or the American Congress.
