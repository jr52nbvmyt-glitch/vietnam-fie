## EV000-46 · The My Lai massacre · 16 Mar 1968 · Son My village (My Lai 4 and My Khe), Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · CANCELLED · 011
*Initiative: allied*
On the morning of 16 March 1968 Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Brigade, Americal Division, operating under Task Force Barker, was landed by helicopter at the hamlet of My Lai 4 in Son My village, in an area of Quang Ngai long held by the VC 48th Local Force Battalion [H47] [H]. It met no resistance. In this war its men searched the hamlet, burned the bunkers they found under the houses, questioned the old men through the interpreter, and were lifted out in the afternoon; Company B, 4th Battalion, 3rd Infantry searched My Khe the same way (P011c) [X-011]. Nobody was killed. The massacre that history records on that morning, in which between 347 people, the US Army's figure, and 504, the Vietnamese figure, mostly old men, women and children, were killed in their homes, on the paths and in an irrigation ditch [H47], does not happen in this war, by the user's stipulation and for no reason inside the war that this record can name [U]. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson flew his OH-23 over the hamlets that morning on an ordinary scout mission [H] [X-011].

What does not follow is as large as what does not happen. There is no report of 128 Viet Cong killed, no brigade investigation conceding about 20 accidental civilian deaths, no year and a half of concealment, and nothing for Ronald Ridenhour to write to Congress about in March 1969 [H47] [X-011]. Task Force Barker's sweep entered the brigade's log as a dry hole, one of dozens that spring in Son Tinh District, where the 48th Battalion had left before the helicopters landed [X-011]. The conditions that historians found behind the massacre stayed in the province: a division assembled hastily, a command climate that measured success by bodies, free-fire areas on the Batangan peninsula and harassment fire on the hamlets at night [H]. The removal is isolated by ruling, and every other killing of civilians in Quang Ngai that year stays where history put it (F011-11). Son My's people, about four to five hundred more than history's by the end of the day, went on living under the Front's administration, paying its taxes and sending its young men to its local force (EC011-6) [X-011].

## P4 · Overview
Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971 [H40]. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 [D] to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971 [H40]. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest [H62]. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win [H]. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington [X-001]. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001]. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle (EV001-8) [X-001].

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses (EV002-6). The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective (EV000-66); Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season (EV000-68); and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically (EV000-71).

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open (EV000-58). Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy (EV000-68). In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home (EV000-71).

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless (EV000-60). The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election (EV002-6). And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute (EV000-72). The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

The officer reforms reach their limit in this phase, and it is the limit their memorandum named: the American lever. As the aid conditions are dropped with Vietnamization, the palace makes the board's general-officer list advisory in January 1970, keeps the board for everything below colonel, and in December 1970 answers a petition of colonels of the classes of 1957–59 by moving about forty of the army's best regimental and battalion commanders to staff and schools (EV007-5). Lam Son 719 goes into Laos weeks later with new commanders in two of the 1st Division's regiments and an Airborne brigade, and fails for the historical reasons, which were decided at corps and in Saigon (EV000-71). Thieu's election of 1971 is run against the background of the colonels' petition, read into the record of the Lower House (EV000-73). The battalions themselves are the best-led the Republic ever had.

Under the withdrawal, the Republic's own plants close the loop that Vietnamization would otherwise have left open: the American equipment handed over in 1969–71 goes into rebuild works and a signal depot that have run since 1966, and by the end of 1971 the RVNAF makes about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, most of its batteries and all of its wire, filled and powered from abroad (EV008-6). In the Delta the subsector advisers hand the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets before they leave (EV008-7). And the Directorate of Defence Industry goes to the palace with the Board, the list, the file and the Commission, keeping its output and losing its honesty (EV002-6).

The schooled sergeant meets the drawdown in both armies. In the American units that stay longest, the sergeant who enforces standards is the man the worst soldiers resent and some of them try to kill, and the schooled sergeant holds more squads together than history's without stopping the decay (EV010-5). In the Republic the gate on NCO promotion is the first of the army's merit rules that the palace breaks, by the National Day decree of 1 November 1970, in the same months it takes the board and the Directorate (EV010-6) [X-010]. The academy survives the advisers, because it was Vietnamese from the start, and the territorial platoon leaders who learned to call fire on a grid call Vietnamese guns when the Americans go (EV008-7).

In this war the home front of 1969–71 has no My Lai and a smaller draft. The Moratorium and the Mobilization of the autumn of 1969 come on their dates without Seymour Hersh's story in their week, and the Peers inquiry, the Calley trial and the national argument of the spring of 1971 do not happen (EV000-63, EV000-72) [X-011]. A quarter of the men the boards would have called have enlisted before their notices came, the boards send about 70,000 fewer notices in 1969 and about 40,000 fewer in 1970, and about one in four of the Army's draftees in the rifle companies of the drawdown chose to be there (EV010-5) [X-011]. None of it changes the withdrawal's schedule, which Washington set in 1969 for its own reasons; it changes the bitterness of the years in which the schedule ran (F011-10, F011-15).

## EV000-58 · Operation Menu · 18 Mar 1969 – 26 May 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005,011
*Initiative: allied*

On the night of 18 March 1969, 60 B-52s took off from Guam on a mission ostensibly against targets in South Vietnam; 48 were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, where COSVN was believed to be, and dropped about 2,400 tons that night [H57]. In this war the 48 were Js, and their unguided carpet was half as dense; about 1,600 tons fell in the boxes, and twelve of the aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s aimed at the coordinates the U-2S had measured over five years in the Fishhook: storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop with a generator, and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload by night (EV003-4) [X-005]. The campaign that followed, codenamed Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner, Supper and Dessert after the base areas it struck, flew about 3,800 B-52 sorties and dropped 108,823 tons until 26 May 1970 [H57]. Here it dropped about 75,000 tons, a fifth of the sorties carrying JDAM against measured structures [X-005]. It was concealed by dual reporting: an overt channel ordered strikes near the border inside South Vietnam, while a back channel ordered the strikes in Cambodia, whose records were destroyed after each mission; neither the Secretary of the Air Force nor its chief of staff was told [H57].

The measured structures died; the base areas did not. In the first six weeks of Breakfast and Lunch about 300 structures that the U-2S had photographed were destroyed, most of them sheds and workshops in clearings [X-005]. By June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, and the clearings were empty when the U-2S next imaged them; the J's precision had a season's targets and then none (F005-16) [X-005]. The rear services cadres, by the account of a COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970, blamed spies in the base areas and searched for beacons in the forest; they found neither, and did the right thing anyway, which was to own nothing that stood in the open [X-005]. COSVN itself, a mobile headquarters of a few hundred people under the canopy, was never located and never struck, as it was not historically (POS005-1) [H] [X-005].

Sihanouk, whose territory it was and whose government had tolerated the base areas and the supply line from Sihanoukville, did not protest publicly [H]. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969; Nixon ordered the leak traced, and the telephones of 13 officials and four journalists were tapped [H57]. Historically those wiretaps were the first of the abuses that led to Watergate; in this war there is no Watergate, the burglary of 1972 never happens, and the wiretaps of 1969 are never exposed by an investigation of it (P011a) [X-011]. The bombing forced the PAVN and VC to disperse and camouflage their Cambodian bases, here a few months earlier and more completely, but did not stop their offensives, and its effect on the war in the South is disputed [H57] [X-005].

## EV000-63 · The Moratorium; no My Lai to reveal · 15 Oct – 24 Nov 1969 · Washington and the United States · HIST-ALT · 000,011
*Initiative: neither*
On 15 October 1969 the Moratorium brought hundreds of thousands of Americans out against the war in cities across the country, and on 15 November the Mobilization brought a march of several hundred thousand to Washington [H]. On 3 November Nixon appealed on television to "the great silent majority" to support his policy of withdrawal on his terms, and the speech was well received [H]. Historically, on 13 November Seymour Hersh published the story of the My Lai massacre, the photographs taken by the Army photographer Ronald Haeberle appeared on 20 November, and on 24 November the Army appointed Lieutenant General William Peers to investigate the cover-up [H47].

In this war there was nothing to publish. No massacre had been committed at Son My on 16 March 1968 (EV000-46), Ridenhour had written no letters, and Haeberle's photographs of Task Force Barker's sweep showed soldiers searching empty bunkers [X-011]. The Mobilization of 15 November marched in a week whose news was the President's speech and the first troop withdrawals, not a massacre (P011c). The antiwar movement's case was the war itself, the casualty lists of 1969 at about 9,400 American dead [H40] and the lack of an end, and it did not need My Lai to make it; it lost the war's single most powerful image of what the fighting did to the villages, and its moral argument was weaker for the loss while its political argument was untouched (F011-5) [X-011]. In the press the incidents that history overshadowed with My Lai, the free-fire areas of Quang Ngai and the refugee camps on Route 1, were reported as they were found, one at a time and smaller [X-011]. No Peers inquiry was appointed. The Army was not made to look at the command climate of the Americal Division, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 for a theater-wide inquiry into how the body count shaped the conduct of operations was not taken up in Washington, because without a scandal no one above MACV wanted it (EC011-7, F011-11) [X-011]. Gallup's share of Americans calling the war a mistake had been 53% in August 1968 and averaged about 55% in 1969–70 [M011·S1]; that verdict was in before November 1969, and it was the verdict the President's withdrawal answered.

## EV000-72 · The war on trial at home · 29 Mar – 30 Jun 1971 · Washington and New York · HIST-ALT · 000,002,006,011
*Initiative: neither*
Historically on 29 March 1971 a court-martial at Fort Benning convicted Lieutenant Calley of the premeditated murder of at least 20 people at My Lai and sentenced him to life imprisonment; two days later Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest pending appeal [H47]. In April 1971 Gallup found 11% of Americans approving the verdict, 70% calling Calley a scapegoat and 79% calling the life sentence too harsh [M011·S8]. In this war there is no massacre, no Peers inquiry and no trial (EV000-46, EV000-63), and the spring of 1971 has no argument about a lieutenant that divided the country along lines that did not run between the war's supporters and its opponents (F011-10) [X-011]. In April veterans opposed to the war demonstrated in Washington and threw their medals onto the Capitol steps [H]; they came without Calley's name in every speech, and their protest's weight came, as it had historically, from who they were. On 13 June the *New York Times* began publishing the Pentagon Papers, the Defense Department's secret history of the war's decisions to 1968, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg; on 30 June the Supreme Court refused to stop publication [H]. The administration's response was the "plumbers" unit formed to stop leaks [H]; in this war its burglaries are never exposed, because the burglary of 1972 that exposed them never happens (P011a) [X-011]. By mid-1971 a majority of Americans told pollsters the war had been a mistake, 60% on Gallup's question [M011·S1], as historically.

In this war the Phoenix programme comes before Congress on the historical date, in the hearings of the House Government Operations subcommittee in July 1971, at which, on the historical course, William Colby testified that more than 20,000 people had been killed under Phoenix since 1968 [H]. Here Colby gave the audited figures instead: since January 1965 about 58,000 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 36,000 tried or held under review, about 15,000 rallied and about 7,000 killed, about 2,600 of them in the programme's own operations and the rest in military operations against men whom the centres had named (F006-13) [X-006]. The hearings were damaging and less damaging than they were historically. The story had been told since Duc Thanh in 1967 (EV006-4); the Commission's quarterly samples since 1965 gave the administration an independent count of unsupported neutralizations, about a tenth in the held belt and a quarter in the contested belt across six years, and the magistrate review and capped detention gave it a legal frame to describe (F002-14) [X-006]. The witnesses who hurt the programme most were former Americans of the PRUs' contested-belt advisory teams of 1967–68, and the subcommittee's report singled out the eleven districts the Commission had already named [X-006]. The Con Son "tiger cages" of July 1970 had happened as they did historically, in a prison the statute did not reach [H] [X-002]. The Public Safety appropriation for 1972 passed with a cut of about a tenth rather than the larger cuts its critics proposed, and the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers were kept [X-002]. In this war the subcommittee heard Colby without the shadow of Son My over the hearing room, and its members, who historically asked whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means, asked instead whether the audit could be trusted; the audit could answer that question, and MACV's word in 1971 was worth a little more than history's (F011-16) [X-011].

## EV010-5 · The sergeants of the drawdown · 1 Jan 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · US Army and Marine Corps units in I, II and III Corps, especially the 23rd (Americal) Infantry Division at Chu Lai and LZ Baldy, Quang Tin and Quang Nam Provinces; the NCO academies in the United States · NEW · 010,011
*Initiative: neither*

The American NCO corps that fought the drawdown was schooled for its grades to the man and too thin to hold the army it led. The sergeants first class and master sergeants of 1969 were on their second or third tours; the staff sergeants and sergeants of the rifle companies were men of two or three years' service, many of them graduates of the NCO Candidate Course, who had taken the Basic Leader Course's lanes as its last module or at Bien Hoa (EV010-2) [X-010]. In the units that stayed longest, the war they were asked to lead was one their soldiers had stopped believing in. Drug use, racial violence and refusals rose through 1969–71 on their historical course, and the Army and the Marine Corps recorded about 600–800 fraggings and attempted fraggings in those three years, most of them against officers and NCOs [E: commonly cited service figures for 1969–71].

The schooled sergeant was both the man who held a squad together and the man its worst soldiers resented most. On 14 March 1970 Staff Sergeant Raymond L. Ochoa, a platoon sergeant of a battalion of the 196th Infantry Brigade at LZ Baldy, who had passed the Advanced Leader Course and had used its counselling and its standards to take four men of his platoon off the line for heroin, was wounded by a fragmentation grenade rolled under his bunk; he lived, and two of the four were convicted [X-010]. The 23rd Division's inspector general counted in 1970 that the platoons whose sergeants held the course and had led them for six months or more had about a third fewer drug cases and refusals than those whose sergeants had been waived or deferred (F010-18) [X-010]. That is the provision at its true weight: it held some squads together, and it did not stop the decay, which came from the war's politics and the draft rather than from the sergeant (F010-10). The MACV academy at Bien Hoa graduated its last class on 30 June 1971 and was handed to the ARVN as a division school [X-010].

In this war the draft half of that sentence was smaller. A quarter of the men the boards would have called had enlisted before their notices came, and in a rifle company of the 196th Brigade in 1970, of about 40–60 men who were draftees on the historical course, about 10–15 had chosen to come (P011b, EC011-4) [X-011]. The division's inspector general, asked in 1971 to break the 1970 figures down by how men had entered the Army, found the volunteers among the former draftees' cohort about as likely as the volunteers of every year to be on the drug and refusal lists, and about half as likely as the men still drafted; in the platoons that had a schooled sergeant, a battalion that had trained and deployed together and three or four such volunteers, he counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer cases than in Ochoa's platoon (F011-19) [X-011]. It changed no fragging that the record names and saved no squad that the war's politics had already lost. The units that went home in 1971 went home as history's did, from a war their soldiers knew was being given up.

## P6 · Overview
Phase VI is the war without the Americans. The Paris Agreement of January 1973 takes US forces out and returns the prisoners; it leaves the PAVN in the South, the Thieu government in Saigon, and the war to be decided between them. The ceasefire never holds. In 1973 Saigon is on the offensive, recovering hamlets and roads, while the North rebuilds; in 1974 the two are evenly matched, and the ARVN, short of ammunition, fuel and spare parts as American aid falls, bleeds in a war of attrition it cannot sustain; in January 1975 the North tests whether the United States will respond to the seizure of a province, and when it does not, it commits its army to the Highlands [H72].

The shape of the phase is three slow years and a fast spring. The constraints that bind the American role are now law, not policy: the Case–Church amendment ends all US combat activity in Indochina from 15 August 1973, and Congress cuts military aid. In Hanoi, the 21st Plenum of October 1973 returns to revolutionary violence, and the PAVN builds a road network east of the mountains, a fuel pipeline into the South and an army of corps. In Saigon, the economy, hit by the oil shock and the departure of the Americans whose spending had sustained it, and a government that has narrowed its base, face the offensive of 1975 with an army spread thin across the whole country.

In this war the army that fights the last three years has been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the abundance of American ammunition, and the ration of 1974 finds it more dependent on volume than the historical one (EV000-89). It fights the attrition of 1974 with better weapons than its enemy and too few rounds to use them, and the hoards it builds against the ration are lost with Hue and the Highlands (EV000-91, EV000-94, EV000-95).

The bombers leave in August 1973 without bombing Neak Luong, and the coordinate cell is disbanded with nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to (EV000-86). The ARVN fights 1974 and the defence of Phuoc Long with plans written for an air force that no longer comes, and loses Thuong Duc and Phuoc Long as history did, with its officers knowing exactly what they lack (EV000-91, EV000-92).

Phung Hoang outlives the Americans as a Vietnamese programme and helps the Republic's offensive of 1973 in the Delta; its national file, consolidated in Saigon since 1971, is still in the Central Intelligence Organization's building at the end of 1975, and the province files of Quang Ngai and Quang Tin are taken when those provinces fall in March (EV000-84, EV000-94, EV000-98). The advisers are gone in March 1973, and nothing of their eighteen months survives them but the counterparts who remember them.

The officer reforms leave the Republic in its last three years an officer corps that knows precisely what it lacks. Its battalion and regimental commanders, educated on a doctrine built around American fire, plan the battles of 1974 for a weight of fire the ration no longer allows (EV000-91). Its staff college writes, in November 1974, the only serious plan the Republic ever had for a defence on reduced resources; in March 1975 the President rejects it entirely and orders everything held, and its method is used a year late, for the evacuation of Da Nang in May (EV000-95, EV011-3).

In this war the last ration also fell on an army that made two-fifths of its own small-arms ammunition, a third of its 105mm rounds and nearly all of its radio batteries, from imported powder and semiconductors (EV000-89). The domestic plants stretched each ammunition and communications dollar, made the attrition of 1974 somewhat cheaper and changed no position the PAVN chose to take (EV000-91), gave Phuoc Long more rounds than it could fire (EV000-92), and in 1975 were still the Republic's (EV011-3).

In this war there is no Watergate, and the President who wrote Thieu his promises is in office to the end of the record. Case–Church passes as historically, so that the bombers he promised cannot lawfully return, but the War Powers Resolution fails in the House, the Republicans of 1974 do not desert him, the midterm costs his party about 22 House seats instead of 49, and the fiscal 1975 appropriation is about $950 million, which the Republic's own plants stretch to buy about what $1.2 billion bought historically (EV000-86, EV000-89, EV011-1) [X-011]. The Republic fights 1974 near the 1973 allowance. In March 1975, with a year's fuel and a President who has asked Congress for its bombers and been refused, Thieu refuses the Dalat staff college's withdrawal and orders the Highlands and I Corps held (F011-20); the Highlands are lost in five weeks instead of ten days and Hue in an ordered withdrawal in April, and on 31 March the Politburo, which historically resolved on 25 March to take Saigon before the rains, has no double collapse to resolve on and puts Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76 (EV000-94, EV000-95, EVMT01-4) [X-011]. The Republic ends 1975 smaller, holding III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, with its general reserve brought back from Da Nang by sea and its fall expected in the first months of 1976 (EV011-3) [X-011].

## EV000-86 · Case–Church and the end of American combat in Indochina · Jun – 7 Nov 1973 · Washington; Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005,011
*Initiative: neither*

In June 1973 Congress passed the Case–Church amendment, which barred any US military activity in or over Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia without congressional approval from 15 August 1973 [H51] [H]. It passed the House 325–86 on 26 June and the Senate 73–16 on 29 June, and the President signed it on 1 July [M011·S6]. In this war it passed in the same words and by about the same margins (P011a). Historically Republicans relented on support for South Vietnam under the pressure of Watergate [M011·S6]; here there was no Watergate, the Senate's televised hearings never opened on 17 May, and the President's own party stood with him on every vote that was about him [M011·S10] [X-011]. But this vote was not about him. The prisoners were home, the troops were out, and 60% of Americans called the war a mistake [M011·S1]; the amendment's margins were veto-proof before a single Republican defection is subtracted, and the Republicans who historically voted for it voted for it here, because their voters wanted Indochina finished (F011-9) [X-011]. Until 15 August the B-52s went on bombing Cambodia, which had continued since the Paris Agreement, in support of Lon Nol's forces around Phnom Penh [H]. Historically on 6 August a B-52 dropped its bombs on the town of Neak Luong on the Mekong, killing about 137 people and wounding about 268, because its navigator had failed to set the offset for the radar beacon on which the strike was aimed [H]. In this war no beacon was used: the J navigated and released on its own GPS, and the strike that night fell on its intended box in the forest east of the river [X-005]. Neak Luong was not bombed, and the American ambassador in Phnom Penh did not have to pay its families a hundred dollars a death [H] [X-005].

On 15 August the bombing of Cambodia ended, and with it the last American combat missions in Indochina [H]. The Js went home to the Strategic Air Command's alert pads, and in this war they stayed on alert at Andersen and U-Tapao through each dry season as the visible instrument of the President's promise to Thieu (EC011-1, F011-3) [X-011]; Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut, which had been folded into the Defense Attaché Office's intelligence branch in March, was disbanded in September, and its folders for every firebase and district town in the South went to Clark Air Base and then to the Defense Mapping Agency [X-005]. The Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell of its own; there was nothing for such a cell to send coordinates to, since no aircraft the VNAF flew could carry a guided bomb, and the request was not pursued (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. Historically on 7 November Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto, eighteen days after the Saturday Night Massacre of 20 October [H] [M011·S10]. In this war there was no Saturday Night Massacre, and the override failed in the House by a handful of votes, the Republicans who had historically broken with a President they were about to impeach holding with one they were not (F011-9) [X-011]. It changed less than it seemed. Nixon's promise to Thieu of a forceful response to a major violation was beyond the President's power to keep without Congress, not because of the War Powers Resolution but because of Case–Church, and Congress would not give it [H] [X-011]. The sanctuary constraint, which had shaped the war since 1964, now covered the whole of Indochina and was written into law.

## EV000-89 · The aid cuts and the shortages · 25 Jan – 9 Aug 1974 · South Vietnam and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,004,008,011
*Initiative: neither*

On 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel to all ARVN units were cut because of budget limits [H71]. Historically US military aid fell from about $1.1 billion in fiscal year 1974 to an appropriation of about $700 million for fiscal year 1975, the July 1974–June 1975 year, the figure given by source as $700–750 million [H72] [H71]. Major General Murray of the Defense Attaché Office warned that cuts in support could roughly be equated with loss of territory, and that aid of $600 million would mean writing the South off [H71]. The ARVN rationed artillery rounds, grounded aircraft and helicopters for lack of fuel and parts, and cut patrols; the soldiers' pay lost most of its value to inflation, and desertion rose [H]. The economy suffered from the world oil shock of late 1973 and the loss of American spending [H].

In this war the ration fell on an army that had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical one (F004-15). The Joint General Staff's directive of February 1974 limited each infantry squad to its basic load for a month's operations, and the squad gun went first: about 200 rounds a gun for a month, which is what an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes [X-004]. Battalion commanders who had learned their trade breaking contacts with the squad gun's volume found that the 1974 ration let them do it about twice in a month, and the advisers' successors at the Defense Attaché Office reported that the infantry, rather than fight with aimed rifle fire as their fathers had fought with the Garand, avoided contacts it could not break with volume [X-004]. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had a separate line, were rationed harder still; by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms for want of ammunition [X-004]. Divisions began to hoard linked 5.56mm in their own ammunition points against the ration, against JGS orders, and by the end of the year the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Divisions held about 9 million rounds outside the corps depots between them [X-004].

The vote was where this war parted from history's. In the spring of 1974 the President was not fighting impeachment; the House Judiciary Committee voted no articles in July, because there was nothing to vote on, and on 9 August, the day history's Nixon resigned and Gerald Ford succeeded him [H71] [M011·S10], this war's Nixon was at San Clemente drafting the message on the fiscal 1975 budget that he sent to Congress on 14 August (P011a, EC011-2) [X-011]. He made Murray's warning his own argument, in a joint appearance with the Secretary of Defense before the House Appropriations Committee's defense subcommittee on 21 May, and he had the votes of about 15–30 Republicans in the House whom history's Watergate summer had sent home to defend themselves [X-011]. The House cut the request anyway, because the war was unpopular and the recession was beginning, and the conference report of the autumn gave the RVNAF about $950 million for fiscal 1975, against history's $700 million and fiscal 1974's $1.1 billion (F011-2) [X-011]. In the House debate the Bien Hoa arsenal was cited by the advocates of the cut as proof that the Republic could make its own ammunition, which was half true, since it made the brass and the steel and not the powder (F008-22) [X-008]; here the President answered it, and the answer bought about $250 million that history's Congress did not vote [X-011].

That was the ration on American stocks, and in this war it was not the whole of the ration. Since 1965 the Aid Compliance Board's domestic share had made the RVNAF buy from its own plants (EV008-1), and by 1974 the Bien Hoa arsenal made about two-fifths of the RVNAF's 5.56mm and 7.62mm cartridges, about a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its mortar rounds, every one of them filled with imported propellant and explosive bought out of the same appropriation (EV008-6) [X-008]. A dollar of propellant, primers and explosive loaded at Bien Hoa bought about one and a half times the rounds of a dollar of finished American ammunition, and the battery works and the signal depot stretched the communications dollar about twice [M008]. Across the whole of the RVNAF's programme the fiscal 1975 appropriation of about $950 million bought about what $1.2 billion had bought on the historical course, with the upper bound marked because it multiplies two judgments that lean the same way (F011-2) [X-011]. From March 1974 the Joint General Staff raised the squad gun's monthly ration to about 400 rounds and the 105mm battery's daily allowance by about a third, and the PRC-25 and PRC-77 batteries that the historical RVNAF rationed through 1974 were issued at the full rate from Go Vap; the territorial posts' radios, which history's district chiefs found going silent in the second half of the year, stayed on the air (F008-23) [X-008]. From the autumn, when the larger appropriation began to arrive, the margin went where the domestic stretch had never reached: aviation fuel and imported parts for the VNAF, diesel for the relief columns and the trucks of the corps' logistics commands, and 155mm and 175mm rounds that Bien Hoa could not forge (F011-21) [X-011]. The VNAF's grounded aircraft, which history counted in hundreds by the end of 1974, were about a third fewer by December [X-011].

The plants also paid wages, and in 1974 that mattered in the households of the army. About 60,000–80,000 people worked in the Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap works, more than half of them women on the battery and assembly lines, and perhaps one ARVN or RF family in eight in the provinces of III Corps had a factory wage in it [X-008]. A private's pay in 1974 bought a fraction of what it had bought in 1971 [H]; a wife's wage at the battery works roughly doubled what the household had [X-008]. The factory families of Bien Hoa were the one part of the army's families that the inflation of 1974 did not reach in full, and they lived beside the plants, not beside the units (F008-21). The larger appropriation did not reach the soldier's pay, which was the Republic's own budget and was paid in piasters the oil shock was eating; the economic aid voted with it, about a tenth larger than history's, held the import programme's rice and fertilizer a little nearer their 1973 level and slowed the piaster's fall in the second half of the year without stopping it [X-011]. Rice was the other side of the household: the pumps the agricultural machinery shops had sold since 1964 had put a dry-season crop on about 300,000 hectares of the held Delta by 1974, and the Delta's surplus that reached Saigon in 1974 was somewhat larger than history's, which held the price of rice in the capital a little below history's in a year when everything else rose [X-008].

## EV000-91 · The war of attrition of 1974 · 16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005,007,008,011
*Initiative: enemy*

From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves [H] [H71]. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November [H71]. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November [H71]. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties [H71]. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost [H71]. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed [H71].

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night [X-004]. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were [H] [X-004].

They bled in a second way that history's Airborne did not. Since An Loc, Kontum and the Lam Son bases the ARVN's I Corps had been taught, by success, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege (F005-20) [X-005]. At Thuong Duc the district town's garrison, the 79th Ranger Battalion, dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, for a siege box that no longer existed; the 304th Division's regiment dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them, and the town fell on 7 August [H] [X-005]. The Airborne's counter-attack on the heights was planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia in 1971 under the Js, and its first assaults on Hill 1062 went forward in the expectation that the PAVN's positions on the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in the monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it [X-005]. I estimate that the Airborne Division's losses in the heights were perhaps a tenth higher than historically, a judgment I hold loosely [X-005]. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

The officer reforms made the attrition of 1974 more legible and no cheaper. The battalion and regimental commanders who fought at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc had been educated on a doctrine in which the infantry finds the enemy and the fires destroy him, and from January 1974 the fires were rationed; their reports to the Joint General Staff in the summer, collected by the Central Training Command, are the most exact account anywhere of what the ration did to each kind of fight, and none of them could change it (F007-23) [X-007]. At Duc Duc the 3rd Division's battalions, under board commanders, held their companies together through 4,700 casualties with fewer men lost to desertion than history's, and held no more ground [H71] [X-007].

The Bien Hoa rounds changed which guns could fire, and the PAVN's choice of ground decided how much that was worth. Through the summer the 105mm batteries of the 3rd Division at Duc Duc and the Airborne on the Thuong Duc heights fired about a third more than the historical ration allowed, on domestic rounds, and every post and company on the ridges kept its radio on the air (EV000-89) [X-008]. It did not change the arithmetic of the artillery duel. The PAVN fought these battles where its 130mm M-46 guns, ranging 27 kilometres, could reach the ARVN's 105mm positions at 11 kilometres from beyond their range, and the guns the domestic rounds fed were the guns its choice of ground made least decisive [H] [X-008]. Thuong Duc still fell on 7 August, Duc Duc still cost the 3rd Division its 4,700, and Phu Loc was recovered in December as history's was; at Rach Bap the III Corps battalions, which drew directly from Bien Hoa, retook the post in the last week of October rather than in November [X-008]. I judge that the domestic rounds and the radios saved perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead in 1974, resting on a third more 105mm fire and a doubled squad-gun ration in the fights the army did accept [X-008].

In this war the strategic raids of the autumn met an army whose larger appropriation had begun to arrive, and they met it less often. The Politburo had watched a President who did not fall all summer, and the B-2 Front's and the Tri-Thien front's plans for raids in III Corps and on the Phu Loc hills in September were cut back by Hanoi to the Phu Loc operation alone, on the reasoning that a President in full possession of his office might find a way round the statute if provoked hard enough before the North was ready (F011-12) [X-011]. At Phu Loc from 28 August the 1st Division's counter-attack on the hills had VNAF UH-1Hs for its lift and resupply on most days instead of on some, because the helicopters had fuel, and the 155mm batteries at Phu Bai fired at about the 1973 allowance from October; the hills were retaken in the third week of November instead of on 10 December [X-011]. I judge that the larger appropriation and the President's restraint on Hanoi's raids together saved perhaps 1,500–4,000 more of the Republic's dead in 1974, a judgment resting on a rate of 5–13% fewer losses in the fights the army accepted with fuller supply and on raids not made, and I keep the reported figure of about 30,000 dead for the year, since the two savings together lie at the edge of its uncertainty (F011-21) [H71] [X-011]. What absorbed the rest of the difference was the PAVN's choice of ground and the ARVN's lack of a reserve: a better-supplied army that could not move its divisions still lost the positions it was tied to.

## EV011-1 · The midterm of 1974 · 5 Nov 1974 · Washington and the United States · NEW · 011
*Initiative: neither*
The congressional elections of 5 November 1974 were held in a recession, a year after the oil embargo, in the sixth year of a Republican presidency, and they went against the President's party as such elections do. Historically the Democrats gained 49 House seats and 5 Senate seats, in the autumn after the resignation and the pardon, and the freshmen of 1975 were the most hostile class to the war Congress ever seated [M011·S4] [H]. In this war there was no resignation and no pardon. The Democrats gained about 22 House seats and 3 Senate seats, the ordinary price of a bad economy and an unpopular war's long tail (P011a, F011-9) [X-011]. The ninety-fourth Congress that met in January 1975 was still Democratic in both houses and still wanted Indochina finished; it had about 27 more Republicans in the House than history's, enough to sustain a veto and not enough to pass anything the Democratic leadership opposed [X-011]. The President had lost no authority he had not already lost to the statute of 1973. In the Politburo's assessment of December, read to the strategy conference that sat from 18 December, the election was noted as a defeat for Nixon smaller than the Americans' own press had predicted, and the conference's conclusion that the United States would not return rested on the law, not on the President (F011-13) [X-011].

## EV000-92 · The fall of Phuoc Long · 12 Dec 1974 – 6 Jan 1975 · Phuoc Long Province (Phuoc Binh and Song Be), III Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,005,008,011
*Initiative: enemy*

On 12 December 1974 the PAVN 4th Corps under Hoang Cam, with the 7th Division, the new 3rd Division and independent regiments including the 141st, 165th, 201st and 271st, and two armoured companies with 14 T-54s, attacked Phuoc Long Province on the Cambodian border [H73]. It was defended mainly by Regional Force battalions, reinforced by the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 7th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 9th Regiment of the 5th Division and the 81st Airborne Ranger Battalion [H73]. The province chief's plan for the defence of Phuoc Binh, drafted in 1973 by officers who had served at An Loc, was a plan to hold the town for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege; it named the bomber cells by the call signs Hollingsworth's men had used in 1972 (F005-20) [X-005]. On 13 December the province chief asked III Corps for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan", and III Corps forwarded the request to the Joint General Staff, which forwarded it to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it [X-005].

<!-- initiative --> In this war the attack was the B-2 Front's decision more than Hanoi's. With a President in Washington who had not fallen, the Politburo was more cautious through the autumn than history's, and the General Staff's plan for the 1974–75 dry season, approved at the conference of 30 September – 8 October under Le Duan, held the main blow for the Highlands and left the B-2 Front a supporting role (F011-12) [X-011]. Lieutenant General Tran Van Tra, the B-2 Front's commander, argued in Hanoi in November for the attack on Phuoc Long as a test of the American response, as he had historically, and against a Politburo that had more reason to fear the answer; he won, on his front's own reckoning that the province could be taken before Washington could decide anything, and on the B-2 command's long rivalry with a General Staff that had kept the decisive operations for the northern fronts since 1968 [H] [X-011]. The test was his, and it was taken on his timetable.

On 18 December, in the meeting at the Independence Palace where Thieu and the Joint General Staff decided not to send the general reserve to Phuoc Long, the request was on the table, and the decision taken was historical: the Airborne Division was committed in I Corps, and Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers [H73] [X-005]. In this war the judgment was put in those words, and with more conviction, because the Republic's officers had seen at An Loc and Kontum what American bombers could do for a besieged town and knew exactly what they were not getting [X-005]. The last ARVN post fell on 6 January 1975, the first provincial capital lost permanently since the Paris Agreement [H73] [H]. The ARVN lost more than 1,000 killed and 2,444 captured; the PAVN about 1,300 killed and wounded [H73]. Saigon did not try to retake it. Washington made no military response [H73]. In Hanoi, where a strategy conference sat from 18 December to 8 January, Phuoc Long answered the question the leadership had set itself: the United States would not return [H71] [H]. The plan for a two-year campaign to end the war in 1976 was adapted to strike in the Central Highlands in 1975 [H].

In this war the defence of Phuoc Binh had more rounds and a radio to the end, and neither reached the reasons it fell. The province's ammunition point held about a third more 105mm rounds than history's, most of them from Bien Hoa, and the Regional Force battalions' posts in the province reported on the air until they were overrun, because their batteries were fresh (EV000-89) [X-008]. The PAVN's 130mm guns and its T-54s decided the siege, as they had historically: the province's two 155mm and four 105mm howitzers were silenced by counter-battery fire in the first week, and the rounds that would have fed them were still in the ammunition point when the 7th Division took it [H73] [X-008]. What absorbed the difference was the decision of 18 December not to send a reserve and the PAVN's artillery, which no rounds for 105mm guns could answer. One thing went to Hanoi that had not gone historically. The 4th Corps' artillery staff reported that the ARVN's guns had fired in the first week at a rate the General Staff's estimate of the 1974 ration did not allow, and the B-2 Front's intelligence section, asked where the rounds came from, answered with the name of the Bien Hoa arsenal and the tonnage of propellant the port of Saigon had landed for it in 1974 (F008-18) [X-008]. It was the first time the North's planners counted the Republic's own industry as part of its strength. Washington's answer came after the fall, and it is the next event (EV011-2).

## EV011-2 · The message on Phuoc Long · 7 Jan – 13 Mar 1975 · Washington, Hanoi and Saigon · NEW · 011
*Initiative: allied*
On 7 January 1975, the day after the last post at Phuoc Binh fell, the President sent Congress a message that history's President never sent. It asked for two things: a supplemental appropriation of $300 million for the RVNAF's ammunition and fuel, and authority under the Case–Church amendment to use American air power in Indochina "in the event of a major offensive by the forces of North Vietnam in violation of the Agreement" (P011a, EC011-1) [X-011]. Historically President Ford asked for the same $300 million in January 1975 and was refused [H]. Here the request for money went to the appropriations committees and the request for authority to the Foreign Affairs and Foreign Relations Committees, and the second was dead within the month: the Senate committee did not report it, and the House leadership, with the votes of the Democratic majority behind it, refused to schedule it [X-011]. The supplemental fared better. A Congress that would not let the bombers fly would pay for rounds, and in the second week of March it voted about $150 million of the $300 million, most of it for fuel and 105mm and 155mm ammunition (EC011-2) [X-011].

The message was read in three capitals, and each read it right. In Hanoi the General Staff's assessment, which reached Le Duan before the final session of the strategy conference on 8 January, said that the President wanted to return and that the Congress would not let him; it was the conclusion Phuoc Long had been fought to obtain, and the plan for the Highlands went forward on it (F011-13) [X-011]. In Washington the President and Kissinger concluded that the promise of 1972 could not be kept with bombers and would be kept with money. In Saigon Thieu read the refusal of authority as Hanoi did and the vote of $150 million as the President's word kept as far as Congress allowed, and he concluded that the money was coming and that he should not give up any province to make it go further (F011-20) [X-011]. That conclusion was in his mind on 11 March, the day after Ban Me Thuot fell.

## EV000-94 · The defence of I Corps · 13 Mar – 18 Apr 1975 · Quang Tri, Hue, Quang Ngai, Quang Tin and Da Nang · HIST-ALT · 000,006,MT01,011
*Initiative: enemy*
Historically, on 13 March Thieu ordered Truong to give up most of I Corps and withdrew the Airborne Division towards Saigon as a reserve; on 20 March he ordered him to hold only Da Nang; on 29 March he ordered him to turn back and retake it, which Truong refused [H75]. The contradictory orders, given as the PAVN 2nd Corps with the 304th, 324th and 325th Divisions attacked from the north and west and the 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade struck in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin, broke I Corps; Hue was abandoned on 25 March, about 6,000 soldiers of the 2nd Division deserted on 28 March, and the PAVN entered Da Nang on 29 March [H75]. Three ARVN infantry divisions and the 147th Marine Brigade ceased to exist, about 58,722 soldiers were captured at Hue, and the PAVN took 129 aircraft, 179 armoured vehicles and 327 artillery pieces [H75].

In this war no order came on 13 March. Thieu's meeting of 11 March at the Independence Palace, with Vien, Lieutenant General Dang Van Quang and Prime Minister Tran Thien Khiem, heard Vien present the Dalat study for a withdrawal to a defensible Republic, and Thieu refused it: with the fuel and rounds of a fiscal 1975 appropriation near the 1974 level, the March supplemental voted and a President in Washington who had asked Congress for the bombers, he would not give up provinces the aid could still defend (EV011-2, F011-20) [X-011]. Truong was told to hold I Corps with what he had, the Airborne Division included. He had planned for it since the autumn, and his plan began with the families: from 15 March the Vietnamese Navy's LSTs and the requisitioned coasters of the Saigon lines carried the dependants of the 1st Division and the Marines from Tan My and Thuan An to Da Nang and on to Cam Ranh, about 70,000 people in three weeks, so that the soldiers on the My Chanh line and in the hills west of Hue had their families behind the Hai Van pass and not on the road (DIRMT01-9) [X-011].

The 2nd Corps attacked on 19 March as it had historically, and northern Quang Tri was given up to the My Chanh line on the 19th–21st in an ordered withdrawal, the line the Marines had held in 1972 [H] [X-011]. In the south of the corps the defence broke as history's did. The 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin, whose families lived in the dependants' quarters at Quang Ngai and Tam Ky and had not been moved, came apart under the PAVN 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade between 22 and 28 March; Quang Ngai city and Tam Ky fell, about 5,000 of the division's men left their units for their families, and the remnants were taken off the beach at Chu Lai on the 30th [H] [X-011]. On the My Chanh and west of Hue the line held. The 1st Division and the Marine Division, with the VNAF's A-37s flying from Da Nang on fuel the appropriation had bought and the 175mm guns at Camp Evans firing on rounds Bien Hoa could not have made, held the 324B and 325th Divisions short of Hue for three weeks (F011-21) [X-011]. When the 2nd Corps cut Highway 1 at Phu Loc behind them in the first week of April, Truong withdrew Hue's garrison by sea from Tan My and over the Hai Van between 14 and 18 April, in an ordered movement with the families already gone; he lost about a third of the 1st Division and most of its heavy equipment, and the divisional hoard at Hue, about 3 million rounds of linked 5.56mm, was destroyed in place on the 17th rather than captured [X-011]. Da Nang was held by the Airborne, the Marines, the 3rd Division and the 1st Division's survivors, behind the Hai Van and the Thu Bon.

In this war the Special Branch's files went as the provinces went. The Quang Tri and Thua Thien centres burned their files on the 19th and the 16th of April under the destruction plan of 1968, because the withdrawal was ordered and the regional chief gave the order; the Quang Ngai and Quang Tin centres, overrun in the collapse of the 2nd Division, did not, and the PAVN's security troops took their district files, about 9,000 names, when they entered Quang Ngai on 25 March [X-006] [X-011]. The I Corps regional office in Da Nang, with its district extracts for all five provinces, was still in the Republic's hands. That the collapse of a corps would deliver its files with it was the risk the memorandum that created them had named (F006-25); in this war only one province's collapse was a rout, and only its files were lost.

## EV000-95 · The battle for the Highlands · 14 Mar – 19 Apr 1975 · Pleiku and Kontum; Phuoc An on Route 21; Route 19 through the Mang Yang pass to An Khe · HIST-ALT · 000,007,011
*Initiative: enemy*

Historically, on 14 March at Cam Ranh, Thieu ordered Phu to abandon Pleiku and Kontum and pull II Corps back to the coast; the chief of the Joint General Staff, General Cao Van Vien, warned against Route 19 because of the French disaster at the Mang Yang Pass in 1954, and Thieu chose Route 7, a disused road in poor condition [H74]. The retreat was ordered verbally and kept secret; some 400,000 civilians joined the columns, the PAVN ambushed them near Cheo Reo, and only the 6th Ranger Battalion and the 19th Armoured Cavalry Squadron arrived in good order; an estimated 155,000 civilians were killed or abducted on the "convoy of tears" [H74].

In this war the plan for a withdrawal existed, and it was refused. In November 1974 a seminar of the Command and General Staff College at Dalat, under Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had gone to its faculty after Quang Tri, wrote a study for the Joint General Staff titled "The Defence of the Republic on Reduced Resources" [X-007]. It concluded that with the aid then expected the RVNAF could not hold the whole country against the forces the North was building, and proposed a withdrawal from Kontum, Pleiku and northern Quang Tri over about three months of the dry season, before any offensive, with routes secured in advance, the families of the soldiers moved first, the province chiefs told, and the Airborne and Marines rebuilt as a reserve [X-007]. Vien sent it to the palace in January 1975, after Phuoc Long, and Thieu did not answer it [X-007]. On 14 March at Cam Ranh he did not take even its conclusion. With a year's fuel and ammunition near the 1973 allowance and a President who had asked Congress for the bombers, he ordered Phu to hold Pleiku and Kontum and to retake Ban Me Thuot, and the study went back into Vien's safe (F011-20, EV011-2) [X-011].

The counter-attack on Ban Me Thuot went in from Phuoc An on Route 21 on 15–18 March with the 23rd Division's 45th Regiment and the 21st Ranger Group, lifted by VNAF CH-47s and UH-1Hs that had fuel to fly, and the PAVN 10th Division, dug in on the high ground west of Phuoc An with the 320A in reserve, broke it on the 18th as it had broken history's 23rd Division counter-attack on the same ground [H] [X-011]. From 20 March the 320A and 968th Divisions closed on Pleiku from the south and west and the PAVN 3rd Division's 12th Regiment and the 95B Regiment cut Route 19 at the Mang Yang pass, and II Corps in the Highlands, about 25,000 regulars and Rangers and the province's territorials, was besieged at Pleiku and Kontum with the Cu Hanh airfield its only line to the coast [X-011]. Kontum was given up on 27 March in an ordered withdrawal down Route 14 by the 22nd and 23rd Ranger Groups, under fire and in good order, because Phu, ordered to hold, chose to hold one town rather than two [X-011].

The siege of Pleiku lasted three weeks, and it was fought on what the appropriation had bought. The VNAF's C-130As and C-123Ks flew from Nha Trang into Cu Hanh by day until 9 April, about 25–35 sorties a day with ammunition, fuel and the wounded out, and the A-37s of the 6th Air Division flew from Pleiku itself until the PAVN's 130mm guns closed the runway on 9 April [X-011]. The 320A's assault battalions came at the town's southern perimeter on 2 and 5 April across open ground and were broken there by the 44th Regiment and the 4th Ranger Group's squads, with the squad guns fed and the artillery firing at the 1973 allowance; the PAVN's own account puts the cost of the two assaults at about 1,200 killed and wounded, and I judge it at perhaps twice that [X-011]. It was the defence's best fight and it did not change the siege. The 10th Division came up from Ban Me Thuot, the 968th's guns had the airfield, and with Cu Hanh closed the town could be fed for about a week. On 14 April Phu broke out down Route 19 with the garrison and about 60,000 civilians, the families of the soldiers at the head of the column under the plan he had written for it in March, and fought through the Mang Yang between 15 and 19 April against the 3rd Division's blocking positions [X-011]. About 11,000 of the 25,000 regulars and Rangers reached An Khe and Qui Nhon with their weapons; the column lost about 9,000 civilians killed or taken, against history's estimate of 155,000 on Route 7 [H74] [X-011].

It was a defeat, and a different defeat from history's. II Corps lost the Highlands on 19 April instead of 25 March, five weeks later and with about half its Highland force still in being rather than two units [H74] [X-011]. The President's refusal of the Dalat study turned a plan that would have saved the Highlands force without a fight into a siege that saved half of it after one; and the siege held the 320A, 10th and 968th Divisions in the Highlands until the third week of April, which is what reached Hanoi's calendar (EVMT01-4). The best piece of staff work the reforms ever produced was refused by a President who believed, on the evidence of the money and the message, that he did not need it.

## EVMT01-4 · The Politburo's calendar: Saigon after the rains · 25 Mar – 14 Apr 1975 · Hanoi and the roads south · HIST-ALT · MT01,008,011
*Initiative: enemy*

Historically the date of the fall of Saigon was set in Hanoi between 25 March and 14 April, and it was set by the rains and by the road. On 25 March, with Hue abandoned and the Highlands column destroyed, the Politburo resolved to take Saigon before the rainy season, which in the southern provinces begins in May and turns the lowlands around the capital to mud; on 31 March, with Da Nang taken, it decided to do it in April; and on 14 April it named the operation the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H]. The 1st Corps, the strategic reserve, was brought south from the North by road, a march of more than 1,500 kilometres, the 2nd Corps came down the coast behind the collapse of I and II Corps, and General Van Tien Dung's plan set the assault for the last days of April because that was when the last corps would be in place and the first rains still a fortnight off [H] [E: road distance from the Thanh Hoa region to Binh Duong by Route 1 and the strategic road].

In this war the Politburo met on 25 March with a different map. Ban Me Thuot had fallen and Quang Ngai was falling, but Pleiku was besieged and holding, Kontum's garrison was fighting its way down Route 14, Hue was held on the My Chanh, and Da Nang and the coast from Qui Nhon south were the Republic's (EV000-94, EV000-95) [X-011]. Le Duan's resolution of that day was the one the October conference had prepared, not the one history's collapse made possible: finish the Highlands and the central coast in the dry season, move the 1st Corps south, and prepare the general offensive on Saigon for the dry season of 1975–76 [X-011]. Tran Van Tra argued in the Politburo's meeting of 31 March, as the B-2 Front's commander, for a thrust on Saigon in May with the 4th Corps, Group 232 and whatever the 1st Corps could bring, on the reasoning that the Republic's reserve was in I Corps and Saigon had nothing behind it; Dung, who had come back from the Highlands front, answered that the 1st Corps could not be at the capital before the third week of May, that the 2nd Corps was held on the My Chanh and the 3rd Corps' divisions at Pleiku, and that an assault into the rains with two corps against five divisions was the error of 1968 [X-011]. Le Duan took Dung's view. The 1st Corps was ordered south by the strategic road to assembly areas in the base areas north of Saigon, to arrive by June and wait [X-011].

The critical path that MT01 named ran through this meeting, and in this war it bent. Historically only a delay of about three weeks or more in the collapse of I and II Corps would have put the assault into the rains and forced Hanoi to choose between fighting in the mud and waiting for the dry season (POSMT01-3). The Highlands held until 19 April, about five weeks past history's 14 March decision and three and a half weeks past the 25 March resolution it made possible; Hue held until 18 April, about three and a half weeks past history's 25 March; Da Nang did not fall in the spring at all [X-011]. On 25 March the Politburo had no double collapse to resolve on, and by mid-April, when the Highlands opened, the calendar left two weeks before the rains for a campaign that needed five. What delayed the collapse was not the arsenal or the officers, which the critical path had already absorbed, but a decision at the top that history's President took one way and this war's took the other: the order of 14 March, given by a man with a year's fuel and a President's message in hand (F011-20, F011-21, EV011-2). It is the first change to the critical path in this record, and it bought a season, not a war.

The base still changed one line of Dung's plan. After Phuoc Long the General Staff counted the Bien Hoa works as part of the Republic's strength, and the order for the general offensive, when it was written in the autumn for the dry season of 1975–76, named the arsenal, the steelworks and the Go Vap plant among the objectives to be taken intact rather than shelled, on the Politburo's instruction that the South's industry be preserved for the reunified country (F008-18) [X-008] [X-011].

## EV000-96 · Xuan Loc · 9–21 Apr 1975 · Xuan Loc, Long Khanh Province, northeast of Saigon · CANCELLED · 000,004,007,MT01,011

## EV000-97 · The President's request; Thieu stays · 10–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,011

## EV000-98 · The fall of Saigon · 29–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon · CANCELLED · 000,006,008,011
*Initiative: enemy*
Historically by late April five PAVN corps, Group 232 and the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th Corps, with 15 infantry divisions and three tank regiments, encircled Saigon in the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H77]. Rockets and artillery hit Tan Son Nhut on the morning of 29 April, killing two US Marine guards, Corporal Charles McMahon and Lance Corporal Darwin Judge, the last American servicemen killed in action in Vietnam; Operation Frequent Wind lifted about 1,373 Americans and about 5,100 Vietnamese to the Seventh Fleet; at 10:24 on 30 April Minh announced an unconditional surrender, and PAVN tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace [H77] [H].

In this war Saigon was not attacked in April 1975. The Ho Chi Minh Campaign was not named, because the Politburo's resolution of 25–31 March put the offensive on the capital back to the dry season of 1975–76 (EVMT01-4) [X-011]. On 29 April 1975 Tan Son Nhut was not shelled, McMahon and Judge stood their watch at the Defense Attaché Office compound and went off it, and Frequent Wind remained a plan in the Seventh Fleet's safe [X-011]. The Defense Attaché Office's evacuation of American dependants, begun in early April, went on through May at the pace of the commercial flights [X-011].

In this war the Central Intelligence Organization's building still held the national Phung Hoang file at the end of 1975, about 240,000 names with photographs and fingerprints for about 90,000, consolidated there since 1971 (EV002-6) [X-006]. The Director General ordered its index microfilmed in May and the film sent to Can Tho, against the day the capital fell, and the order was carried out [X-011]. The Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap works were still the Republic's, and in the summer of 1975 they were the Republic's largest source of small-arms rounds and batteries for an army that had lost its northern hoards (EV011-3) [X-008] [X-011].

## EV011-3 · The rains: the Republic after the spring offensive · 1 May – 31 Dec 1975 · Da Nang, Qui Nhon and the central coast; Saigon; Hanoi and the strategic road · NEW · 011
*Initiative: both*
The Republic that entered the rains of 1975 had lost the Highlands, Quang Tri, Thua Thien, Quang Ngai, Quang Tin and Phuoc Long, about a third of its army's infantry and most of the heavy equipment of II Corps and the 1st and 2nd Divisions; it held III and IV Corps whole, Da Nang and the Quang Nam lowlands, and the coast from Qui Nhon south (EV000-94, EV000-95) [X-011]. Its general reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, was at Da Nang, five hundred kilometres from the capital the next offensive would aim at. On 6 May Vien sent the Dalat study back to the palace with a covering note that it was now a plan for the defence of Saigon, and Thieu, who had refused it in March, took its method this time [X-011].

Operation SOUTHERN WALL, the evacuation of Da Nang, ran from 20 to 31 May under a plan the Joint General Staff staffed for a month from the Dalat study's annex on movement. The families went first, from 8 May, by the Vietnamese Navy's LSTs, the Saigon coasters and two chartered American freighters; the territorials of Quang Nam held the Thu Bon and Dien Ban for ten days while the 3rd Division, the Marines and the Airborne thinned the perimeter brigade by brigade and embarked at Tien Sa and the Han River docks [X-011]. The PAVN 2nd Corps, which had been ordered to take the city before the northeast monsoon and was not ready to assault it until June, pressed the perimeter with the 304th and 325th Divisions from 24 May and took the airfield on the 30th, as the last Marine battalion went aboard [X-011]. About 70,000 soldiers and about 250,000 civilians were lifted, against history's chaos at the same docks in March (EV000-94); about 4,000 men of the rear guard and the territorials were lost [X-011]. By the end of June the Airborne and Marine Divisions were rebuilding at Vung Tau and Long Binh as the reserve the Dalat study had asked for in November 1974 (C011-3).

The coast went as the season allowed. Qui Nhon was given up on 12 June after the 22nd Division, which had held it since March, was outflanked by the 3rd Division and the 968th coming down Route 19; the division came out by sea, as it had historically in April, with about two-thirds of its battalions [H] [X-011]. Tuy Hoa fell in July. The line on the central coast stood at the Khanh Hoa border north of Nha Trang through the rains, held by the 22nd Division's survivors, the 3rd Airborne Brigade and the territorials of Khanh Hoa and Ninh Thuan [X-011].

In Hanoi the Politburo spent the rains preparing the offensive it had put off. The 1st Corps reached its assembly areas north of Saigon by the strategic road in June, the pipeline was extended to Loc Ninh, and the Soviet deliveries of 1975, which had been planned for a campaign ending in 1976, were brought forward: the General Staff asked Moscow in May for the 130mm ammunition and the tank replacements of 1976 to be shipped in the autumn of 1975, and Moscow, which had its own reasons to see the war ended before the American election year, shipped most of them [X-011]. Beijing, which had no wish to see Hanoi's victory hastened by Soviet guns, sent rice and trucks and no more [X-011]. The resolution of October 1975 set the general offensive on Saigon for the first months of 1976, with five corps, as soon as the ground in the eastern provinces was dry [X-011].

In Saigon and Washington the rains were spent on money and on the families. The fiscal 1976 appropriation was voted in November at about $800 million, less than the Republic needed to replace what it had lost in the spring and more than it would have received from a Congress that had seen it collapse; the RVNAF's ration in the autumn of 1975 was near the 1974 level, and the Bien Hoa arsenal ran three shifts [X-011]. The soldiers' families from I and II Corps, about 400,000 people, were in camps around Saigon, Vung Tau and Cam Ranh; the government paid the dependants' allowance in rice from the Delta's 1975 crop, which the Republic still held and which was about history's 1974 harvest, and the piaster lost about a third of its value between April and December [X-011]. Desertion in the units whose families were in the camps was about twice the rate in the units whose families had stayed in III and IV Corps [X-011]. At the end of 1975 the Republic of Vietnam stood, smaller by half its territory and a third of its army, with its reserve rebuilt, its capital unattacked and an enemy of five corps preparing to attack it in the dry season.

## P6 · How the phase ends
In this war Phase VI does not end with the fall of Saigon. On 31 December 1975 the Republic of Vietnam holds III and IV Corps, the coast from Nha Trang south and about half the population of 1974; the PAVN holds the Highlands, I Corps and the central coast north of Khanh Hoa, and five corps are assembled for an offensive on the capital in the first months of 1976 (EV011-3) [X-011]. That is a conclusion and not an assumption. The date of 30 April 1975 was set in Hanoi by a resolution to finish before the rains, and the resolution was made possible by the double collapse of I and II Corps in the second half of March; in this war Thieu's decision of 11–14 March went the other way, the Highlands were lost in a five-week siege instead of a ten-day rout, Hue in an ordered withdrawal on 18 April instead of a collapse on 25 March, and the Politburo, without a collapse to resolve on, put Saigon back to the dry season (EV000-94, EV000-95, EVMT01-4) [X-011]. The mechanism was political and at the top: a President in Washington who did not fall, an appropriation near the 1974 level, and a President in Saigon who believed, on that evidence, that he need not give anything up (F011-20, F011-21). It bought a season, and it was bought at a price. The refusal of the Dalat study cost the Republic half its Highland force and the 2nd Division, which a planned withdrawal in the dry season would have saved, and its reserve spent the spring at Da Nang, five hundred kilometres from the capital, until SOUTHERN WALL brought it home in May.
