# Memorandum 011 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier
<!-- audit -->
*Audit — Memo 011, Part 1/3 · v1.0.6 · provisions: 3 (P011a, P011b, P011c) · dossier fields: 16/16, 16/16, 16/16 (floor 16) · tagged figures: 217 · untagged warnings: 0 · sources: 12 · designations: ~21 (floor 20) · variables pushed: 9 · structural deviation: none*

This grant gives me no weapon and no man. It gives me time. Every provision before it has added something to the force in the field. This one removes three things from the war at home, the three that history says cost the war most of its American patience: the burglary that brought down the President who signed the peace, a quarter of the induction notices that took sons who did not want to go, and the morning in Son My that became the war's emblem. I have argued for ten years that the clock is the enemy I cannot fight. Washington has now reached into the clock itself. I read the grant as three provisions, because the three acts work on different parts of American politics, at different dates, through different people: no Watergate (P011a), volunteers in place of a quarter of the draft (P011b), and no My Lai (P011c).

The grant was put to the authority who sent it, and the rulings stand as given [U]. The package is the name for the three items and adds nothing of its own. Watergate is removed at its root: the break-in never happens. The volunteers are the same men who were drafted historically. A quarter of them walked into the recruiting office before the notice came, so the force is no larger, and the draft calls are a quarter smaller. My Lai is removed as an isolated event, stipulated, with no cause found for it inside the war, and every other crime of this war stays where history put it.

One warning governs all three dossiers. Each provision subtracts something, and a subtraction can be overrated as easily as an addition. Removing a scandal does not add votes for a war that Americans had already decided was a mistake. On Gallup's question, 60% said so in 1971 and the same 60% said so in 1973, before Watergate's worst months [S1]. The question I have to answer for each provision is not whether America would have liked the war better. It is how much more time, money and authority Washington would have granted, to whom, and when.

## §1.1 No Watergate: the break-in never happens — P011a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** The men of the Committee for the Re-election of the President do not break into the Democratic National Committee's offices in the Watergate building in Washington. They do not do it on the night of 28 May 1972, the first entry, when telephones were bugged [S10]. They do not go back on the night of 17 June, when five burglars were arrested at 2:30 in the morning [S10]. With no arrests there is no trail of money to the re-election committee and no cover-up to conduct. That removes the Senate Watergate Committee's televised hearings, which history opened on 17 May 1973 [S10], and the special prosecutor. It removes the Saturday Night Massacre of 20 October 1973 [S10], the tapes, the House Judiciary Committee's articles of impeachment of 27–30 July 1974 [S10], the resignation of 9 August 1974 [S10] and the pardon of 8 September [S10]. Richard Nixon serves his second term to its end, 20 January 1977 [D].

**What it is not.** The grant removes the break-in, not the man or the White House that ordered it [U]. The abuses that came before May 1972 stay in the war: the wiretaps on 13 officials and four journalists ordered in May 1969 after the *New York Times* reported the Cambodian bombing, and the "plumbers" unit formed after the Pentagon Papers in June 1971 [H]. What changes is that no Watergate investigation ever exposes them. They stay buried, as many abuses of power by many administrations have stayed buried, and the price of that is not mine to weigh. I record it and move on.

**Designation, manufacturer, quantity.** There is no manufacturer. The "quantity" is two break-ins removed [U], and with them the whole sequence that history built on them. The real-world date the provision rests on is the night of 28 May 1972 [S10], and I take that as its arrival date. Before it, nothing in the war is different.

**Unit organisation in theater.** None. The provision acts in Washington, on three bodies whose tolerance for this war I now have to track separately: the administration, which decides what to ask for and whether to use force; the Congress, which decides what to pay for and what to forbid; and the public, which decides who sits in both. It reaches Saigon through the President's letters to Thieu, and it reaches Hanoi through the Politburo's reading of American power.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The provision consumes no personnel spaces [U]. No troop ceiling is set in this exercise (CONFIG), and none would be touched in any case. The people the provision acts on are about a dozen men. They are the President; his chief of staff H. R. Haldeman and his domestic adviser John Ehrlichman; his counsel John Dean; his Attorney General John Mitchell, who ran the re-election committee; and the national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, whose diplomacy in 1973–74 history conducted for a President who was steadily losing his power [H]. Historically every one of the first five was gone by May 1973 or caught up in the scandal, and Kissinger worked for a failing President [H]. Under this provision they stay, or leave only for ordinary reasons [E: no Watergate departures]. The personnel effect that matters is at the top of the American government and nowhere else.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The provision gives the President back the instruments history took from him in 1973–74. None of them is new. They are the ordinary powers of a re-elected President whose party has not deserted him.

**The veto, made credible.** In June 1973, history's Congress forced the issue of the Cambodian bombing by attaching a cut-off to must-pass appropriations, and the Case–Church amendment passed the House 325–86 and the Senate 73–16 [S6]. Those are veto-proof margins, and they were reached at a moment when, by the record, Republicans were relenting on support for South Vietnam under Watergate's pressure [S6]. Without Watergate, the President's veto is the same instrument. What changes is the number of Republicans willing to stand behind it.

**The enforcement threat.** On 14 November 1972 the President wrote Thieu that the United States "will react very strongly and rapidly to any violation of the agreement", and both sides took that and the assurances that followed to mean the B-52s would return [S5]. The instrument behind the threat is the bomber force: the B-52D and B-52G of the Strategic Air Command at Andersen and U-Tapao, the F-4E, A-7D and F-111A of the Seventh and Thirteenth Air Forces in Thailand, and the AC-130 gunships. They were the force of Linebacker II in December 1972 [H]. The threat is only as good as the President's freedom to use it. History says Watergate weakened and distracted Nixon, and that the commitments were not upheld [S5].

**The appropriation request.** A President asks for aid; Congress grants it. In April 1975 history's President Ford asked a joint session for $722 million in military and $250 million in economic and humanitarian aid for South Vietnam [S9], an unelected President asking a Congress elected in the Watergate autumn. Under this provision the same request comes from Nixon, to a Congress elected in a normal sixth-year midterm, and it comes earlier, because a President with his authority intact asks before the collapse rather than during it [E: D5].

**What it cannot compel.** The President cannot repeal a law Congress passed by veto-proof margins. He cannot spend money Congress has not appropriated. He cannot make the public believe the war is worth fighting again. The provision restores a President's ordinary power. It does not give him extraordinary power.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what the provision lets each side learn, and how reliably.*

**What Washington learns.** A President not under siege reads the war's cables himself, and his national security staff is not partly taken up with his defence. Historically, from the spring of 1973, the Situation Room's attention competed with the hearings, the tapes and the special prosecutor [E: S5's "weakened and distracted"]. Under this provision the reports from the Defense Attaché Office in Saigon on the North's road-building, its fuel pipeline into the South and its new corps reach a President who has time to act on them [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

**What Hanoi learns.** This is the input that matters most. The Politburo watched American politics closely and drew conclusions from it. The 21st Plenum of October 1973 returned to revolutionary violence [H]. In January 1975 the North tested whether the United States would respond to the seizure of a province, and when it did not, it committed everything [H]. Under this provision the North reads a President at the height of his electoral authority, who bombed Hanoi in December 1972 and whose word to Thieu is on paper. Hanoi does not see a President fighting impeachment. The North's sensors are the American press, the Congress and the diplomats in Paris, and every one of them would now tell it a different and more dangerous story [E: the Politburo's reading of US politics in the chapters' P6 events].

**What Saigon learns.** Thieu kept the President's letters and believed them [H]. Here, nothing in 1973–74 shows him that the man who wrote them can no longer keep them. That is not entirely to the good, and I return to it in D11.

**Reliability.** The inputs are good on intentions and poor on capability. Hanoi will learn quickly that the President is strong. It will take it longer to learn that Case–Church binds him anyway.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity: what the provision delivers, when, and its rate limit.*

The provision delivers its effect in three windows, and the effect is different in each.

**May 1972 – May 1973: almost nothing.** History's Watergate was a minor story through the 1972 campaign. Nixon was re-elected on 7 November 1972 with the largest plurality in American history [S10]. The Easter Offensive, Linebacker, the October agreement, Linebacker II and the Paris Agreement all ran their course before the scandal had any grip on policy [E: S10 dates against the P5 and P6 chapters]. The provision changes nothing in this window that I can identify.

**May 1973 – August 1974: the President's authority intact while Congress acts anyway.** This is the window that history's Case–Church amendment falls into, signed 1 July 1973 and binding from 15 August [S6]. My judgment is that some version of it still passes without Watergate. The war was unpopular: 60% called it a mistake in 1973 [S1]. The prisoners were home, the troops were out, and the margins in June were veto-proof even before Republican defections are subtracted [S6]. What the provision changes is the shape of the law and what follows it. I put the chance that the 15 August cut-off survives in substantially history's form at about 60–75% [X-011]. The remaining chance is a weaker law, with a later date or an exception for responding to a major violation [X-011]. The War Powers Resolution, which history passed over a veto on 7 November 1973, eighteen days after the Saturday Night Massacre [H], is the place where the provision bites hardest. Without the massacre I judge the override about as likely to fail as to succeed [X-011].

**August 1974 – April 1975: the money and the threat.** History's fiscal 1975 appropriation for the RVNAF fell to about $700 million from about $1.1 billion the year before [H]. The 1974 midterms gave the Democrats 49 House seats and 5 Senate seats [S4]. A sixth-year midterm in a recession year would have cost the President's party seats anyway. I judge the swing without Watergate at about 15–30 House seats [X-011]. On that Congress and with that President, I judge the fiscal 1975 appropriation at about $850 million – $1.1 billion rather than $700 million [X-011]. That is a judgment about a vote that never happened, and it carries the tag forever. The enforcement threat in the spring of 1975 is the other half: a President who has not resigned, facing the fall of Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, with or without the 15 August prohibition.

**Rate limit.** The provision's capacity is capped by Congress's own appetite. It restores a President. It does not restore 1965.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

The chain runs from the President through Kissinger and Ambassador Graham Martin to Thieu, and from the Secretary of Defense through the Defense Attaché Office under Major General John Murray to the Joint General Staff [H]. Historically that chain went on functioning through 1973–74, but the top of it was failing. By the summer of 1974 Thieu was holding letters from a man who no longer held the office [H]. Under this provision the letters and the man stay together. When Murray warned, as history records, that cuts in support could roughly be equated with loss of territory [H], his warning goes to a President who can still carry it to Congress himself.

The RVNAF talks to no new system. What changes for the Joint General Staff is its planning assumption. A staff that believes the B-52s may return plans its defence differently from one that knows they will not, and how that difference is managed is the subject of D11.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions: where the provision acts and what it needs to work.*

**Reach.** Washington only, from 28 May 1972. It does not reach the battlefield in 1972. It does not reach the countryside in any year. It reaches the RVNAF only through the appropriation and the enforcement threat, and both pass through a Congress the provision changes only partly.

**Preconditions.** The provision needs three things to matter. First, a war still in progress after mid-1973: the chapters hold one, the ceasefire that never held and the war of 1974 [H]. Second, a Congress that would have voted differently without the scandal, which is plausible at the margin and doubtful at the core, since the votes on Case–Church preceded Watergate's worst months [S6] [S10]. Third, a President willing to spend his restored authority on Saigon rather than on everything else a second term wants. That third condition is the one I am least sure of. A Nixon who had won his peace and brought the prisoners home had every political reason to call the war finished. Whether he would have bombed again in the spring of 1975, against the Case–Church prohibition or by asking Congress to lift it, is the question this provision raises and cannot answer by itself.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The provision has no footprint of its own. It acts on the RVNAF's logistics only through money. The chapters record the 1974 ration: on 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel to all ARVN units were cut for budget reasons, the ARVN rationed artillery rounds, and the VNAF grounded aircraft and helicopters for lack of fuel and parts [H]. An appropriation of $850 million – $1.1 billion against history's $700 million [X-011] is the difference between that ration and a looser one. It is not the difference between rationing and plenty. In 1974–75 the world oil shock also raised what every gallon cost, and the provision does nothing about that [H].

What the money buys has to be shipped through Saigon, Cam Ranh Bay and Da Nang and distributed by an RVNAF whose own logistics system carries it, since the American contractors and advisers have largely gone by 1973 [H]. Each extra dollar is worth less than a dollar of 1972, because the system that turns it into rounds at the gun line is thinner.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

Nothing to transfer. The provision is the American political system behaving differently. What it gives the RVNAF is the inheritance of Vietnamization on better terms: more of the ammunition, fuel and spares the RVNAF was built to consume, and a longer belief that the air power it was built around might return. The second is double-edged. An army that expects the B-52s fights 1974 as an army that still has a reserve, and it may delay the hard reorganisation, a shorter line and a smaller country, that only an army without one undertakes. The Republic's own planners wrote such a defence in 1974, and the chapters record that the President took its conclusion and not its method in March 1975 [H]. A Thieu who still believes in the letters is less likely to take even the conclusion [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

### D10 · Employment envelope

The provision performs to its full value under four conditions. The war must still be undecided in 1973–75, which in the chapters it is. Congress must be closely enough divided that a smaller midterm swing and a stronger President change votes on aid. Hanoi must still be weighing whether America will return when it plans its offensives. And the President must want to spend his authority on the war.

It collapses toward nothing if the President, having had his peace and his prisoners, chooses not to spend his capital on Saigon. It collapses too if the Case–Church prohibition stands in its historical form and no President will defy it. It collapses if the oil shock and the 1974 recession make any foreign aid unpopular whoever sits in the White House. And it collapses if the North's calendar for 1975 is driven by the balance of forces in the South rather than by its reading of Washington.

Tempo is not the question here. The question is duration. The provision runs for the whole second term. Its value peaks in two moments: the summer of 1974, when the fiscal 1975 appropriation is voted, and March–April 1975, when the North commits.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The first failure: the law stands anyway.** Case–Church was passed by margins Watergate did not create [S6]. If it stands in its historical form, the President's restored authority meets a statute he cannot lawfully break. Enforcement by bombing then needs a new vote in a Congress that has just ended the war. This is the most likely partial failure, and I give it more weight than any other.

**The second failure: the President chooses not to.** A second-term President with a peace to his name has many other uses for his capital. These include détente with Moscow, the opening to China, the oil crisis and a recession. The threat was made in 1972 to get Thieu to sign. Whether Nixon would have made it good in 1975, against Congress and against his own interest, is a judgment about one man that no document settles.

**The third failure: Saigon is made complacent.** This is the failure the provision causes rather than fails to prevent. A Thieu who believes in the letters and an RVNAF that plans for American air power may do less of what an army that knows it is alone must do. They may hold every province, refuse to trade territory for a shorter line, and keep the general reserve committed in I Corps. If the bombers do not come, the 1975 collapse comes from a worse plan.

**What a partial failure looks like.** The most probable outcome is somewhere between the failures. A modestly larger appropriation is likely, the B-52s are probably still barred by law, and the threat is a little more credible in Hanoi in 1974 and a good deal less credible by March 1975. That moves the money and the date. It does not obviously move the outcome.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The provision is at its strongest the day it begins, because a President's authority wears with time and is not renewed. Its month-one value is nil: in June 1972 Watergate cost nothing yet. Its peak runs from mid-1973 to the end of 1974, when history's President was collapsing and this one is not. Its value then declines steadily into 1976–77, because a second-term President becomes a lame duck as the 1976 campaign begins and his successor is chosen. What declines first is the enforcement threat. A threat made in January 1973 is fresh, and the same threat in April 1975, after two years in which the bombers did not return to punish the war of 1974, is stale [E: judgment from the record cited in this field]. What lasts longest is the appropriation, because an aid programme once voted creates its own constituency [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is the historical American political position of 1973–75, and the margins are judgments, not measurements. Against history's fiscal 1975 military appropriation of about $700 million [H], the provision's best estimate is about $850 million – $1.1 billion, a margin of about 20–55% [X-011]. Against history's midterm swing of 49 House and 5 Senate seats [S4], about 15–30 House seats [X-011]. Against history's President, whose approval stood at 24% in early August 1974, with 57% saying his conduct warranted removal [S11], a President whose approval falls only as second-term approvals ordinarily fall [E: judgment from the record cited in this field]. Against history's War Powers override, a coin toss [X-011]. Against the Case–Church amendment, probably no change in its substance [X-011].

The comparative benchmark I care about most is Hanoi's reading. Here I cannot give a number. I can only say the margin is real and smaller than the story of Watergate makes it sound.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P011a -->
- It does not make the war popular. Gallup's "mistake" share was about 60% in 1971 and in 1973, before Watergate's worst months [S1]; the provision acts on the President's authority and the size of the 1974 midterm swing, not on opinion of the war.
- It probably does not repeal Case–Church. The amendment passed 325–86 and 73–16 in June 1973 [S6], veto-proof margins reached before the Saturday Night Massacre of 20 October 1973 [S10]; I judge a 60–75% chance the 15 August 1973 cut-off survives in substantially its historical form [X-011].
- It does not restore American combat power. The B-52s, fighter-bombers and gunships of 1972 are withdrawn on the historical timetable; the provision changes whether a President could send them back, not whether they are there.
- It changes nothing before May 1973: the 1972 election, the Easter Offensive, Linebacker and Linebacker II and the Paris Agreement run as history ran them.
- It reaches the battlefield only through money and Hanoi's reading of American will; it touches no hamlet, no regiment and no road.
- It may make Saigon complacent: a Thieu who still believes the President's letters is less likely to plan a defence without the bombers.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P011a -->
It denies Hanoi the evidence of American paralysis in 1973–74: a President fighting impeachment, then resigning, and a successor without a mandate facing a Congress elected in the scandal's wake. It does not deny the North the Case–Church prohibition, which Hanoi can read in the statute book whoever is President.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P011a -->
This is a real provision, and it is smaller than its name. Removing Watergate keeps a re-elected President in office with his authority intact through the two years when the Republic was left to fight alone. On my best judgment, that is worth a fiscal 1975 military appropriation of perhaps $850 million – $1.1 billion instead of $700 million, a midterm swing of 15–30 House seats instead of 49, and an enforcement threat Hanoi has to take more seriously in 1974 [X-011]. It is probably not worth the repeal of Case–Church, which Congress passed by margins Watergate did not create, and it does not change the American public's settled view that the war was a mistake. Its largest effect is on the North's calendar, which in 1975 tested whether America would answer the fall of a province. Whether a strong Nixon would have answered it against the law is the question that decides what this provision is worth. I cannot answer it from the record, and I will not pretend to.

## §1.2 Volunteers in place of a quarter of the draft — P011b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** Of the men the Selective Service System inducted into the armed forces from the opening of the campaign to the end of inductions, one in four instead walks into a recruiting office before his notice comes and signs an enlistment contract, the DD Form 4, of his own will [U]. The same men serve. They serve in the same services, the same specialties and the same units, on the same terms and the same twelve-month tours in Vietnam [U]. What changes is how they came, and therefore how many notices the local boards had to send. The draft calls are a quarter smaller in every month of every year [D].

**Quantity.** The Selective Service System gives the Vietnam War's inductions as 1,857,304 men from August 1964 to February 1973 [S2]. A quarter is about 464,000 men who now come as volunteers [D]. Year by year, the provision cuts the induction calls as follows [D]:

| Year | Historical inductions [S2] | Inductions under the provision [D] |
|---|---|---|
| 1964 | 112,386 | about 84,000 |
| 1965 | 230,991 | about 173,000 |
| 1966 | 382,010 | about 287,000 |
| 1967 | 228,263 | about 171,000 |
| 1968 | 296,406 | about 222,000 |
| 1969 | 283,586 | about 213,000 |
| 1970 | 162,746 | about 122,000 |
| 1971 | 94,092 | about 71,000 |
| 1972 | 49,514 | about 37,000 |

In the peak year, 1966, the boards' monthly average falls from about 31,800 notices to about 23,900 [D].

**Who they are.** By the Senate Armed Services Committee's figures as cited, about 648,500 draftees served in South Vietnam from January 1965 to May 1973 [S12]. Under the provision about 162,000 of those men are volunteers [D]. Almost all of them are in the Army, because the Marine Corps took few draftees and the Navy and Air Force none [S3]. Most of them are in the Army's infantry, because that is where the draftees went [E: the Army's assignment of draftees to the combat arms; S3's draftee share of Army enlisted dead].

**Unit organisation.** None of its own. The provision is a change in the status of about one draftee in four, inside every Army rifle company from the 1st Infantry Division at Lai Khe to the 23rd (Americal) Infantry Division at Chu Lai.

**Reading stated.** I take "volunteers" to mean men who chose to serve, not men who enlisted to escape a worse assignment. History's "draft-motivated" volunteers, men who enlisted because the draft was coming and chose their service and specialty to stay out of the infantry, are already counted among history's volunteers. These new volunteers enlisted for the same jobs the draft would have given them, and that is the reading the user's ruling sets [U].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The provision consumes no personnel spaces [U]. The force is the size history made it, and no troop ceiling is set in this exercise (CONFIG). The people the provision acts on are about 464,000 men and their families [D]. It also acts on the roughly 4,000 local boards of the Selective Service System, whose clerks and volunteer members sent the notices [E: the Selective Service System's local-board structure of the 1960s]. Their workload falls by a quarter. Their visibility in every county falls with it.

The provision does not change the term of service. History's draftee served two years, and a Regular Army volunteer usually enlisted for three [E: the period's enlistment terms]. Under the user's reading the same men serve on the same terms [U]. If the volunteers served three-year enlistments instead, the Army would need about a third fewer of them each year, and the draft calls would fall by more than a quarter. I state this and do not take it. D14 records it.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The provision gives no new authority to anyone. It reduces the use of the most coercive authority the American government exercised during this war: the power to order a man to report for induction and to prosecute him if he did not.

That power had consequences the record counts. About 210,000 men were formally accused of draft offences, and about 570,000 were classed as draft offenders [S7]. Of those, 8,750 were convicted and 3,250 jailed [S7]. Somewhere between 30,000 and 100,000 left the country, most of them for Canada [S7]. More than half of the 27 million men eligible were deferred, exempted or disqualified [S7], and the deferments, for college, for marriage, for some occupations, were the system's deepest unfairness.

The provision does not touch the deferments. It does not touch the men who refused, because the new volunteers come from among those who went. What it does is cut by a quarter the number of times the instrument was used. That is about 464,000 fewer orders to report [D].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what the provision changes in what each side learns.*

**What America learns.** The draft carried the war into every family with a son of nineteen. A quarter fewer notices means a quarter fewer families who learn about the war that way. In a country where, about six months into the large-scale commitment, 60% said the war was not a mistake, and where that share fell to 44% by October 1967 [S1], the draft was one of the main ways the war reached people who would not otherwise have thought about it.

**What the casualty lists say.** Of all the war's dead, almost 70% were volunteers [S3]. Of the enlisted dead, 63.3% were [S3]. In the Army, almost half the enlisted dead were volunteers and the rest were draftees [S3]. Under the provision a quarter of those draftee dead are volunteers, which changes the status of about one in thirteen of all the dead [D]. It is not a change in how many die. It is a change in how many families buried a son the government had taken against his will.

**What the enemy learns.** Hanoi watched the American antiwar movement and drew hope from it [H]. The draft resistance was one of its most visible faces: the card burnings, the trials, the young men in Canada. With a quarter fewer notices there are fewer resisters to count, though the record does not let me say how many fewer, because the resisters were not drawn evenly from the men who were called [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

**Reliability.** The provision acts on an input I cannot measure directly: how much of the war's unpopularity came from the draft itself, as against the casualties, the cost and the lack of a visible end. My judgment is that the draft was the sharpest single grievance and not the largest.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

The provision's throughput is fixed by the table in D1: about 464,000 men across the war, at a rate that follows the draft calls [D]. Its effect has three channels.

**The domestic channel.** Fewer notices mean fewer families coerced, fewer men for the resistance to organise, fewer trials and fewer young men abroad. That last effect is smaller than the reduction in notices, because the men who would have resisted are disproportionately among those still drafted, not among the new volunteers [E: judgment from the record cited in this field]. My estimate is that draft resistance falls by something less than a quarter: perhaps 10–20% fewer men accused of draft offences [X-011].

**The force channel.** A quarter of the Army's draftees in Vietnam now chose to be there. History's draftees were not worse soldiers on average, but in 1969–71, when the Army's indiscipline peaked and the war's purpose had gone, the man who had been taken against his will was more likely to be the man who refused an order, used heroin or turned on his own officers [E: the Army's own analysis of indiscipline by entry status is not in my sources]. I judge the provision a modest improvement in unit quality concentrated in those years [X-011].

**The political channel.** The draft's politics bit hardest in the extension fights, in 1967 and 1971, and in the lottery of December 1969 [H]. The provision does not abolish the draft. It makes it smaller in every year, and so it takes some of the heat out of every fight about it.

**Rate limit.** The provision's effect is capped by the casualties. A quarter fewer draftees does not change the number of the dead, and the dead drove opinion more than the notices did.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

The chain is the Selective Service System's: the national headquarters under General Lewis Hershey and, from 1970, Curtis Tarr, then state headquarters, then local boards, then the notice [H]. The provision acts at the bottom of that chain. It also acts at the recruiting office, where the Army's recruiters sign about 464,000 more men over the war than history's did [D]. The Army's recruiting command needs more recruiters to do this: perhaps 5–10% more in the peak years [E: recruiter workload proportional to volunteers signed]. Those recruiters serve in the United States and spend no spaces in theater.

The provision has no interface with the RVNAF. It does not change South Vietnam's own conscription, which drafted its men under its own laws and had its own resistance [H].

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** The United States from January 1964 to the end of inductions in 1973, and from there into every Army unit in Vietnam where a draftee served. It does not reach the Marine Corps, the Navy or the Air Force, whose enlisted men were already volunteers [S3]. It does not reach the National Guard and the reserves, which history largely kept out of the war and where many draft-motivated volunteers went [H].

**Preconditions.** The provision needs a supply of willing men. The user stipulates that it exists [U]. It also needs the Army to take them in the infantry, where most of them would historically have been drafted, rather than in the specialties volunteers usually chose. The user's reading puts them in the same jobs, and I hold to that. And it needs the war to go on long enough for the draft calls to matter, which it does.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The provision costs almost nothing in money. It may cost something in recruiting: about 464,000 more men signed by recruiters over nine years [D], at the same pay and under the same contracts as other volunteers. It changes nothing in theater: the men eat the same rations, use the same M16A1 rifle, M60 machine gun, M79 grenade launcher and AN/PRC-25 radio, ride the same UH-1H and CH-47, and come home on the same day. It does not need anything RVNAF cannot supply, because it has nothing to do with the RVNAF.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

Nothing to transfer. The provision is an American institution behaving differently. There is a lesson the Republic of Vietnam might have taken: that the war's soldiers, given a reason, would come. But the Republic's own manpower problem was different and harder. It mobilised a much larger share of its men under a general mobilisation law from June 1968, against an enemy that also conscripted in the same villages [H]. A volunteer share in the ARVN depended on pay, posting near home and a family that could live, which the RVNAF could not give on the scale needed [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

### D10 · Employment envelope

The provision performs to its full value when the draft is large and the war is unpopular. That means 1966–69, when the calls ran above 200,000 a year [S2] and opinion turned. It performs least in 1964, when the war was small and the draft had not yet become a grievance, and in 1971–72, when the calls had fallen below 100,000 a year [S2] and the lottery had already removed the worst of the uncertainty.

It collapses toward nothing if the war's unpopularity was driven by the casualties and the lack of progress rather than by the draft. That is a real possibility, and it is the reading I give the most weight to.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The first failure: the casualties still decide.** The provision changes who came, not who died. If the turn in opinion was driven by the dead and by Tet, as I think it largely was, the provision buys a smaller share of tolerance than its size suggests.

**The second failure: the resisters are still drafted.** The men who would have resisted are not drawn evenly from the men who were called. A quarter fewer notices could mean much less than a quarter less resistance, if the new volunteers were disproportionately the men who would have gone anyway.

**The third failure: the deferments remain.** The draft's deepest unfairness was who was not called: the college men, the married men, the men with a doctor who would write a letter. The provision does not touch that. The working-class and minority families who carried the draft's burden still carry three-quarters of it.

**What a partial failure looks like.** A smaller and less visible draft that still provokes the same campus protests, because the students were protesting the war and their own deferments' fragility, not the number of notices sent.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The provision does not degrade. It is a constant proportion of a draft whose size varies. Its effect is strongest in 1966–69, when the calls were largest [S2], and falls with them after 1970. What declines first is its political value. After the lottery of December 1969 and the falling calls of 1970–72 [S2], the draft was already shrinking as a grievance, and the provision's extra cut matters less each year.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is history's draft. Against history's 1,857,304 inductions, the provision makes about 1,393,000 [D]. Against history's peak year of 382,010 inductions in 1966, about 287,000 [D]. Against history's roughly 30% draftee share of the war's dead [S3], about 22–23% [D]. Against history's 210,000 accused of draft offences [S7], perhaps 170,000–190,000 [X-011].

The benchmark in the field is the Army rifle company of 1969–71. I cannot give a number for how much better a company is when one draftee in four has become a volunteer. I judge it modest and real [X-011].

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P011b -->
- It does not make the force larger or the war cheaper in men: the same men serve, in the same jobs, on the same terms and tours [U]. If the volunteers had signed three-year Regular Army enlistments instead of serving two years as draftees, the Army would have needed fewer men and the draft calls would have fallen by more than a quarter; the ruling does not take that reading, and I do not.
- It does not reduce casualties: about 30% of the war's dead were draftees [S3]; under the provision a quarter of them, about one in thirteen of all the dead, die as volunteers instead [D], and the number of the dead is unchanged.
- It does not touch the deferments, which spared more than half of the 27 million eligible [S7], or the class and race of who was drafted.
- It does not end draft resistance: the men who would have resisted are disproportionately among those still drafted; I judge resistance falls by perhaps 10–20%, not 25% [X-011].
- It does not reach the Marine Corps, Navy or Air Force, whose enlisted men were already volunteers [S3], or the RVNAF.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P011b -->
It denies Hanoi a quarter of the most visible face of the American antiwar movement: the induction notices, the draft trials and the young men in Canada. It denies the Front nothing in the South.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P011b -->
This is a modest provision with a real effect. A quarter fewer induction notices, about 464,000 fewer over the war [D], takes some heat out of the war's sharpest domestic grievance in every year from 1965 to 1972. It improves the morale of Army rifle companies at the margin, most of all in the bad years of 1969–71, and it changes the status of about one in thirteen of the dead [D]. It does not change the number of the dead, the deferments that made the draft unjust, or the casualty lists and the absence of victory that turned the country against the war. I judge it a small gain in the public's tolerance and a small gain in unit quality [X-011]. That gain would buy a few months of patience at the margins, not a different war at home. If the turn of opinion was driven more by the draft than I think, it buys more. If it was driven by the dead, as I think it largely was, it buys less.
<!-- /card -->

## §1.3 No My Lai — P011c

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read as an institutional provision: D3 is read as instruments and authorities, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput and capacity, D6 as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF, D7 as reach and security preconditions.*

**What it is.** On the morning of 16 March 1968, Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Infantry Brigade of the Americal Division, operating under Task Force Barker, is landed by helicopter at the hamlet of My Lai 4 in Son My village, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province, and it does not kill the people it finds there [H]. Company B, 4th Battalion, 3rd Infantry does not kill the people of My Khe [H]. The provision is stipulated: no cause inside the war is given, and nothing else about the brigade, the division or the command climate changes [U]. The companies search the hamlets, find no enemy, and leave, as companies did in Quang Ngai on many mornings that year [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

**Quantity.** One event removed [U]. Between 347 people, the US Army's figure, and 504, the Vietnamese figure, live [H]: mostly old men, women and children. With the killing gone, everything that followed from it goes too. There is no false report of 128 Viet Cong killed and no brigade investigation to concede about 20 accidental civilian deaths [H]. There is no year and a half of cover-up, no Ronald Ridenhour letters to Congress in March 1969, no Seymour Hersh report of 13 November 1969, and no Peers inquiry [H]. There is no court-martial at Fort Benning convicting Lieutenant William Calley on 29 March 1971 [H].

**What it is not.** The provision is isolated by ruling [U]. Every other killing of civilians by American, Korean, South Vietnamese or enemy troops in this war stays where history put it. The Americal Division is the same division, assembled hastily, in the same province, under the same measure of success [H]. The provision removes one morning. It does not remove the conditions that produced it, and I will not pretend that it does.

**Unit organisation.** None. The provision acts on one company on one morning, and on the American public's knowledge of the war from November 1969.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The provision consumes no personnel spaces [U]. The people it acts on are the villagers of Son My, who live, and the men of Charlie Company, who do not carry what they did for the rest of their lives. It also acts on the investigators, prosecutors and officers of the Peers inquiry, whose work never begins [H], and on the generals of the Americal Division, whose careers are not ended by the cover-up [H]. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson and his crew, who landed their OH-23 between the soldiers and the villagers [H], fly an ordinary mission that morning.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.* The provision takes away an instrument, and it is the opponents of the war who lose it. My Lai was the most powerful single piece of evidence that the war's methods produced crimes. It gave the antiwar movement its emblem, the press its proof, and Congress a reason to question what MACV reported. It gave Hanoi's propaganda its best material. All of that goes.

It also takes away an instrument from the Army itself. The Peers inquiry was the Army's own investigation of how a massacre was committed and concealed. Its findings forced a reckoning with the command climate, the body count and the reporting chain that history's Army undertook, imperfectly, in the 1970s [H]. Without My Lai there is no Peers inquiry. That is a loss to the institution, and I take it seriously in D11.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.*

**What America learns.** In November 1969, history's public learned that American soldiers had killed hundreds of civilians in a morning and that their commanders had concealed it for a year and a half [H]. Under this provision the public does not learn it, because it does not happen. What the public still learns, from other incidents, from the press and from the soldiers who come home, is that the war is fought in villages and that villagers die in it. That knowledge does not depend on My Lai.

**What the public thought of what it learned.** The record of opinion is not what the story of My Lai suggests. In April 1971, when the Calley verdict came, 11% of Americans approved it. 56% disapproved because they thought others shared the responsibility, 70% thought Calley a scapegoat, and 79% thought the life sentence too harsh [S8]. Mass opinion did not turn against the war because of My Lai. It turned against the trial of one lieutenant for a war it was already tired of. Where My Lai did its damage was in the press, the universities, the Congress and the moral case against the war: among the people who shaped opinion more than among the people who answered polls.

**What MACV learns.** Here the provision costs me something. My Lai was concealed by the chain of command for a year and a half [H]. Its exposure told me, and history's commander, that the reporting system could hide a massacre. Without it, I learn that lesson later or not at all.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

The provision's direct effect is between 347 and 504 lives [H]. Against the civilian dead of the war, which ran into the hundreds of thousands on every count, that is a trace [E: the war's civilian dead in the published estimates]. The honest weight of the provision is not in that number, though the number is the reason it matters.

Its political effect runs from November 1969 to the end of the war. It is concentrated in four moments: the Hersh report in November 1969, in the weeks of the Moratorium and the Mobilization [H]; the Calley trial and verdict in March 1971 [H]; the veterans' protests of April 1971 [H]; and every later debate in which My Lai was the example. I judge the provision a small gain in the public's tolerance and a larger reduction in the credibility gap between what MACV reported and what the press reported [X-011].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

The chain the provision acts on is the one that failed: from the company to Task Force Barker, to the 11th Brigade, to the Americal Division, and up to MACV. Historically it reported a victory and buried a massacre [H]. Under this provision it reports a search of empty hamlets, and the report is true [E: judgment from the record cited in this field].

The provision touches the GVN in Quang Ngai a little. Son My was a Front village in a Front province, held by the VC 48th Local Force Battalion for years [H]. Its people do not become supporters of the government because they were not killed. But the Front loses a story it told in every hamlet of the province, and the province chief loses nothing he had.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** One village in Quang Ngai on one morning, and from November 1969 the American public's knowledge of the war. It does not reach any other village, any other unit, or any other morning [U].

**Preconditions.** None. The provision is stipulated. That is also its limit. Because it is stipulated, nothing in the war explains why it did not happen. The 11th Brigade's companies stay the companies they were, and the brigade's other mornings in Quang Ngai in 1968 stay as history records them.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

None. The provision has no footprint and needs nothing.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

Nothing to transfer. The provision is an event that does not happen. The South Vietnamese forces had their own record of crimes against civilians, and the enemy had his, at Hue in 1968 among other places [H]. None of that changes.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The provision acts at full value from November 1969, when history's revelation came, until the war's end. Before November 1969 it has no political effect, because the massacre was hidden. Its value in the field is only the lives saved on 16 March 1968.

Its political value collapses toward nothing if the American turn against the war was settled before November 1969. On the Gallup record it largely was. 53% called the war a mistake in August 1968, and the 1969–70 average was 55% [S1]. My Lai came after the turn, not before it.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The first failure: another emblem.** My Lai was not the only atrocity of the war, and the provision is isolated by ruling. The press and the antiwar movement had other incidents, and they would have found and used another. The emblem's power came partly from its scale and partly from the cover-up. A smaller emblem is still an emblem.

**The second failure: no reckoning.** This is the failure the provision causes. The Peers inquiry made the Army look at the command climate that measured success by bodies, at the reporting chain that buried a massacre, and at the training that did not teach men to refuse an unlawful order [H]. Without it, the same command climate runs longer unexamined in the Americal Division and elsewhere. The officer and NCO education this exercise has already put into the force acts on that climate, but this provision does not. I count the absence of the reckoning as a real cost. It is the counter-directional effect I expect Part 2 to examine.

**The third failure: the credibility gap remains.** The gap between MACV's reports and the press's was wide before My Lai and stayed wide after it. Tet, the body count and the optimism of 1967 made it [H]. The provision narrows it. It does not close it.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The provision does not degrade. It is one event, removed. Its political value peaks in November 1969 and March 1971, the two moments when history's My Lai dominated the news, and declines after 1971, when the war was ending for Americans and the question was how fast. What declines first is the reduction in the credibility gap, because other stories filled the gap within months.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is history's war. Against history's 347–504 villagers killed at Son My [H], none. Against history's Calley conviction and the national argument of 1971 [S8], none. Against the civilian dead of the whole war, a trace [E: judgment from the record cited in this field]. Against the credibility gap, a real narrowing, which I put at a noticeable change and no more [X-011]. Against the public's view of the war, which turned before My Lai was known [S1], a small change [X-011].

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P011c -->
- It saves the 347–504 villagers of Son My [H]; it does not change the conditions that produced the massacre. By ruling it is isolated and stipulated [U]: the Americal Division, the 11th Brigade, Task Force Barker, the body-count command climate and every other atrocity of the war stay as history recorded them.
- It does not make the war popular: Gallup's "mistake" share was already 53% in August 1968 and averaged 55% in 1969–70 [S1], before My Lai was known in November 1969.
- It does not remove a turn in mass opinion: in April 1971 only 11% approved the Calley verdict, 70% thought him a scapegoat, and 79% thought the life sentence too harsh [S8].
- It removes the Peers inquiry and the Army's reckoning with the command climate and reporting chain that produced and concealed the massacre; that cost is real.
- It does not touch the countryside beyond one village: Quang Ngai remains a Front province, and Son My remains a Front village.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P011c -->
It denies Hanoi and the Front the war's most powerful single piece of propaganda, at home and abroad, and the antiwar movement its emblem. It denies the enemy nothing in the field.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P011c -->
Between 347 and 504 people live, and that is reason enough to be glad of this provision [H]. Measured as an act of war policy, it is small. My Lai did not turn the American public against the war, which had turned before the massacre was known [S1], and when the public did react, in 1971, it sided with the lieutenant [S8]. What My Lai did was give the war's opponents their emblem and the credibility gap its starkest proof. Removing it narrows that gap noticeably and adds a little to the public's tolerance [X-011]. It also removes the Peers inquiry and the reckoning that followed. Because the provision is isolated by ruling, the command climate that produced the massacre runs on unexamined. That is the provision's most important effect on the war, and it points the wrong way.
