## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I took over on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war being lost, and for eleven years of this record it stayed the war I took over, made better inside and lost on the same day. This memorandum is the first that changes the day. It does so without a soldier, a weapon or a reform in the theater. It changes three things in the United States: a burglary at the Watergate that never happens, a quarter of the young men the draft boards called who enlisted before their notices came, and a morning at Son My in March 1968 when nobody was killed. The first of them reached the place where the war was decided, which was the Independence Palace on 11 and 14 March 1975. The other two reached the American public and stopped there.

The Republic's interior was already not history's. On the first day it decreed land to the tiller, set up a commission against corruption, gave itself a rural police and province chiefs chosen by examination, and put a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks watched the enemy's rear, and B-52Js with guided bombs waited on Guam for targets Washington would let them strike. Every soldier carried a modern carbine, every regular squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman, and every post of the held belt a radio made in Saigon. Since 1957 every officer had been educated for his grade, every private trained to the standard of 2026, and every sergeant schooled for his. The Aid Compliance Board had made the army buy its cartridges, batteries and radios from its own plants at Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap from 1965. None of it changed which fights the enemy accepted, and all of it was lost in 1975 because the war was decided above it.

The volunteers began with the war. From 1964 about a quarter of the men the boards would have inducted enlisted first, under the ruling that they were the same men in the same jobs (P011b) [U]. In 1966–68 that was about 70,000–95,000 fewer induction notices a year, fewer families coerced, fewer draft trials and a quieter campus (F011-15) [X-011]. It did not change a battle. The volunteers went where the casualties fell, as the draftees had, and died at the same rate; the war's American dead are history's on this account (F011-15). In the main-force war of 1965–67 the Americans won their battles at the Ia Drang, in the Bong Son plain and along the DMZ, and the enemy declined every fight he did not want. The measured siege became his worst battle, Phung Hoang broke the Front's district committees in the held belt three years before history's, and the land programme titled most of the tenanted rice land of the Delta. The public's verdict on the war moved on the casualty lists, from about a quarter calling it a mistake to 47% in October 1967 [M011·S1], and the quieter draft did not reach it.

Tet came on its historical dates and turned on its historical battles. Six weeks later, on the morning of 16 March 1968, Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry landed at My Lai 4 in Son My village, searched the hamlet, burned the bunkers under the houses and was lifted out in the afternoon (EV000-46). Nobody was killed. That is the stipulation, isolated: nothing inside the war prevents the massacre, and nothing inside the war is changed to explain it [U]. The conditions that historians found behind it stayed in Quang Ngai: a division assembled hastily, a body count that measured success, free-fire areas on the Batangan and harassment fire on the hamlets at night (F011-11). Son My's four to five hundred people went on living under the Front's administration, paying its taxes and sending its young men to the 48th Local Force Battalion (EC011-6). The Republic gained nothing in Son Tinh District it could count. The Front lost the story it would have told in every other village. On 31 March the President stopped the reinforcement and the bombing and withdrew from the election, as history's did, and in November a successor committed to withdrawal was elected.

The war of the drawdown in 1969–71 was quieter at home for two reasons that never met in a single event. The Moratorium and the Mobilization of October and November 1969 marched as history's did, but in a November whose news was the President's speech to the silent majority and the first withdrawals, because there was no Hersh story to publish and no Haeberle photographs of a ditch (EV000-63). The antiwar movement lost its most powerful image of what the fighting did to the villages and kept its argument, which was the war itself; the share of Americans calling it a mistake averaged about 55% in 1969–70 and reached 60% in 1971 as it had historically [M011·S1]. There was no Peers inquiry, no Calley trial and no national argument in the spring of 1971 about a lieutenant (EV000-72). The cost of that silence was the inquiry that never came: the Army was never made to look at the command climate of the Americal Division, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 to examine how the body count shaped operations was not taken up, because without a scandal nobody above MACV wanted it (F011-11). In the rifle companies of the drawdown, ten to fifteen men in a company of the 196th Brigade had chosen to come, and in the platoons that also had a schooled sergeant and a battalion that had trained together the inspector general counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer drug and refusal cases (EV010-5, F011-19). It changed no fragging the record names. In July 1971 Colby went before the House subcommittee with an audit of Phoenix and without the shadow of Son My over the room, and the subcommittee asked whether the audit could be trusted rather than whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means (EV000-72, F011-16).

The year 1972 runs as history's. The Easter Offensive was stopped at An Loc and Kontum by American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts and lost at Quang Tri by the orders of the corps and the division. On 14 November Nixon wrote to Thieu that he would take swift and severe retaliatory action if Hanoi violated the agreement [M011·S5]. Nobody broke into the Democratic National Committee's offices, on 28 May or on 17 June, and nothing in 1972 turned on that: Nixon won the election as history's did, and the agreement was signed in Paris on 27 January 1973 (EV000-83). The Americans were out by the end of March.

The line bends in 1973, and it bends less than it seems to. In June Congress passed the Case–Church amendment by 325–86 and 73–16, as history's had, because this vote was not about the President: the prisoners were home, the troops were out, and 60% of Americans called the war a mistake (EV000-86) [M011·S6] [M011·S1]. The bombing of Cambodia ended on 15 August, and with it American combat in Indochina, by law. The Senate's Watergate hearings never opened, there was no Saturday Night Massacre, and the War Powers override failed in the House by a handful of votes. That changed little, because Case–Church already denied the President the bombers his letters had promised. The binding constraint on American support moved from the President to Congress (F011-7). In Hanoi the Twenty-first Plenum resolved in October to continue by revolutionary violence and the PAVN rebuilt on the historical schedule, with about a tenth more to make good from 1972 and filling it within a season (EV000-87, POSMT01-2).

The difference was in the money, and the money was in the vote of 1974. History's President spent the spring and summer of 1974 fighting impeachment and resigned on 9 August; this war's President spent them on the Republic's appropriation (EV000-89). He made Major General Murray's warning his own argument before the House defense subcommittee on 21 May, and he had 15–30 Republican votes in the House that history's Watergate summer had sent home to defend themselves. The House cut the request anyway, and the conference report gave the RVNAF about $950 million for fiscal 1975 against history's $700 million (F011-2). Because the Republic's own plants stretched every dollar of propellant and parts, that bought about what $1.2 billion had bought on the historical course, the upper bound marked because it multiplies two judgments that lean the same way. From the autumn the margin went where the domestic stretch had never reached: aviation fuel and parts for the VNAF, diesel for the relief columns and 155mm and 175mm rounds that Bien Hoa could not forge. The VNAF's grounded aircraft were about a third fewer by December (F011-21). The Politburo had watched a President who did not fall all summer, and it cut the B-2 Front's and the Tri-Thien front's autumn raids back to Phu Loc alone. The 1st Division retook the Phu Loc hills in the third week of November instead of on 10 December, and I judge that the Republic lost about 1,500–4,000 fewer dead in 1974 than history's 30,000 on this provision's account (EV000-91). The midterm of 5 November cost the President's party about 22 House seats and 3 Senate seats rather than 49 and 5, which left a Congress that still wanted Indochina finished and could no longer override him (EV011-1).

The enemy answered on his own timeline and through his own argument. Tran Van Tra, the B-2 Front's commander, argued in Hanoi in November for an attack on Phuoc Long as a test of the American response, against a Politburo with more reason to fear the answer than history's had, and he won (EV000-92). Phuoc Long fell on 6 January 1975 as it had historically, because Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without bombers, and because the PAVN's 130mm guns and T-54s decided the siege. The next day Nixon sent Congress the message history's President never sent: $300 million and authority under Case–Church to use American air power against a major offensive (EV011-2). The authority was dead within the month; in March Congress voted about $150 million. Hanoi, Washington and Saigon each read the message correctly, and Thieu's reading was the one that mattered. The authority was refused, but the money was coming, and he concluded that he should not give up a province to make it go further (F011-20).

That conclusion was in the room on 11 March 1975, the day after Ban Me Thuot fell, when General Cao Van Vien presented the Dalat study for a withdrawal to a defensible Republic. Thieu refused it. There was no order on 13 March to give up I Corps and no order on 14 March to abandon the Highlands by Route 7 (EV000-94, EV000-95). This is where history broke. Truong held I Corps on a plan that began with the families, moving about 70,000 dependants of the 1st Division and the Marines by sea behind the Hai Van, and the My Chanh line held for three weeks. The 2nd Division, whose families had not been moved, came apart in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin as history's I Corps had. Hue was withdrawn in order between 14 and 18 April, and Da Nang was held. In the Highlands the counter-attack from Phuoc An broke on 18 March. Kontum was given up on 27 March in an ordered withdrawal down Route 14, and Pleiku stood a three-week siege on what the appropriation had bought. On 2 and 5 April the 44th Regiment and the 4th Ranger Group broke the 320A's assault battalions on the southern perimeter. On 14 April Phu broke out down Route 19 with the soldiers' families at the head of the column, and about 11,000 of 25,000 regulars and Rangers reached the coast, with about 9,000 civilians lost against history's 155,000 on Route 7.

The Highlands fell on 19 April instead of 25 March, and Hue on 18 April instead of 25 March. On 25 March the Politburo had no double collapse to resolve on. Le Duan took Van Tien Dung's view over Tra's argument for a thrust in May: an assault into the rains with two corps against five divisions would be the error of 1968, and the 1st Corps was sent south to assembly areas to wait (EVMT01-4). The critical path that MT01 named needed a delay of about three weeks in the collapse of I and II Corps (POSMT01-3). It got three to five weeks, and the delay came not from the arsenal or the officers but from a decision at the top that history's President took one way and this war's took the other. Xuan Loc was not attacked. Thieu did not resign, Minh did not take office, and on 29 April Corporal McMahon and Lance Corporal Judge stood their watch at the Defense Attaché Office compound and went off it (EV000-96, EV000-97, EV000-98).

The price of the delay was the plan Thieu refused. The Dalat study would have brought the Highlands force out in the dry season without a fight; the siege brought out half of it after one, and the Republic's reserve spent the spring at Da Nang, five hundred kilometres from the capital. On 6 May Vien sent the study back as a plan for the defence of Saigon, and Thieu took its method. Operation SOUTHERN WALL lifted about 70,000 soldiers and 250,000 civilians out of Da Nang between 20 and 31 May, the families first, and the Airborne and Marines were rebuilt at Vung Tau and Long Binh as the reserve the study had asked for in November 1974 (EV011-3). Qui Nhon went in June and Tuy Hoa in July. Congress voted the refugee money and $175 million for the RVNAF in May, and about $800 million for fiscal 1976 in November. In Hanoi the rains were spent bringing the 1st Corps south, extending the pipeline to Loc Ninh and asking Moscow to ship the 130mm ammunition and the tanks of 1976 in the autumn of 1975, which Moscow did, while Beijing sent rice and trucks and no more. The resolution of October 1975 set the general offensive on Saigon for the first months of 1976, with five corps.

The war ends where `history/endstate.toml` says it ends: on 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south under the President it has had since 1967, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976. This is rung (b), dependent survival, by the test of the ladder, because Saigon stands past 30 April 1975. In its own terms it is a reprieve of about a year, bought by an American President who stayed in office long enough to ask Congress for money it would partly give. It was spent by a South Vietnamese President who believed, on that evidence, that he need not give anything up. Every provision before this one made the Republic better inside and did not touch the level at which it was lost. This one touched that level from Washington. It gave the Republic a season and could not give it American power.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- null P2 -->
The volunteers act here, and below a step. Ruling A puts the same men in the same jobs, so the main-force war of 1965–67 has the same American units, tactics and dead (F011-15). ## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

The phase changes in one event, and the change is a stipulation: on 16 March 1968 the massacre at Son My does not happen (P011c, EV000-46). The ruling made the removal isolated, with no cause inside the war [U]. So the chronicle shows what does not follow, not why it does not happen. Task Force Barker's sweep entered the 11th Brigade's log as a dry hole. There was no report of 128 Viet Cong killed, no concealment to come apart and nothing for Ridenhour to write about in 1969. The conditions historians found behind the massacre stay in the province: the body count, the free-fire areas and the harassment fire, and every other killing of civilians in Quang Ngai that year stays where history put it (F011-11).

The second change in the phase is in the end vector and not in an event. American political tolerance moves from the historical state to 0..+1, with the public as the binding facet, because the volunteers' effect on the draft peaks in 1966–68: about 70,000–95,000 fewer induction notices a year, fewer families coerced and fewer draft trials (F011-15). Public opinion binds because the administration's tolerance in 1968 was set by the public's reaction to Tet and the casualty lists, not by Congress, which had not yet refused anything. The facet moves to 0..+2 and the parent to 0..+1, because the gain on the public was too small to reach the decision of 31 March.

My Lai adds nothing to tolerance in 1968, and that is not an oversight. Historically the massacre was concealed until November 1969. Its removal acts on the American public only from that month, in Phase IV (F011-1). In 1968 its only effect is in Son My itself, where about four to five hundred more people are alive at the end of the day, under the Front.

Two findings are carried here. F011-11 says that no massacre means no reckoning. F011-10 says that the attention of 1969–71 moves elsewhere. The first is the cost the phase plants. The Americal Division's command climate goes unexamined through the drawdown, and the war loses the one event that historically forced the Army to look at how a company could do what Charlie Company did.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy detected nothing, because nothing happened. The 48th Local Force Battalion had left Son My before the helicopters landed, as it left most sweeps in Son Tinh District that spring, and it came back to a village whose people were alive and still under its administration (EC011-6). Its recruiters and tax collectors had four to five hundred more people to work on. The Front's province committee lost the story it would have told in every other village of the district about what the Americans did. I judge that cost as political, unmeasurable in the phase's variables and larger than the recruits it gained. Hanoi's decisions of 1968, the second and third offensives, the talks in Paris and the acceptance of the bombing halt, were taken on Tet's losses and the American election (EV000-49, EV000-52, EV000-53). Nothing in the package reached them.

### Causal chains

The chain that begins here, C011-2, lands in Phase IV and is written there.

### Downstream

Phase IV loses the revelation of November 1969, the Peers inquiry and the Calley trial (EV000-63, EV000-72), and with them the national argument of 1971. It also loses the inquiry into the command climate that the scandal forced (F011-11). Phases V and VI are untouched by this removal. Son Tinh District stays an ordinary contested district of Quang Ngai, and in March 1975 it is overrun with the province in the collapse of the 2nd Division (EV000-94).

### Clock

The clock in 1968 ran on Tet, the casualty lists and the request for 206,000 more men, and the President stopped it on 31 March (EV000-45). The volunteers took some heat out of the draft and changed none of those three. The 53% who called the war a mistake in August 1968 were history's 53% [M011·S1]. The phase's floor is not met in events. One event changes against a floor of three, and the reason is the provision's shape (G7). The package changes one event of 1968 by stipulation and acts otherwise only through the draft. Every other event of the phase turns on things the package does not reach, and writing others would be padding.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

This is where the volunteers and the removal of My Lai act together, on the public, while American troops are still in the field (F011-1). The Moratorium marched without a massacre to reveal in its second month (EV000-63). The spring of 1971 had no Calley verdict to divide the country along lines that did not run between the war's supporters and its opponents (EV000-72). The Pentagon Papers took the attention instead, on their historical dates (F011-10). The public's tolerance moves to +1..+3. It moves through the absence of a scandal and through a draft a quarter smaller, and its binding facet is still the public. It binds because the President's schedule of withdrawal was set by his reading of the public and not by Congress, which in 1969–71 constrained only Cambodia and Laos (EV000-70). The parent moves to 0..+2 and the schedule does not move, because Nixon was already withdrawing as fast as Vietnamization allowed and slower than the public wanted.

The verdict did not change, and this is the most important fact in the phase. Gallup's share calling the war a mistake averaged about 55% in 1969–70 and reached 60% in 1971 [M011·S1]. It had passed 50% by August 1968, more than a year before Hersh's story. My Lai historically confirmed a verdict that was already in. Removing it takes away the confirmation and not the verdict (F011-5).

The volunteers' second effect is in the companies. EV010-5 carries it at length and embodies F011-19. In a rifle company of the 196th Brigade in 1970, ten to fifteen of the men who would have been draftees had chosen to come. The division's inspector general found the volunteers of that cohort about half as likely as the men still drafted to be on the drug and refusal lists. Where a schooled sergeant and a battalion that had trained together were already holding the line, he counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer cases. It saved no squad the war's politics had already lost.

The Watergate removal touches the phase at one point. The wiretaps of May 1969, ordered to trace the leak about Menu, are never exposed by an investigation of a burglary that never happens (EV000-58). The "plumbers" of 1971 are formed on the historical date and their burglaries are never exposed either (EV000-72). Nothing in 1969–71 turns on them. Their exposure, which mattered, would have come in 1973–74.

### Enemy response and initiative

Hanoi read the American public through the same press it had always read, and found the antiwar movement as strong in October 1969 as history's. The movement's strength came from the casualty lists and the war's lack of an end, which the package did not touch. COSVN's Resolution 9 of July 1969 and the North's turn to protracted war after Tet were taken on the losses of 1968 and the American withdrawal (EV000-61), and their authors had no reason to revise them. The Front's propaganda lost Son My as an example and used the free-fire areas and the refugee camps of Quang Ngai, which history's My Lai had overshadowed. They were reported in the American press one at a time and smaller (EV000-63).

### Causal chains

**C011-2** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase III, EV000-46) No massacre is committed at Son My on 16 March 1968, so there is nothing for Ridenhour to report, Hersh to publish or Peers to investigate in 1969 (EV000-63) → the Army is never made to look at the Americal Division's command climate, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 for a theater-wide inquiry into how the body count shaped operations dies in Washington for want of a scandal (F011-11, EC011-7) → in July 1971 the House subcommittee hears Colby's audited Phoenix figures without Son My's shadow over the room, and asks whether the audit can be trusted rather than whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means (EV000-72) → MACV's word in 1971 is worth a little more than history's, so the removal of an American atrocity ends by making the Republic's own intelligence programme easier to defend in Congress, while the body-count culture that produced the atrocity goes unexamined (F011-16).

### Downstream

Phase V receives a public a little less hostile than history's and a President no stronger, because his support in 1972 rested on the withdrawal and the prisoners and not on the public's view of the war. Phase VI receives the volunteers' last effect: the draft ended in early 1973 [M011·S2] with fewer draft-evasion cases to resolve. About 210,000 men were accused historically [M011·S7], and here about a quarter fewer were ever called. It also receives a war that Americans remember a little more as fought by men who chose to go (F011-18). Neither changes a vote of 1973–75. The vote that does change is the Watergate removal's, and it begins in Phase VI.

### Clock

The clock ran on withdrawal, and withdrawal was the President's answer to it. The package wound it back a little in 1969–71, through a quieter November 1969, a spring of 1971 without Calley and draft calls a quarter smaller, and it did not change the rate at which the troops came home (EV000-60). A provision that makes the public less angry about a war it has already judged a mistake buys the President time he had decided not to use for escalation. Nixon spent it on the withdrawal's pace and on Cambodia and Laos, which ran on their historical dates (EV000-66, EV000-71).

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

The phase turns on one removal and the chain it starts. The break-in of 1972 never happens, so there are no Senate hearings in May 1973, no Saturday Night Massacre, no impeachment and no resignation (F011-9). The President who wrote the letters of 1972 is in office until January 1977. Three things follow, and only the third reaches the war.

First, the law that ended American combat in Indochina passes as history's did. Case–Church carried the House 325–86 and the Senate 73–16 in June 1973, before the Watergate hearings had made any Republican defect, and its margins were veto-proof without a single Republican defection [M011·S6] (EV000-86). What absorbs the change is the voters of the Republicans who voted for it, who wanted Indochina finished whoever was President (DIRMT01-4). The War Powers override fails, and it changes little, because Case–Church already denied the President the bombers. The binding constraint on American support moves from the President to Congress (F011-7). That is why `US.political_tolerance` binds on Congress in Phase VI and not on the public. The public's verdict stood at 60% "mistake" and the President's will to support the Republic was at its strongest, so the facet that limited what reached Saigon was the one that held the purse and the statute.

Second, the appropriation of 1974 is fought for by a President who is not fighting impeachment, and it is larger (F011-2, F011-17). In fiscal 1975 the RVNAF received about $950 million against history's $700 million (EV000-89). Through memorandum 008's domestic stretch that bought what about $1.2 billion had bought historically. The upper bound is marked, because it multiplies two judgments that lean the same way (DIRCP02-3). The money reached what the stretch never had: aviation fuel and parts, diesel for the relief columns, and the heavy rounds Bien Hoa could not forge. That is the strongest positive effect, F011-21, and it is grounded in two dated events. The first is Phu Loc: the 1st Division retook the hills in the third week of November 1974 instead of on 10 December, with helicopters that had fuel on most days (EV000-91). The second is Pleiku: on 2 and 5 April 1975 the 44th Regiment and the 4th Ranger Group broke the 320A's assault battalions on the southern perimeter, with the squad guns fed and the artillery firing at the 1973 allowance (EV000-95). The binding facet of `ARVN.self_sufficiency` is still fire support. The money fed the 105s and 155s, and the PAVN's 130mm still outranged every gun the Republic could feed. The gain on logistics is therefore a gain on the parent only within the range the binding facet allows (DIRR01-3).

Third, and against the provision's direction, the money and the message made Thieu plan for a rescue the law forbade (F011-20). On 7 January 1975 Nixon asked Congress for $300 million and for authority to use air power, and received half the money in March and none of the authority (EV011-2). Thieu read the refusal correctly and the money as the President's word kept as far as Congress allowed. On 11 March he refused the Dalat study, and on 14 March he ordered Phu to hold Pleiku and Kontum and retake Ban Me Thuot (EV000-94, EV000-95). This is the counter-directional finding at work, and in this war it is also the mechanism of the gain. A President who believed he need not give anything up gave no order to give up the Highlands by Route 7 and no contradictory orders to Truong. Without those orders the collapse of I and II Corps came three to five weeks late. The net, judged in both directions as DIRMT01-5 requires, runs as follows. The refusal cost half the Highland force, the 2nd Division and the reserve's spring at Da Nang, all of which a planned withdrawal in the dry season would have saved. It gained a season, a capital not attacked in April, a government still in office, and 146,000 fewer civilians lost on the Highland road than history's convoy of tears, a figure resting on two estimates of very different quality (EV000-95). I judge the gain the larger, because the alternative to the refusal in this war was not the Dalat study carried out but history's order of 14 March. Thieu was the same man in both wars, and only the money and the message changed his answer.

The PAVN's 1972 losses are restated under DIRMT01-6. They were about a tenth above the historical figure, concentrated in the divisions struck at An Loc and Kontum, and were replaced within the North's 1973 flow. The 1974–75 divisions were worse led and on time (POSMT01-2, EV000-87). They are not part of this memorandum's mechanism. The delay of 1975 came from the top of the Republic, not from the bottom of the PAVN.

The Republic's state at the end of 1974, before the last offensive (DIRMT01-3), is judged in the snapshot. Night control in the held belt of the Delta and the Saigon region was about one to three steps better than history's, and the Front's district committees there had lost about twice the historical share of their members in 1973 (EV000-84). The government's standing in the hamlets was history's, after the decrees of 1972. The army's battalions were better led, its divisions and corps no better, and its reserve was tied to I Corps. It had fought the year near the 1973 allowance and lost perhaps 2,500–6,500 fewer dead than history's 30,000 on the two rates of EV000-91. The President was more confident in his ally than history's, and his base was no broader.

By region (DIRMT01-8). In the Delta the effect is economic and not military. IV Corps was never attacked in 1975, and the dry-season crop on about 300,000 hectares of the held Delta, with the economic aid about a tenth larger than history's, held the price of rice in Saigon a little below history's in 1974 (EV000-89); at the end of 1975 the Delta is the Republic's whole and is its granary. In the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps the effect is mixed. Quang Ngai and Quang Tin collapsed in March as history's did, because the 2nd Division's families had not been moved. Hue was given up in order on 18 April, Da Nang was evacuated under SOUTHERN WALL in May, and Qui Nhon and Tuy Hoa went in June and July (EV000-94, EV011-3). In the Highlands the effect is a siege instead of a rout. Pleiku held three weeks and about 9,000 civilians were lost on Route 19 instead of 155,000 on Route 7 (EV000-95). The record does not say how the Jarai and Bahnar of Pleiku and Kontum fared under the PAVN after April, and I name that as a gap rather than fill it. In the Saigon belt the effect is the whole of the gain: the capital was not attacked, Xuan Loc held through the rains, and the Bien Hoa arsenal ran three shifts (EV000-96, EV011-3).

The soldier's family (DIRMT01-9) is where the spring of 1975 was decided, as POSMT01-6 found, and this memorandum confirms the finding from the other side. In I Corps Truong moved the dependants of the 1st Division and the Marines first, about 70,000 people by sea in three weeks from 15 March, and those divisions held the My Chanh and withdrew in order. The 2nd Division's families lived in the dependants' quarters at Quang Ngai and Tam Ky and were not moved, and about 5,000 of its men left their units for them (EV000-94). At Pleiku the families went at the head of the breakout column. At Da Nang they went from 8 May, twelve days before the soldiers. What reached POSMT01-6's mechanism was not a reform of the army. It was time, and a corps commander's plan that time allowed him to carry out. The soldier's pay was the Republic's own budget, in piasters the oil shock was eating. The larger appropriation did not reach it, and the economic aid voted with it slowed the piaster's fall in the second half of 1974 without stopping it (EV000-89, DIRMT01-11).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's decisions in this phase had authors, and they disagreed (DIRMT01-10). The Politburo under Le Duan watched a President who did not fall and was more cautious through 1974 than history's. The General Staff's plan for the dry season, approved at the conference of 30 September – 8 October, held the main blow for the Highlands, and Hanoi cut the B-2 Front's and the Tri-Thien front's autumn raids back to Phu Loc alone (EV000-91, F011-12). That was reaction: the threat worked while it was untested.

<!-- initiative --> The test was the B-2 Front's own decision. Lieutenant General Tran Van Tra argued in Hanoi in November 1974 for the attack on Phuoc Long, as he had historically, and he won against a Politburo with more reason to fear the answer (EV000-92). Three things drove him. The first was his front's own reckoning of what the B-2 needed to know before it could plan its part of the 1975–76 campaign. The second was the southern command's long view that Hanoi's caution had cost it 1968's chances. The third was a reading of American politics that was right: the President wanted to return and Congress would not let him (F011-13, F011-14). Nothing the Republic or the Americans did provoked it. Tra chose the province, the moment and the test, and Hoang Cam's 4th Corps carried it out with the 7th and 3rd Divisions. The test cost the North about 1,300 killed and wounded and answered the question the conference of 18 December – 8 January had set itself.

The second disagreement decided the date. On 31 March 1975 Tra argued for a thrust on Saigon in May with the 4th Corps, Group 232 and what the 1st Corps could bring, on the reasoning that the Republic's reserve was in I Corps. Van Tien Dung answered that the 1st Corps could not reach the capital before the third week of May, that two corps would be attacking five divisions in the rains, and that this would repeat the error of 1968. Le Duan took Dung's view (EVMT01-4). The North's decision to wait was its own, taken out of its own doctrine and the memory of 1968, on a map the Republic's March had drawn.

The adaptation cost the North a year and nothing more. It spent the rains bringing the 1st Corps south, extending the pipeline to Loc Ninh and asking Moscow to ship the 130mm ammunition and the tanks of 1976 in the autumn of 1975 (EV011-3). The Soviet and Chinese supplies behaved as variables with their own politics. Moscow shipped most of what was asked, because it wanted the war ended before the American election year. Beijing, which had taken the Paracels in January 1974 and had no wish to see Hanoi's victory hastened by Soviet guns, sent rice and trucks and no more (EV000-88, EV011-3). The October 1975 resolution set the general offensive on Saigon for the first months of 1976.

### Downstream

No phase follows in the record. The war does not end with it, and §4.N proposes a redraw. The downstream of this phase is the offensive of 1976. The Republic meets it with III and IV Corps, a rebuilt reserve, a full arsenal, its Delta and the capital's belt, a ration near 1974's and no American air power. The North meets it with five corps and the Soviet deliveries of 1976 brought forward.

### Clock

In Phase VI the clock is the appropriation cycle, and it has two hands. The President's hand runs slow. He stays in office and spends his capital on the Republic, and the administration's tolerance is at +5..+8 at the end of 1975. Congress's hand runs as history's did on combat and more slowly on money. Case–Church stands, the bombers' authority is refused in January 1975, and every dollar after it is voted for fuel, rounds and refugees. The public's hand does not move: 60% called the war a mistake in 1973 and nothing in the package changed that [M011·S1]. A provision that keeps a President in office does not make a war popular. What it does is put the question of April 1975 to a Congress through a President who can put it. Congress answered with money and not with power, and the money bought a season.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the cumulative force now changes the war's end as well as its interior. This memorandum makes the change, and it makes it in 1974–75.

The Republic of Vietnam stands at the end of 1975, against a fall on 30 April 1975. It has lost the Highlands, Quang Tri, Thua Thien, Quang Ngai, Quang Tin, Phuoc Long and the central coast north of Khanh Hoa, and about a third of its army's infantry. It holds III and IV Corps whole, the coast from Nha Trang south, about half the population of 1974, its Delta, its capital and its plants (EV011-3). Its government is Thieu's, with the Senate's confidence. History had Minh's surrender (EV000-97, EV000-98). Saigon was not attacked in April and Xuan Loc was not fought (EV000-96). The capital's fall is expected eight to thirteen months after history's.

Formations: the Airborne and Marine Divisions, whose brigades history's spring broke at Hue, Da Nang and Suoi Ca, come out of Da Nang under SOUTHERN WALL and are rebuilt at Vung Tau and Long Binh as a general reserve. The 1st Division loses about a third of itself at Hue rather than ceasing to exist. Of II Corps' Highland force, about 11,000 of 25,000 regulars and Rangers reach the coast with their weapons, against history's 6th Ranger Battalion and 19th Armoured Cavalry Squadron. The 2nd Division and the Highland force's other half are lost as history's were (EV000-94, EV000-95).

Lives. In 1974 the Republic lost perhaps 1,500–4,000 fewer dead on this memorandum's account, and 2,500–6,500 fewer with memorandum 008's, against about 30,000 (EV000-91). On the Highland road about 9,000 civilians were lost against history's estimate of 155,000, a difference that rests on a contested historical figure and my own judgment of a column a fifth the size (EV000-95). At Son My between 347 and 504 people were not killed on 16 March 1968 [H47] (EV000-46). Corporal McMahon and Lance Corporal Judge live. The American dead of the war are otherwise history's (F011-15).

Money: about $950 million for fiscal 1975 against $700 million, $150 million in March and $175 million in May 1975 against nothing, and about $800 million for fiscal 1976 (EV000-89, EV011-2, EV000-97, EV011-3).

What has not changed is as concrete. The Paris Agreement and Case–Church passed as they did historically. No American aircraft flew in combat over Indochina after 15 August 1973. The PAVN rebuilt on its historical schedule, took Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot on their historical dates, and kept the initiative in every fight but the siege of Pleiku. The 2nd Division dissolved toward its families as history's I Corps did. Hanoi never wavered in its aim. The margin over history is a year, and it is a large margin for a provision that put no American soldier, aircraft or dollar into the war except through a vote.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The outcome in its own terms comes first (G5). It is a reprieve of about a year. The Republic survives the spring offensive of 1975 with its capital, its south and its government. It does so because a President in Washington who did not fall got Congress to pay for most of a year's war, and a President in Saigon, holding that money and that promise, refused to give anything up. The record ends on 31 December 1975 with five PAVN corps assembled for an offensive on Saigon in the first months of 1976 and no American air power to meet them. On the ladder it is rung (b), dependent survival. It is the first change of rung in this record.

**The rung, and why (b) and not (a).** DIRMT01-1 warned that a change of date does not by itself move the rung between (a) and (b) "while the ladder's overlap stands". The test of v1.0.6 removed the overlap. Condition b2 names the date: Saigon stands past 30 April 1975. Its twin, US combat forces staying, describes a different war. Saigon stands at the end of 1975, eight months past the date, and the script requires the rung the test supports. So the move to (b) is the test's verdict and not a date carried into a rung. I hold it on the war as well. The Republic that enters 1976 has fought an offensive without American air and kept its heartland, which history's never did. Part 2 expected that the provision would not reach the critical path. The test in the chapter found that it did, through the one premise Part 2 had not traced to the end: Thieu's decision of 11–14 March 1975 was itself a function of the money and the message (F011-20). I report that as a correction of Part 2's expectation, not as a confirmation of it.

**Why not (c).** Rung (c) needs the Republic to hold the countryside in every corps region without American ground troops, and to stand at the end of the record with American air power available. It holds neither. I and II Corps are lost, northern Binh Dinh was lost in 1972, and Case–Church forbade the air power from August 1973 and was not lifted when the President asked (EV011-2). The rung test also passes c1 on 1972, when the RVNAF stopped the Easter Offensive at An Loc and Kontum with American air power and no American ground units. That was true historically too, so it is not this memorandum's gain. POSMT01-1 said the rung would not move until a provision reached an air arm the Republic owns, sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation, or the choice of the corps commanders. This provision reached the appropriation itself. It moved the rung one step and no further, because it reached neither the air arm nor the corps commanders. The corps commanders in this war were the same men; what changed was the order they were given.

**The date of the fall (DIRMT01-1).** Saigon was not attacked in 1975, and my estimate of its fall is between February and May 1976, in the dry season, from an offensive by five corps. Four mechanisms in the cumulative force pull it later. The first is the rebuilt reserve, the Airborne and Marines at Vung Tau and Long Binh. The second is the arsenal running three shifts, with the ration near 1974's on a fiscal 1976 appropriation of about $800 million. The third is the Delta's rice and posts. The fourth is a government still in office whose Senate gave it confidence. Four mechanisms pull it earlier. The Soviet deliveries of 1976 were brought forward into the autumn of 1975. The Republic lost a third of its infantry and the heavy equipment of II Corps and the 1st and 2nd Divisions. The PAVN's 130mm still outranges every gun the Republic can feed. And there is no American air power. The second set dominates, because the 1976 offensive meets the Republic on ground it must hold, the capital's approaches, with no mechanism in the force that changes the artillery duel. An offensive of five corps is the one the Republic could not stop in 1975 when it came, and nothing it gains in the rains changes the arithmetic. The reprieve could stretch past the dry season of 1976 only if Congress paid for re-equipment, which the May vote refused. It could also stretch if the Politburo waited again, which its October resolution rules out.

**Is the 1974 vote still history's? (DIRMT01-2).** No. Under DIRMT01-2 the appropriation of 1974 and the refusal of April 1975 were to be treated as events a provision can change, and this provision changes both. POSMT01-5 held the vote as history's until a provision changed what the Republic needs Congress to pay for. This one changed Congress's paymaster instead, and the position is reversed below. The April request is now Nixon's, smaller and earlier, and Congress voted the refugee money and $175 million of $400 million (EV000-97).

**The running sums (DIRMT01-2).** American dead saved: memoranda 001–010 judged about 3,700–7,800 (memorandum 010 §4.N). This memorandum adds the two Marines of 29 April 1975 and nothing else. The volunteers die at the draftees' rate (F011-15), so the sum stays at about 3,700–7,800, six to thirteen percent of the historical dead. It supports the lower half of the range of `US.casualties` it already sits in, and it does not move tolerance (POSCP01-1). The Republic's soldiers saved: memoranda 009 and 010 judged about 8,000–23,000 in 1964–72. Memorandum 008 judged 1,000–2,500 in 1974 at a third more 105mm fire, and this memorandum adds 1,500–4,000 in 1974 at a rate of 5–13% fewer losses in the fights accepted with fuller supply and on raids not made (EV000-91). That gives about 10,500–29,500 to the end of 1974. It is shown at work at Rach Bap and Phu Loc in 1974 and at Pleiku in 1975, as DIRCP01-2 requires, and it is a judgment on its rates. For 1975 I give no sum. The soldiers lost at Pleiku and in the Mang Yang, at Hue and at Da Nang must be set against those history's spring lost and those it never fought at Xuan Loc and Saigon, and the record does not count either side closely enough to subtract. Civilians: the 347–504 of Son My [H47], and about 146,000 fewer lost on the Highland road, a difference whose historical term is itself an estimate (EV000-95).

**Floors not met, and why (G7).** Part 2 of this memorandum finished with two warnings that its text did not explain. It had four employment concepts against a floor of six, because a political provision has few ways to be employed: the letters, the supplemental, the volunteer and the district exhaust them, and three more were abandoned for stated reasons. It had about thirteen equipment designations against twenty, because the package's instruments are votes, letters and induction notices, and the designations it names are the ones its downstream effects reach (the B-52J, the GBUs, the C-130A, the UH-1H, the 105mm, 155mm and 175mm). In Part 3, Phase III changes one event against a floor of three, for the reason stated in its Clock. These are the provision's shape and not shortfalls of the analysis.

**Phase redraw (G2).** Phase VI ends on 31 December 1975 with the war unfinished, and the record cannot say how the Republic falls or whether it does. I propose that the next checkpoint add a Phase VII, "The last dry season (Jan 1976 – )", and move EV011-3's preparations into its opening. Until then the end state is open on its date, and every later memorandum tests its effects against the offensive of 1976 as this one tested its own against the critical path of 1975.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **Saigon stands at the end of 1975.** The Republic is not attacked at its capital in April 1975, Thieu does not resign and Minh never takes office. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps, the coast from Nha Trang south and about half its 1974 population, a reprieve of about a year over history's 30 April (EVMT01-4, EV011-3).
- **The critical path of 1975 bent for the first time.** I and II Corps collapsed three to five weeks later than history's: the Highlands on 19 April and Hue on 18 April instead of 25 March, with Da Nang held through May. On 25 March the Politburo put the assault back to the dry season of 1976 (EV000-94, EV000-95, EVMT01-4). That had been the threshold since MT01.
- **The Republic fought 1974 on about the 1973 allowance.** It had about $950 million for fiscal 1975 against $700 million, buying about what $1.2 billion had bought, with fuel, aircraft and heavy rounds reached and 1,500–4,000 fewer dead (F011-21; EV000-89, EV000-91).

- **The Republic carried out its only planned withdrawal.** SOUTHERN WALL lifted about 70,000 soldiers and 250,000 civilians out of Da Nang in May, families first, and rebuilt the Airborne and Marines as a reserve (EV011-3).
- **No massacre at Son My.** No revelation, no Calley trial and no national argument in 1971. A quieter draft took 70,000–95,000 fewer notices a year in 1966–68, and the public's tolerance was a little higher in 1968–71 (EV000-46, EV000-63, EV000-72).
- **The rung moved from (a) to (b),** by the test of rules/RUNGS.toml and on the war.

### What has not changed

- **American combat in Indochina ended by law in August 1973.** Case–Church passed as history's did, and Congress refused the President's request for the bombers (EV000-86, EV011-2). No American aircraft returned.
- **Hanoi's aim and its initiative.** It rebuilt on schedule, chose Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, and in October 1975 set the offensive on Saigon for 1976 (EV000-87, EV000-92, EV000-93, EV011-3).
- **The cost of Thieu's refusal.** The Dalat study was refused in March. The 2nd Division dissolved toward its families, and half the Highland force and the heavy equipment of II Corps were lost, all of which a planned withdrawal in the dry season would have saved (F011-20; EV000-94, EV000-95).
- **The public's verdict.** 53% in 1968, about 55% in 1969–70 and 60% from 1971 called the war a mistake, as history's did. The package removed two emblems and not the argument (F011-5) [M011·S1].
- **The American dead, and the command climate behind My Lai.** The volunteers die at the draftees' rate (F011-15). With no scandal, no one examined how the body count shaped the conduct of operations (F011-11).
- **The artillery duel and the soldier's pay.** The PAVN's 130mm outranged every gun the money fed, and the larger appropriation never reached the soldier's piasters (EV000-91, EV000-89).
- **The end is expected.** The Republic is expected to fall in the dry season of 1976 to five corps and no American air power (§4.N).
