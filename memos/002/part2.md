## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

These two provisions give the Republic a government in the district for the first time, and that is the frame for everything below. The land programme of the first memorandum changed what the government's arrival means to a tenant. The police reform (P002a) and the selection of chiefs on merit (P002b) change who arrives: a district chief chosen for ability who stays long enough to learn his villages, and a constable who sleeps in the village, knows its families and reports to a Special Branch that keeps the files. Neither adds a rifle to the hamlet at night. Both change what the rifles already there are for, and who is watching.

**Hamlet security.** Both provisions join it, and neither is it. The police post of 8–12 constables is not a fort and cannot hold against a VC platoon [M002]; the district chief commands no new soldiers. What the police adds to hamlet security is knowledge and continuity: a man who is present every night and knows whose son has gone. What the chief adds is the decision, taken every evening in 240 district towns, about whether the Popular Forces sleep in the hamlets or in the town. That decision is the mechanism of night control (POSTNA-2), and until now it has been taken by officers who bought their posts, expected to lose them at the next coup, and had every reason to keep their men close to the district headquarters.

**Finding the infrastructure.** The police reform is the first instrument in theater that addresses the second half of the find-and-fix problem as its main purpose (A6). The family census, the identity card, the resident constable and a single Special Branch with sole charge of the files make the infrastructure findable in the villages the government holds. It is newly viable as a system, not merely strengthened: before 1 January 1964 there was no rural police, no reliable count of who lived in a hamlet, and four intelligence services competing for the same informants (G-3).

**Subversion.** The Special Branch is the natural owner of the defector. A cadre who wants out can now walk to a man in the village who will take him to a Special Branch officer the same night, rather than to an army post where he may be shot at the wire. Chieu Hoi, begun in 1963 [H], gains a front door in every held village.

**Advisory effort.** The selection of chiefs is the largest advisory provision the theater can receive without a single new American. A district adviser has had a counterpart who changed with every coup; now he has one who outlasts the adviser's own twelve-month tour, and an Inspectorate to which his evidence can go without passing through the corps commander.

**Sustainment.** Small. About 610 Americans between the two provisions, about 180 of them military spaces [M002]; some helicopter hours for the Field Force and the inspectors; card stock and printer ribbons through Saigon. The scarce thing these provisions consume is not tonnage but able officers, and that cost is analysed under opportunity cost.

**Interdiction, inside the country.** The police joins interdiction in one narrow and real way: resource control on the roads and waterways. The Trail ends at the border, but the enemy's rice, medicine, batteries and cash for the base areas of War Zones C and D and the U Minh are bought or taxed in the Delta and the coastal plain and carried by sampan and ox-cart along roads and canals that a checkpoint can watch. That is a small fraction of his requirement in 1964 and a larger one later, and it is the only point at which these provisions touch the main force's sustainment.

**Finding, fixing and striking the main force.** Not joined in the first order, and I will not pretend otherwise. A PAVN regiment in a base area is no more visible to a constable than to a district chief.

**F002-1** (SECURE, ADVISE) **The chief and the constable are each other's precondition, and together they are a district government.** A police reform without a competent chief produces what the historical war produced after 1969: constables in villages whose district chief used them as his rent collectors or ignored their reports, and a Special Branch whose files went to whoever paid [M002]. A merit-selected chief without a police has the old problem in a new form: he can decide to keep his Popular Forces in the hamlets, but he does not know which hamlets matter, who in them is the Front's, or whether the village chief's report is true. Together they form the Malayan district war executive committee in Vietnamese dress: a chief who commands the Regional and Popular Forces, a police chief who knows the villages, a Special Branch officer who knows the infrastructure, and an American adviser who sees all three, meeting every week in the district operations room. Take Duc Hoa district in Hau Nghia, on the edge of the Plain of Reeds, in late 1965: a district chief in his eighteenth month, a police station of forty, posts in five of its nine villages, and a Special Branch section of fifteen with a file on every village committee. That district can do something none of the 239 districts of January 1964 could do: decide on Monday which hamlet the Popular Forces will sit in on Thursday night, because it knows that the village finance cadre will be there [E: the Malayan pattern applied to the districts of the period]. This pairing is major. It is the mechanism by which every other pacification provision, the land programme above all, is delivered.

The other pairings are real and smaller. The Commission found, in its first year, that it could prove a commander's guilt and then had no one to put in his place (F001-5); the list now names the replacement, ranked, vetted and trained, and a relief that took months now takes weeks [M002]. The Commission's investigators, in turn, are what keep the list honest: the asset declaration is filed with them, and a leaked examination is their case. The land register and the census are two records of the same households made for different purposes, and their overlap is the most powerful and most dangerous fact in either programme, which the perverse effect below addresses. And the Muster Service's identity cards for the army [M001] and the police's identity cards for the population use the same enrolment kits, the same fingerprint practice and some of the same contractor technicians; the Republic now has two national registers built to the same standard, and a man who deserts from the army appears in both.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

Untouched in the first order. The VC main force, which this headquarters carries at about 21,000–23,000 in January 1964 [M001] and growing, moves under canopy in the base areas, and the PAVN regiments that begin arriving in late 1964 never pass through a village that has a constable. The police reform's negative specification says so in its first line [M002], and I hold to it.

There is a thin second-order connection, and it is worth naming because it will grow. A main-force regiment in War Zone D or the U Minh eats rice bought or taxed in the villages, uses porters conscripted from the villages, and depends on village and district rear-service cadres to collect, store and move both. Those cadres are part of the infrastructure, and they are exactly the men the Special Branch is built to find. A rear-service cell in the villages along the Dong Nai that is rolled up in 1966 does not find the regiment it fed, but it tells the Special Branch where the regiment's rice went, and that is a lead an airmobile brigade can use. I put this at minor weight in 1964–65 and modest weight from 1966, when the base areas around Saigon depend more on rice from the populated belt and the US divisions can act on a lead within hours.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

This is the chain the police reform was built for, and for the first time in the war every link exists. Identification starts from the census: a family booklet listing who lives in each house, checked at random by night, which turns an absence into a fact and a stranger into a question [M002]. The resident constable turns the fact into knowledge, because he knows which absence means a son in the forest and which means a son in Saigon. The Special Branch turns knowledge into a file, with the rule of three independent sources for each target [M002], and keeps it in one place. The arrest is made by police on a warrant, in a held village by the constables with the Popular Forces standing by, and in a contested one by a Field Force platoon at dawn. A magistrate reviews it within 72 hours [M002]. Detention is capped and reviewed. The cadre who is held long enough to see that he will not be tortured, and that his family is not being punished, is the Special Branch's best future informant.

The chain is strongest at its front and weakest at its back. In a village the government holds at night, identification is good within six months of the post's arrival [E: Malayan experience of new police posts]. In a contested village, identification depends on informants who travel to market, on defectors and on prisoners, and is slower and less reliable. In a village the Front holds, the chain does not begin. The back of the chain, the courts, the detention centres and the time of trained interrogators, is the binding constraint by 1966 (F002-4).

Two things distinguish this from the historical Phoenix programme of 1968–72, beyond the four years. It is run by the police rather than by a coordinating committee of agencies that kept their own files, so there is one record and one owner [M002]. And it runs without quotas. The historical programme's 81,740 claimed neutralisations, of which 26,369 were killed and about 87% of those killed in ordinary military operations, were produced against monthly targets [M002]. A Special Branch that is measured on convictions and on the number of village committees that stop functioning, not on the number of names, produces fewer names and more effect.

### Dependence on SECURE

Total, and the point of the first memorandum applies with equal force here (POS001-1). A constable in a village the VC hold at night is a hostage, and his post is a store of M1 carbines and a radio awaiting collection. The police reform's value in any village equals the night security beside it; it raises that value, because security with knowledge is worth more than security without it, but it does not create the security.

The provincial reform is different in kind, because it touches the security itself. The district chief's evening decision about where the Popular Forces sleep is the single largest lever on night control available without new troops. In 1964 the Popular Forces, about 185,000 men in squads and platoons by the government's own rolls [E: the period's territorial strength, before the musters' correction], sleep largely in their own fortified posts along the roads, because the posts are where the district chief can count them and where they are least likely to be overrun in a way that ends his career. A chief who expects to hold his district for three years and to be inspected at night by a body he does not command has a different calculation: the Inspectorate counts Popular Forces in the hamlets at night, not in the posts [M002]. That is the only measure he cannot produce himself, and it is the right one.

So the two provisions sit on opposite sides of the security problem. The police depends on it absolutely. The chief makes it more likely. Neither makes it certain, and in the districts around the base areas, where the VC can concentrate a local-force battalion against a hamlet in a night, a resident squad of Popular Forces and a constable still die unless a reaction comes in the dark, by Regional Forces in boats, by an M113 troop of an ARVN armored cavalry squadron where the roads allow, by an AC-47 from December 1964 [H], or by an American unit from 1965.

### The ADVISE cluster

Both provisions feed it, and the provincial reform feeds it more than any provision yet. The historical advisory effort's central failure at province and district level was continuity: the adviser served twelve months, his counterpart was replaced at every coup, and neither ever knew the other's district for long enough to change it. The list's tenure of two to three years [M002] means that for the first time the Vietnamese outlasts the American. The adviser's leverage changes character with it. Before, it was personal and rested on the money he could release; now it is institutional, because his reports go to the Inspectorate as one input among several, and an Inspectorate finding upheld by the Board removes a chief without the American having to ask for it [M002]. That costs the Republic far less legitimacy than the Aid Compliance Board's suspension does (POSTNA-6), and I would rather use it.

The police reform feeds the advisory effort more narrowly. About 300 Public Safety advisers, against 144 in December 1965 historically [M002], put one American police adviser in each province and in about 80 priority districts. They advise policemen, who are civilians, and they do it through the aid mission rather than through this headquarters. That is right for the police and awkward for me: the Special Branch's product on the infrastructure reaches MACV's intelligence system through the province, not through my own channel, and my J-2 will complain about it within the month.

Neither provision starves the cluster. One draws on it: the selection of chiefs takes officers who would otherwise be candidates for battalion and regimental command, and some of them are the officers my advisers in the divisions most want to keep (F002-6).

### Employment concepts

#### EC002-1 · The settled post

*P002a.* The core employment of the rural constabulary: occupying a village post and keeping it. A post is occupied only in a village the district has rated held by day and contested at most lightly by night [M002]. The sequence runs over ninety days. On day one a Police Field Force platoon of about 40 [M002] arrives by truck or by LCVP of a River Assault Group, with the village's Popular Forces platoon and the district Special Branch officer, and stays a week while the post is fortified: a walled compound of laterite and sandbags, a radio room, a family quarter, and a cleared field of fire. The resident constables, 8–12 men with Smith & Wesson Model 10 revolvers, M1 and M2 carbines and one M1918A2 automatic rifle [E: the National Police's period armament], move in with their families on day three; the family is the point, because a constable who brings his wife and children is a constable who intends to stay. The Field Force platoon leaves on day seven and remains on call at the district town, about 30–90 minutes away by road or boat.

The post's daily cycle is two men on watch through the night, a patrol of four by day through the village's hamlets, the market morning in the village centre, the census check on three to five households chosen at random each night with the Popular Forces squad [M002]. Its link to the district is an HT-1 or TR-20 hamlet radio [H] in 1964, answered at the district by an AN/PRC-10, and replaced by an AN/PRC-25 from 1965 [H], checked in on the hour after dark. The post's minimum viable package is a resident Popular Forces platoon in the village at night, a working radio, and a reaction that can reach it in under an hour. Without the Popular Forces it degrades to a target; without the radio to a hostage. The first ninety days are the most dangerous, because the post knows little and the VC know exactly where it is [M002]. By the provision's schedule about 600 posts are manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,500 by the end of 1965 [M002], about 12 new posts a week across the country in 1964 and about 17 a week in 1965, each settled post freeing its Field Force platoon for the next.

#### EC002-2 · The census and the night check

*P002a.* The identity and census teams of six [M002] work a village for about a week: a household register made from the family booklets, a card with photograph and two fingerprints for every adult, and a copy of the register to the district identity office and the Special Branch section. A team registers about 150–200 adults a day in a village it can reach [E: the offline enrolment kit's throughput with queueing], so a village of 3,000 adults takes about three weeks of one team. The work only begins after the post is settled. In a held village the census is the post's tool: its randomly chosen night checks, done by a constable with two Popular Forces men, ask one question of each house, whether the people sleeping in it are the people in the booklet. A man listed and absent three nights running is a fact; a man present and unlisted is a question [M002]. Each post can sustain about 20–30 checks a week without losing its daytime patrol [E: shift arithmetic for a post of ten].

The check's value is not mainly in what it catches. It is that the family knows the absence will be noticed, and the son who has gone to the forest becomes a matter between the family and the state rather than between the family and the Front. That is the beginning of the infrastructure's exposure, and it is also the beginning of the family's danger (F002-9).

#### EC002-3 · The target folder

*P002a.* The Special Branch section of about 15 in each district [M002] keeps a folder on every village committee: the secretary, the finance and economy cadre, the security cadre, the youth and women's association heads, the guerrilla squad leader. A name enters the folder from the census, a defector, a prisoner, a captured document or a paid informant run by the Special Branch, not by the district chief [M002]. A name becomes a target only with three independent sources. An arrest in a held village is made by the constables at the post at night, with the Popular Forces outside; in a contested village by a Field Force platoon on a dawn cordon, its squads carrying M1 carbines and from 1965 an M79 grenade launcher each, moving at about 0400 from the district town by road or by sampan, cordoning the hamlet by first light, entering at dawn with the constable who knows the house, and out by mid-morning, before the local-force company can react. A district section working well can prepare about two to four target folders to the three-source standard a week and execute about one or two arrests a week [E: Malayan Special Branch case loads scaled to a district section]. The arrested man is before a magistrate within 72 hours and in the province detention centre within a week [M002]. The output that matters is not the arrest count but the number of village committees whose finance and security cadres have been taken and not replaced within a season, and by that measure a good district section takes about one village committee out of action every two months [E: the same case loads].

The concept depends on helicopters in one case: the dawn arrest in a village off the roads and canals, where a UH-1B lift of two ships with a Field Force squad replaces the road move. Without them it degrades to arrests only where roads and waterways reach, about two-thirds of the Delta's villages and fewer in the Centre [E: the period's road and canal net].

#### EC002-4 · Resource control on roads and waterways

*P002a.* Checkpoints on the main roads and canals at the edge of held territory: Route 4 from Saigon to Can Tho, Route 1 along the coastal plain, the canals of the Delta where they leave the held belt for the Plain of Reeds and the U Minh. Each is a fixed post of about 12 constables with a Popular Forces section, inspecting cargoes by day and closed by night [E: the Malayan checkpoint pattern]. The rice is counted against a family's ration card, medicine against a pharmacist's register, batteries and radios against a licence [M002]. A checkpoint on a busy canal inspects about 200–400 sampans a day, which is too many to search properly [E: Delta canal traffic of the period], so the Special Branch's informants pick the boats that matter.

It is the only employment concept in either provision that touches the main force, and it does so through his stomach and his medicine chest. Its failure mode is predation: a checkpoint is the most lucrative post a policeman can hold, and the toll gate is the commonest corruption in every police force that has run one [M002]. Its pairing with the Commission (P001b) is therefore not a nicety but its condition of use.

#### EC002-5 · The district operations room

*P002a with P002b.* Every week, on a fixed day, the district chief convenes his police chief, his Special Branch officer, his Regional and Popular Forces commanders, his deputy for administration and the American district adviser in the district operations room. They go through the district village by village: where the posts are, where the Popular Forces slept last week and will sleep next, which target folders are ready, which hamlets the Inspectorate visited, which land titles are disputed, and which villages are next for a post. The meeting takes two to three hours and produces the following week's night plan [E: the Malayan district war executive committee]. Its minimum viable package is a chief in his post for at least six months and a police station with a working Special Branch section; without the section it is the old district staff meeting, and without a chief who stays it is a new meeting every few months with nobody remembering the last.

This is the concept that turns two provisions into one district government. It costs nothing but the chief's attention, and it is where I expect the war in the countryside to be won or lost district by district.

#### EC002-6 · The night inspection

*P002b.* An Inspectorate team of four [M002] arrives at a district town without notice, typically by road in the late afternoon or by a VNAF O-1 Bird Dog or Air America liaison flight to the province capital and on by jeep. It spends the first night going to the district's hamlets with the American district adviser or the province police, counting the Popular Forces where they are actually sleeping rather than where the chief's map says they are. The next two days are the district's books, its land titles, its detention register and its complaints. The team writes a report of about ten pages within a week and the Board publishes a summary within a month [M002]. Each team inspects about one district a week, so 30 teams inspect about 30 districts a week and every district about once a year, with about four unannounced night visits a year per district [M002].

What matters is the first removal. An Inspectorate whose findings remove no one is a ritual within a year. The Board's first removals for cause, which I expect in late 1964 and the first half of 1965, are the provision's real test [M002].

#### EC002-7 · The Field Force as infantry (abandoned)

*P002a.* In the summer of 1964, with the ARVN's battalions committed around Saigon and the VC mobile-war campaign rising, corps commanders will ask to put Field Force companies into the line as light infantry, and some province chiefs will do it without asking [E: the historical pressure that MAAG applied to the Civil Guard and the use of the Field Force after 1966]. A company of 150 with carbines and automatic rifles is a weak infantry company and a strong police unit, and used as infantry it loses men it cannot replace from a training pipeline built for policemen. The provision's own failure modes name militarisation as the commonest end of a rural police [M002]. I expect the practice to be abandoned within a few months, after a Field Force company is mauled in a sweep in Binh Duong or Hau Nghia, and to be forbidden by a Directorate order that the corps commanders will resent. The constraint it names is one the dossier did not surface: in the Republic's crisis of 1964 every armed man is pulled toward the battle, and only an explicit rule, enforced from Saigon, keeps the police doing police work.

#### EC002-8 · Census by sweep (abandoned)

*P002a.* The obvious shortcut: put the census teams in with an ARVN battalion's cordon-and-search of a contested village, register every adult in a day under the battalion's guard, and leave. It produces cards fast and is worse than useless. The cadres register under their own names or false ones; the registration gives them valid cards; the teams leave at dusk with the battalion; and the census of a village the government does not hold at night is a list the Front can take from the village office the following week [E: the logic of EC001-4 and the Can Giuoc killings (EV001-3)]. The concept is tried in the first months in the provinces around Saigon, where the Hop Tac plan of mid-1964 [H] creates pressure for visible results, and dropped once the first cards turn up on dead guerrillas. The rule it leaves is simple: no census without a settled post.

#### EC002-9 · Arrest quotas and rewards by head (abandoned)

*P002a.* Proposed in the first year by those in Saigon and Washington who want the infrastructure's destruction measured, and rejected before it starts. Quotas produced false arrests and padding in the historical Phoenix programme [M002], and a reward per head produces the same and worse: the debtor denounced by his creditor, the feud settled with a warrant. Rewards are paid instead for information leading to a conviction, to informants run by the Special Branch, which is slower and is the Malayan practice [M002]. The pressure for a number will not go away, and it will come back whenever Washington asks how the war against the infrastructure is going (F002-11).

#### EC002-10 · Civilian chiefs in contested districts (abandoned)

*P002b.* The provision allows civilian chiefs only in districts rated secure [M002], and in its first year there will be pressure, from the aid mission and from reformers in Saigon, to extend them into contested districts as a sign that the government is civilian. Tried in a handful of districts in early 1965, it fails for the reason the dossier gives: the district chief commands the Regional and Popular Forces, and a civil servant cannot do it in a district under attack, both because the territorial officers will not obey him and because he does not know how [M002]. Some of those civilians will be killed. The concept is abandoned, and the civilian becomes the deputy for administration beside a military chief, which is where his skills are worth most.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**F002-2** (SUBVERT, SECURE) **The infrastructure leaves the held village, and some of it goes to the town.** Denied anonymity in a village with a post and a census, the village committee does what every clandestine organisation does under police pressure: it moves out of reach and works in. The legal cadres, men who lived openly in the village and did the committee's work by day, go underground, which makes them less useful to the Front and less visible to me. The committee itself moves to a hamlet the government does not hold, or to the field huts and the edge of the forest, and comes into the held village only at night, through a guerrilla escort, for specific tasks. The tax collector stops living among his taxpayers and starts visiting them. That is a real gain: an infrastructure that visits governs less well than one that lives there, and every visit is a risk the Special Branch can wait for. It is also a real loss, because the man the constable could watch is now a man he cannot see.

Part of the infrastructure goes the other way, into the towns. Saigon, Cholon, Can Tho and the province capitals are where a man without a reliable card has always been safest, and the census will reach the towns last and least well, because their populations move [E: the census sequence in D5 of Part 1]. The Saigon–Gia Dinh special zone committee and its sapper and assassination units already live in the city; the police reform pushes more of the Front's apparatus toward them. That is where the Special Branch is weakest and where the National Police has always worked as a city police: on the political opposition and on the generals. I put the displacement as major for the infrastructure in the held belt and moderate overall, because what moves to the cities is smaller than what is lost in the villages, and because in 1968 the cities are where it will matter.

**F002-3** (ADVISE) **The price of a province moves from the appointment to the examination, the command and the contract.** A post that cannot be bought is not a post that cannot be profited from. With the appointment closed, the patronage system moves to the places the list does not cover: the regimental and divisional commands that the corps commanders still fill, the customs posts and port offices, the district police posts themselves, and the province's contracts for construction, rice and fuel, which a chief who did not pay for his post still controls. The examination becomes the new point of purchase, as the dossier foresaw [M002]; the first leak is a matter of when, not whether. And the corps commanders, who lose the province chief as a client, gain influence over him in another way, because they still command his military operations and can make his district ungovernable [M002]. The displacement is moderate. Corruption that has to work through a contract audited by the Commission (P001b) and a chief who expects to stay is more expensive and more visible than corruption that was the price of the post.

### Bottleneck migration

**F002-4** (SUBVERT, FIND) **Against the infrastructure, the constraint moves from knowing who to doing something lawful about it.** Before the provision the binding constraint on the attack on the infrastructure was identification: no one knew who the village committee was. In the villages the government holds, the census, the post and the Special Branch section break that constraint within about a year [E: the occupation schedule]. The new constraint is at the back of the chain, in three places. The first is trained Special Branch officers who can run informants and interrogate without torture: the provision trains about 7,500 by 1966 [M002], but a good handler takes two years to make, and in 1964–65 the sections will be staffed by men who learned in Diem's political police. The second is the courts: a magistrate's review within 72 hours and trial for the serious cases require judges and prosecutors that the provinces do not have, and the Republic's answer historically was administrative detention without trial under the An Tri procedure [H], which the provision caps at two years with six-monthly review [M002]. The third is detention space: the province prisons of 1964 were built for criminals, and Con Son is where political prisoners went [H].

This is the memorandum's most useful bottleneck, because it names what to buy next. A police reform that identifies the infrastructure and cannot try it will produce either full prisons and released cadres or, under pressure, the summary killing that the historical programme drifted into. The next provision against the infrastructure should be judges, prosecutors and detention under review, not more constables. I put it as major.

**F002-5** (ADVISE, SECURE) **In the provinces the constraint moves from the chief to the men above and below him.** Before the provision the binding constraint on provincial government was the chief himself: chosen for loyalty, rotated by coups, and paid less than he could take. The list removes the worst of that within two years [M002]. Two constraints are exposed. Above the chief, the corps commander, who still commands his military operations and who did not choose him; in I and II Corps in 1964–65, where corps commanders are powerful and politically ambitious, a list-appointed chief can be made to fail. Below him, the village chief and the village council, who are still elected or appointed in the old way, who are the Front's first targets, and whom no list reaches. In 1966 the constraint shifts again, to the candidate pool. The first list draws from perhaps 900–1,200 credible candidates for about 620 posts [M002]; the second list, filling the vacancies left by promotions, deaths and removals, draws from the remainder, and the third from men the first two passed over. The quality of the chiefs will fall in 1966–67 unless the National Institute of Administration and the officer corps produce more candidates than the war consumes. That makes the course at Vung Tau and the Institute's output the long-run constraint, and I would expand both now. I put this as moderate.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F002-6** (ADVISE, SECURE) **The scarce thing these provisions consume is able men, and the army pays for them.** Neither provision consumes much of what is usually scarce in this theater. Helicopter hours for the Field Force's dawn arrests and the inspectors' visits are perhaps 300–500 hours a month by 1965 [E: two-ship lifts for about a fifth of arrests and liaison flights for the Inspectorate], a small fraction of what the US Army's aviation companies fly. The American personnel, about 610, are visible only to the aid mission's budget. What the provisions consume is officers and young men.

The list takes about 290 chiefs and about 330 deputies, most of the chiefs from the ARVN's majors, lieutenant colonels and captains [M002]. Those are the same officers who command the battalions. The examination selects for ability, and the ablest captains and majors are disproportionately the ones the ARVN's divisions most need; my advisers in the 7th and 21st Divisions will say so by the summer of 1964. I estimate that the list takes 80–120 officers in 1964–65 who would otherwise have commanded battalions or served as regimental executive officers [E: the share of chief posts filled from serving field-grade officers of above-average reports]. That is a real loss to the divisions in the two years when they are being beaten at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai. I accept it, because a district chief who holds his villages at night does more for the war in 1964–65 than one more competent battalion commander does in a sweep, but I record that it is a cost and that the divisions will feel it.

The police takes about 50,000 young men between January 1964 and the end of 1966 [M002]. Historically police service was a haven from the draft and the police ranks filled with men avoiding the army [H]. The provision's training standard and pay attract better recruits than the historical police did; they are also men who might otherwise be in the Popular Forces of their own villages or the ARVN. In a country mobilising toward about 514,000 men under arms [M001] and losing about 73,000 a year to desertion [M001], 50,000 policemen are a noticeable draw on the same age group. I put the whole opportunity cost as moderate.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F002-7** (SECURE, ADVISE) **The province turnover of 1964–65 stops, and the late programmes of 1966–69 become refinements rather than inventions.** On the historical course every change of government in Saigon from November 1963 to June 1965 replaced most province chiefs and many district chiefs, so that the pacification apparatus had no continuity at all [M002]. If the Board survives Khanh's coup of 30 January 1964, which is the provision's first test and comes before its first list is published [M002], that turnover is erased for the rest of the period: the coups of September 1964, December 1964, February 1965 and June 1965 change governments and leave the provinces alone. That is worth more to the countryside than any provision of equipment. The generals' attention that went into placing their men in the provinces after each coup goes somewhere else, and I expect it to go into the commands the list does not cover (F002-3), which is a cost, and into the Board itself, which is the risk the counter-directional finding addresses.

The police reform erases four later inventions. The Police Field Force, created historically in January 1966 [M002], exists from 1964. The extension of the police to the villages, which historically came only in 1969 [M002], begins in 1964. The ICEX programme of June 1967 and Phung Hoang of December 1967 [H] no longer have to create a coordinating apparatus, because the Special Branch already owns the files; they become, if they come at all, an American push to add the CIA's Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the army's intelligence to a Special Branch system that exists. And the gap between 1964 and 1969 in which the infrastructure in the government's own held belt went untouched, taxing and recruiting under the noses of the district towns, is closed in the districts where the posts settle. I put the erasure as major, because it removes four years from the most important programme of the population war.

### Perverse effect

**F002-8** (SECURE, SUBVERT) **The Front kills the best men first, and an overrun post arms it.** A competent district chief and a resident constable are worth more to the government than an incompetent chief and a policeman who lives in the town, and for exactly that reason they are worth more to the enemy dead. The Front's security sections have always chosen targets by effectiveness: the village chief who works, the teacher who is respected, the official who is honest [H]. The list gives them a published ranking. I expect the assassination of district chiefs and their deputies, of police post commanders and of Special Branch officers to rise sharply from mid-1964, and the enemy to choose districts whose chiefs stand high on the list. A wave of such killings in one district leaves the post to whoever will take it, which is the failure the dossier names [M002].

The posts add a second perverse effect. Each holds about ten carbines and an automatic rifle, a radio, ammunition and the families of its constables. In the first ninety days a post in a lightly contested village is the easiest target the Front's local-force companies have in 1964, and each one taken arms a guerrilla platoon and tells every other village that the government's men can be killed in their beds. I estimate 60–120 posts overrun or abandoned in 1964–65, most in the provinces around Saigon and in Quang Ngai and Binh Dinh, with perhaps 400–900 constables killed [E: a loss rate of 5–8% of posts a year in their first year, against the period's loss of isolated outposts]. That is a moderate effect against a programme of 1,500 posts, and its weight depends entirely on whether the Popular Forces sleep beside the post (EC002-1).

**F002-9** (SUBVERT, SECURE) **The census is a list of loyalties, and lists can be captured.** The family census, the land register and the Special Branch files together make the most complete record of the Republic's rural population ever compiled: who lives in each house, who holds which title, whose son is in the forest, who informs. In a district the government holds, that record is how the infrastructure is found. In a district that falls, it is how the government's people are found. A district town overrun for a night, as several will be in 1964–65, can lose its identity office and its Special Branch section's files, and the Front will use them exactly as it used the posted claimant lists at Can Giuoc (EV001-3). The danger is greatest at the end of the war: when the Republic falls, on whatever timeline, the census and the Special Branch files will be in the province capitals for the victors to read, and they will hand over the names of every informant and every family that sided with the government [E: the logic of the record; historically the police files fell largely intact in 1975 [H]]. The provision's rule that files never leave the Special Branch [M002] protects against leaks, not against capture. A destruction plan for the files of every district and province, ordered now and rehearsed, is the remedy. I put this as moderate now and as a heavy weight on the end of the war, whenever and however it comes.

**F002-10** (SECURE) **A chief who is good is promoted away from the district that needed him.** The list promotes through itself: a district chief who does well is the natural candidate for a province, and a province chief for a corps staff or a regional directorate [M002]. Every success removes a man from the place where he succeeded, and his successor starts the eighteen-month learning curve again. The two-year minimum limits it, and the deputy for administration carries continuity, but in 1966–67 the best districts will be the ones that lose their chiefs. A rule that a chief is promoted in place, by grade and pay, for a third year, is cheap and should be in the Board's first regulations. I put this as minor.

### Anticipation

**F002-11** (SUBVERT, FIND) **The infrastructure declines contact with the constable, and the arrest count falls as the war improves.** The enemy's principal adaptation in this theater is to decline battle, and the infrastructure's version of declining battle is to stop being where the police are. Within a year of a post settling, the village committee has moved out (F002-2), its legal cadres have gone underground, and the Special Branch section that took two village committees in its first six months takes almost none in its second, because there is almost no one left in the village to take. The metric that Washington will ask for, infrastructure arrested and convicted, rises in the first year, peaks, and falls in exactly the districts where the provision has worked best. Arrests climb only in the contested belt beyond the posts, where the chain is weakest.

This is an improvement in the war that makes a metric worse, and I say so plainly in advance. The measures that show it are the ones the enemy cannot help generating: tax receipts in captured ledgers, the age of captured guerrillas, the number of village committees that captured documents show to be functioning, and the night checks that find the booklet and the house in agreement [M002]. A rising count of arrests in a district two years into the programme is a sign of failure, not success. If Washington is given the arrest count, it will demand quotas (EC002-9), and quotas will produce the false arrests that rebuild the Front's case. I put this as major, for the same reason the anticipation finding on titles was major in the first memorandum: it decides whether the provision is measured honestly.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F002-12** (SUBVERT) **Hanoi reads the police correctly within months, and the provinces wrongly for a year.** There is nothing covert about either decree; both are read on Radio Saigon on the arrival date. The Front's leaders know what a rural police and a Special Branch did to the Malayan Communist Party; they studied it, and the Lao Dong's own security apparatus is a police state's [H]. I expect COSVN to issue a directive by about March 1964 that names the rural police as a priority target above the Popular Forces, and to propagate it to the district committees by mid-year, about three to four months from COSVN to a district cell in the Delta [E: the period's courier times and the speed of the 1964 directives on the land reform (EV001-3)]. Its content will be right in three respects: kill the constables in their first months before they know the village; put agents into the Special Branch, which is recruiting fast and vetting slowly; and fight the census by confiscating identity cards in the villages the Front reaches, so that a family without a card becomes suspect to the government and cannot leave for the town, which was the Front's historical practice against the 1959 card [H].

It will be wrong in two. It will treat the police as an American instrument, a "CIA police," and its propaganda will say so, which is partly true of the funding and false of the men; a constable who is the son of the next village is a poor target for that line, and the propaganda will work better in the towns than in the villages. And it will read the provincial reform as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, which on the evidence of 1963–64 is a reasonable reading, and it will take the Front about a year, until the first list-appointed chiefs have held their districts through a coup, to see that the chiefs are not changing. When it does see it, the conclusion it draws is the one that hurts: the competent chiefs are the targets (F002-8). What the enemy gets right about this capability is that it is slow and that it depends on Saigon. The Front can wait for a coup that captures the police, and it can do much to help one along. I put this as major.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F002-13** (SUBVERT) **The cheap counters are penetration and card confiscation; the expensive one is leaving the village.** Penetration of the Special Branch is very cheap: one well-placed agent in a province command costs the Front almost nothing and can hand it the informant list. The Malayan Special Branch was penetrated, and the Republic's police has had the enemy's agents in it for a decade [M002]. Confiscating cards is cheap where the Front holds the night and costs it a little goodwill. Killing constables is cheap in a post's first months and becomes expensive when the post has a Popular Forces platoon beside it and a reaction behind it, because an attack then costs a local-force platoon, not a security cell. The expensive counter is the one the police forces on the infrastructure in every village where it works: leaving the village. An infrastructure that visits instead of living with its people taxes less, recruits less and governs less, and its cadres spend their nights moving rather than organising. That cost is real and it accumulates.

The exchange therefore depends on the night, as it did for the land programme. Where the government holds the hamlets after dark, the Front pays the expensive price and the Republic wins the exchange at about the cost of a constable's pay. Where it does not, the Front pays the cheap price and the Republic loses posts and constables. Against the provincial reform the cheap counter is assassination and the cheaper one is a coup; the Front does not have to pay for the second at all. I put this as moderate.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F002-14** (ADVISE) **The loop through Washington runs through the words "secret police," and through honest bad news.** Theater effect: an American-funded police with a Special Branch, a national fingerprint register and administrative detention. American political effect: a target for every critic of the war, first in the press and later in Congress. The historical police programme drew its heaviest fire late, in the Phoenix hearings and the Con Son tiger-cage photographs of 1970 [H]. This one starts five years earlier and is larger, so the fire comes earlier, probably from 1966, when the war becomes a domestic issue and a Saigon government uses its police against the Buddhists (F002-16). The return path is the aid mission's Public Safety appropriation and Congress's tolerance for funding it; a cut would starve the identity system's card stock and the Americans who train the Special Branch, the two things the Republic cannot yet supply [M002]. The safeguards, magistrate review and capped detention, are the provision's defence in this loop as well as in the village, and their enforcement matters to Washington as much as to Long An.

The second loop runs the other way. A chief who does not expect to lose his post for bad news reports bad news. The Inspectorate checks returns against measures the chief does not produce [M002]. Together they mean that the province reporting of 1964–65 is worse than the historical reporting was, because it is truer, in the months when Washington is deciding whether to send combat troops. That shortens the credibility gap later (POSTNA-5) and makes the case for American intervention in 1965 harder to argue down; it does not, on my reading, change the decision, which was driven by the main-force crisis, not by province returns. I put the whole loop as moderate.

### Open channel

**F002-15** (SECURE, ADVISE) **The identity card makes the Republic's conscription work, and the Front's harder.** A mechanism the nine channels do not name: manpower. The Republic in 1964 cannot find its draft evaders or its deserters, because a young man who leaves his unit or ignores his call-up and goes home, or to another province, has no reliable identity and no one who will report him. The census and the card change that in the held areas. A deserter at home is now a man listed in a booklet, checked at night, and known to a constable; the Muster Service's roll (P001b) and the police register name the same man [M001]. The deterrent is modest, because constables are reluctant to arrest the sons of their own villages, and because the punishment of deserters was historically light and often skipped. I estimate it reduces desertion by perhaps 5–10% in the Delta's held provinces once the census is complete, from 1966 [E: the share of deserters who historically returned to their home villages].

It works the same way on the enemy's conscription. The Front's draft of young men in the villages it reaches by night becomes visible in the villages the government holds, because a son who goes to the forest is an absence in the booklet, and the family is visited. That raises the price of conscription in the contested belt and, at the margin, pushes it toward the villages the Front holds outright, which are already drained [E: the logic of the census]. I put the whole channel as moderate.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F002-16 · Counter-directional** (SECURE, ADVISE) **The Republic builds the instrument its worst government will use, and makes the provinces the prize of every coup.** The obvious direction of these provisions is a better, more lawful government in the countryside. The effect against it is that they build, in a coup-prone state, a centralised police with a national register and a Special Branch, and a Board that decides who governs every province, and both are prizes. The police under Diem was Nhu's political instrument, and Khanh arrested its chief on the day of his coup [M002]. In 1966 the Director General of National Police, General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, led the forces Ky sent to crush the Buddhist Struggle Movement in Da Nang and Hue [H]. In this war the police Loan inherits is larger, better organised, has a Special Branch with files on every province, and has a census that tells it who lives in the pagoda quarter of Hue. If it is used in 1966 as the historical police was, it will be used more efficiently, against Buddhists and students rather than against the Party, and it will cost the government more in the cities of I Corps than the historical repression did. The same is true of the Board: a junta that captures it gets a list of every chief in the country and a lawful reason to replace them.

Its true weight is moderate, not major. The primary effect of the provisions, a district government that can find the infrastructure and hold its hamlets, is larger and lasts longer than any one coup's misuse of them, and the historical repression of 1966 happened without them. But it is a real risk, it is the risk the enemy most wants to see happen, and the remedy is political: an American line, drawn now and held at the first test, that the Special Branch's files on the Buddhists and the generals are not its business, and that a government which uses the police against the pagodas loses the Public Safety programme. I would rather draw that line in January 1964 than discover in May 1966 that I should have.

**F002-17 · Strongest positive** (SECURE, SUBVERT) **The Republic acquires a government in the district four to five years early, and the infrastructure in the government's own belt is ground down before 1968 instead of after it.** Historically the infrastructure in the districts the government held by day taxed, recruited and organised under the noses of the district towns until 1968–69, when the losses of the Tet offensives, the Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix finally reached it; communist cadres later called 1968–72 their hardest years [H]. In this war the means that hurt it then, a police in the villages, a Special Branch that owns the files, and a chief who stays, exist from 1964–65 in the held belt. By the end of 1966, in the 1,500 villages with settled posts, I would expect the village committees to have been driven out of perhaps half and degraded in most of the rest [X-002], a judgment built from the occupation schedule and the target-folder case loads of the third employment concept, discounted for penetration and the cadres' withdrawal. That is roughly a third of the Republic's villages, and they are the ones with most of its rice and people.

Three things follow, each larger than the provision's direct result. The land programme becomes deliverable: a titled family in a village with a post and a chief who keeps the Popular Forces in the hamlets has a reason to keep its title and someone to tell when it is threatened, which is the protection the Can Giuoc title holders lacked (EV001-3). The Front's tax and recruitment base shrinks toward the villages it holds outright, which are the poorest and the most drained. And the American divisions of 1965–67 arrive in a countryside where the districts can tell them which villages matter. In combination with the land programme, this is the first provision to touch the third gap at its root (G-3), and it is transformative for the population war in the held and contested belt. It does nothing to the main force or the conventional war, and its value in any village equals the night security beside it. Within that limit I will not understate it: it is the instrument the theater needs assessment said was missing.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

The district adviser's job changes more than anyone's. His counterpart now stays, and his reports now have somewhere to go that is not the corps commander. I will direct province and district advisers to report on their chiefs to the Inspectorate through the province senior adviser, as one input among several, and to stop the informal practice of lobbying the corps for a chief's removal; the Inspectorate is the lever now, and a second American channel would discredit it. MACV J-2 gains a new source on the infrastructure in the Special Branch's product, and loses the monopoly it never really had; I will put a liaison cell of about a dozen officers into the Directorate's Special Branch headquarters in Saigon rather than demand that the files come to us. The advisory effort's quality problem (G-6) is unchanged by these provisions, but its continuity problem is halved.

### US conventional forces

The American divisions of 1965–67 arrive in a countryside in which, in the held and contested belt, a district can tell a brigade which villages matter and which cadres feed which base area (the find-to-fix chain above). A brigade of the 1st or 25th Infantry Division operating in Binh Duong or Hau Nghia in 1966 should have a Special Branch liaison at its operations centre, and the rear-service leads the police produce are worth acting on within hours. US units must also learn to leave the police alone: an American cordon-and-search that detains a village's men without the Special Branch present spoils a year of the post's work in a morning. I will write that rule into the III MAF and Field Force orders before the divisions arrive.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

Little changes. The CIDG camps of the 5th Special Forces Group sit mostly in the highlands and along the borders, among Montagnard and minority populations whom the census reaches last and the police barely at all. The camps' civic action and intelligence nets in the lowland districts near them, in Tay Ninh and the Plain of Reeds, should share with the district Special Branch, and will be told to.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

The divisions lose some of their best majors and captains to the list (F002-6) and gain, from 1965, a police that can take over the population control tasks they did badly: checkpoints, detention, the screening of men taken in sweeps. Their intelligence sections and the Military Security Service lose the primacy on the infrastructure they never used well, and will resist [M002]; the Joint General Staff must order them to pass every prisoner and document from the villages to the Special Branch within 48 hours, and the musters and the Commission (P001b) give me the means to see whether they do. The division commanders also lose their hold on the province chiefs who used to be their clients, which is a grievance (F002-16).

### RF/PF and territorial security

This is where both provisions land hardest. The Popular Forces' place at night is decided by a chief who expects to stay and to be inspected (EC002-6), and the night inspection counts them in the hamlets rather than in their posts. Their squads now share each held village with a police post, and the division of labour must be explicit: the Popular Forces hold the village at night and patrol outside it; the constables keep the post, know the families, do the census checks and make the arrests [M002]. The Regional Forces companies become the district's reaction force for the posts as well as the hamlets, which adds to the demand on them that the land programme's escorts already make (F001-6). The territorial forces' strength on paper is unchanged by these provisions; their use is transformed in the districts with a good chief, and unchanged in the rest.

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. Neither provision touches the air war against the North or its political management.

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged in the first order. The resource-control checkpoints (EC002-4) interdict the in-country supply of rice, medicine and cash, which is the main force's southern sustainment, not the Trail's tonnage, and the effect is small before 1966.

### Arc Light

Unchanged. The B-52F strikes that begin the Arc Light campaign on 18 June 1965 [H], and the B-52D campaign that follows, strike base areas no police reaches. The Special Branch's rear-service leads may refine a target box occasionally; that is not a change in employment.

### Airmobile formations

A small new demand. The Field Force's dawn arrests in villages off the roads and canals, and the inspectors' movements, take perhaps 300–500 helicopter hours a month by 1965 (F002-6), flown by UH-1B and later UH-1D companies on the same tasking as the land programme's survey parties and the muster teams (P001a, P001b). The 1st Cavalry Division, with its UH-1D and CH-47A battalions from September 1965, is unaffected.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The River Assault Groups' LCM-6 and LCVP craft carry Field Force platoons to Delta villages on the canals and support the canal checkpoints (EC002-4). The Junk Force and, from 1965, the Market Time and later Game Warden patrols gain a shore partner: a police that can check a sampan's cargo against a family's ration card and arrest on what it finds. The patrol boats' inspections of sampans become more useful once a Special Branch can tell them which boats matter.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Transformed, as erasure (F002-7). ICEX in 1967 and Phung Hoang in December 1967 [H] are no longer the creation of an apparatus against the infrastructure but additions to one that has worked since 1964–65 [M002]. My recommendation, when the Mission proposes them, will be to fold the Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the army's intelligence into the Special Branch's district sections, to keep the three-source rule, and to refuse quotas. The land register's use as a list of persons in 1968, recorded in the current Phase III chapter [M001], happens instead from 1964–65 through the census, with the Commission's sampling of neutralisation reports as the check on padding [M001].

### Third-country forces

The Australians bring the Commonwealth's police experience and about 40 British, Malaysian and Australian police advisers sit outside US spaces [M002]; from 1966 the 1st Australian Task Force in Phuoc Tuy can work with a provincial Special Branch on the Malayan pattern its officers know. The Korean divisions in II Corps, from late 1965, operate in coastal provinces where their cordon-and-search methods will collide with the police's, and the corps adviser must manage it. The US Marines' Combined Action Platoons in I Corps, from August 1965 [H], pair naturally with a village post: a Marine squad, a Popular Forces platoon and a constable in one village is the strongest hamlet security package in theater.

### Prior provisions

**P001a · Land-to-the-Tiller Programme.** Its employment changes in three ways. The title-and-militia concept (EC001-3) gains a third element, the constable, and in villages with a settled post the titled family has someone to report a threat to. Titling teams can now follow the post rather than the escort company, which reduces the land programme's demand for Regional Forces companies (F001-6) in the districts where posts exist. And the land register and the census become one household record, which is the strongest identification tool in the theater and the most dangerous list in it (F002-9).

**P001b · Commission Against Corruption.** Its replacement problem (F001-5) is largely answered by the list, which names a vetted replacement in weeks. It gains a large new jurisdiction in the police, whose checkpoints and identity offices are the commonest points of petty corruption, and it vets every candidate for the list. Its Muster Service's identity cards and the police's share kits and fingerprint practice, and its paymasters' practice of paying at the parade becomes the model for the police pay statute's independent paymaster.

Unchanged: none. Both earlier provisions change.
