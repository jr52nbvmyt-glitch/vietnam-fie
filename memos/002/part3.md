## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I now command is the same war on its borders and a different war in its villages, and the difference has grown. The first memorandum gave the Republic something to defend in the hamlets. This one gives it a policeman who lives there, a Special Branch that knows who the Party's men are, and a district chief who stays long enough to learn his district. Together they give the Republic, in the part of the country it holds at night, something it never had on the historical course until 1969: a government in the district. They do not give it a single battalion, and they do not move the date on which Saigon falls.

The war opens on 1 January 1964 with four decrees instead of two. Minh's council promulgates the land reform and the Commission Against Corruption, and with them a National Police rebuilt around the village and a Provincial Administration Selection Board that will choose every province and district chief by examination from a ranked list (EV002-1). The decrees are read on Radio Saigon that evening. The landlords protest the land decree; the officers who will lose their provinces say nothing yet, because the list does not exist yet.

Thirty days later Khanh takes power, as he did historically, and does what every coup-maker did: he detains the police chief and names his own men as acting chiefs in eleven provinces and districts. The difference is that this time the Americans have something to pull. The Aid Compliance Board gives notice that a province whose chief is not taken from the list will get no counterpart funds, the Director General, Vo Dinh Cuong, is back at his desk in three days, and Khanh decides that a Board he cannot staff is worth more to him as a reputation for reform than as a fight with the Embassy (EV000-1). The first list is published on 27 April. Nine of Khanh's eleven acting chiefs are gone by June. It is a partial failure of the reform's first test, and it sets the pattern that will hold for six years: each government that inherits the Board finds it cheaper to keep than to break.

Through 1964 and the first half of 1965 the Republic comes apart in Saigon exactly as it did. Khanh's charter, the September coup attempt, the High National Council, the fall of Khanh, Quat, and the arrival of Thieu and Ky all run on their historical dates. Two things in that sequence are new. In September the plotters' broadcast promises to restore the corps commanders' authority over the provinces, which means the end of the Board, and its failure within a day attaches the provincial reform, like the land decree, to the Young Turks who defeated it. And the Special Branch, which watches the generals as well as the Front, tells Khanh and the Embassy two days ahead that the 7th Division's battalions are moving (EV000-4). A police that is not one faction's instrument raises the price of a coup by a little. It does not stop one.

In the countryside the posts follow the titling line. The first is settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964; by July 1965 there are about 1,100, each a brick house with ten constables, a radio and, by rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village (EV002-2). The Front reads them correctly within weeks and tells its district committees to kill constables before they learn the village. Where the rule is broken it works: about 85 posts fall and about 410 constables die by July 1965, almost all of them in posts settled ahead of night security. Where the rule is kept, four posts in five that are attacked hold. The first harvest of the Special Branch is about 1,900 arrests on evidence, and the magistrates let a third of them go, which annoys the Branch and reassures me. The Front's own agent copies the Dinh Tuong informant cards for half a year before he is found, and eleven men die for it. The programme works where the government holds the night and is a list of targets where it does not, which is what its design said.

None of this touches the main-force war. Binh Gia, Song Be, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai go as they went. The PAVN regiments come down the Trail on the schedule their training set. Pleiku, Flaming Dart, Rolling Thunder and the Marines at Da Nang follow, and in July the President commits the Army. I would like to say that a better district government in the Delta changed that decision. It did not. The decision was made on the destruction of ARVN battalions in Phuoc Long and Quang Ngai, and nothing in this memorandum saved one of them.

The Front takes a year to understand the provincial reform. It reads the first list as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, which on the record of 1963–64 is a reasonable reading. By the summer of 1965 it can see that the new chiefs have stayed through three governments, and it draws the conclusion that hurts: the chiefs who keep their Popular Forces in the hamlets are its most dangerous enemies. Between August 1965 and June 1966 it kills nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs (EV002-3). The Board answers each death in about three weeks, from the same list, and the districts do not drift back to the towns while they wait. The Front never again kills chiefs at that rate, because it has killed the easy ones and the rest have learned.

The American war begins its full stride at the Ia Drang, and behind it the police war has its first complete success. Operation NIGHT LANTERN goes back in November 1965 to Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, the Long An districts where the Front shot title holders off a posted list in 1964 (EV002-4). A province chief and a district chief from the list, Long An's own Regional and Popular Forces, two Police Field Force companies, 140 constables and a Special Branch section of 45 settle 14 village posts in five months. On the night of 19 January 1966 the 506th Local Force Battalion comes back to Phuoc Lai, where the killings began, and is beaten off by a Popular Forces platoon, ten constables and an RF company that comes down the canal in sampans before two in the morning. Three weeks later a night check in Long Huu finds a card made out for an older man, and the thread leads to the district committee's security chief. By April the committee has moved to the edge of the Rung Sat and visits instead of living among its people, its tax take is a third of the year before, and the 400 titles held in the district office since 1964 are handed to their families in their own hamlets. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 lives. It did in six months what no sweep of Long An had done in six years, and it is the clearest single case of what these provisions are for.

The cost of the instrument comes due in the spring of 1966. Ky moves against Thi, the Struggle Movement takes Da Nang and Hue, and Ky removes Cuong by a decree his statute forbids and gives the police to Nguyen Ngoc Loan (EV000-26). The Embassy protests and does not pull the lever, because the Directory is the government the President has just embraced at Honolulu. Loan takes the Field Force into Hue with the census of the pagoda quarter in his hands, and about 1,300 people are detained. The magistrates' review, the one safeguard he cannot suspend without a decree the Americans have priced at the whole Public Safety programme, releases most of them by October. But the first American stories about an "American-built secret police" date from that June, three years ahead of the historical Phoenix stories, and a few dozen of Hue's student leaders, knowing that the census found them, go to the Front's city committee. That is the counter-directional finding made real. I had asked in January 1964 for a line drawn and held at the first test; it held in the provinces and failed in Saigon.

Through 1966 and 1967 the large operations run as they did: Masher/White Wing, Hastings, Attleboro, Cedar Falls, Junction City, Con Thien, Dak To. Each wins its battles and leaves the war as it found it. Beneath them the held belt is cleared. By 1967 the Special Branch's arrests there have fallen to about 40 percent of their peak, and the systems analysts in Washington read it as failure and ask for quotas (EV002-5). I tell them in writing that the count is falling because there is almost no one left in the villages to arrest. We win half the argument: quotas only beyond the posts. Where they run, false arrests follow, about ten complaints to the Commission for every one from the districts without them. And the cadres who left the villages have not vanished. They have gone to Can Tho, My Tho and the edges of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knows far less than one in a village of 2,000. When CORDS is created in May 1967 it finds a police of about 70,000 and a Special Branch with three years of files, and ICEX is built on the Branch's district offices instead of beside them (EV000-35).

Tet comes on the historical night and is decided where it was decided: in Saigon, at Hue, at Khe Sanh, and in Washington. In the held belt of the Delta the police changes the night in the way the land programme did, and along the same line. The urban sections know about a third of the legal cadres who surface to lead the uprising, and take about 1,700 of them in the first fortnight; the guides who were to bring the battalions into Can Tho do not arrive, because the night check took them two weeks before; and the 21st Division goes into the battle nearly at strength, because the census has made its deserters findable at home (EV000-42). Hue is the other side of the coin. The Front's lists there are better than they were historically, because its agent copied the Republic's own census and the Struggle's student leaders knew how it was kept, and police officers, constables, census clerks and their families are about a fifth of the identified dead (EV000-44). I do not raise the total. I say only that the record we built to find the Party's men was used to find ours, as I warned it would be, and that the Directorate orders the destruction plan for every file in the country within six weeks.

The President's decision of 31 March, Abrams, Paris, the bombing halt and Nixon's election run on their dates. Phoenix arrives in this war as a new name over an old institution: in the districts where the Special Branch has run target folders for three years or more, the Commission's audit finds about one claimed neutralization in ten unsupported; where the Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the sector intelligence officers run them, about one in two (EV000-54). Phoenix is the instrument of feud its critics described where the institution is youngest, and a refinement of four years' work where it is oldest.

In 1969 the Front concedes the villages as it conceded the land. COSVN's Resolution 9 tells the Delta's party committees to govern villages with settled posts by visiting cadres who sleep elsewhere, and to put their best security men into penetrating the Special Branch and the provincial administration (EV000-61). The countryside of 1969–71 is the best the government ever has, as it was historically, and better than that in the held belt. But the historical police grows into the same villages after 1969, and the gap this memorandum opened in 1964 narrows to experience and files. The American lever lapses with the withdrawal. Thieu does not abolish the Board; he replaces its two generals with his own officers and has his Prime Minister appoint the third name on the list for half the province vacancies (EV002-6). The list still excludes the incompetent and the bought. It no longer excludes the loyal and mediocre. The Phoenix hearings of July 1971 are less damaging than they were, because the Commission's audits and the magistrates' review give the administration something lawful to describe, and the Public Safety appropriation survives with a cut of about a tenth (EV000-72).

From there the war runs on its historical rails. The Easter Offensive of 1972 is fought by PAVN divisions with T-54s and 130mm guns against ARVN divisions and American air power, at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc, and a village constable is no factor in it. The Paris Agreement leaves the PAVN in the South. Congress writes the end of American combat into law and cuts the aid. In 1975 the blow falls at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, Thieu orders the Highlands abandoned, and on 30 April the PAVN enters Saigon. The Republic falls on the historical date, rung (a), collapse.

What this memorandum has changed is what the Republic was in the years it lived: a state that could govern its own belt at night five years earlier than it historically did, with a police that arrested on evidence and chiefs who were chosen for ability. What it has not changed is that the Republic died of a conventional invasion after the Americans left, and that no policeman in any village could have stopped the tanks. When the files of 1964 are captured in the province capitals in 1975, whether the thermite charges are fired will decide how many of the people who trusted the police survive the peace. That question lies after the war's last date, and I record it here because I signed the order that created those files.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The police and the provincial reform change Phase I only in the districts the government already holds, and only from the spring of 1964. The bottleneck was always going to be the first coup: a decree thirty days old, with no list yet published, met a coup-maker whose first instinct was to put his men in the provinces. The decree survived because the Aid Compliance Board's power over counterpart funds (P001b) gave it the one thing a Board without troops needed, a consequence for ignoring it. The interaction with the first memorandum is what makes these provisions work at all: the land programme's titling teams chose the villages, the posts settled behind them, and the Commission's lever protected the list (F002-1). Where all three met, as in the inner ring of Gia Dinh under Hop Tac, the hamlets held through the coups of 1964–65 even when the ARVN battalions assigned to the ring were pulled to Saigon.

The cost is shown in the casualty returns of the posts, and it falls exactly where the negative specification said it would. A post settled without its Popular Forces platoon, like Long Dinh on Route 4 on 3 October 1964, is a gift of nine carbines to the Front's guerrillas (F002-8). A post settled with its platoon and a reaction within the hour usually holds. The programme did not make the Popular Forces stay in the hamlets; it made the price of their absence visible within weeks, and the province chiefs from the list paid attention to that price, which the patronage chiefs had not.

The strongest effect in this phase is invisible in its own time. By the end of 1964 the Board's register had become the first honest record of what the Republic's officers could do, and by July 1965 about two-thirds of the chiefs had come through it. No battle in Phase I turned on it. Every district in Phase II did.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy answered the police quickly and correctly. The COSVN directive of March 1964 named the constables a priority target above the Popular Forces, and its three instructions, kill them early, penetrate the Special Branch, confiscate the cards, were the right counters (F002-12). The Dinh Tuong penetration, found in February 1965 after eleven informants were dead, showed the second working. Card confiscation worked in the villages the Front held at night, where a family without a card could not travel to the market town without being stopped, and so tied the population to the Front a little more tightly; in the held villages it was not possible.

The enemy answered the provincial reform slowly and wrongly for a year, because it read the list as another reshuffle. That error was reasonable. It cost the Front a year in which the new chiefs learned their districts unmolested.

The great decision of the phase was Hanoi's own and owed nothing to the police: the Ninth Plenum's line that victory must come before the Americans could intervene in strength, and the commitment of the 325th Division's regiments down the Trail. The provisions did not hasten it. The Front's district committees noticed the posts. The Politburo did not need to.

### Causal chains

No chain terminates in Phase I. C002-1 begins here, with the first list of 27 April 1964 that gave Long An a province chief from the examination; it lands in Phase II.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a police of about 45,000 growing to 70,000, about two-thirds of its chiefs from the list, and an enemy who has learned to kill constables and has not yet learned to kill chiefs. It also inherits the precedent of the first test, which is double: the Board survives a coup if the American lever is pulled at once, and the police chief can be detained by the coup's first patrol. The first half of that precedent holds until 1970. The second comes back in March 1966.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is where these provisions do their work, and the work is the clearing of the government's own belt. On the historical course the infrastructure in districts the government held by day taxed, recruited and organised under the noses of the district towns until 1968–69. Here it is driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, because the means that historically reached it after Tet, a police in the village, a Special Branch that owns the files and a chief who stays, exist from 1964–65 (F002-17). NIGHT LANTERN is the case in miniature, and the strongest positive effect of the memorandum grounded in a dated event: on 19 January 1966 at Phuoc Lai a Popular Forces platoon and ten constables, with a reaction that came down the canal in the dark, held the village where the Front had shot title holders eighteen months before, and in June the district committee's own ledger showed its tax take cut by two-thirds.

The interaction with the land programme is what turns this from a police success into a political one. A titled family in a village with a post and a chief who keeps the platoons in the hamlets has a reason to keep its title and someone to tell when it is threatened (F002-1). GOLDEN SHEAF in the western Delta had stopped at the line the Regional and Popular Forces could hold; with posts and the Special Branch behind it the line holds more firmly, though it does not move much further, because the police follows security and does not create it.

The bottleneck moved, as the second part said it would, from identifying the infrastructure to doing something lawful with it: trained Special Branch handlers, magistrates and detention under review (F002-4). Then it moved again, to the towns. The infrastructure that left the villages went to Can Tho, My Tho and the outskirts of Saigon, and the arrest count in the held belt fell by 60 percent from its 1966 peak (EV002-5). That is an improvement in the war that makes the metric worse, and I said so in writing on 3 April 1967 against the request for quotas. We won half of that argument. The half we lost produced the false arrests that quotas always produce, in the contested belt where the institution was youngest.

The counter-directional finding came true in the Buddhist crisis (F002-16). The police that Ky used in Hue was the one this decree built, with a census that told it who lived in the pagoda quarter. The repression was more efficient than the historical one, and its costs, the "secret police" stories in the American press and the students who went to the Front, were paid in 1966 and again in Hue in 1968.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's answer to the provincial reform, once it understood it, was the war on the district chiefs of 1965–66 (EV002-3). It cost the Republic nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs in eleven months, and cost the Front its best security cells, five of whose teams the Special Branch took within the year. It did not empty the districts, because the list filled each vacancy in about three weeks.

Its answer to the police was to leave the villages, which is the infrastructure's way of declining battle (F002-11). That was expensive. An infrastructure that visits instead of living among its people taxes less, recruits less and governs less, and its cadres spend their nights moving rather than organising (F002-13). It was also an investment the Front would draw on at Tet, because the cadres who left went to the towns.

<!-- initiative --> The Front's largest decision of the phase was not a response to anything this headquarters did. In December 1965 the Twelfth Plenum in Hanoi resolved to meet the American intervention with a main-force war and to seek decisive victories in the South, and through 1966 COSVN under Nguyen Chi Thanh carried that line out in its own way, upgrading guerrilla units to local-force battalions and local-force battalions to main-force regiments, and stripping village cadres and guerrillas to fill them [H]. It was driven by doctrine and by the politics of the Party: Thanh's conviction, against Giap's caution, that the Americans could be beaten in large battles in the South, and Le Duan's need to show Hanoi's allies a war that was being won [H]. In this war the upgrading had a consequence the Plenum did not intend. It took from the villages of the Delta, in 1966, the young guerrillas who were the infrastructure's protection against the posts, in the year the posts were being settled, and the 506th's failure at Phuoc Lai was in part a failure of a battalion that had lost its best companies to a regiment in War Zone C [X-002].

### Causal chains

**C002-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV002-1) The first list gives Long An a province chief and Can Giuoc a district chief chosen by examination, who stay → they plan NIGHT LANTERN around settled posts, the census and the target folder, and the Special Branch breaks the Can Giuoc district committee's security section in February 1966 → the district committee, unable to protect its cadres, calls on the 506th Local Force Battalion to destroy the posts by force, and the battalion fights at Phuoc Lai on ground the province had prepared, against a platoon with a reaction behind it → the one main instrument the Front had in Long An's rice districts was spent defending the infrastructure rather than preparing for the offensive, and the province committee moved it out of Can Giuoc to the edge of the Plain of Reeds, leaving the rice districts with no local-force cover at all.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits a held belt with almost no resident infrastructure, a Special Branch with four years of files and new urban sections that know a part of the cadres who have moved to the towns, a police of about 80,000, and chiefs who have mostly held their districts for two or three years. It also inherits three liabilities: a police directed since 1966 by a general who wanted quotas, a census of Hue that the Front has copied, and Hue students in the Front's city committee who know how the census is kept. The first two are mine to manage. The third I could not.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet is decided by the main forces and by Washington, and the police changes neither. What it changes is the offensive's grip on the towns of the held belt and the composition of the dead in Hue. In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the cadres who surfaced to lead the uprising had settled in the towns after leaving the villages, and the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of them (F002-2). The displacement that had made the arrest count fall in 1967 handed the Branch a harvest in February 1968. Where the guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later, and at Can Tho two of them had been taken in the night check a fortnight before. The census also made the Republic's own army a little stronger where it mattered: the 21st Division, whose deserters could now be found at home, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its roll (F002-15).

In Hue the mechanism ran the other way. The census and the Special Branch files were a list of loyalties, and the Front read it (F002-9). Its agent in the Hue identity office and the student leaders who had gone over in 1966 gave its security section houses rather than neighbourhoods, and police officers, constables and census clerks made up about a fifth of the identified dead. I do not count it as more killing. I count it as a more exact choice of whom to kill, made with the Republic's own record.

Phoenix comes into a countryside where its work had been done for four years in the held belt (F002-7). The Commission's audit shows that the programme was as good as the institution beneath it: one unsupported neutralization in ten where the Special Branch was old, one in two where the PRUs and the S2s ran the folders.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's urban network, swollen by the cadres who had left the villages, was its means of acting in the towns at Tet, and it was more exposed for having moved. Its Hue security section used what it had stolen with precision. After the offensives COSVN's own reporting began to describe the held belt as a region where the movement had "no resident apparatus" and could act only by visiting teams from the forest, a description that historically fitted only some provinces and only after 1969 [X-002].

The third offensive of August–September found fewer guides in the held belt still. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands, already lowered by the land programme, fell further where the census showed which sons had gone to the forest and the family was visited the next morning.

### Causal chains

**C002-2** [war-shaping] The police reform builds a family census of Hue that records every household, including the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university → in the Buddhist crisis of 1966 Loan uses it to detain about 1,300 people in Thua Thien and Quang Nam, and several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders, knowing they have been found, go to the Front's Hue city committee with what they know of how the census is kept → in 1967 the city committee places an agent in the identity office's records room and copies the census → in February 1968 the execution lists in Hue are drawn by house, and the Republic's own police, constables and census clerks are about a fifth of the identified dead, the instrument built to find the Party's men having been turned, through the Buddhist repression it enabled, against the men who built it.

**C002-3** [months] The family census reaches the home districts of the 21st Division's soldiers in 1966 → a deserter at home is listed in a booklet, checked at night and known to a constable, and the division's desertion rate falls → the division is close to its muster-roll strength in January 1968 and its reaction company is full → when the Popular Forces on the Xa No canal report the columns on the night of 30–31 January, there are men to send, and Can Tho, the corps headquarters, is cleared in about four days with far less of the town destroyed than historically.

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits a held belt without resident infrastructure, a Phoenix programme whose quality is measured by an outside auditor, a police of about 95,000 with every file under a destruction plan, and an urban Front apparatus badly reduced by its own surfacing. It also inherits the gap's narrowing: from 1969 the historical police grows into the same villages, and what this memorandum keeps after that is experience and files, not institutions the Republic would otherwise lack.

### Clock

The provisions' effect on American tolerance in 1968 is small and runs both ways. The image of Tet in the United States was the embassy wall and Loan's pistol in a Saigon street, and Loan in this war is the Director General of the police this headquarters asked Congress to fund; the "secret police" stories that began in 1966 had prepared the ground for that photograph. Against that, the province reporting that reached Washington in 1966–67 had been truer, because chiefs who did not expect to lose their posts for bad news reported it, and the credibility gap that Tet opened was opened a little less widely by the reports that had preceded it (F002-14). Neither effect moved a vote in Congress or a date in the President's calendar. The decision of 31 March stands on its date.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

In Phase IV the provisions' advantage over history shrinks, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964. The rural police extension, the Field Force, Phoenix and an accelerated police growth to about 100,000 come on the historical course in 1969–71, and the gap narrows to two things: chiefs who have governed their districts for five or six years instead of two, and Special Branch files that run back to 1964 (F002-7). The end vector of the phase reflects that: the infrastructure is weaker and better known than historically, by the same band the first memorandum left it, and no further.

The provisions meet the same limit the Commission met. The Board and the police survived four governments because the American lever made breaking them expensive, and when the lever lapsed with the withdrawal, Thieu captured them without breaking them (EV002-6). The list still excluded the incompetent; it stopped excluding the loyal. The Special Branch's Saigon headquarters turned toward the opposition, as every historical police chief had turned it (F002-16). Below the palace the posts, the census and the district sections went on, because they were too many and too local to be worth capturing.

### Enemy response and initiative

Resolution 9 conceded the villages with settled posts, as it conceded the land (EV000-61). The Front's answer was to visit rather than live, and to put its best security cadres into penetrating the Special Branch and the provincial administration (F002-13). That was the cheap counter, and it was well chosen for a war that Hanoi now intended to win by conventional invasion rather than by the infrastructure: an agent in a province Special Branch would be worth more in 1975 than a village committee in 1970.

The decision for the Easter Offensive, taken after Lam Son 719, is Hanoi's own and owes nothing to the police.

### Causal chains

**C002-4** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase II) The arrest count in the held belt falls from 1966 because the infrastructure has left the villages, and Washington asks for quotas → this headquarters and the Commission win quotas only for the contested belt and an outside audit of every neutralization from 1968 → the Commission's quarterly samples show unsupported neutralizations falling from about a third to about a sixth by 1971, and the magistrates' review gives the programme a legal frame → when Phoenix comes before the House subcommittee in July 1971, the administration has an independent count to set against the critics', and the Public Safety appropriation passes with a cut of about a tenth, so that the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers survive into 1972, which on the historical course they barely did.

### Downstream

Phase V inherits a countryside that is better governed and better known than history's, a Board and a police captured at the top by the President, and a Front that has bet on conventional invasion. None of it bears on the armoured columns of 1972. Phase VI inherits the files, under a destruction plan the Directorate has rehearsed since 1968.

### Clock

The provisions slow the clock slightly in these years and cannot stop it. The Phoenix hearings of July 1971 were less damaging than historically, and the police programme kept its appropriation, but the clock in 1969–71 ran on the withdrawal timetable, My Lai, Kent State and the Pentagon Papers, and none of those was touched. A better district government in the Delta did not bring one American home sooner or keep one there longer. It only meant that the country the Americans left was governed better in the villages than the one they left historically.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- null P5 -->
<!-- ripple-addressed -->
The provisions do not bite on 1972 because the Easter Offensive is a conventional invasion by PAVN divisions with T-54 tanks and 130mm guns, decided at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc by ARVN divisional leadership and B-52s. A constable has no answer to a tank, and the chiefs' competence does not reach the corps commanders who lost Quang Tri. The countryside's better governance means the Front's local forces add little to the offensive in the Delta, as they added little historically after 1968. Phase IV's end vector is unchanged by this memorandum, so nothing arrives in 1972 for the phase to answer.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- null P6 -->
The provisions do not change the end because the Republic falls to a conventional offensive after the aid cuts, at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, and to Thieu's order to abandon the Highlands. The police and the chiefs strengthen the Republic's hold on the villages in the ceasefire war of 1973–74, but that war was not where the Republic was lost historically either, and I cannot show it changes a date. Their largest effect falls after 30 April 1975, outside the chapters: whether the destruction plan for the files is carried out decides how many of those who trusted the police survive the peace (F002-9).

## §4.H Historical comparison

The cumulative force has now changed four things from history, all of them in the countryside the government held at night, and none of them in the war of the main forces.

First, the Republic had a government in the district from 1964–65, four to five years before the historical one. Province and district chiefs came from a ranked list, stayed at least two years, and were replaced from the same list in about three weeks when the Front killed them; historically chiefs changed with every coup, and Thieu could not find competent replacements when he sacked 16 of them in 1968 [M002]. The held belt was cleared of resident infrastructure before Tet rather than after it, and NIGHT LANTERN shows the difference in one place: a district where the Front shot title holders in 1964 was a district where the Front's local battalion lost a night attack and its tax take fell by two-thirds in 1966 [X-002]. That is a substantial improvement over history, and in the held belt I think it is the largest single change either memorandum has made.

Second, the Republic's own records improved, and so did its reporting to Washington. The census, the Board's register and the Inspectorate gave the Republic true figures for its population, its officers and its provinces; the Commission's audits put an outside check on Phoenix from 1968, and the unsupported share of its claims fell from a third to a sixth by 1971 [X-002]. This is an improvement in the honesty of the war's measurements, which on this theater's record is worth a great deal.

Third, the costs were real and specific: about 410 constables killed by July 1965 and more after, nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs in 1965–66, a more efficient repression of the Buddhists in 1966, and in Hue in 1968 the Republic's own census turned into execution lists [X-002]. The police that was built to arrest on evidence was captured at the top by Ky in 1966 and by Thieu in 1970–71, exactly as the counter-directional finding predicted.

Fourth, nothing in the main-force war changed. Every battle from Binh Gia to Xuan Loc, every American decision from July 1965 to the aid cuts of 1974, and the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 stand on their historical dates. No life was saved in the Easter Offensive or in the last campaign by these provisions. Lives were saved in the villages of the held belt in 1966–68, where an infrastructure that would otherwise have taxed, conscripted and executed was not there to do it; I cannot count them honestly and I will not invent a figure.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state remains rung (a), collapse, on the historical timeline. The two memoranda together have moved the deficit the Theater Needs Assessment named first, the political one, further than I expected: the Republic now gives its peasants land in 1964, governs its own belt with a police and chiefs chosen for merit from 1965, and has its army counted honestly. Under POSTNA-1 that is the precondition for any rung above (b), and it is now met in the Delta and the coastal lowlands the government holds.

It is not sufficient, and POS001-4 says why. The Republic fell to a conventional invasion after the Americans left, and a provision that clears the infrastructure does nothing to the PAVN's corps, the Trail, the sanctuaries or the American aid appropriation of 1974. Rung (b), dependent survival, would require that the Republic hold while American combat forces are present and fall within about two years of their leaving; it did hold while they were present, historically as here, and fell two years after they left, which is the historical outcome and is still called (a) because the date did not move. To make (b) or (c) reachable the cumulative force needs something these provisions are not: the means to stop the 1975 offensive, by the ARVN's leadership at corps and division, its mobility and its supply after the aid cuts, or by American air power that Congress no longer permits.

The war's shape has not outgrown its phases. The held belt now runs ahead of history inside every phase, but the phase boundaries are set by Hanoi's offensives and Washington's decisions, and neither has moved. I propose no redraw.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The Republic governs its own belt at night four to five years early.** Chiefs from the list, posts beside the Popular Forces and a Special Branch that owns the files cleared the held belt of resident infrastructure by 1967; on the historical course that happened in 1969–71. This is the largest change either memorandum has made, and it is a genuine one.
- **NIGHT LANTERN is the proof in one district.** Can Giuoc, where the Front shot title holders in 1964, was held in 1966 by its own platoons and constables, and the families got their titles back in their own hamlets.
- **The land programme becomes deliverable.** A titled family now has a constable to tell and a chief who keeps the platoon in the hamlet; the land reform of 001 and the police of 002 are one programme in the villages (F002-1).
- **The provinces survive the coups.** The Board outlived four governments to 1970 and filled vacancies in weeks; the districts no longer stop when Saigon changes hands.
- **The war's numbers are more honest.** The census, the Inspectorate and the Commission's audit of Phoenix gave Washington truer province reporting from 1964 and an outside count of Phoenix claims from 1968.
- **Tet in the held belt of the Delta went better again,** with urban cadres known, guides taken and the 21st Division nearly at strength.

### What has not changed

- **The main-force war,** every battle from Binh Gia to Xuan Loc, and the decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces.
- **Tet's political verdict and the American clock:** the decision of 31 March 1968, the withdrawal timetable and the aid cuts stand on their dates.
- **The coups and the capture of the police at the top:** Ky took the police in 1966 and Thieu the Board in 1970–71, as the counter-directional finding said; a stronger police was used more efficiently against the Buddhists.
- **Hue:** the Republic's own census fed the Front's execution lists in 1968.
- **The end:** the Easter Offensive, Paris, Phuoc Long, Ban Me Thuot and the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975; rung (a).
