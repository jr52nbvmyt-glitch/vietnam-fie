*COMUSMACV to CINCPAC · Saigon*

The provision names two things, and I have filed them as two: a reform of the Vietnamese National Police, and a reform of provincial administration that selects province and district chiefs on merit. Both are institutional provisions, so the sixteen fields are read in the institutional sense, and the reading is stated at the head of each field where it differs from the literal one. The message gives neither a date nor a scale. I have taken the campaign's opening date, 1 January 1964, as the arrival of each [U: arrival defaults to campaign_opens]. I have read "granted" as I read it for the land and anti-corruption decrees: the decree-laws are promulgated, the money is appropriated and the organisations exist in skeleton on that date [U]. I have not read it to mean that the police will be honest, that the generals will give up their provinces without a fight, or that the Viet Cong will leave a constable alone in a village at night; those are the analysis. A 2026 configuration of a police reform or a selection system is the design a practitioner of 2026 would bring, informed by sixty years of police-building and civil-service reform that succeeded and failed. Its equipment is the equipment of 2026 that works in 1964, which excludes anything that needs satellites, a data network or a mobile telephone, and I say where that matters.

These two provisions fill the gap the first memorandum left. The land programme and the Commission Against Corruption both depend on a police that will execute a warrant and protect a new owner, and on a province chief who wants them to work [M001]. On the opening date neither exists.

## §1.1 National Police Reform Programme, 1964 — P002a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read for an institutional provision: the law, the organisation that executes it, its target and its structure in the provinces.*

**Designation.** The National Police Reform Programme of 1964: a decree-law of the Republic of Vietnam promulgated on the arrival date [U], reorganising the Directorate General of National Police around the countryside rather than the cities. It has six parts. A resident rural constabulary puts a police post in every village and a police station in every district. A rebuilt Police Special Branch takes sole charge of intelligence on the Viet Cong infrastructure. The Police Field Force is raised two years earlier than it was historically. A national identity and family census re-registers every adult. A police statute fixes pay, careers and discipline. Judicial safeguards govern arrest, interrogation and detention.

**What it replaces.** The National Police was created by Diem's decree of June 1962, which merged the existing security and police agencies into one Directorate General of about 16,000 uniformed and plainclothes men [S1]. In January 1964 it is about 19,000–22,000 strong [S2] [S3]. It is a city force: its operations were confined to urban areas and were extended to the villages only in 1969 [S4]. Its head, General Mai Huu Xuan, is about to be arrested in Khanh's coup of 30 January [S8], and the force has been every regime's political instrument since 1955 [H]. The Americans called it the "white mice", for its timidity after Diem's fall [S3]. The Republic also has at least eighty security organisations, overlapping and competing [S7].

**The design.** The provision takes its design from three places. The first is Sir Robert Thompson's advice to Diem of November 1961: the population is protected by police, not by soldiers, and intelligence belongs to one organisation, ideally the police Special Branch [S7]. That advice lost to MAAG's view in 1962 [S7]; the provision adopts it. The second is Malaya, where the police grew from 11,285 men in 1948 to 30,000 regulars and 30,000 special constables, more than twice the army [S7], and where every civilian carried an identity card [S19]. The third is the record of police reforms since 1990. Georgia replaced its traffic police in 2004 with a new, better-paid patrol force recruited from outside the old one [S15]. The Patten reform of Northern Ireland policing (1999–2001) changed who joined the police and who could hold it to account [S16]. Afghanistan's police-building after 2002 is the warning: rapid numbers with little training produce a force that preys on the population.

The decree's articles are these [U: the provision's design].

1. **A rural constabulary.** Every village (*xa*) receives a police post of 8–12 constables under a police sergeant, resident in the village and housed with their families in a fortified station. Every district receives a police station of about 40 under a police lieutenant. Village constables are recruited in the province, not in the village, and posted outside their home district, so that the constable is known but not kin [E: the Malayan and Royal Irish Constabulary practice of posting outside the home locality].
2. **Special Branch primacy.** The Special Branch (*Canh Sat Dac Biet*) becomes the sole agency responsible for intelligence on the VCI, with a section in every district station. ARVN intelligence, the Military Security Service and the province's own intelligence services pass what they learn about the infrastructure to the Special Branch, which holds the files [U]. Main-force intelligence stays with the army.
3. **The Police Field Force.** A lightly armed mobile police formation of one company in each province and two battalions in national reserve, raised from 1964 rather than January 1966 [S5]. Its task is to clear and hold a village long enough for the constabulary to settle in, to reinforce a post under attack, and to make arrests in force [S2].
4. **Identity and census.** Every adult over 15 is re-registered with a new identity card that carries a photograph and two fingerprints, and every household with a family census booklet listing who lives in it. The 1959 identity programme already required cards [S6], and Diem used its registry against dissenters [S6]; the provision rebuilds it for a different purpose and puts safeguards on its use (article 6).
5. **A police statute.** Police pay is set at parity with an ARVN soldier of the same grade plus a rural hardship allowance and family housing at the post [U]. Pay is delivered by the independent paymaster of the Commission Against Corruption's Pay and Muster Verification Service, not by the policeman's commander [M001]. Promotion is by examination and record. Dismissal is for cause, through a police discipline board on which the Commission sits [U].
6. **Judicial safeguards.** An arrest must be reviewed by a magistrate within 72 hours [U]. Interrogation is recorded and torture is a criminal offence for the interrogator and his superior [U]. Administrative detention without trial (*an tri*) is decided by a Provincial Detention Review Board chaired by a judge, capped at two years and reviewed every six months [U]. The rule that each target of an operation against the infrastructure needs three separate sources of evidence, which Phoenix adopted only later [S14], applies from the first day [U].

**Manufacturer.** The Ministry of the Interior and the Directorate General of National Police, with the aid mission's Public Safety Division supplying advisers, money, equipment and training. A US Army military police and Criminal Investigation Division contingent supplies training teams. A small British, Malaysian and Australian contingent of serving and retired police officers advises the Special Branch and the Field Force [U].

**In-service date.** No reform of this design was made in the Republic. The historical reference points are the 1962 decree [S1], the Field Force of 27 January 1966 [S5], the extension of police services to the villages in 1969 [S4], and the force's peak of 103,859 in February 1971 [S1].

**Quantity.** One national programme. The National Police grows from about 19,000–22,000 in January 1964 [S2] [S3] to about 45,000 by the end of 1964, about 70,000 by the end of 1966 and about 95,000 by the end of 1968 [E: training throughput in D5]. That is faster than the historical growth in its first year and roughly level with it after, because the historical force reached 53,000 in December 1965 [S3] and 88,000 by 1970 [S2]. **The provision does not make the police much bigger than it became. It makes it rural five years sooner, gives it a Special Branch with the files, and pays and disciplines it.** That is the honest summary of the quantity.

**Organisation in theater.**

|---|---|---|---|

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

*Read as staffing: Vietnamese and American.*

**Vietnamese.** About 70,000 by the end of 1966 [E: D1], composed roughly as follows: about 24,000 in village posts [D], about 9,700 in district stations [D], about 6,700 in province commands [D], about 7,500 Special Branch [E: D1], about 9,000 Field Force [E: D1], about 2,400 in identity teams [D], and the balance in Saigon and the cities. The binding shortage is not numbers but literate, honest men of the right age who are not already in the army. Every one of them is of military age, and police service gives exemption from the draft [H]. In 1968 it cost $425 to buy a place as a secret-police agent as a haven from the draft [S10]. A police job that keeps a man out of the infantry is a thing that can be sold, and the provision's first defence against that is recruitment through a competitive examination with its results posted, the method the provincial reform uses for chiefs (P002b).

**Where the men come from.** Three pools [E: the period's manpower]. The best are former Civil Guard and Self-Defence Corps men with a record, transferred voluntarily. The largest is school-leavers from the province towns who pass the entrance examination. The third is Regional Forces NCOs released to the police at the rate of about 2,000 a year [E: a modest draw on a force of about 96,000 [M001]], which is the arrangement Thieu made in 1969 when he transferred 13,000 ARVN to the police [S4]. Women are recruited for the identity teams, records and searches of women at checkpoints [U]; in 1971 the force had 3,144 women [S1].

**American.** About 520 [E: the components below]. About 300 Public Safety advisers of the aid mission: two in each province, one in each of about 80 priority districts, and about 130 at the Directorate, the training centres and the identity register [E: against the 144 the Public Safety Division had in December 1965 [S3]]. About 150 Army military police and CID personnel in mobile training teams and at the training centres [E: twelve teams of about ten and centre staff]. About 70 contractor technicians for the identity system's enrolment kits, card production and the register [E: D5]. The British, Malaysian and Australian advisers, about 40 [U], are outside American spaces. For scale, Michigan State's police project at its height had 33 advisers [S6].

**Personnel spaces.** About 150 military spaces [D]. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so nothing is foreclosed by number. The roughly 370 civilians cost what every American space costs (G-16): protection, rotation, and the charge that the police is an American police. That charge will be made loudly, by Hanoi and by American critics, and it is the reason the advisers sit with the province commands and the training centres, not in the village posts.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities, with the police's actual weapons stated as well, because a policeman's weapon is part of what he is.*

**The authorities.** The decree gives the National Police five powers that matter. Arrest on warrant, and without warrant on reasonable suspicion subject to review within 72 hours [U]. Search of persons, vehicles and boats at checkpoints, and of houses on a warrant [U]. Compulsory registration of every adult and every household, with an offence for failing to register a person sleeping in the house [U]. Resource control: licences for the movement of rice above a household quantity, of medicines, of radios and of batteries, enforced at road and waterway checkpoints, as in Malaya and as the police practised from 1965 [S3] [S18]. Recommendation of administrative detention to the Provincial Detention Review Board [U].

**What it cannot compel.** It cannot compel a villager to inform, though it can make it safe for him to do so. It cannot compel the army to hand over its intelligence, though the decree orders it. It cannot compel a province chief to let the police arrest his friends, which is where P002b and the Commission Against Corruption come in. It cannot compel a judge to sit in a district town that is mortared at night.

**The money.** A policeman at full strength costs about $500–700 a year in pay, allowances, food, uniform, weapon and ammunition at 1965 prices [E: ARVN private-soldier costs of the period plus housing and the rural allowance]. At 70,000 men the running cost is about $35–50 million a year [D], paid in piasters from counterpart funds, against Commercial Import Program obligations of $397 million in 1966 [M001]. Capital costs over three years, about $60–90 million [E: 2,400 village posts at about $8,000–12,000 each, district stations, vehicles, radios and the identity system], fall mostly in 1964–66. The American advisory and training cost adds about $15–25 million a year [E: about 520 people at full cost plus training-centre operation].

**Weapons.** The village constable carries a carbine or shotgun and a revolver; the post holds a light machine gun for its own defence [U]. In 1964 the carbine is the M1 or M2 and the revolver the Smith & Wesson Model 10 or M1917, with the M1911A1 pistol for officers and the M3A1 submachine gun in station armouries [S1]; the 2026 configuration replaces the carbine with the M4 carbine and adds a 12-gauge pump shotgun of Remington 870 class to each constable's equipment, because the policeman's weapon must be good enough to defend the post against a probe and no better, or he becomes a soldier [U]. Each post has an M60 machine gun for the post's defence [U], where the historical police had at best an M1919A4 [S1]. The Field Force platoon carries M16-family rifles, an M79 grenade launcher and a machine gun, with 24 shotguns in each company's pool [S5] [U: M16A1 rather than the historical Garands and carbines from the start], and no mortars [S5]. That last is deliberate: the Field Force calls the district's artillery or the Regional Forces' mortars; it does not fight battles.

**What the police does not carry.** No police unit has indirect fire, armoured vehicles beyond a few V-100 Commando cars for the reserve battalions, or air support of its own [U]. A post attacked by a VC company holds only until the Popular Forces, the Regional Forces or the district's artillery reach it (D10).

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what the police learns, how, and how reliably in contested hamlets and at night.*

**What it needs to know.** Who lives in each house, who is a stranger, who leaves at night, who pays the Front's tax and who collects it, and who in the hamlet is the Party's secretary, its security chief and its tax collector. The army asks where the battalion is; the police asks who the hamlet chief's nephew meets at the market. This is the infrastructure half of the find-and-fix problem (A6), and the police is the instrument built for it.

**How it learns it.**

- *The resident constable.* A man who sleeps in the village, buys rice in its market and settles its quarrels learns who belongs and who does not within weeks. This is the input no other agency has. It is also the input that stops at the edge of what the villagers are willing to say.
- *The family census.* A booklet for every household, listing who lives in it, checked by night visits at random. A man listed in a house and absent from it three nights running is a fact; a man sleeping in a house and listed nowhere is another. The Malayans and the British in Kenya found the census more productive than any interrogation [E: the general finding of the Malayan and Kenyan Special Branch literature]. In 1965 the police's family census sweeps and checkpoints were already finding VC: over one year they produced 4,813 VC and 1,733 deserters [S3].
- *The identity card.* A card with a photograph and fingerprints makes a false identity expensive. The 2026 configuration is an offline enrolment kit: a ruggedised laptop, a fingerprint scanner, a camera and a card printer running on a generator or battery [E: the current biometric voter-registration kit], with the register held on encrypted drives and replicated by courier between the district, the province and Saigon. **In 1964 there is no network to check a card against.** Verification at a checkpoint is by eye against the photograph, and against the district's printed list of wanted and missing persons; a fingerprint can be checked only at the district station, by a policeman with the kit. The card's value is in what it costs the enemy to forge, not in instantaneous checking.
- *Informants.* Paid, protected and run by the Special Branch, not by the province chief. The best informants in Malaya were surrendered enemy personnel turned by Special Branch officers [E: the Malayan Special Branch record], and the Chieu Hoi programme, open since April 1963, provides the same pool here [H].
- *Interrogation and documents.* Prisoners and captured documents from the army's operations pass to the Special Branch. Recorded interrogation, under the decree's rules, produces less in the first hour and more in the first month, because a prisoner who is not tortured can be turned.
- *Checkpoints.* Road and waterway checkpoints see who moves rice, medicines and batteries, and in what quantity. The Malayan food-denial system turned the checkpoint into an intelligence instrument [S18].

**Triple canopy.** Irrelevant to the police, which works where people live. The canopy matters to the Field Force only when it follows a cadre into a base area, which it should not do.

**Night.** The resident post is the only part of the government's apparatus that is in the village at night, and night is when the VC come to the village. A post that is still there in the morning has seen who came. A post that is not has told the village who holds it. Night is therefore both the programme's main input and its main exposure.

**Monsoon.** The police does not depend on the weather. Flooding in the Delta from about August to November [E: the regional hydrology] moves the checkpoints from the roads to the waterways, which is the reason for the river police.

**Reliability in contested hamlets.** The information is excellent in hamlets the government holds by night, partial in hamlets it holds by day, and worse than none in hamlets the VC hold, because a constable there learns only what the Party wants him to learn. Informants in contested hamlets are killed when discovered, and the enemy's security sections are good at discovering them. A Special Branch that has been penetrated is a Special Branch that feeds its own informants to the enemy (D11).

**Which half of find-and-fix (A6).** The infrastructure half, squarely. The police does nothing to find a PAVN regiment in War Zone D. What it can do is name the men inside the hamlet who feed, tax and recruit for that regiment, and that is the half of the problem the theater has had no instrument for (G-3).

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity: men trained, villages covered, persons registered and cases processed a month, and the rate limit.*

**Training.** The elementary training centre at Rach Dua near Vung Tau runs a 12-week basic course [S1]. The provision expands it and adds three regional centres, at Hue, Ban Me Thuot and Can Tho [U], to a combined throughput of about 2,500 recruits a month [E: four centres of about 2,500 places each at a 12-week cycle, less wastage]. The village constable then takes a four-week rural course in the community, the census and the defence of the post [U]. Special Branch officers take a 10-week course with British and Malaysian instructors [U]. Field Force officers and NCOs train for 8 weeks [S5], historically in Malaysia and the Philippines [S5]; here in-country, with Malaysian instructors [U]. The National Police Academy at Thu Duc trains officers [S1].

**Growth.** At about 2,500 recruits a month and about 15% annual wastage [E: deaths, dismissals, desertion and transfers], the force adds about 25,000 a year net [D], which takes it from about 20,000 to about 45,000 in 1964 and about 70,000 by the end of 1966 [D]. The constraint is instructors and literate recruits, not money. The danger is Afghanistan: recruiting faster than training and supervision allow, and putting men in villages who learn their trade from the population they are supposed to police.

**Village coverage.** The constabulary occupies villages in the order security allows. At full coverage of about 2,400 posts, the first year can man about 600 posts and the second about 900 more [E: about a third of the year's trained men to village posts]. That covers the villages the government holds by night and some of those it holds by day. In January 1964 the government holds by night perhaps a fifth of the Delta's hamlets [M001]. **The rate limit is security, not training**, exactly as it was for the land programme.

**Identity and census.** An enrolment team of six registers about 150 adults a day [E: the rate of current biometric registration teams, halved for war and generators]. At 400 teams working about 20 days a month, about 1.2 million adults a month [D]. The adult population over 15 is about 8 million [E: a population of about 15–16 million with about half under 15]. In theory registration takes seven months; in practice, limited to accessible areas and to teams that must move under escort, about 60% of adults are registered by the end of 1965 [E: the accessible share of the population, which is weighted towards towns and held villages].

**Cases.** A district station's criminal investigation section of about eight [E: D1] can open and close perhaps 30–60 cases a month [E: rural policing workloads]. The Provincial Detention Review Board can hear about 200–400 cases a month in a large province [E: summary hearings]. In 1965 the police's sweeps and checkpoints were arresting VC and suspects at the rate of hundreds a week across the country [S3]; the review boards are a rate limit within months, and they must be, because a review board that keeps up by approving everything is not a review.

**What the Field Force does in a month.** A provincial company of 150 [E: D1] can clear and hold one or two villages at a time for the two to four weeks it takes to settle a post [E: Field Force practice in the late 1960s], about 25–50 villages a year per province [D]. Its rate matches the training pipeline's output of village constables.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** Minister of the Interior → Director General of National Police → regional director → province police chief → district police chief → village post. The province police chief serves the province chief for operations, as all police did historically [S4], but his appointment, promotion, discipline and budget run through the Directorate [U]. That double line is the only way to stop a province chief from using his police as his own; it is also a source of friction that P002b's merit-selected province chief is meant to reduce.

**Special Branch line.** The Special Branch reports up its own line from district to Saigon, and the files never leave it [U]. The district Special Branch officer briefs the district chief and the district's Popular Forces commander on what they need to act, not on his sources. The province chief cannot demand an informant's name.

**Physical communications.** Each village post has an HF radio and a VHF set on the district's net [U], in 1964 the AN/GRC-9 and AN/PRC-10 until the AN/PRC-25 arrives in 1965 [H]; the 2026 configuration adds a solar-charged VHF handheld of current type for each constable on patrol and a battery bank at the post [U]. A post that cannot call help at night is not a post (POSTNA-2). The identity register moves by courier.

**Talking to ARVN.** The police talks to the RVNAF through the district chief, who commands the Popular Forces and the Regional Forces in his district [S12] and in whose operations centre the district police chief sits. Special Branch primacy on the VCI means that ARVN's intelligence officers and the Military Security Service must pass what they learn about the infrastructure to a policeman. Diem kept the services competing on purpose, to protect himself from a coup [S7], and the generals who succeed him have the same reason. Integration therefore depends less on the decree than on a district chief who makes the two sit in the same room every morning.

**Talking to Americans.** The Public Safety advisers talk to the province police chief; the US district adviser talks to the district chief. MACV's intelligence system gets the Special Branch's product on the infrastructure through the province and the Directorate, not by direct access to the files [U]. That is a deliberate loss for this headquarters and a gain for the Republic: a Special Branch that hands its files to the Americans is a Special Branch whose informants will soon be dead.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions: which provinces, districts and villages it covers, and what security it needs.*

**Where it goes first.** The constabulary goes first where the population is dense and the government holds by night or nearly: the provinces around Saigon, the Delta rice provinces along the Bassac and the Mekong in the areas of the ARVN 7th Division and 21st Division, and the coastal plain of the Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Thuan. It does not go into the Highlands' Montagnard villages, where the village police model does not fit and the Special Forces' programmes and the Montagnards' own leaders hold whatever authority exists [U].

**Security categories.**

| Village category | What the police does |
|---|---|
| Held by the government by day and night | Full post of 8–12; census and identity completed; Special Branch section working; checkpoints on the roads |
| Held by day, contested at night | The post goes in behind the Field Force, with a Popular Forces platoon resident beside it, or it does not go in. Census and identity from the district office by day; no informant is run from inside the village until it is held at night |
| Held by the Viet Cong | No post. Special Branch works on the village from outside, through informants who travel to market, defectors and prisoners |

**Security needed.** A post of 8–12 police is not a garrison. It can hold off a guerrilla squad and call for help; it cannot hold against a VC local-force company. It needs a Popular Forces platoon resident in the same village, a fortified station, a radio, and a reaction from the district that arrives in the dark. Without them the provision buys dead policemen and lost carbines. Those are the conditions of POSTNA-2, and the police does not change them; it adds a policeman to the security that holds the village, and turns that security into knowledge of who is in it.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

*Read as what the programme consumes and what the RVNAF cannot supply.*

**Construction.** About 2,400 village posts of the fortified type, each a station with family quarters, a wall, a bunker and a radio mast, at about $8,000–12,000 each [E: masonry, fencing and labour at 1965 prices], built mostly by local contractors under district supervision. That is about $20–30 million [D]. Each is also a target: in 1968 the police lost 818 killed in the Tet offensive alone [S4], many at isolated posts.

**Vehicles and boats.** About 1,500 M151 quarter-ton and M37 three-quarter-ton trucks, about 1,000 motorcycles and about 300 boats over three years [E: one vehicle for each district station and Field Force platoon, motorcycles for district patrols, boats for the Delta]. In 1971 the force had 4,450 vehicles and about 830 motorcycles [S1].

**Radios.** About 3,000 HF and VHF sets for posts and stations, and about 20,000 handhelds [E: one set per post or station with spares, one handheld per constable on patrol]. Batteries are the consumable that matters: every post needs a resupply of about 30–50 kg of batteries a month or a solar array [E: current handheld and base-set consumption]. Solar charging is the 2026 configuration's practical gift, because a post that depends on battery resupply by road in the monsoon goes off the air.

**The identity system.** 400 enrolment kits, card printers and consumables for about 8 million cards [E: D5], with about 20 spare kits in each region and a repair cell in Saigon. Card stock and printer ribbons are imported. The register's master copy and its replicas need generator power, air conditioning and secure storage at the Directorate and at four regional centres.

**Specialists RVNAF cannot supply.** Forensic scientists, fingerprint examiners, trained interrogators who do not torture, criminal investigators, and Special Branch handlers. The Americans and the Commonwealth supply the instructors; the Republic must supply the men.

**What only the RVNAF can supply.** The Popular Forces platoon in the village, the Regional Forces' reaction, the district's artillery and medical evacuation. The police is an addition to the territorial forces, not a substitute for them.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

*Read as Vietnamization: whether the Republic can run it without Americans.*

It is Vietnamese from the first day. The policemen, the Special Branch officers and the district chiefs who direct them are Vietnamese; the Americans advise and pay. What depends on Americans is narrower: the identity system's spare parts and card stock, about $15–25 million a year in advisory and training cost [E: D3], and the Public Safety Division's role as the force's protector against generals who want a political police. The first two are cheap and transferable by the late 1960s. The third is not transferable at all, and when the Americans leave, the force is as honest as the government that commands it.

**On what timeline.** The training centres can be run by Vietnamese instructors within three years [E: the Michigan State model, in which Vietnamese instructors were "running their own show" by 1958 [S6]]. The Special Branch needs about five years to grow officers who can run informants without foreign help [E: Malayan Special Branch experience]. The identity system needs American or contractor maintenance for its first three to four years and then Vietnamese technicians trained on it [E: the kits' service life].

**What happens if it is transferred.** It stays. A police force, once built, is the most durable instrument a state possesses; the question is whose instrument it becomes. Historically the National Police was authorised at about 130,000 in 1973 [S1] and did not stop the collapse of 1975, because the collapse was a conventional invasion that a police force cannot stop.

### D10 · Employment envelope

*Read as the conditions under which the programme performs, and the conditions under which it fails.*

**Performs to specification when:** the village is held at night by Popular Forces resident in it; the post has a working radio and a reaction that comes; the constables are paid in full and on time by a paymaster who is not their commander; the Special Branch's files stay inside the Special Branch; the province chief wants the police to work, or fears the consequences if it does not; the review board actually reviews; and the decree survives the next coup.

**Performance collapses when:** the Field Force clears a village and leaves before the post is settled; the VC take a post and its weapons, and the enemy learns that posts can be taken; the constables are unpaid and take their pay from the market; the police arrest on quotas rather than evidence; a coup purges the Directorate and puts a faction's man at the head of the Special Branch; the army refuses to share; or the police become the instrument of the province chief's business.

**Tempo over weeks.** A village post is not a sortie. Its value accumulates over months, as the constables learn the village and the village learns that they stay. That is its strength and its vulnerability: the post is most exposed in its first three months, when it knows little and the VC know exactly where it is [E: Malayan and later Vietnamese experience of new posts].

**Night.** The post must be manned and alert every night, by men who have been on patrol all day. A constabulary of 8–12 can sustain two men on watch through the night and a patrol of four by day [E: shift arithmetic for a post of ten]. It cannot sustain a night patrol as well; that belongs to the Popular Forces.

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **Capture by a faction.** The commonest failure of police reform in a coup-prone state: the police becomes one general's instrument against the others. This is what the police was under Diem, and what it was about to be again in January 1964, when Khanh arrested its chief [S8]. Partial capture is a Directorate that is honest about the VC and political about the generals. Total capture is a Special Branch whose files are used to arrest the regime's opponents and whose informants are told to watch the Buddhists, not the Party.
2. **Predation.** The policeman takes his living from the population: the checkpoint becomes a toll gate, the identity card a source of fees, and the arrest a means of extortion. Phoenix's officials took bribes to release suspects [S14]. Partial predation is common and survivable; total predation turns the police into the enemy's best recruiter.
3. **Torture and false arrest.** Quotas produce false arrests [S14]; interrogation without rules produces confessions that name the innocent and drive their families to the Front. The decree's safeguards exist to prevent this, and they fail when no one enforces them.
4. **Penetration.** An enemy that has had agents in the police and the RVNAF for a decade will have agents in the Special Branch. A penetrated Special Branch hands over its informants and warns its targets.
5. **Overrun posts.** Isolated police posts in contested villages are destroyed, their weapons taken and their families killed. A wave of such losses ends the rural constabulary in that district.
6. **Militarisation.** Under pressure the police becomes a weak second army: the Field Force fights battles and the village posts become outposts. That is what MAAG wanted in 1961 [S7], and what happened to the Civil Guard [S6]. A militarised police loses the thing that made it worth having, the policeman's relation to the village.
7. **Bureaucratic war.** The army's intelligence services and the Military Security Service refuse Special Branch primacy and keep their own files. Partial refusal is likely and survivable; total refusal leaves the Special Branch with the constable's view and nothing more.

**What the force does in the window.** When a district's posts are lost or its Special Branch is compromised, the district falls back on what it had before: Popular Forces in outposts and the army's sweeps, which is to say, on nothing that identifies the infrastructure.

### D12 · Degradation curve

*Read as the programme at month one against month twelve, and the institution over years.*

**Month one.** A decree, a Directorate under a new chief, training centres taking their first expanded intakes, a few hundred existing policemen sent to the first village posts around Saigon, and identity teams starting in the towns. The Special Branch is the old Sûreté's men and their files.

**Month twelve.** About 600 village posts manned, most of them in held villages around Saigon, in the Delta's populous provinces and on the coast [E: D5]. Perhaps a fifth of the posts in contested villages have been attacked, and some have been lost [E: the enemy's predictable response]. About 25,000 new police are trained [E: D5]. The identity programme has registered most of the towns and the held villages. The Special Branch is beginning to hold district files that are better than the army's.

**What declines first.** Discipline, as the force grows faster than its sergeants and lieutenants. Then the review boards, which are overrun by cases. Then the honesty of the checkpoints. The force's quality in year three depends on whether the first two years' dismissals for cause were real.

**What improves.** The Special Branch. Its files improve with every arrest, defector and document, and its value at year five is many times its value at year one, provided it is not purged.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P002a -->
- **It does not find the main force.** The police addresses the infrastructure half of find-and-fix and does nothing about a PAVN or VC regiment in a base area (A6).
- **It does not hold a village.** A post of 8–12 constables cannot hold against a VC company; it depends on resident Popular Forces and a reaction that comes at night (POSTNA-2). The police adds knowledge to security; it is not security.
- **It does not work in VC-held villages.** There it works from outside, through informants, prisoners and defectors, and a constable placed in such a village is a hostage.
- **It does not make the police honest by decree.** Pay, an independent paymaster, a discipline board and the Commission Against Corruption reduce predation; they do not end it, and the force's quality follows the honesty of the government that commands it.
- **It is not a larger police than the historical one after 1966.** Its strength roughly matches the historical growth from 1966; its difference is composition, rural presence, the Special Branch and the statute.
- **It does not make an identity card verifiable at a checkpoint.** There is no network in 1964; a card is checked by eye against its photograph, and its fingerprints only at the district station.
- **It does not end the competition among intelligence services.** It orders the army and the Military Security Service to share; their compliance depends on the district chief and the generals.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P002a -->
Where the government holds a village at night, it denies the VCI anonymity: a stranger is listed nowhere, a cadre's absence is noted, a tax collector who visits is seen by a constable who stays. On the roads and waterways it raises the cost of moving rice, medicine and cash to the enemy. It denies him nothing in the villages he holds, and nothing at all against his main force.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P002a -->
This is the instrument the theater has lacked for the half of the war that the army cannot fight: a policeman who lives in the village, a Special Branch that knows who the Party's men are, and a register that makes a stranger visible. It is not a larger police than the Republic eventually built, and it does not hold a single village by itself; its value equals the night security beside it, exactly as the land programme's does. Where that security exists, it is the difference between a hamlet the government guards and a hamlet the government knows, and over years that difference is how an insurgency's infrastructure is ground down. Its greatest risks are political: a coup that captures it, a police that preys, and a Special Branch the enemy penetrates. If it survives them, its effect on the infrastructure is substantial and cumulative; it does not touch the main force or the conventional war.

## §1.2 Provincial Administration Reform, 1964: merit-based selection of province and district chiefs — P002b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read for an institutional provision: the law, the bodies that execute it, the posts it fills and its structure.*

**Designation.** The Provincial Administration Reform of 1964: a decree-law of the Republic of Vietnam promulgated on the arrival date [U], establishing that every province chief, district chief and deputy for administration is appointed from a ranked list produced by an independent Provincial Administration Selection Board, holds the post for a guaranteed term, and is removed only for cause established by an independent Inspectorate of Provincial Administration, by the Commission Against Corruption or by court-martial.

**What it replaces.** In 1964 the Republic has 44–45 provinces and about 239–243 districts [S4] [S12]. The province chief is almost always an ARVN lieutenant colonel or colonel; he commands the Regional Forces and is responsible for the province's civil administration [S12]. The district chief is a captain or occasionally a major, directing the Regional and Popular Forces and the district's civil affairs [S12]. Chiefs are appointed by whoever holds power in Saigon or the corps, and by the late 1960s by the President rather than the corps commanders [S12]. The posts are the core of the patronage system [S10]. They change with each coup: the coup of November 1963 swept away Diem's province chiefs [H], and Khanh's coup of 30 January 1964 will sweep away many of their successors [H]. Over the war the system's record is consistent. In January 1968 General DePuy recorded that "Corrupt Province Chiefs have not been removed" [S9]. By August 1968 Thieu had sacked 16 province chiefs and wanted to sack more, but "competent replacements are hard to find" [S10]. Thieu appointed loyalists who used their posts for gain [S13], and in the last years a corps commander filled provincial posts "on the basis of various personal gratuities" [S13].

**The design.** The province and district chief is the government in the countryside. He commands the territorial forces, directs the police, signs the land titles, controls the province budget and decides whether the district's Popular Forces sleep in the hamlets or in the district town. No provision that leaves his selection unchanged can change what the government is at the level where the war is fought. The design draws on three references. The Indian Administrative Service selects about 180 officers a year from over a million applicants by competitive examination [S17], trains them, posts them first as sub-divisional magistrates and then as district collectors [S17], and has spent seventy years learning that the politician's instrument against a merit-selected officer is the punitive transfer [S17]. The French prefectoral corps, the model the Republic's administration descends from, appoints prefects from a professional corps rather than from the party in power [H]. The Malayan Civil Service's district officer chaired the district's war executive committee alongside the police and the army [H]. The provision combines the IAS's selection, the prefect's professional corps and the Malayan district officer's authority, and adds the protection of tenure that India lacks.

The decree's articles are these [U: the provision's design].

1. **The Selection Board.** A Provincial Administration Selection Board of seven: a senior judge as chairman, the Director of the National Institute of Administration, two general officers named by the Joint General Staff, two senior civil servants named by the Ministry of the Interior, and one member named by the Chief of State. Members serve staggered four-year terms and can be removed only by a two-thirds vote of the Board itself or conviction for a crime. The Commissioner Against Corruption attends for vetting without a vote [M001].
2. **Eligibility.** For province chief: officers of the rank of major and above, and civil servants of the senior administrative grade with eight years' service. For district chief: captains and above, and civil servants of the administrative grade with four years' service. Every candidate files an asset declaration with the Commission [U].
3. **Selection.** A written examination in administration, law, public finance, land and police law, and the conduct of counter-insurgency; a three-day assessment of practical problems, a simulated district crisis and a structured interview; a review of the candidate's record, including his commanders' reports and, where he has served with them, his American advisers' reports as one input among several; and vetting by the Commission and the Military Security Service. The Board ranks the candidates and publishes the list.
4. **Appointment.** The Prime Minister appoints from the top three on the list for each vacancy. He may refuse all three only once per vacancy, in writing, with reasons published in the official gazette [U].
5. **Tenure.** A chief holds his post for at least two years and normally three. He is removed before that only for cause: an Inspectorate finding of incompetence or neglect upheld by the Board, a charge by the Commission, or court-martial; or by promotion through the list. A change of government does not vacate a post [U].
6. **The Inspectorate of Provincial Administration.** An independent body of about 120 inspectors in 30 teams of four [E: enough to inspect every province annually and every district at least once a year], reporting to the Board and published in summary. It inspects unannounced, including at night, and uses measures that the inspected chief does not produce himself (D4).
7. **Pay.** A province and district allowance that roughly doubles the officer's or civil servant's pay while he holds the post, with housing, and an audited representation fund in place of the informal levies that pay for entertainment today [U].
8. **The course.** A ten-week Provincial Administration Course at Vung Tau before posting, for every chief and deputy [U]. The historical war had such a school for province chief designees, and in January 1968 they were still not being sent to it [S9].
9. **Deputies for administration.** Every province and district has a civilian deputy chief for administration, a graduate of the National Institute of Administration selected through the same system, who runs the budget, the land service and the civil offices [U].
10. **The enforcement link.** A province whose chief is appointed outside the list loses its counterpart-fund allocation through the Aid Compliance Board until the appointment is regularised [M001].

**Manufacturer.** The Government of Vietnam's Ministry of the Interior and the Prime Minister's office, with the National Institute of Administration as the Board's secretariat, the aid mission's public administration division as adviser, and MACV's province and district advisers as one source of evidence for the Inspectorate.

**In-service date.** No such system existed in the Republic. The reference points are the historical appointment system [S12], the province chief course that existed and was not used [S9], and the National Institute of Administration, founded at Dalat in January 1953 and moved to Saigon as a four-year programme [S6].

**Quantity.** About 290 chief posts: 44–45 province chiefs and 239–243 district chiefs [S4] [S12]. About 330 deputies for administration: one in each province and district, less those districts too small to need one [E: a deputy where a district has a civil office worth running]. About 620 posts in all [D], filled by competition over the eighteen months from January 1964 [E: D5].

**Organisation.**

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

*Read as staffing: Vietnamese and American.*

**Vietnamese.** About 330 in the Board, assessment centres, Inspectorate and school [D], plus the about 620 chiefs and deputies themselves [D], who are not new staff but the occupants of posts that already exist. The scarce resource is not staff but the candidate pool. ARVN has, in 1964, perhaps 3,000–4,000 officers of the rank of captain to colonel who are not commanding battalions or above or holding key staff posts [E: an officer corps of the period's size, less the commands and staffs], of whom perhaps one in five has the education and record to be a credible candidate [E: the period's officer corps, drawn heavily from the urban educated class]. The civil service adds perhaps 300–500 graduates of the National Institute of Administration and senior provincial civil servants with field experience [E: the Institute's output since 1953 and the provincial service]. A credible field of about 900–1,200 candidates for about 620 posts [D] is thin. **Merit selection selects the best of who is there; it does not create the men who are not.**

**American.** About 90 [E: the components below]. About 20 in the aid mission's public administration division advising the Board, the Inspectorate and the school [E: two or three advisers to each body and the course]. About 15 assessment-centre specialists, mostly contractors, for the first two years [E: designing and running a three-day assessment of the 2026 type]. About 30 military spaces from the province and district advisory teams, whose share of time on inspection evidence and the course is counted as a space [E: about one advisory officer in each region and the course's American faculty]. About 25 more civilians in regional offices supporting the Inspectorate [E: about six in each regional office].

**Personnel spaces.** About 30 military spaces [D]. CONFIG sets no ceiling. The real cost of the American presence is not spaces but visibility: a Board that appears to select American candidates selects men who are thereby marked as American. That is why the American role is advice to the Board and evidence to the Inspectorate, not a seat or a vote (POSTNA-6).

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities: the law, the powers, the money, and what they can and cannot compel.*

**What it can compel.** The decree makes appointment outside the list unlawful and a chief so appointed without authority to sign a province budget, a land title or a counterpart-fund disbursement [U]. That is the operative power: a chief appointed outside the list cannot spend money, and the Aid Compliance Board will suspend the province's counterpart funds until he is replaced [M001]. It makes removal without cause unlawful and gives the removed chief a right of appeal to the Board. It gives the Inspectorate rights of entry to every office, every outpost and every record of the province and district, by day and by night.

**What it cannot compel.** It cannot stop a general who has taken Saigon by force from abolishing the Board, and the Board has no tanks. It cannot make a junta's prime minister appoint from the top of the list rather than the third name. It cannot make an officer who passed the examination brave, or a civil servant who passed it an able commander of territorial forces. It cannot fill a post with a merit that the candidate pool does not hold. And it cannot stop the corruption that does not run through the appointment: the contract, the rice, the sale of draft exemptions, the deals of the corps commander.

**The money.** The province and district allowance for about 620 posts at roughly a doubling of pay costs about $1.0–1.5 million a year [E: officers' and civil servants' pay of the period at about $80–100 a month, doubled, with housing]. The Board, the Inspectorate, the school and the assessment centres cost about $2–3 million a year [E: staff of about 330 and their operations]. That is about $3–5 million a year in all [D]. The cost of the existing system is not in any budget: a post bought for a price is repaid from the province's money, the Regional Forces' pay and the population [S10] [S13].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what the system learns about candidates and incumbents, how, and how reliably.*

**About candidates.** The written examination measures knowledge. The assessment measures judgment under pressure in simulated problems, and is the 2026 method's main advance over the IAS's examination and interview [E: assessment centres in current public-sector senior selection]. The record review measures what the candidate has done, through reports written by men who may be his patrons or his rivals. The vetting measures what he owns and whom he knows. None of these measures courage, or how a man commands Popular Forces at night, and the first year's results will show which of them predicts performance in this war.

**About incumbents.** The Inspectorate uses measures the chief does not generate himself (A13):

- *Unannounced night visits.* Are the district's officials sleeping in the district town or in Saigon? Are the Popular Forces in the hamlets after dark, or in the district town? Is the police post manned and its radio answering?
- *Pay musters.* Do the Regional and Popular Forces on the rolls exist? The Commission's Pay and Muster Verification Service already counts them [M001].
- *Budget audits.* Was the province's development budget spent, and on what?
- *Complaints.* A complaints box at every district and a travelling inspector who hears complaints in the market without the district chief present.
- *Adviser reports.* The American province and district advisers' reports are one input; the CORDS of the later war collected dossiers on venal and incompetent chiefs [S11], and the provision makes that evidence go to a Vietnamese body with the power to act on it.
- *The enemy's own behaviour.* Where the enemy's tax collection, recruitment and incidents rise or fall, from captured documents and Special Branch reporting (P002a).

The Inspectorate does not use the hamlet scores the chief reports, or body count, as evidence of his performance (A13).

**Triple canopy, night, monsoon.** Irrelevant to selection. Night is the Inspectorate's main instrument: a district chief's performance is visible at night in a way it never is by day. The monsoon limits the inspectors' movement in the Delta and the Highlands from about May to October [E: the regional climate], which is the reason for boats and helicopter time in D8.

**Which half of find-and-fix (A6).** Neither directly. A competent district chief makes both halves work better, because he directs the Popular Forces that hold the hamlets, the police that identify the infrastructure (P002a) and the Regional Forces that react; but the provision itself finds no one.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity: candidates assessed, posts filled and inspections made, and the rate limit.*

**First cycle.** The Board is constituted in January 1964 and holds its first examination in February [U]. About 1,000 candidates sit [E: D2]; about 400–500 pass to assessment [E: about half]; two assessment centres processing about 100 a week [E: D1] assess them in about five weeks [D]. The first list is published in April 1964 [D]. **Until then, incumbents remain in post as acting chiefs** [U], which is the moment of greatest risk (D11).

**Filling the posts.** About 290 chief posts and 330 deputy posts [D] cannot all change at once without stripping the provinces of the only men who know them. The provision replaces about a third of chiefs in the first cycle, those the Inspectorate's first sweep finds worst, and the rest as their two-year terms end [U]. Incumbents may sit the examination, and a chief who passes keeps his post with a fresh two-year term [U]. By mid-1965 about two-thirds of chiefs are list appointees, and by the end of 1965 nearly all [E: the replacement schedule].

**The course.** Sixty students at a time on a ten-week cycle is about 300 a year [D], enough for about 200 incoming chiefs and deputies a year after the first wave [D]. The first wave of about 400 [E: a third of chiefs and most deputies in 1964] takes about 18 months to pass through it [D], so some first-wave chiefs are posted before they are trained [U]. That is a compromise with the war, stated as one.

**Inspection.** Thirty teams of four [E: D1] can each inspect a province and its districts in about three to four weeks [E: about six districts a province at two to three days each, plus the province headquarters and night visits], so the whole country about once a year [D]. Unannounced night visits to districts can be more frequent, about four a year per district [E: a share of the teams' time].

**Tenure.** Two to three years in post against the historical turnover, in which a province might have three or four chiefs in two years during 1964–65 [E: the coups of the period]. That is the provision's main performance claim, and the one with the most evidence behind it: the district chief who knows his villages is a different instrument from one who arrived in March and will be gone in October.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Civil chain.** Prime Minister → Minister of the Interior → province chief → district chief → village chief. The village chief is elected [S12], and the provision does not change that.

**Military chain.** Corps commander → province chief as sector commander of the Regional Forces → district chief directing Regional and Popular Forces [S12] [S2]. The provision keeps the chief's double role, civil and military, because the territorial forces must be commanded by the man who governs the territory. It removes the corps commander's power to appoint or dismiss him [U]. That is the friction at the centre of the provision: the corps commander can still give orders to a province chief he did not choose and cannot dismiss. Historically the corps commanders' power over the provinces was one of the reasons they were the kingmakers of 1964–65 [H].

**Selection chain.** The Board's list goes to the Prime Minister and is published; the Inspectorate's summaries go to the Board and are published; full reports go to the Prime Minister, the Commission and the chief concerned.

**Talking to ARVN.** A province chief is an ARVN officer, or a civil servant commanding ARVN soldiers through an officer deputy for security [U]. He talks to the division in his area through the corps. In districts the Inspectorate rates secure, a civilian district chief may be appointed with the Popular Forces commander as his military deputy [U]; by the end of 1966 perhaps a quarter of districts are of this kind [E: the share of districts held securely by then].

**Talking to Americans.** The American province senior adviser and district adviser are the chief's counterparts. What changes for them is that the chief stays and that their evidence about him has somewhere to go. The provision does not make them inspectors, and the Inspectorate does not take their word alone.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Where it applies.** Every province and district of the Republic, including the Highlands and the cities' outlying districts [U]. Selection needs no security; governing does. A competent chief in a district the VC hold by night governs a besieged district town, and the provision cannot change that by itself.

**Where it bites first.** In the provinces where the chief has been most plainly a patron's appointee: the Delta provinces whose rice and rents made the posts valuable, the provinces around Saigon where the corps and the capital's factions placed their men, and the central coast, where the Buddhist crisis made provincial posts political [E: the pattern of the period's appointments].

**Security of the chiefs.** Province and district chiefs are targets. The enemy assassinates competent officials and leaves incompetent ones alone, because the second kind work for him [E: the VC practice of selective terror against effective officials, recorded throughout the period]. A merit-selected chief is therefore more likely to be killed than his predecessor, and the provision must expect it.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

*Read as what the programme consumes and what the RVNAF cannot supply.*

**Small.** About $3–5 million a year [E: D3]. Thirty inspection teams need vehicles, boats in the Delta, and about 100–150 helicopter hours a month across the country for remote districts, flown by the UH-1B and CH-34 companies, with O-1 Bird Dog liaison flights for single inspectors [E: perhaps a third of inspections by air]. The school at Vung Tau needs a building and quarters. The assessment centres need rooms and staff.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** The design of the examination and assessment in the first two years, which is a specialist trade; American and Commonwealth advisers supply it.

**What only the Vietnamese can supply.** Candidates, a Board that stays honest, and a Prime Minister who appoints from the list.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

*Read as Vietnamization.*

It is Vietnamese from the start: a Vietnamese board choosing Vietnamese officials. Its dependence on Americans is in two places. The assessment design needs foreign specialists for about two years [E: D2]. The enforcement link through the Aid Compliance Board is American money [M001], and when that lever lapses with the drawdown, the system survives only on its Vietnamese patrons. The second dependence is the important one, and it is the same dependence the Commission Against Corruption has (POS001-2).

**What happens if it is transferred.** It survives as long as a government finds it useful. A government that has found merit-selected province chiefs to be better at holding the countryside than its friends will keep them; a government more afraid of a coup than of the Viet Cong will not. Thieu's record suggests which way that choice went historically [S13].

### D10 · Employment envelope

*Read as the conditions under which the programme performs, and the conditions under which it fails.*

**Performs to specification when:** the government in Saigon wants competent chiefs more than it fears them; the Board is not packed; the examination is not leaked; the American lever is pulled when a chief is appointed outside the list; incumbents are replaced at a pace the provinces can absorb; and the chiefs, once appointed, are given the police, the Regional and Popular Forces and the budget they need.

**Performance collapses when:** a coup abolishes or packs the Board; the Prime Minister uses his choice of three to pick the least able loyalist each time; a corps commander makes a province ungovernable for a chief he did not choose; competent chiefs are killed faster than they can be replaced; or the system becomes a formality that ratifies the generals' choices.

**Over time.** The system's value accumulates. A province chief who serves three years learns his province; a district chief who is promoted from a district to a province through the list carries what he learned; and an officer corps that sees provinces given for ability begins to compete for them on that basis. These are effects over years, not weeks.

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **The first coup.** Khanh's coup of 30 January 1964, thirty days after the decree, is the provision's first test, and it falls before the first list is published (D5). A coup-maker's first acts are to reward his allies and place his men in the provinces. Total failure: Khanh abolishes the Board and appoints his men. Partial failure: he keeps the Board and installs his men as "acting" chiefs while the list is prepared. Survival requires that the American lever be pulled at once, against the first government that tests it, and that Khanh see more advantage in a system that gives him a reputation for reform than in one more batch of loyal colonels.
2. **Capture of the Board.** The Board's general officers and civil servants are named by the government; a government that wants a pliant Board names pliant members as terms expire. Partial capture is a Board that ranks honestly but leans to one faction's officers. Total capture is a list that is a faction's patronage with an examination attached.
3. **Leaks and purchase.** The examination is leaked or the assessors bought. Purchase moves from the appointment to the selection, and the price rises.
4. **The third name.** The Prime Minister consistently appoints the third name on each list. That still excludes the worst, and so is not a total failure, but it rewards loyalty within a merit band.
5. **The wrong merit.** The examination selects able administrators who are poor commanders of territorial forces, or brave officers who cannot run a budget. The assessment and the course are meant to catch this; the first year's Inspectorate reports will show whether they do.
6. **Protection of the incompetent.** Tenure protects a bad chief the Inspectorate has not yet caught. The two-year minimum is a cost accepted for the larger gain.
7. **Assassination.** Competent chiefs are the enemy's targets; a wave of killings in a district leaves the post to whoever will take it.
8. **Resentment.** The generals and colonels who lose their provinces are a coup constituency. The reform is a grievance in every junta from 1964 to 1965.

**What the government does in the window.** When a chief is removed or killed, his deputy for administration acts until the list supplies a successor, which takes a few weeks rather than the months of the patronage system [U]. That is one of the reform's quiet gains: the deputy is a professional, and the province does not stop.

### D12 · Degradation curve

*Read as the programme at month one against month twelve, and the institution over years.*

**Month one.** A Board appointed, an examination being written, incumbents acting, and a coup thirty days away.

**Month twelve.** About a third of chiefs replaced from the first list [E: D5], the rest examined and either confirmed or awaiting replacement. The course running. The Inspectorate's first national round half complete. The first removals for cause, which are the system's real test, because the Inspectorate's credibility depends on whether its findings remove anyone [E: the general experience of inspectorates].

**What declines first.** The Board's independence, as the government replaces members whose terms expire. Then the Inspectorate's willingness to find against well-connected chiefs. The course is the most durable element, because it is a school, and schools outlive the policies that created them.

**What improves.** The candidate pool. Each cycle draws more officers who expect provinces to be given for ability and prepare for the examination, and the National Institute of Administration's output grows to meet the demand for deputies.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P002b -->
- **It does not create competent men.** It selects the best of a thin pool of perhaps 900–1,200 credible candidates for about 620 posts; merit selection cannot select merit that does not exist.
- **It does not survive a coup by itself.** The Board has no force; it survives a coup only if the coup-maker sees more advantage in keeping it, or the American lever is pulled at once.
- **It does not end corruption.** It closes the sale of the post; it does not close the contract, the rice, the draft exemption or the corps commander's business.
- **It does not change the corps commanders.** They still command the divisions and the province chiefs' military operations, and they can obstruct a chief they did not choose.
- **It does not make a chief able to govern a district the enemy holds at night.** Selection needs no security; governing does.
- **It does not make chiefs civilian.** Most remain officers, because the territorial forces must be commanded; civilian chiefs are confined to districts rated secure.
- **It does not act at once.** The first list is published in April 1964; incumbents act until then; full replacement takes until the end of 1965.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P002b -->
It denies the enemy the province chief who works for him by default: the venal, absent or incompetent chief whose Popular Forces sleep in the district town and whose administration drives the population to the Front. It denies him nothing he can win by assassination, which is why competent chiefs become his targets.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P002b -->
This is the cheapest provision the theater could receive and among the most consequential, because the province and district chief is the government at the level where the war is fought: he commands the territorial forces, directs the police, executes the land reform and decides whether the hamlets are held at night. A chief chosen for ability and kept for two or three years is worth more to a province than any equipment. Its limits are severe and political. It selects from a thin pool, it survives only as long as the junta that keeps it, and its first test, Khanh's coup of 30 January 1964, comes before its first list is published. If the American lever holds it through 1964–65, its effect on provincial administration is substantial and lasting, and it makes every other pacification programme work better. It does not touch the main force or the conventional war.
<!-- /card -->
