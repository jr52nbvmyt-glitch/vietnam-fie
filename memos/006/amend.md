## P1 · Overview

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964 [H40], none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year [H40]. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon; at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve and its regiments in set-piece battles, and at Song Be the government holds a province capital only narrowly and with American firepower. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

Five things in this phase are new, and all of them begin on its first day. Neither the coups in Saigon nor the summer offensive turns on any of them.

The first two belong to the Republic itself. On 1 January 1964 the junta decrees land to the tiller, confirming by name what the Viet Minh and the Front had already given, and an independent Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it (EV001-1); the same session gives the Republic a resident rural police with a Special Branch, and province and district chiefs chosen by examination from a ranked list (EV002-1). Khanh's coup tests both within a month and both survive it (EV000-1). By the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the musters have shown the RVNAF to be about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls [X-001]; about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the Front has killed about 410 constables, nearly all in posts settled ahead of night security (EV002-2) [X-002]. The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

The third is what this command can see. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks fly from January 1964. The coastal picket closes the trawler route within a year, and Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months before it did historically (EV003-1, EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats near the destroyers, Pierce Arrow is not flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the real attack of 2 August alone (EV000-3).

The fourth is in the hands of the men who fight: modern carbines for the whole RVNAF and the territorials, and a belt-fed gun and a marksman in every regular squad (EV004-1). In the held belt a post with its Popular Forces platoon now holds about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2). Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy still chooses where and when to fight, but they are lost at a smaller price and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out (EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18). The outposts that fall give the Front the same carbines.

The fifth waits on Guam: B-52Js carrying GPS-guided bombs and, from March 1964, a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut that measures the U-2S's imagery into targets for them (EV005-1). Washington refuses every request to use them over the North (EV000-15), and the first Arc Light strike of June 1965 flies without tankers and finds as little as history's did (EV000-20).

A sixth thing begins on the same first day and does not show until the second year. The decree of 1 January 1964 puts a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch and orders a coordinating centre in every province and district, built on the posts and files the police decree creates; the centres are staffed by the Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the sector S2, forbidden quotas, held to warrants under the police statute, and audited by the Commission (EV006-1). About 100 district centres are open by the end of 1964 and all of them by the end of 1965. In the same month the Army begins to select its field advisers by board, to train them for eight months in Vietnamese and in advising, and to keep them eighteen months; the first of them reach the subsectors and battalions in October. In March 1965 the centre at Hoc Mon breaks the district committee above the hamlets the police had already cleared, and the Front learns that leaving a village no longer puts its leaders out of reach (EV006-2).

## EV006-1 · The Phung Hoang decree, the first centres and the first selected advisers · 1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Special Warfare School at Fort Bragg and the Defense Language Institute at Monterey; the district towns of the held belt in IV and III Corps · NEW · 006
*Initiative: allied*

The Phung Hoang decree was the third signed in the session of 1 January 1964, after the land decree and the police decree, and it was the shortest of the three. It created a national Phung Hoang Committee under the Minister of the Interior, a national coordinating centre of about 150 staff in the Directorate of National Police, and an intelligence and operations coordinating centre in every province and district as the Special Branch's sections opened there: a PIOCC of 25–40 in each province capital and a DIOCC of 10–15 in each district office [M006]. Each centre sat in the Special Branch's office, under the district or province chief, with a Special Branch officer as its head, an officer of the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and the census teams and the Chieu Hoi office feeding it [M006]. Three articles were this headquarters' and were fought for in the drafting: no centre would be given a monthly target or credited with a number; no person would be arrested on a centre's file except on a magistrate's warrant under the police statute, on the three-source rule; and the Commission Against Corruption would sample the centres' reports of persons neutralized, name by name, from the first quarter of 1965 [M006]. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the same statute as a police arm, about 44 units of 60–100 men under the province chiefs, with a rule that a raid's object was a prisoner [M006].

The national centre's director was Lieutenant Colonel Tran Dinh Hieu, a Military Security officer who had served in the Sûreté's political section before 1954 and had been passed over twice under Diem for refusing to work on the Can Lao's lists [X-006]. Beside him sat an American cell of about 60, and in Saigon an exploitation detachment of about 80 Vietnamese-speaking analysts and translators, drawn from the 500th Military Intelligence Group, the Agency's station and the language school, under Lieutenant Colonel Martin J. Ashby of the US Army [X-006]. The detachment's work was the method the 2026 practice calls F3EAD and the period had no name for: every arrest and every rallier was exploited within the week, every captured document translated, every name placed on a chart of the district committee, its sections, its couriers and its families, and the chart returned to the district centre with the next questions [M006]. About 1,500 SEEK II kits enrolled every detainee and rallier by fingerprint, iris and face against an offline watchlist [M006·S3]; the kits were the one instrument of 2026 the centres kept whole, and the Muster Service's technicians maintained them with their own (F006-3).

The centres opened behind the posts, not ahead of them. The first DIOCC opened at Phu Tan in An Giang on 16 March 1964, two weeks after the first village post; by the end of June there were about 40, all in districts of the Delta and around Saigon where the posts already stood, and by 31 December about 100 DIOCCs and 20 PIOCCs [X-006]. In the contested districts of the coastal Centre the province chiefs asked for centres before they had posts, and Hieu refused them; that refusal, which Ashby supported and this headquarters endorsed, kept the method out of the villages where a file could only be built from a rallier's grudge (EC006-12 abandoned) [X-006].

The advisers came by another road. In January 1964 the Department of the Army opened the Selected Advisory Corps to volunteers and convened its first boards in February: captains, majors and senior NCOs with a combat or command record, assessed over two weeks at Fort Bragg for temperament, judgment and aptitude for language, of whom about half passed [M006]. The first class of about 1,100 began the course on 3 February: six weeks of advising, about 24 weeks of Vietnamese at the Defense Language Institute, and four of weapons, combat medicine and the calling of fire and air [M006]. They reached the field from 1 October, replacing advisers on the old short tour as each left, and by the end of December about a third of the field posts below corps were held by men who had chosen to be there, could speak to their counterparts without an interpreter and would stay eighteen months [M006]. Among them was Captain Samuel J. Whitcomb, a Korean War NCO commissioned in 1956, who took over the Can Giuoc subsector on 9 October, four months after the killings there (EV001-3), and on his first night asked the district chief, in Vietnamese, which families still held their titles at the district office and why [X-006]. The Front noticed the new advisers before this headquarters measured them: a COSVN security bulletin of December 1964, captured in 1966, warned district committees that "the new American advisers speak our language and must be assumed to understand what is said in front of them" [X-006].

## EV002-2 · The first posts and the Front's answer · 1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 002,004,CP01,006
*Initiative: both*

The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night (EC002-1) [X-002]. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village [X-002]. From February the platoon beside the post carried M4A1 carbines with red-dot sights, drawn under IRON GATE (EV004-1); the constables, whom the rearmament did not cover, kept the carbines of 1950 [X-004]. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965 [M002]. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads (EC002-2) [M002]. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered [X-002].

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected (F002-12). A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night [X-002]. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966 [X-002]. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required (F002-8) [X-002].

Where the platoon was there, it now held. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about nine in ten held, against about four in five that the programme's design would have given them with the platoons' old weapons [X-004]. The case that the Delta's advisers told for a year was the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140 against a post whose PF platoon had zeroed its carbines in March. The platoon's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and the company's first assault, which on the Front's own experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause; the second was not pressed, and the company withdrew before the Regional Forces boat company from Ham Long arrived at 0320, leaving eleven dead on the bund and taking its wounded [X-004]. The Front learned that lesson faster than this headquarters expected. From December its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in, and, against the posts that held, the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market (F004-1, F004-2) [X-004]. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts [X-004].

The weapons the Front took changed with its targets. Of the roughly 300 weapons lost with the police posts, nearly all were the constables' old carbines; but across the countryside the outposts that did fall, the Popular Forces deserters who walked away with their arms, and the ARVN battalions ambushed in the summer of 1965 gave the Front between about 10,000 and 30,000 M4A1 carbines by the end of 1965, with their sights (F004-7) [X-004]. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and each one needed the Republic's own 5.56mm to fire.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not [X-002]. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed (F002-13) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

From the summer of 1964 the Special Branch's harvest had a second layer. The district centres that opened behind the posts took each arrest and each rallier and asked a different question of them: not who in this hamlet belongs to the Front, but who sends the hamlet's cadres their orders, who collects the tax above the village, and where the district committee's couriers sleep (EC006-1) [X-006]. Of the roughly 1,900 people the Branch arrested in the held belt by July 1965, about 260 were members of district or village party committees or their security and finance sections, against perhaps 100 that the Branch's own folders would have reached without the centres' charts [X-006]. The biometric enrolment caught its first case in December 1964 at Cho Moi in An Giang, a finance cadre released by a magistrate in August on a bribe paid through a cousin, who was rearrested in November under a new name with a new identity card and found by the kit in forty seconds (F006-3) [X-006]. The Commission prosecuted the magistrate's clerk in 1965. The Front's answer to the centres came, as its answer to the posts had, within a season: from January 1965 its district committees in the held belt began moving their offices out of the villages to the contested edge, and the Dinh Tuong clerk who had copied informant cards in 1964 was the first of the penetrations the Branch would find in the centres rather than the posts (F006-5).

## EV006-2 · Operation STILL WATER · 8 Mar – 30 Jun 1965 · Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province, inside the Hop Tac ring northwest of Saigon · NEW · 006
*Initiative: allied*

STILL WATER was the first operation in the war aimed at a district committee rather than at the hamlets beneath it, and it was the case for the method. By February 1965 Hoc Mon had 11 village posts settled under the police decree, a Popular Forces platoon in each, about 70 percent of its families on the census, and a district chief from the first list, Major Pham Van Truc; the Front's hamlet cells in the posted villages had mostly been arrested or had left (EV000-5, EV002-2) [X-006]. What remained was the Hoc Mon district committee of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region, about 14 members with a security section, a finance section and about 40 couriers, which had moved in January into the hamlets along the Saigon River at the district's northern edge, toward the Ben Cat and Cu Chi base areas, and governed the posted villages from there by messenger [X-006]. The DIOCC at Hoc Mon, opened in July 1964 under Captain Ngo Van Tam of the Special Branch with 12 staff and two American advisers, had by February a chart of that committee with 31 names, 19 of them confirmed on three sources, built from 44 arrests, 23 ralliers and a cache of finance-section receipts found by a PF patrol in December [X-006].

The operation was Tam's plan, approved by Truc and by the Gia Dinh province chief on 1 March. It used no regular troops. Two Police Field Force companies and the Gia Dinh PRU of about 80 men made the arrests; the Regional Forces companies of Hoc Mon and Go Vap, with M4A1 carbines and their M249s, held the cordons at night; a reaction platoon of the Gia Dinh sector and two UH-1Bs of the 120th Aviation Company stood by at Tan Son Nhut for the reaction; and every arrest went first to the biometric kit and then to a magistrate within 72 hours [X-006]. Captain Whitcomb's counterpart in the next province was not involved; the American subsector adviser at Hoc Mon, Captain Luis Arredondo, was, and by the rule of the decree he did not go on the raids (EC006-13 abandoned) [X-006].

The turn came on the night of 26–27 March. A finance-section courier arrested on 19 March, a woman of 19 who carried tax receipts in the hem of a conical hat, rallied on the 24th after the Chieu Hoi officer offered her brother's release from a prison on Con Son, to which he had been sent in 1962 under Diem without trial, and named the house in Tan Thoi Nhi where the committee's security section met twice a month [X-006]. The PRU went in at 0300 under a moon nearly full with two Police Field Force platoons in the cordon. Four men were taken alive in the house, including the section's deputy chief, with a steel box of informant reports on the posts of four villages; a fifth was killed in the paddy behind it after firing on the cordon with a K-50 sub-machine gun; one PRU member died [X-006]. The box named nine of the committee's own informants inside the posted villages, three of them PF soldiers [X-006].

By 30 June the operation had arrested 63 people on warrant, of whom the magistrates released 17; 9 of the committee's 14 members were dead, in prison or had rallied, 2 of them killed resisting arrest; the remaining five had moved north into Cu Chi [X-006]. The Commission's first audit sample, drawn in July from STILL WATER's files, found 4 of the 63 arrests unsupported and the release of one man already paid for, and sent both cases to the Special Court [X-006]. The Front did not rebuild the Hoc Mon committee inside the district; it governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi until 1968, and the tax receipts captured in 1966 showed collections in the district's posted villages at about a fifth of 1964's [X-006]. In this war STILL WATER was a small operation, 63 arrests and three deaths in four months, and it changed the war in one particular: it showed the Front that the government could follow its leaders out of the villages it had cleared, three years before the historical Phoenix tried to (F006-26).

## P1 · How the phase ends

By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though none of the general reserve has been wiped out. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and outside the held belt the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security it needs. Inside it the summer is not the historical one: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's [X-001]. Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

About 1,100 village posts are settled, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure has begun to leave the villages around the posts (EV002-2) [X-002]. The sea route to the coast is closed and the Cambodian route open, and the enemy's rear is on the U-2S's maps while his regiments under the canopy and his cadres in the hamlets are as hidden as before. The general reserve enters Phase II weakened but whole, and the Front enters it with between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines (EV004-1) [X-004]. On Guam the B-52Js hold about 1,400 measured targets in the cell's folders and authority to strike none of them in the North (EV005-1, EV000-15).

Phung Hoang ends the phase with about 140 district centres open, every one behind a settled post, and a method that has reached above the hamlet in one district on the edge of Saigon and in a dozen in the Delta (EV006-1, EV006-2) [X-006]. About 1,100 selected advisers are in the subsectors and battalions, speaking to their counterparts without interpreters; the rest of the field posts are still held on the old tour. The Front has begun to move its district committees out of the held belt, which costs it the villages' daily obedience and gains it the safety of the contested edge (F006-4).

## P2 · Overview

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967 [H40]. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed most. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 titled to about 640,000 families [X-001]. GOLDEN SHEAF, its first clearing operation in the western Delta, shows what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of what the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark (EV001-6, F001-4). The Commission survives the Buddhist crisis and the election behind the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 convicts its first general (EV001-7). With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case (EV002-4). The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its cadres into the towns (EV002-3, EV002-5), and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before (EV000-26). None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

The war of the enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch counts the Trail's trucks from December 1965 (EV003-3); the U-2S photographs the dispersed fuel drums before the POL strikes fly (EV000-27); and by March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree, from the radar's count of convoys out of Kompong Speu, that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4). The counts show the bombing raising the toll on the transit without lowering what arrives, and change no rule in Washington.

Every American and South Vietnamese infantryman carries a modern carbine, and every squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman. The Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle (EV000-23, EV000-34). The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by the corps, and the districts they leave pay for it (EV004-2). None of it changes who chooses the battle.

Above the Trail the B-52J is allowed against fixed crossings in the panhandle from April 1966 and cuts every bridge it can measure; the enemy answers with fords, causeways and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before (EV005-2). At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is measured every night and lifts three weeks early at a heavy price to the PAVN (EV000-36). It is the first siege the enemy loses badly, and Westmoreland draws a lesson from it that will cost the coastal hamlets.

Three changes of 1964 run through the phase without altering its battles. Phung Hoang's centres cover every district by the end of 1965 and break the Front's district administration in the held belt before Tet rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN takes the Can Giuoc committee itself, not only its security chief (EV002-4). The method spreads by its own success into the contested belt, where it becomes the historical Phoenix, and a PRU raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 makes "the American Phoenix" a press story three years early (EV006-4). The American battalions arrive and leave as units for twelve-month tours, trained together and commanded for the whole of them; they fight better in the contacts the enemy grants and get no more of them, and the enemy learns to aim at the week each one changes over, as it did at Dien Ban in August 1966 (EV006-3). And the field advisers, now chosen and trained and on eighteen-month tours, are the men who carry what the American battalions forget each year.

## EV002-4 · Operation NIGHT LANTERN · 15 Nov 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
NIGHT LANTERN went back to the districts where the land programme had learned its first lesson. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings (EV001-3), the titles of about 400 families were still held in the district office, because no one could protect the families who kept them. Long An had been given a province chief from the first list in June 1964, Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, an engineer officer who had placed third in the country in the examination, and Can Giuoc a district chief from the second, Major Le Cong Phuong, who had grown up in Can Duoc [X-002]. In September 1965 they proposed to the American province adviser an operation built not around a regiment but around the settled post (EC002-1), the census and night check (EC002-2) and the target folder (EC002-3), in the 14 villages of the two districts between Route 5 and the Vam Co [X-002].

The forces were Long An's own: eight Regional Forces companies, 26 Popular Forces platoons raised in the villages themselves, two Police Field Force companies of about 150 men each with M1 and M2 carbines, M1918A2 automatic rifles and 60mm mortars, 140 rural constables for the 14 posts, the province Special Branch section of about 45, and four census teams [X-002]. Fire support was two M101 105mm howitzers at Can Giuoc and an AC-47 of the USAF 4th Air Commando Squadron on call at night; lift was the RF's sampans, two LCVPs of the River Assault Group at Nha Be, and UH-1B helicopters when III Corps would release them [X-002]. The rule was Lan's: no post settled until its PF platoon slept in the same hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour, and no arrest without three sources [X-002].

The posts went in from 15 November, one village at a time, working out from Can Giuoc town along the canals. The turn came in two steps. On the night of 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th Local Force Battalion attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, the village where the killings of June 1964 had begun. The PF platoon and the ten constables held the post and the hamlet behind it for three hours; an RF company came down the canal in sampans and reached the post at 01:40, and the AC-47 kept the attackers off the paddy until it arrived [X-002]. The 506th left 38 dead and 14 weapons; the defenders lost 9 killed, 3 of them constables [X-002]. On 4 February a night check in Long Huu found a young man whose card had been made for someone two years older; his booklet did not match the house, and within a week the courier route he served had led the Special Branch to the chief of the Can Giuoc district committee's security section, arrested on 11 February with his files [X-002]. The Can Giuoc DIOCC, open since August 1964 in the Special Branch's office beside the district chief's, took the files apart in nine days: the section chief's notebook listed the committee's courier stations by the names of the families who kept them, and the census matched 17 of the 22 to households and three to households that had been registered under false names in 1965 [X-006]. On the night of 24–25 February the Long An PRU and a Police Field Force platoon, in sampans with an RF company holding the canal behind them, took the district committee's deputy secretary and its finance chief in a hamlet of Phuoc Vinh Dong at the edge of the Rung Sat, where the committee had moved in January believing itself beyond the posts (EC006-4) [X-006]. Neither man fired. The deputy secretary rallied in April and spent the rest of the year with the province centre, drawing the chart of the Long An province committee above him (EC006-2).

By 30 April all 14 posts were settled and held. The Special Branch had arrested 212 people on three-source evidence, of whom the magistrates released 71, and 64 cadres and guerrillas had rallied to the police posts, which they found easier to approach than an army camp (F002-2) [X-002]. What was left of the district committee, five of its eleven members, moved deeper into the Rung Sat and visited its villages instead of living in them, and the Can Giuoc tax ledger captured in June showed collections for the first quarter of 1966 at about a third of the same quarter of 1965 [X-002]. The about 400 titles held since 1964 were handed to their families in April, in their hamlets [X-002]. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 RF, PF and police dead. It was the first operation in the war in which the province chief, the police and the land programme acted as one district government, and it did in six months what no battalion sweep of Long An had done in six years (F002-1, F002-17).

The American who saw the whole of it was Captain Whitcomb, the Can Giuoc subsector adviser, whose eighteen months ran from October 1964 to April 1966 and so spanned the killings' aftermath, the settling of the posts and the operation itself; his successor arrived on 1 March and they worked the district together for six weeks (EC006-8) [X-006]. Whitcomb's report of 30 April said three things this headquarters quoted to Washington: that the committee's arrest, not the battalion's sweep, was what broke the tax; that the Commission's sample of NIGHT LANTERN's 212 arrests had found 9 unsupported, all made in the first fortnight before the centre's charts were complete; and that the families who received their titles in April had asked him, not the district chief, whether the arrests would last (F006-26) [X-006].

## EV006-3 · The relief at Dien Ban · 8–31 Aug 1966 · Dien Ban and Dai Loc Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon · NEW · 006
*Initiative: enemy*

The relief at Dien Ban was the first time the enemy attacked an American battalion in the week it changed over, and it showed both halves of what unit rotation had done. The 1st Battalion, 9th Marines had come ashore at Da Nang in July 1965 and had spent a year in the hamlets of Dien Ban south of the airfield, the same companies under the same commander throughout [X-006]. Under the rotation it was to be relieved in place by the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, which had formed at Camp Pendleton in October 1965, trained together for nine months and sailed from Okinawa in late July under Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey; the two battalions were to overlap from 8 to 26 August, company by company [X-006]. The relief dates had been fixed in March and were known, as all such dates were, to every Vietnamese who sold the Marines ice or washed their clothes (F006-21).

The 1st Battalion, 26th Marines arrived knowing nothing of Dien Ban. Its men had trained on the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 for six months and fired them as squads that had not changed since January (EC006-6); its officers had studied Ellison's area from the outgoing commander's reports and an overlay; none of them had walked the paddies at Phong Thu or Ky Lam, where the R-20 Doc Lap Battalion and the Front's Dien Ban district company had laid mines along the bunds for a year and knew every one of the old battalion's habits [X-006]. What Dien Ban did have was continuity in the other places this headquarters had put it. The ARVN 51st Regiment's battalion advisers and the Dien Ban subsector adviser, Major Carl E. Haney, were all men of the Selected Advisory Corps on eighteen-month tours, two of them in their thirteenth month, and the district chief and the DIOCC had been in place since 1965 (F006-2, F006-9) [X-006]. Haney walked each of Dorsey's company commanders over his ground with the Popular Forces platoon leaders of the hamlets it held, and gave each a list, in English and Vietnamese, of the PF corporals who would tell a Marine what the hamlet knew (EC006-7) [X-006].

The Front had read the calendar too. On the night of 18–19 August, with two companies of the old battalion already embarked at Da Nang and two of the new on the ground for less than a week, the R-20 Battalion and the district company attacked Company B, 1st Battalion, 26th Marines in its new position at the hamlet of La Huan, about 4 km west of Route 1, at 0130 under a thin moon, with 82mm mortars from across the Song Ky Lam and a sapper assault from the south through a tree line the outgoing company had kept clear and the new one had not yet learned to watch [X-006]. The first assault put a section of sappers inside the wire. What followed was the half of the event the rotation had bought: the company's platoons, which had trained together for nine months, closed on the breach without waiting for orders, the squad guns took the paddy south of the wire under grazing fire, and by 0230 the sappers inside were dead and the second wave had not crossed the bund [X-006]. A PF platoon in the next hamlet, which Haney had introduced to the company's commander on the 14th, reported the R-20's withdrawal route by AN/PRC-25 at 0310, and two F-4Bs of Marine Aircraft Group 11 and the battalion's 81mm mortars hit it at the Song Ky Lam crossing before dawn [X-006].

The relief window had cost the Marines 14 killed and 37 wounded, about four times the battalion's average night that summer, and the enemy about 40–60 dead, of whom 23 were found [E: the company's count of bodies inside and outside the wire and a captured R-20 casualty return of September]. The outgoing battalion would not have been hit that way; it knew the tree line. The incoming one, under the old individual system, would have been a battalion of strangers at the breach, and I believe the sappers would have held the breach long enough to reach the company's ammunition point (F006-6) [E: the company's own account of the first fifteen minutes, and the period's experience of penetrated positions]. MACV drew two orders from Dien Ban: from October 1966 reliefs were staggered across six weeks by company and kept off the dark of the moon, and no incoming company took over a position without a night with the outgoing one beside it (EC006-7) [X-006]. Both orders added men in theater at every relief. The R-20 Battalion drew its own lesson in a document captured in November: the new Marines "fight like the old ones in their third month and know the ground like the old ones in their first" [X-006].

## EV006-4 · The Mo Duc raid and the Phoenix story · 11 Feb – 30 Jun 1967 · Duc Thanh village, Mo Duc District, Quang Ngai Province; Saigon; Hanoi; Washington · NEW · 006
*Initiative: both*

The raid at Duc Thanh was what the method became where the night was not held, and it reached the American press within a month. By the end of 1966 the held belt had little left for the district centres to find (EV002-5), and the corps commanders and the CORDS planners wanted the same results from the contested provinces of I and II Corps, where the Front still ran most villages after dark [X-006]. Quang Ngai's province chief opened DIOCCs in four contested districts in the autumn of 1966 against Hieu's advice, and the Quang Ngai PRU, which had been raised under the statute in 1964 and had operated by its rules in the coastal towns, began to raid on files built mostly from ralliers and from the S2's informants (F006-15) [X-006]. On the night of 11 February 1967 a PRU team of 24 men, acting on a file that named a hamlet schoolteacher in Duc Thanh as the Mo Duc district committee's propaganda chief on two sources, both ralliers from the same hamlet, went into his house at 0200; a man in the house fired a pistol, the team fired back with M4A1s and an M249, and the teacher, his wife, his father and two of his children died [X-006]. The district committee's propaganda chief was a different man, arrested in May; the schoolteacher had been named by a rallier whose family had a quarrel with his over a well [X-006].

The Front's answer was ready because Hanoi had decided, for its own reasons, to fight on a new front. The Thirteenth Plenum of January 1967 had adopted the line of "fighting while negotiating", and on 28 January Foreign Minister Nguyen Duy Trinh had said that talks could follow an unconditional halt in the bombing [H]; the decision came from Hanoi's reading of Soviet pressure, the American election calendar and the world's opinion of the bombing, and from nothing this headquarters did [H]. Its consequence for the Front's propaganda apparatus was a demand for material that would carry abroad. Liberation Radio broadcast the Duc Thanh deaths on 19 February as "a massacre by the American Phoenix murder teams", and the story reached an American wire service in Saigon through a Quang Ngai stringer on 2 March; by 10 March two national newspapers had run it, with the programme's American name, the CIA's role in its design and the PRUs' American pay (F006-23) [X-006]. On the historical course the Phoenix stories began in 1970 [H].

Two things were different from what the historical stories would have found, and both mattered. The Commission's inspectors had the Duc Thanh file within a week and published their finding on 6 April: the file had met the three-source rule only by counting two ralliers from one feud as independent, the warrant had been signed by a district judge who had not read it, and the PRU had entered without a capture plan [X-006]. The PRU team leader was tried before the Special Court in June and convicted of manslaughter; the judge was removed; Hieu withdrew the centres' authority to issue raid files in the four contested districts of Quang Ngai and in eleven others in I and II Corps until each had a post and a platoon in the village concerned [X-006]. And the national committee published its first audited count on 30 June: since January 1965, about 14,500 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 9,800 were tried or held under review, about 3,900 had rallied and about 800 had been killed, about 300 of them in the programme's own raids, with the Commission's sample finding about one claim in ten unsupported in the held belt and one in four in the contested belt [X-006]. The count was small, and it was the first figure on the attack on the infrastructure that the newspapers did not later have to correct. It did not undo Duc Thanh. The word Phoenix was in the American vocabulary three years early, and it meant what the Front had said it meant.

## EV002-5 · The arrest count falls · Feb – Nov 1967 · Saigon; Phong Dinh, Long An and Vinh Long Provinces; and the towns of the Delta · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
In the first quarter of 1967 the Special Branch's arrests of infrastructure in the held belt of the Delta fell to about 40 percent of their peak of the second quarter of 1966, and the Phung Hoang centres' neutralizations of district-level cadres fell with them, from about 140 a quarter in 1966 to about 50 (F006-18) [X-002] [X-006]. The Office of the Secretary of Defense's systems analysts read the decline as a programme losing its grip, and in February Robert Komer, then the President's special assistant for pacification, proposed monthly arrest targets by province as the measure of the attack on the infrastructure [X-002]. This headquarters answered in writing on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working (F002-11): in the districts with posts three years old there was almost no one left in the villages to arrest, and the measures the enemy could not fake, captured tax ledgers, the count of functioning village committees in captured documents, and the age of captured guerrillas, all showed the infrastructure shrinking there [X-002]. The Director General of National Police, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, wanted the targets; Commissioner Khoa opposed them on the record, and so did Hieu, whose decree of 1964 forbade them in terms [X-002] [X-006].

The decree held, and the half-victory of the historical-course compromise was a whole one on paper: no centre and no province was given a target in 1967, and the held belt was measured by captured documents and the contested belt by the Commission's audit [X-006]. It was less than a whole one on the ground. The pressure that would have gone into targets went instead into extending the method to the contested belt, where it produced what the abandoned target list had predicted, raids on thin files, and after Duc Thanh the extension was halted village by village (EV006-4, F006-15) [X-006]. In the second half of 1967 the Commission received about 190 complaints of false arrest from the contested districts with centres and about 40 from the held belt, against the 430 that targets had produced on the earlier course (EC002-9) [X-006]. And the infrastructure that had left the held villages had not disappeared. The Special Branch's provincial sections reported through 1967 that legal cadres and their families were settling in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the outskirts of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000 (F002-2) [X-002]. The Branch built urban sections to follow them, slowly. The war against the infrastructure had moved toward the towns, and so, as the Branch's own reports warned in November, had the Front's means of acting there.

## EV000-35 · CORDS is established · 9 May 1967 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,006
*Initiative: allied*
On 9 May 1967 the President placed the American pacification effort, civil and military, under MACV as Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support, with Robert W. Komer as one of Westmoreland's deputies, holding ambassadorial rank and authority over military personnel and resources, the first civilian to serve in such a role inside a US military command [H37]. It followed the failure of the Office of Civil Operations, set up in November 1966 to coordinate the civil agencies under civilian leadership [H37]. CORDS was about 85 percent military [H37]. Each corps had a CORDS deputy, usually civilian, under the corps senior adviser, and each province an American province senior adviser with civilian and military staffs alongside the Vietnamese province chief, usually an ARVN colonel [H37].

Komer's priorities were the territorial forces and the attack on the VC infrastructure. The Regional and Popular Forces numbered about 300,000 in 1967 [H37]; their advisory presence was later multiplied through Mobile Advisory Teams [H37]. In mid-1967 CORDS created the Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme, ICEX, to target the VCI, which became the Phoenix programme at the end of the year [H]. The Hamlet Evaluation System, introduced in January 1967, began rating every hamlet monthly on a scale reported by the American district advisers [H]; its figures were at once an indispensable record and a measure produced by officers rated on the result (A13). CORDS solved the American management problem of pacification. It did not solve the Vietnamese one, that security in the hamlet at night depended on Vietnamese forces and officials who had to stay there.

In this war CORDS is established on the historical date and in the historical form; the provisions did not create a single manager [X-001]. What CORDS inherits is different. The American land advisers, about 260 in 1964 and about 340 by 1967, pass into it as a land reform division under the CORDS deputy, and the provincial land services' register of titled parcels, which by May 1967 lists about 740,000 hectares and the households that hold them, becomes the first hamlet-by-hamlet household record the American pacification effort has ever had [X-001] [M001]. Komer adds titled hectares as a factor in the Hamlet Evaluation System's economic rating from its first revision. This headquarters objected in writing on 22 June 1967 that a title measured the government's paperwork and not the peasant's allegiance, that the Front had told its cadres to let peasants take titles and keep paying the Front's tax, and that titles issued would rise fastest where they meant least (F001-10) [X-001]. The objection was recorded and the factor kept, at half the weight Komer had proposed [X-001]. The Phung Hoang centres, which had used the land register since 1964 to locate the families of known cadres, passed into CORDS's reporting with it; the Commission had been receiving complaints about that use since 1965 (F001-1) [X-001] [X-006].

In this war CORDS inherits a police as well as a land register. The aid mission's Public Safety Division, about 300 advisers in 1966, passes into CORDS as its Public Safety Directorate, and with it a National Police of about 70,000, about 1,500 village posts, and a Special Branch with district sections in about 200 districts and files on the infrastructure kept since 1964 [M002] [X-002]. There is no ICEX in this war, because there is nothing for it to create: the Phung Hoang centres have sat in those district sections since 1964, under the district chief, with the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and every district has one by the end of 1965 (EV006-1, F006-13) [X-006]. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the police statute in 1964 and answer to the province chiefs, not to the Agency; their methods are inside the statute's safeguards in law, and Duc Thanh in February showed how far that law reached in a village the government did not hold at night (EV006-4) [X-006]. Komer's contribution was the money to put a centre's American adviser in every contested district, which Hieu accepted, and a proposal for monthly targets, which the decree forbade (EV002-5) [X-006]. What the American pacification effort created in 1967 on the historical course, here it found already running, and the province senior advisers found a counterpart chief who had been in his post for two or three years (F002-7) [X-002].

## P2 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam [H40] and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year [H40]. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December [H9], and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December [H39]. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000 [H39], and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view" [H39]. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi [H]. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general (EV001-6, EV001-7) [X-001]. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside [X-001].

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest (EV002-4, EV002-5) [X-002]. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

The enemy's external supply is measured: the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count, the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what the South's forces need from the North (EV003-3, EV003-4) [X-003]. That knowledge is honest from 1965 rather than from 1970, and it has not changed the rules, the bombing or the strategy. Its main effect on Washington has been to deliver bad news early.

The RVNAF enters 1968 with the rifles it has carried for four years and a Regional Force that the corps commanders want for their own operations, bound since 1967 by the rule that keeps it in its provinces (EV004-2). The American army has had no rifle crisis and no inquiry, and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights; it is the same army in every other respect, fighting the same battles on the enemy's terms.

The bombers have learned two things in two years: that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot (EV005-2, EV005-3), and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle (EV000-36). The second lesson is the one the headquarters remembers going into 1968.

Phung Hoang has a centre in every district and an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, about one in five of them members of district or village committees, and the Front's district administration in the held belt now governs from the contested edge by courier (EV006-2, EV002-4, F006-4) [X-006]. The word Phoenix has been a press story since March 1967 (EV006-4). About 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps; the American battalions have changed over once or twice each as units, have fought better in the contacts they were granted, and have learned at Dien Ban to keep their reliefs off the dark of the moon (EV006-3) [X-006]. None of it has changed who chooses the battle.

## P3 · Overview

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war [H40], and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end [H40].

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp [H39]. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone [H39].

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way; Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington (EV000-45) [X-001]. The difference is in the Delta and in the hamlets. The Front enters the offensive with a smaller base in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre, recruits fewer men there in 1968 (EV000-52), and loses the towns of the western Delta faster because the hamlets around them warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers (EV000-42). When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled, and the Commission audits the new Phoenix programme against the land register (EV000-54) [X-001]. The American political calendar is not changed by any of it.

The police changes Tet in the same places and in the same way: in the held belt of the Delta the Front's urban cadres are known and its guides do not arrive (EV000-42), and in Hue its lists are better because the Republic's own census fed them (EV000-44). Phoenix, here, is only the name Washington learned for a programme whose district centres had run since 1964 without quotas and under an audit (EV000-54).

Above all this the radar watches the border, and at Khe Sanh it gives the headquarters and the White House the most complete picture of an enemy concentration either has ever held, while the forces attacking the cities move among the Tet crowds where no radar can tell them from anyone else (EV000-41). After the halt it measures exactly how the North uses it (EV003-5), and Commando Hunt opens with a second ledger beside the Air Force's claims (EV000-55).

The ARVN and the territorials go into Tet with the weapons the historical ARVN received only after it. In the district towns, which the first wave attacked with local forces, about a third of the attacks never get inside the compound walls; in the cities, where the PAVN and the main force fight block by block, the rifles lower the cost and do not shorten the battles (EV000-42, EV000-44). The mobilization of June arms its new territorials from stock within the month (EV000-51).

In the hills of Quang Tri the siege of Khe Sanh is measured every night and its trenches destroyed before they reach the wire; the PAVN pulls back about two weeks early, at a much heavier price, and the garrison loses about a third fewer men (EV000-41). It is the most complete American victory of the war on the ground and changes nothing in Washington; the brigade pulled from Thua Thien's hamlets to win it is missing when the Front returns to them. Abrams refuses to make the victory a doctrine of border bases and closes Khe Sanh on the historical date (EV000-51), and Commando Hunt opens against a panhandle net built since 1966 to be cut (EV000-55).

The district centres change Tet in the held belt in one more way: the leaders who came into the Delta towns to direct the uprising had been charted for three years, and a third of them were taken in February (EV000-42). In Hue the province centre's informant cards burn in the first hour and the district centres' do not (EV000-44). The American battalions that fight the offensive have been together for months, and the advisers beside the ARVN have been in their posts for a year; the battles are shorter at the edges and the same at their centres.

## EV000-42 · The Tet Offensive: the first wave · 30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,004,006
*Initiative: enemy*
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps [H39]. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet [H]. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country [H39]. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital [H39].

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns [H]. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed [H39]. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing [H39]. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed [H39].

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces [X-001]. In IV Corps it goes differently in two of them. At Can Tho, the corps headquarters on the Bassac, the battalions that attacked the town and the Binh Thuy air base moved up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh that GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left in the hands of their own Popular Forces (EV001-6); PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, two of them fought, and the 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault [X-001]. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters, and Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, with far less of the town destroyed than on the historical course [X-001]. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days [X-001]. At Ben Tre, in Kien Hoa, the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta, where the government had titled little outside the town because it held little outside the town, the battle and the destruction were as they were historically [X-001]. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964 (F001-4, F001-17). Allied and enemy losses for the country as a whole are not materially changed by it, and I do not adjust them [X-001].

The police and the chiefs changed the first wave in the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon, and changed it less than they might have, because the infrastructure they had driven from the villages had gone to the towns (EV002-5). In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced on 31 January to lead the uprising, and in the first two weeks of February arrested about 1,700 of them on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs [X-002]. Where the Front's guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later: the battalions that attacked Can Tho waited on the night of 30–31 January for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal who never arrived, because both had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier [X-002]. The ARVN 21st Division, whose desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its strength on the muster roll, and the half of the ARVN that was on Tet leave elsewhere was a smaller half in IV Corps (F002-15) [X-002]. In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands, where the police counted for little against PAVN regiments and VC main-force battalions, the battle went as it did historically [X-002].

The rifles changed the first wave everywhere the ARVN and the territorials fought, and most where the fight was small. Historically most of the ARVN went into Tet with the M1 Garand and the carbine, the M16 having reached only the Airborne, the Marines and a few regiments in 1967, and the Regional and Popular Forces with weapons of the Korean War; American officers watched ARVN soldiers in Saigon and Hue fight with eight-round rifles against attackers with AK-47s and B40s, and the M16 airlift to the ARVN was one of the first decisions after the offensive [H]. In this war every ARVN soldier and every territorial had carried a modern carbine for four years. The difference is clearest in the district towns, where the first wave was made by VC local-force battalions and companies against garrisons of an RF company or two, a PF platoon and the district police. Of the 64–72 district towns attacked historically, I estimate that the attackers penetrated about two-thirds on this course against nearly all historically, and that the ones they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, as at Long Huu in 1964 (EV002-2) (F004-16) [X-004]. At Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st with one squad gun lent by the 7th Division and its own carbines, and the battalion, which had planned to be through the town by 0400, was still in the market when the 7th Division's 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived at 0730 [X-004]. In the provincial capitals and the cities, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments and the fighting was block by block, the rifles changed casualties and not outcomes. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,500, against 2,788 historically, and do not adjust the enemy's losses, which were never measured to a precision the change would show in [X-004].

Phung Hoang added a layer to that harvest. The province committees of Phong Dinh, Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long had sent their senior cadres into the towns to direct the uprising, the men who were to form the "revolutionary committees" of each city, and the PIOCCs at Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long had carried charts of those committees since 1965 with photographs from the biometric enrolment of their arrested relatives and ralliers (EC006-1) [X-006]. Of about 140 province- and district-committee cadres who came into the three towns, about 50 were taken by 15 February, most of them on the identifications of ward police who had their photographs, and the Can Tho "revolutionary committee" never held its first meeting [X-006]. The ARVN battalions that cleared the three towns had American advisers who had been with them for about a year, and in Can Tho the 21st Division's 1st Battalion, 31st Regiment had an adviser, Captain Jerome Pike, who took the call of a PF corporal on the Xa No canal in Vietnamese at 2340 on 30 January and moved the battalion's reaction company to the university before the attackers reached it (F006-2) [X-006]. The American units committed to Tet in IV Corps, the 9th Division's 2nd Brigade and the Mobile Riverine Force, had been together since their arrival in 1967 and fought the streets of My Tho and Vinh Long as the units they had trained as [X-006]. None of this touched Saigon, Hue or the Highlands, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments that no district centre had ever charted.

## EV000-44 · The battle of Hue · 31 Jan – 2 Mar 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · HIST-ALT · 000,002,004,006
*Initiative: enemy*
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River [H45]. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions [H45]. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division [H45].

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle [H45] [H]. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west and did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February [H45]. The ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel; the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March [H].

The squad weapons made Hue somewhat less costly to retake and no shorter. On the first night the Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound with M4A1s and four squad guns against the 800th and 802nd Battalions' assault teams, where historically it held with M16s it had only recently received; the difference was small, because the Hac Bao was among the few ARVN units historically re-armed by then [X-004]. Across the river the Marines fought the street battle with two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman with an M110A2 who, in a city where the PAVN held the upper floors and the far side of every street, could put rounds into a window at 200 metres from cover that a squad's rifles could not reach. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines' after-action report credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and the public health building between 4 and 6 February (EC004-5) [X-004]. What took the blocks was what took them historically: the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, tear gas and men going through walls. The weather, the Citadel's walls and the PAVN's reinforcement from the west set the length of the battle, and the rifles touched none of them. I put the ARVN dead at about 410 and the American at about 215, against 458 and 250 historically [X-004].

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded, on the historical course [H45]. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source [H45]. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed [H45]. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800 [H45] [H]. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war.

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966 (EV000-26), and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household (F002-9) [X-002]. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one [X-002]. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor [X-002].

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order that this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred: every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a rehearsal every quarter [X-002]. In the provinces the plan was rehearsed. Whether it would be carried out on the day it was needed was a question for another year.

The Thua Thien province centre was in the province headquarters south of the river, which the attackers held from 31 January until the 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines retook it on 6 February [H45]. Its informant cards were not in the attackers' hands. The PIOCC's chief, Major Ton That Dinh Lang, had kept the cards of the province's 340 informants in a single safe, against Hieu's rule, because he did not trust his district centres' security; when the attack reached the compound wall at about 0330 on 31 January he burned the cards in the safe with a can of gasoline and was killed an hour later on the stairs [X-006]. The district centres of Huong Thuy and Phu Vang, outside the city, kept their copies and lost them when their offices were overrun on 31 January and 1 February; the Hue city security section's lists for the southern suburbs included about 60 informants and centre staff named from those files, of whom about 40 were among the dead found in the graves at Phu Thu and Da Mai Creek [X-006]. The national file in Saigon, which held every one of those names, was never in danger in 1968. It was the first time the record showed what a fused file was worth to the enemy, and the destruction order of 9 March was written with the Huong Thuy file in front of it (F006-25).

## EV000-54 · The Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix · 1 Nov 1968 – Jan 1969 · The countryside of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,006
*Initiative: allied*
On the historical course Phung Hoang, the Vietnamese programme to coordinate the attack on the VC infrastructure, had been established by decree in December 1967 out of the American ICEX programme of June 1967, and Thieu put his own decree behind it in July 1968 [H52]. Its instruments were district intelligence centres, the CIA-run Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the police; its method was to identify, capture, turn or kill the cadres of the parallel administration [H52]. On 1 November 1968, at the urging of Komer and his successor William E. Colby, the government launched the Accelerated Pacification Campaign, a three-month drive to raise about 1,000 contested hamlets to relative security before any ceasefire could freeze the map, using RF and PF, Revolutionary Development cadre and the new People's Self-Defence Force, with regular forces screening [H].

The campaign met its targets by the Hamlet Evaluation System, a scale filled in by American district advisers under pressure to show progress (A13), and in many places those hamlets did become more secure after dark, because the VC local forces that had protected the infrastructure had died in the offensives [H]. Phoenix's own figures, 81,740 people "neutralized" between 1968 and 1972, 26,369 of them killed, were produced against monthly quotas that encouraged false arrests and padding, and about 87 percent of the killings credited to it were by ordinary military operations [H52]. Communist cadres later described 1968–72 as their hardest period, and some American advisers concluded that Phoenix had been a mortal threat in some provinces and a paper programme in others [H52].

In this war the campaign is launched on the historical date under the same men, and Phoenix is not launched at all, because Phung Hoang has run in every district since 1965 without quotas (EV006-1) [X-006]. Thieu's decree of July 1968 is issued, as a reaffirmation that adds his name to Khanh's [X-006]. Three things are different. The district intelligence centres have worked from the land register since 1964, which by November 1968 records about 900,000 titled hectares and the households that hold them; for the first time a district knew who lived in each titled hamlet, and who had left it for the Front, from a record compiled for another purpose and not for the quota [X-001]. And the Commission Against Corruption's inspectors, under the decree of 1964 rather than an agreement Colby made, had sampled the neutralization reports of 40 districts in IV and III Corps each quarter since 1965 and checked each name against the register, the province prison rolls and the PRU and police reports [X-001]. The sample reported in February 1969, the sixteenth, found about one claim in seven unsupported across the 40 districts: fewer than on the earlier course, because there were no quotas to pad and the districts had learned since 1965 that a name would be checked (F001-1, F001-3, F006-13) [X-006]. False arrests for ransom were still cases before the Special Court, about 30 in the quarter [X-006]. The register had been made public in 1964 only as parcel numbers, after Can Giuoc (EV001-3); in 1968 it was used, for the first time, as a list of persons, and the Front's propaganda said so [X-001].

The third is that Phung Hoang was not a new instrument here but an old one with a new urgency. The centres had sat in the Special Branch's district offices since 1964 (EV006-1), and the Special Branch had been running target folders in the held belt since then (F002-7) [X-002]. In the held belt the unsupported share in the February sample was under a tenth; in the districts recovered after Tet, where the Accelerated Pacification Campaign pushed the centres into villages a few weeks old, it was about a quarter, and in the eleven districts where the province chiefs let the PRUs and the sector S2 build the files, about two-fifths [X-006]. Without quotas the contested belt did not become a paper programme, but where the files were thin it became the raid on a rallier's grudge, as at Duc Thanh, and the Commission's cases came mostly from there (F006-15) [X-006]. The audited count for 1968 was about 9,600 neutralized, with about 1,400 killed, of whom about 500 in the programme's own operations; on the historical course the claims for the year were larger and were never audited [X-006]. In the held belt it was a refinement of what the police had done for four years, and the Front's cadres there had left before it began (F002-11).

## P3 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent [H40].

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that. About 900,000 hectares have been titled, the Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier [X-001]. The American decision of 1968 is unchanged, and so is its timing.

The National Police numbers about 95,000 and the Special Branch has buried its first constables of the Tet battles; the infrastructure in the held belt is weaker than anywhere since 1959, and every file it keeps is now held under a destruction plan [X-002].

The enemy's rear enters Phase IV counted: the surge after the halt is measured, the first dry season of Commando Hunt is scored against the radar as well as the claims (EV003-5, EV000-55) [X-003], and the American decision of 1968 is unchanged by any of it.

The RVNAF ends 1968 larger and armed throughout with weapons the historical one was still waiting for; its new territorials have rifles and too few sergeants (EV000-51) [X-004]. The gap the rearmament opened over history is now closing from the historical side, as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969.

The PAVN leaves 1968 having learned that it does not lay siege to a position the Americans can measure; that is the one way of choosing its battles it has lost [X-005]. It chooses all the others. The panhandle is harder to cut than history's, because the bombers taught its engineers how (EV000-55).

Phung Hoang enters 1969 with five years of files, an audited count the press has learned to quote, and no quotas; the Front's Delta committees have lost a third of the leaders they sent into the towns (EV000-42, EV000-54) [X-006]. The Hue district files have shown what the national file would be worth to the enemy (EV000-44). The selected advisers are in every field post, the first of them on their second tours, and the American battalions have fought the year as units.

## P4 · Overview

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971 [H40]. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 [D] to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971 [H40]. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest [H62]. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The revelation of My Lai, the Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the trial of Calley and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win [H]. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington [X-001]. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001]. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle (EV001-8) [X-001].

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses (EV002-6). The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective (EV000-66); Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season (EV000-68); and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically (EV000-71).

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open (EV000-58). Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy (EV000-68). In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home (EV000-71).

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless (EV000-60). The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election (EV002-6). And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute (EV000-72). The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

## EV000-60 · Midway, the first withdrawals and the Nixon Doctrine · 8 Jun – Dec 1969 · Midway Island; Guam; South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: allied*
On 8 June 1969 Nixon met Thieu on Midway Island and announced the first withdrawal of US troops, 25,000 men, to be completed by the end of August [H]. Elements of the 9th Infantry Division left the Mekong Delta in July, and the whole 3rd Marine Division had left I Corps by the end of the year [H] [H58]. On 25 July, at Guam, Nixon set out what became the Nixon Doctrine: the United States would honour its treaty commitments and provide a nuclear shield, but would look to its Asian allies to provide the manpower for their own defence [H]. Further withdrawals were announced in September and December [H]. For the troops still in Vietnam, the withdrawals made every American death harder to justify. Discipline in the US Army declined through 1969–71; drug use, racial violence and "fragging", attacks on officers and NCOs with grenades, became endemic in rear areas and in some combat units [H]. The withdrawals were faster than the RVNAF's improvement, and they ran on a timetable set by American politics, not by the war.

In this war the withdrawals are of units, and they are cleaner for it. Under the individual system the first increment, Keystone Eagle, took the 9th Division's units home with whichever men had the least time left, and filled the units that stayed with the rest, so that the withdrawal broke up every company it touched [H]. Here the 9th Division's battalions and the 3rd Marine Division's had each arrived as units and left as units, whole, on the dates their tours ended; no company that stayed received the short-timers of one that went, and the commanders of the battalions that stayed kept men they had trained [X-006]. The cost was arithmetic: an increment had to be made of units whose tours ended in the window, so the withdrawal schedule set by Washington had to be met by shortening some tours and lengthening none, and about 12,000 men in 1969–70 went home one to four months early with their battalions [X-006]. The breakdown of discipline was not prevented. Fragging, drugs and racial violence rose in 1970–71 in battalions that had trained together, as the best study of the war had led this headquarters to expect; they rose somewhat less inside the rifle companies and as much in the rear (F006-16) [X-006]. Unit rotation preserved the unit and could not preserve the war's purpose in the minds of men who knew it was ending.

## EV002-6 · The Board and the Directorate in the palace · Jan 1970 – Sep 1971 · Saigon and the provinces · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
The Provincial Administration Selection Board had outlived four governments because each had found it cheaper to keep than to break, and because a province chief appointed outside the list could not sign a budget, a title or a counterpart-fund disbursement while the Aid Compliance Board stood behind the rule [M002]. By 1970 the American lever was lapsing with the withdrawal (EV001-8), and Thieu, preparing for the election of 1971, wanted the provinces [X-002]. He did not abolish the Board. As the terms of its two general officers expired in January 1970 he replaced them with officers of his own staff, and from that spring his Prime Minister, General Tran Thien Khiem, appointed the third name on the list for about half the province vacancies and for most of those in the provinces with the largest electorates [X-002]. The list still excluded the incompetent and the bought; it no longer excluded the loyal mediocre [X-002].

The Inspectorate's reports, which had gone to the Board and the Commission since 1964, were routed through the presidential Inspectorate General from July 1971 under the decree that subordinated the Commission (EV001-8) [X-002]. The National Police had followed the same road earlier: its Director General had been the President's appointee in fact since 1966 (EV000-26), and in 1970–71 the Special Branch's Saigon headquarters spent a growing share of its effort on the opposition, the An Quang Buddhists and the Ky camp, as historical police chiefs always had (F002-16) [X-002]. The rural posts, the census and the district sections went on much as before, because they were too many and too local for the palace to want them [X-002]. In September 1971 province chiefs campaigned for Thieu as they did on the historical course; the difference was that about two-thirds of them were men who had passed an examination and served a district first, and the villages they governed were governed better than they were represented [X-002].

The palace took Phung Hoang's national file the same way, without abolishing anything. In February 1970 Khiem, who was Minister of the Interior as well as Prime Minister, moved the national centre from the Directorate of National Police to the Central Intelligence Organization's building and replaced Hieu, who resigned rather than serve under the new chairman, with a Military Security colonel of the President's staff [X-006]. The file held about 210,000 names by then, every one entered to the three-source standard and many with fingerprints and photographs, and it now served a second purpose: from the spring of 1970 the national centre vetted every candidate for province and district office and every officer above major against it, and its analysts were asked to chart the An Quang Buddhists' committees and the Ky camp's officers by the same method they had used on the Front [X-006]. For the election of 1971 the provincial copies of the file were called in to Saigon "for consolidation", and about two-thirds of the PIOCCs were left with district extracts; from September 1971 the complete national file existed in one building (F006-25) [X-006]. Ashby's successor protested in writing in October 1970 and was told, correctly, that the decree of 1964 placed the committee under the Minister of the Interior [X-006]. The district centres went on working the Front as before, and their audited count did not fall; what had changed was who else the file was for.

## EV000-72 · The war on trial at home · 29 Mar – 30 Jun 1971 · Fort Benning, Washington and New York · HIST-ALT · 000,002,006
*Initiative: neither*
On 29 March 1971 a court-martial at Fort Benning convicted Lieutenant Calley of the premeditated murder of at least 20 people at My Lai and sentenced him to life imprisonment; two days later Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest pending appeal [H47]. His sentence was later reduced, and he was paroled in 1974 [H47]. In April veterans opposed to the war demonstrated in Washington and threw their medals onto the Capitol steps [H]. On 13 June the *New York Times* began publishing the Pentagon Papers, the Defense Department's secret history of the war's decisions to 1968, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg; on 30 June the Supreme Court refused to stop publication [H]. The administration's response, the "plumbers" unit formed to stop leaks, led to Watergate [H]. By mid-1971 a majority of Americans told pollsters the war had been a mistake [H].

In this war the Phoenix programme comes before Congress on the historical date, in the hearings of the House Government Operations subcommittee in July 1971, at which, on the historical course, William Colby testified that more than 20,000 people had been killed under Phoenix since 1968 [H]. Here Colby gave the audited figures instead: since January 1965 about 58,000 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 36,000 tried or held under review, about 15,000 rallied and about 7,000 killed, about 2,600 of them in the programme's own operations and the rest in military operations against men whom the centres had named (F006-13) [X-006]. The hearings were damaging and less damaging than they were historically. The story had been told since Duc Thanh in 1967 (EV006-4); the Commission's quarterly samples since 1965 gave the administration an independent count of unsupported neutralizations, about a tenth in the held belt and a quarter in the contested belt across six years, and the magistrate review and capped detention gave it a legal frame to describe (F002-14) [X-006]. The witnesses who hurt the programme most were former Americans of the PRUs' contested-belt advisory teams of 1967–68, and the subcommittee's report singled out the eleven districts the Commission had already named [X-006]. The Con Son "tiger cages" of July 1970 had happened as they did historically, in a prison the statute did not reach [H] [X-002]. The Public Safety appropriation for 1972 passed with a cut of about a tenth rather than the larger cuts its critics proposed, and the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers were kept [X-002].

## P4 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving [H40]. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before [H62]. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed (EV000-65) [X-001]. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it (EV001-8) [X-001]. These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal [H56] [H]. The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent (EV002-6) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

The radar's ledger has made the interdiction debate honest and changed nothing it was about: the Trail is larger at the end of 1971 than ever, the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia is mapped and open, and the aircraft that count both will leave with the Americans (EV000-68) [X-003].

The ARVN's I Corps ends the phase believing, on the evidence of Lolo and Sophia, that a besieged base under American bombers holds (EV000-71). It is true, and it is the belief that will cost it most when the bombers go [X-005].

Phung Hoang ends 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon, serving the President as well as the war (EV000-72, EV002-6) [X-006]. About 3,500 selected advisers remain, with the territorials and the province teams more than with the divisions; the American battalions have gone home whole and their discipline has decayed on its historical course (EV000-60) [X-006].

## P6 · Overview

Phase VI is the war without the Americans, and the end of the Republic of Vietnam. The Paris Agreement of January 1973 takes US forces out and returns the prisoners; it leaves the PAVN in the South, the Thieu government in Saigon, and the war to be decided between them. The ceasefire never holds. In 1973 Saigon is on the offensive, recovering hamlets and roads, while the North rebuilds; in 1974 the two are evenly matched, and the ARVN, short of ammunition, fuel and spare parts as American aid falls, bleeds in a war of attrition it cannot sustain; in January 1975 the North tests whether the United States will respond to the seizure of a province, and when it does not, it commits everything [H72].

The shape of the phase is three slow years and seven fast weeks. The constraints that bind the American role are now law, not policy: the Case–Church amendment ends all US combat activity in Indochina from 15 August 1973, the War Powers Resolution limits the President's power to send forces back, and Congress cuts military aid. Watergate removes the President who gave Thieu his assurances. In Hanoi, the 21st Plenum of October 1973 returns to revolutionary violence, and the PAVN builds a road network east of the mountains, a fuel pipeline into the South and an army of corps. In Saigon, the economy, hit by the oil shock and the departure of the Americans whose spending had sustained it, and a government that has narrowed its base, face the offensive of 1975 with an army spread thin across the whole country. When the blow falls at Ban Me Thuot in March, Thieu's decision to abandon the Highlands turns a defeat into a rout, and in eight weeks the Republic falls. The PAVN enters Saigon on 30 April 1975.

In this war the army that fights the last three years has been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the abundance of American ammunition, and the ration of 1974 finds it more dependent on volume than the historical one (EV000-89). It fights the attrition of 1974 and the stand at Xuan Loc with better weapons than its enemy and too few rounds to use them, and the hoards it builds against the ration are lost with I Corps (EV000-91, EV000-96).

The bombers leave in August 1973 without bombing Neak Luong, and the coordinate cell is disbanded with nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to (EV000-86). The ARVN fights 1974 and the defence of Phuoc Long with plans written for an air force that no longer comes, and loses Thuong Duc and Phuoc Long as history did, with its officers knowing exactly what they lack (EV000-91, EV000-92).

Phung Hoang outlives the Americans as a Vietnamese programme and helps the Republic's offensive of 1973 in the Delta; its national file, consolidated in Saigon since 1971, is taken intact on 30 April 1975 with the files of Da Nang a month earlier, and it serves the victors' security service as the most complete list of their enemies they will ever hold (EV000-84, EV000-94, EV000-98). The advisers are gone in March 1973, and nothing of their eighteen months survives them but the counterparts who remember them, who are on the lists.

## EV000-84 · The ceasefire that never held · 28 Jan – Dec 1973 · The countryside of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: allied*
In the last days before the ceasefire both sides grabbed for hamlets and roads, raising flags to claim territory, the "war of the flags"; the ARVN recovered most of what the PAVN and VC had seized [H]. Fighting resumed in earnest by March [H51]. Through most of 1973 the Saigon government was on the offensive: the Communists, rebuilding supply lines and wary of provoking the return of American air power, largely stayed on the defensive, while the ARVN and territorial forces pushed into PRG-held areas [H72]. Both sides violated the agreement from the start; the ICCS could not stop it, and Canada left the commission in July 1973 [H]. From late 1973 the PAVN grew bolder as the American political system turned on itself, and by early 1974 the two sides fought roughly on equal terms [H72].

In this war the Republic's offensive of 1973 had one instrument the historical one lacked: district centres nine years old in the Delta, with files the American departure did not take with it. When the American advisers left the centres in March 1973, the centres stayed, under the police statute, as they had always been; the Phung Hoang name was retired in December 1972, as historically, and the work went on under the Special Branch [H] [X-006]. In the "war of the flags" of January–February and the push into PRG-held hamlets that followed, the IV Corps provinces moved first into villages whose infrastructure the centres had charted, and the Front's district committees in Chuong Thien, Vinh Binh and Kien Hoa lost about a fifth of their members in 1973, about twice the historical rate [X-006]. It was a gain in the hamlets and not on the frontiers, and it changed nothing in the balance of 1974, when the PAVN's divisions decided what the hamlets were worth (POS001-4).

## EV000-94 · The collapse of I Corps · 13–30 Mar 1975 · Quang Tri, Hue and Da Nang · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: enemy*
On 13 March Thieu ordered Truong to give up most of I Corps and withdrew the Airborne Division towards Saigon as a reserve [H75]. On 20 March he ordered Truong to hold only Da Nang; on 29 March he ordered him to turn back and retake it, which Truong refused [H75]. The contradictory orders, given as the PAVN 2nd Corps with the 304th, 324th and 325th Divisions attacked from the north and west and the 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade struck in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin, broke I Corps [H75]. Northern Quang Tri was lost by 18 March; Hue was surrounded by the evening of 25 March and abandoned; about 6,000 soldiers of the 2nd Division deserted on 28 March; and the PAVN entered Da Nang on the afternoon of 29 March [H75]. Soldiers abandoned their units to find their families, and the sea evacuation from Da Nang became chaos, with people fighting to board the ships [H75]. I Corps had about 134,000 men against more than 75,000 PAVN and VC; three ARVN infantry divisions and the 147th Marine Brigade ceased to exist, and about 58,722 soldiers were captured at Hue [H75]. The PAVN took 129 aircraft, 179 armoured vehicles and 327 artillery pieces [H75]. The best corps in the ARVN, under its best commander, had been destroyed without a decisive battle.

In this war the PAVN's security troops who entered Da Nang on 29 March went first, by a list prepared in Hanoi, to the I Corps regional office of the Special Branch and the Quang Nam PIOCC [X-006]. The destruction plan of 1968 had been rehearsed every quarter for seven years; on 28 March the regional chief, who had sent his family to the docks that morning, did not order it, and the clerks who might have done it on their own had gone to find theirs [X-006]. The Quang Tri and Thua Thien centres had burned their files on the 18th and 25th; Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai did not, and the regional office held district extracts of the national file for all five provinces with about 40,000 names and about 11,000 photographs [X-006]. That the collapse would deliver the files with the corps was the risk the memorandum that created them had named (F006-25).

## EV000-98 · The fall of Saigon · 29–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: enemy*
By late April five PAVN corps, Group 232 and the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th Corps, with 15 infantry divisions and three tank regiments, encircled Saigon in the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H77]. Rockets and artillery hit Tan Son Nhut on the morning of 29 April; two US Marine guards, Corporal Charles McMahon and Lance Corporal Darwin Judge, were killed, the last American servicemen killed in action in Vietnam [H]. At 10:51 Operation Frequent Wind began: Marine and Air Force helicopters lifted 395 Americans and more than 4,000 Vietnamese from the Defense Attaché Office compound, and 978 Americans and about 1,100 Vietnamese from the embassy roof and grounds, to the ships of the Seventh Fleet [H77]. Ambassador Graham Martin left on a helicopter at 04:58 on 30 April [H77]. Thousands of Vietnamese who had worked with the Americans were left behind [H].

At 10:24 on 30 April Minh announced an unconditional surrender [H77]. PAVN tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace, Tank 390 through the main gate, and at 11:30 a soldier raised the flag of the Front over the palace [H77]. The Republic of Vietnam had ceased to exist. Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City; Vietnam was formally reunified as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam in July 1976 [H].

In this war the Central Intelligence Organization's building held the national Phung Hoang file, about 240,000 names with photographs and fingerprints for about 90,000, consolidated there since 1971 (EV002-6) [X-006]. The destruction plan for the building required the order of the Director General of the CIO, who left by the embassy's helicopters on 29 April; a section chief and two clerks burned the index and part of the photographic file in the building's courtyard on the morning of the 30th and were arrested in it that afternoon [X-006]. The PAVN's security troops, with the Ministry of the Interior's southern section behind them, had the rest of the file by evening [X-006]. It is not possible to say how many of the men and women sent to re-education camps after 1975, or kept there longest, were sent because their names were in it; the camps' own records are not available, and the victors had other lists [X-006]. It is possible to say that the file held the names of every informant, centre officer, PRU member, Chieu Hoi rallier and selected adviser's counterpart of eleven years, that it was the most complete record of the Republic's friends the war produced, and that this headquarters built it.
