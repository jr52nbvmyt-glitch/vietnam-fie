## §4.0 The course of the war

The war still ends on 30 April 1975, and this memorandum has made the end more cruel in one exact particular. Everything else it does is inside the war, not at its edges: it gives the Republic a way to reach the men who govern the Front's villages from above them, four years before history did; it gives the American battalions men who know one another; and it gives the ARVN and the territorials Americans who can talk to them and stay. None of the three touches who chooses the battle, the sanctuaries, or the American clock. I will say what they did change at its full weight, because some of it was large.

The war opened on 1 January 1964 as it has in every memorandum since the first: Diem two months dead, the Strategic Hamlet Program collapsing, and a junta in Saigon that signed, in one session, a land decree, a police decree and, this time, a third and shorter one. The Phung Hoang decree put a committee over the new Special Branch and ordered a coordinating centre in every province and district as the branch opened its offices there. It forbade quotas in terms. It required a magistrate's warrant for every arrest on a centre's file, and it gave the Commission Against Corruption the right to check every claim of a person neutralized, name by name. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the same statute, as police, under the province chiefs. Lieutenant Colonel Tran Dinh Hieu, a security officer whom Diem had twice passed over for refusing to work on the Can Lao's lists, took the national centre, and an American detachment of about 80 Vietnamese-speaking analysts under Lieutenant Colonel Martin Ashby sat beside him and did the one thing that 2026 could teach 1964 without its machines: they took every arrest, every rallier and every captured notebook apart within the week, and drew the district committee above the hamlet as a chart of names.

The centres went where the posts were and nowhere else. That was Hieu's rule and the most important decision of the year. The province chiefs of the coastal Centre asked for centres before they had posts, and he refused them, because a file built in a village the government did not hold after dark is a rallier's grudge with a stamp on it. About 100 district centres were open by Christmas 1964, all in the Delta and around Saigon, behind the 600 posts of the police decree. In the same months the Army chose its first class of field advisers by board, sent them for eight months of Vietnamese and advising, and from October put them into the subsectors and battalions on eighteen-month tours. Captain Samuel Whitcomb took over Can Giuoc on 9 October, four months after the killings there, and on his first night asked the district chief, in Vietnamese, which families still held their titles at the district office and why. A COSVN security bulletin of December warned district committees that the new Americans understood what was said in front of them.

The Front's answer to the centres was the answer it had given to the posts: it moved. From January 1965 its district committees in the held belt left the villages for the contested edge and governed by courier. That was the gain and the limit in one move. In March the centre at Hoc Mon, on the edge of Saigon, followed the Hoc Mon committee out to the hamlets along the Saigon River. A courier of nineteen, arrested with tax receipts sewn into her hat, rallied when the Chieu Hoi officer offered her brother's release from Con Son, where Diem had sent him without trial, and named the house where the committee's security section met. The Provincial Reconnaissance Unit went in at three in the morning, took four men alive and a steel box of reports on the posts of four villages, and by June nine of the committee's fourteen members were dead, in prison or had rallied. The Front governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi for the next three years, and collected about a fifth of its old tax there.

Above all this the summer of 1965 ran as before. Ba Gia and Dong Xoai were lost at a lower price, the Marines and the airmobile division came, and the President took the decision of July. The district centres did not slow the PAVN regiments in the Highlands by an hour. They were not built to.

Phase II was the American war at full stride, and its battles were the same battles. What changed was the men who fought them and the villages behind them. Every American battalion now arrived as a unit that had trained together for six or seven months, served twelve months under one commander, and was relieved in place by another unit with an overlap. The new man died less often because there was no new man; the platoon knew its sergeant and the company its captain; the battalion that fought at night fought as it had rehearsed. I cannot show this in a battle that history records, because the battalions of 1965 had arrived as units on the historical course too, and the decay of the individual system came in 1966 and after. I can show what it bought and what it cost in the one place the record has, which is Dien Ban in August 1966.

The 1st Battalion, 26th Marines came to relieve the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines in the hamlets south of Da Nang, and the dates had been fixed in March and were known to every woman who washed the Marines' clothes. On the night of 18–19 August, with half the old battalion already on the ships, the R-20 Battalion sent sappers through a tree line the old company had kept clear and the new one had not learned to watch. They got inside the wire. Then the half of the bargain that the rotation had paid for appeared: platoons that had trained together for nine months closed on the breach without orders, the squad guns took the paddy under grazing fire, and by half past two the sappers inside were dead. A Popular Forces platoon whose leader the subsector adviser, Major Carl Haney, had introduced to the company commander five days earlier reported the withdrawal route by radio, and two F-4Bs caught it at the river crossing. Fourteen Marines died, four times the battalion's average night. The enemy had found the week each American battalion was a stranger and aimed at it; the battalion had been strange to the ground and not to itself. MACV staggered its reliefs from October and kept them off the dark of the moon, and both orders cost men in theater. The R-20's own verdict, captured in November, is the best summary of the provision I have seen: the new Marines "fight like the old ones in their third month and know the ground like the old ones in their first."

Haney is the other half of that night. The adviser was the continuity the American battalion lacked, and that is the second thing this memorandum did. By 1967 about 7,000 selected advisers held every field post below corps. They spoke to their counterparts. They stayed long enough to see the second season. They reported what they saw, because their careers were protected and their promotion depended on the board, not on the counterpart's success. They did not acquire one ounce of authority. A battalion commander who would listen listened longer; one who would not, did not.

The third thing went further and then went wrong at its edge. In the held belt the centres finished what the police had started. NIGHT LANTERN in Can Giuoc took not only the district committee's security chief in February 1966 but, two weeks later, its deputy secretary and its finance chief, in a hamlet at the edge of the Rung Sat where they believed themselves beyond reach. The deputy secretary rallied and spent the year drawing the province committee above him. By the end of 1966 the Front's district administration in the held belt was broken: not driven out, which the police had done, but beheaded, two years before Tet and three before the historical programme reached the same layer. That is the strongest positive effect of this memorandum, and I do not want it lost in what follows.

What followed was success sending the method where it did not belong. By 1967 the held belt had little left to find, and the corps commanders wanted the same results from the contested provinces of I and II Corps. On 11 February a PRU team in Mo Duc, acting on a file built from two ralliers of one family feud, killed a schoolteacher, his wife, his father and two of his children. Hanoi had decided at the Thirteenth Plenum, for its own reasons, to fight on the diplomatic front, and its radio had the story in eight days and an American wire service in three weeks. "The American Phoenix" was in the newspapers in March 1967, three years early. The Commission's inspectors found the file's flaw within a week, the team leader was convicted in June, the raid files were withdrawn from fifteen contested districts, and the first audited count was published: about 14,500 neutralized since 1965, about 800 killed, one claim in ten unsupported in the held belt and one in four outside it. It was the only figure on the attack on the infrastructure that the newspapers never had to correct. It did not undo Mo Duc.

Tet came on its historical dates and was decided where it was decided historically, in Saigon, in Hue, at Khe Sanh and in Washington. In the Delta towns it was decided earlier. The province committees sent their senior men into Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long to form revolutionary committees, and the province centres had carried their photographs for three years; about 50 of 140 were taken in the first fortnight, and the Can Tho committee never met. In Hue the province centre's chief burned his informants' cards in his safe as the attackers reached the wall and was killed an hour later on the stairs. The district centres outside the city did not burn theirs, and about forty of the people named in them were among the dead found at Phu Thu and Da Mai Creek. The order of March 1968 to keep every file under a destruction plan was written with that file in front of it.

After Tet there was no Phoenix to launch. Thieu reaffirmed a decree four years old; the Accelerated Pacification Campaign pushed the centres into villages a few weeks recovered, and there the files were thin and the Commission's cases came. The audited count for 1968 was about 9,600, and nobody had to discover in 1969 that it was false.

Phase IV ran on its historical rails. The American battalions went home as units, whole, and the companies that stayed were not filled with other units' short-timers; the breakdown of discipline came regardless, a little less inside the rifle companies, as much in the rear. Phoenix came before Congress in July 1971 with an audited count since 1965 of about 58,000 neutralized and about 7,000 killed, and the hearings were less damaging than history's because the story was four years old and the figures were not in dispute. The selected advisers stayed on eighteen-month tours as their numbers fell, and the territorials they advised were the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans left. The divisions, whose commanders the adviser could not change, were not.

And the palace took the file. In February 1970 the national centre was moved into the Central Intelligence Organization's building; Hieu resigned rather than serve the new chairman; and from that spring every candidate for province office and every officer above major was vetted against 210,000 names entered to the standard of a warrant. For the election of 1971 the provincial copies were called in "for consolidation". From September 1971 the complete national file existed in one building.

Phase V turned, as it did historically, on PAVN armour, American bombers and the competence of three ARVN commanders, and nothing in this memorandum moved any of the three. The selected advisers at An Loc and Kontum were better linguists than history's and called the same bombers.

In 1973 the centres outlived the Americans and helped the Republic's offensive in the Delta take about a fifth of the Front's district committees in three provinces, about twice the historical rate. It did not matter in 1974, when the PAVN's divisions decided what the hamlets were worth. In March 1975 the Quang Nam centre and the I Corps regional office did not burn their files, because the man who had to give the order had sent his family to the docks that morning. On 30 April the Director General of the Central Intelligence Organization left by helicopter, the destruction of the national file needed his order, and a section chief and two clerks burned the index in the courtyard before they were arrested in it. The rest went to the victors' security service by evening: every informant, every centre officer, every PRU member, every rallier and every counterpart of every selected adviser for eleven years.

That is where the line bends and where it holds. This memorandum bent the war inside the held belt further than any before it: the Front's district government there was broken in 1966, not 1969, and the method that broke it was audited, warranted and without quotas from its first day. It made the American battalion a better instrument and the adviser a truer witness. It did not change who chose the battles, what the sanctuaries regenerated, or when the American public stopped paying; and it handed the victors, at the end, a list this headquarters made with care. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975. I signed the decree that built the file, and I would sign it again, and I would want the destruction order in the hands of a man who could not leave.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

Phase I changes in the held belt and nowhere else, and the reason is in the ordering of the decree. The centres were placed behind the posts of the police decree, so Phung Hoang added a second layer to a Special Branch that already knew the hamlet: the committee above it, named from the arrests and ralliers the branch was already making (F006-1). Of about 1,900 arrests in the held belt by July 1965, about 260 were members of district and village committees, perhaps two and a half times what the branch's folders alone would have reached [X-006]. Operation STILL WATER at Hoc Mon is the case for the method and its strongest positive effect at small scale: nine of a district committee's fourteen members taken in four months, sixty-three arrests on warrant, four found unsupported by the audit, and a tax take in the posted villages down to a fifth (EV006-2, F006-26). The biometric enrolment caught its first rearrested cadre under a second name at Cho Moi in December 1964, which is the kind of gain that never makes a report and closes the oldest hole in the district magistrate's door (F006-3).

The advisory corps arrived slowly by design. About 1,100 of the first class were in post by the end of 1964, so most of the summer of 1965 was fought with the old short-tour advisers beside the selected ones (F006-8). At Ba Gia and Dong Xoai the general reserve was advised by men of both kinds, and I do not adjust either battle; the advisers of 1965 could not change the generals' plans, and the plans were the problem. Unit rotation did not bite in Phase I. The first American battalions arrived in 1965 as units on the historical course too.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front read the centres within a season, as it had read the posts (F006-20). Its answer was to move the district committees out of the held belt from January 1965 and govern by courier from the contested edge, which cost it daily control of the posted villages and bought its leaders a year of safety, until STILL WATER showed that a courier could be followed (F006-4). Its second answer was penetration, aimed now at the centres rather than the posts; the Dinh Tuong clerk who had copied informant cards in 1964 was the first of a series (F006-5). Its third was propaganda, and it was premature: the bulletin of December 1964 warning that the new Americans understood Vietnamese was a security precaution, not yet the "Phoenix" campaign of 1967. None of this was unprovoked; the enemy's initiative of this memorandum falls in Phase II.

### Causal chains

The chains of this memorandum begin here and land later: C006-1 and C006-4 begin with the centres and the advisers of 1964 and are placed in Phases II and IV, where their terminal links fall.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits district committees outside the held belt, a method proven at Hoc Mon, and about a third of the field posts held by selected advisers, with the rest filled by the end of 1965. Phase III inherits charts of the province committees that will come into the towns at Tet. Phase VI inherits the file.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II carries all three provisions at once, and each works through a different channel. Phung Hoang finishes the held belt. NIGHT LANTERN in Can Giuoc took the district committee's deputy secretary and finance chief, not only its security chief, and the rallied deputy secretary drew the province committee above him (EV002-4). By the end of 1966 the Front's district administration in the held belt governed from outside it, and its leaders there had been arrested, killed or replaced at a rate history reached only in 1969–70 (F006-26). That is the strongest positive effect of this memorandum, grounded in Can Giuoc and Hoc Mon, and it is the reason the end vector for knowledge of the infrastructure moves to its top band in this phase. When the centres' count fell in 1967 because the work was done, it was read as success, not failure, because there were no quotas to miss (F006-18).

The method's success is also its perverse effect. With the held belt cleared, the pressure moved it into the contested belt, where files were built from ralliers and the night was not held, and at Duc Thanh a PRU team killed five members of the wrong family (EV006-4, F006-15). The audit, the Special Court and the withdrawal of raid files contained the damage inside the Republic and not outside it.

Unit rotation works at the scale of the battalion. EV006-3 is the event narrated at length for a downstream effect: the enemy timing his attack to the relief window, and the adviser and district chief carrying the knowledge across it (F006-6, F006-2, F006-9). The battalion held the breach because it had trained as a unit, and was hit because it had arrived as one. Across the phase I put the American battalions' gain at fewer men lost in their middle months and better fights at night and in the ambush; my estimate of 3–6 percent fewer American killed in 1966–69, perhaps 1,000–2,000 lives, is a judgment resting on that rate and shown so far only at Dien Ban, and it is not to be repeated as a measured figure until a second event carries it [X-006]. It is not enough to move the casualty band, and I have not moved it. Fifteen to twenty-five thousand more men were in theater at the peak because of the overlaps, and the expansion battalions of 1966 came two to three months later than they would have (F006-12).

The advisory corps moves the ARVN's effectiveness a band in this phase, together with the rifles of 1964: the battalions and subsectors now have advisers who speak to them and stay, and the territorials most of all (F006-8). The weak link moves from the adviser to the counterpart, and so to the division commanders, whom no adviser could change (POSCP01-3).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's responses were the ones predicted. COSVN learned the relief calendar within a year and aimed at it at Dien Ban; it learned the language-trained adviser at once (F006-21). It poisoned files where it could, and the Mo Duc rallier's feud was not the Front's design but served it as well (F006-20, F006-22). It moved its legal cadres into the towns, where the centres knew less (EV002-5).

<!-- initiative -->
The enemy's initiative came from Hanoi's own politics. In January 1967 the Thirteenth Plenum of the Lao Dong Central Committee adopted the line of "fighting while negotiating", and on 28 January Foreign Minister Nguyen Duy Trinh said that talks could follow an unconditional halt in the bombing [H]. What drove it was Hanoi's reading of Soviet pressure for negotiations, of the American election calendar and of the world's view of the bombing, and the leadership's need to give the diplomats a front of their own; nothing this command did provoked it. Its consequence for this memorandum was a demand on the Front's propaganda apparatus for material that would carry abroad, and the apparatus found Duc Thanh within eight days. The Phoenix story of March 1967 was Hanoi's initiative meeting this headquarters' mistake.

### Causal chains

**C006-1** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV006-1) The district centres chart the committees above the hamlets in the held belt from 1964 and STILL WATER and NIGHT LANTERN take them (EV006-2, EV002-4) → the Front's district committees govern the held belt from the contested edge by courier, and the held belt has almost nothing left for the centres to find by 1967 (F006-4, F006-18) → the corps commanders and CORDS push the method into the contested belt, where files are built from ralliers' grudges and the PRUs raid on them (F006-15) → at Duc Thanh a raid on a feud's file kills a schoolteacher's family, Hanoi's new diplomatic front carries it to the American press, and the word Phoenix enters the American vocabulary three years early as the name of a murder programme, the success of the method in the villages it was built for having made it notorious in the ones it was not (EV006-4).

**C006-2** [months] Unit rotation fixes each American battalion's relief dates months ahead and the whole battalion changes over in a fortnight (EV006-3) → the Front learns the calendar and attacks the incoming company at Dien Ban in its first week, through ground the outgoing company knew (F006-6, F006-21) → MACV staggers reliefs over six weeks by company, keeps them off the dark of the moon and requires a night side by side at every position (EC006-7) → the overlap grows from two or three weeks to six for each battalion, the peak strength in theater rises by the 15,000–25,000 men that the expansion battalions of 1967 then wait for, and the enemy's tactic against the relief is paid for in American spaces and not in American dead (F006-12).

### Downstream

Phase III inherits a held belt whose district committees are broken and whose province committees are charted, a programme with an audited count and a bad name, American battalions that have changed over once or twice as units, and advisers in their second year in every field post. The Hue district files, outside the city, are not yet under a destruction plan.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet changes in the Delta towns and not in its decisive places. The province centres of Phong Dinh, Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long had charted the committees that came into Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long to form the uprising's governments, and took about a third of their members in the first fortnight (EV000-42, F006-1). An adviser who took a PF corporal's call in Vietnamese moved a reaction company in Can Tho before the attackers reached the university (F006-2). In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands the attackers were PAVN regiments and main-force battalions that no district centre had charted, and the battle went as before.

Hue shows the counter-directional finding for the first time in the chronicle: the fused file is the most valuable list in the country to whoever captures it (EV000-44, F006-25). The province centre's chief burned his cards and the district centres did not, and about forty of the people the district files named were among the dead.

After Tet there was no new programme to launch, and the absence is the change (EV000-54, F006-13). The quotas, the Phoenix of 1968 that padded its count and arrested for ransom, and the 1969 discovery that a third of its claims were false do not occur. The audited count for 1968 is about 9,600, and the Commission's sample finds one claim in seven unsupported, most of them in the districts recovered after Tet [X-006]. Unit rotation and the advisers lower the cost of the year's battles at the edges, and I do not adjust the year's casualty totals, which were never measured to the precision the change would show in.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's response to the loss of its town committees was the historical one, enlarged: it filled the Delta's district committees after 1968 with northerners and with cadres from outside the province, men who did not know the villages and whom the centres did not know either. That is a cost to both sides. The Hue city security section's use of the district files was not an initiative but a doctrine; it had prepared lists of whom to kill since 1967, and it used the best lists it could get.

### Causal chains

**C006-4** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I) The selected advisers speak Vietnamese, are chosen for it and stay eighteen months, and the corps keeps that tour when every other American tour is twelve (EV006-1) → by 1968 about 15,000–20,000 of them have served, and their reports from the subsectors and battalions are the honest record of the territorial war that Abrams uses for "one war" (F006-24, F006-23) → the Army's personnel system, lobbied by that constituency, keeps the corps at eighteen-month tours through the drawdown and cuts the division advisory teams before the province and district ones → when the last Americans leave in 1973 the territorials and the districts are the best-advised part of the RVNAF and the divisions the least, so that the advisory effort's gain is concentrated in the war of the hamlets and absent from the war of 1972 and 1975, which was decided by divisions (§4.P5).

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1).** C002-2, from memorandum 002, ran from the Hue census of 1964 through Loan's Buddhist detentions of 1966 to the execution lists of 1968, drawn by house. In this war it resolves as predicted on the census and differently on the files: the city security section had the census, and the district centres' files gave it the informants as well, while the province centre's cards were burned. The chain gains a branch rather than a new end.

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits a programme with no quotas to abolish and no scandal to discover, an audited count since 1965, and a national file that has not yet been moved. The Delta's district committees are being rebuilt by outsiders.

### Clock

Tet spends the American clock on its historical dates and the Phoenix story does not move it further, because it had already been spent on that story in 1967. The credibility gap is narrower in one particular: when the historical Phoenix figures would have been discredited in 1969–70, there is nothing to discredit (F006-13). Unit rotation saves some American lives and does not change the casualty lists of 1968 by a band; the decision of 31 March is unchanged (POSCP01-1).

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

The phase runs on its historical rails, and the provisions change three things along them. The withdrawals are of whole units, so the battalions that stay are not broken up to send others home, at the cost of about 12,000 men leaving early with their units (EV000-60). The breakdown of discipline comes regardless, as the card predicted: unit rotation does not prevent it and may hide its worst acts behind cohesion (F006-16). My Lai, which the record leaves as history, is the hard case for that finding: Charlie Company of the 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry had trained and deployed together as a unit on the historical course as well [H], and its cohesion was a cohesion in silence. I have not changed that event, because nothing in this memorandum would have changed it.

The palace takes the national file, and this is where the counter-directional finding becomes the war's business (EV002-6, F006-25). It is the same pattern the record already holds for the police and the Board: the institution survives below the palace, and the palace uses it above. The file vetted candidates and charted the opposition, and for the election of 1971 it was consolidated in one building. That decision is the second link in C006-3.

Phoenix's hearings are less damaging than history's because the figures are audited and the story is old (EV000-72). Knowledge of the infrastructure stays a band above history at the phase's end, because the files are seven years deep rather than three; the infrastructure's strength stays where the record had it, because the historical programme caught up after 1969 and the Front regenerated from the North. The advisory corps keeps the ARVN a band above history through the phase, in the territorials and the districts more than in the divisions.

### Enemy response and initiative

COSVN Resolution 9 of 1969 turned the Front back to protracted war and to the attack on pacification, as historically. Its cadres in the Delta, many of them outsiders since 1968, found district centres that had their predecessors' charts and none of theirs; the centres' yield in 1969–70 came mostly from ralliers among them. The Front's propaganda against "the American Phoenix" ran from 1967 rather than 1970, and by 1971 it had told its story too often to make news with it.

### Causal chains

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1).** C002-4 ran from the falling arrest count of 1966 through quotas only in the contested belt and an outside audit from 1968, to the House hearings of July 1971 and an appropriation cut by a tenth. In this war it resolves differently at its second and third links and the same at its fourth. There are no quotas anywhere and the audit runs from 1965, so the unsupported share is about a tenth in the held belt throughout rather than falling from a third; but the method's extension into the contested belt produces the Mo Duc raid, and the press story four years before the hearings. At the hearings the administration again has an independent count, and the appropriation passes with the same cut, now because the story is old rather than because the count is new. The end is the same and the road to it is not, which is the kind of result the directive asked this review to look for.

C006-4 is placed in Phase III, where its middle links fall; its terminal link lands in this phase and the next, in the drawdown's choice of which advisers to keep.

### Downstream

Phase V inherits a territorial force better advised than any history had and division commands no adviser could change. Phase VI inherits the national file in one building, and the destruction plan that depends on one man's order.

### Clock

The clock is spent by 1969 and the provisions do not refill it. Unit withdrawals make the drawdown look more orderly on television and change no vote in Congress. The Phoenix hearings of 1971 cost the administration less than history's did, and the Public Safety appropriation is cut by the same tenth; the credibility gap is narrower by the difference between an audited count and a discredited one, and tolerance does not move a band (POSCP01-1).

The Easter Offensive is decided by PAVN armour, American bombers and three ARVN commands, at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc, and this memorandum moves none of them. ## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

Phase VI holds two of this memorandum's effects, one small and good and one large and bad. The district centres outlive the Americans and help the Republic's offensive of 1973 take about a fifth of the Front's district committees in three Delta provinces, twice the historical rate (EV000-84); it is a gain in the hamlets, and the record's settled judgment that the hamlets do not decide 1974–75 holds (POS001-4). Then the collapse delivers the files. In I Corps the Quang Nam centre and the regional office do not burn theirs (EV000-94), and in Saigon the destruction of the national file waits on an order from a man who has left (EV000-98). The counter-directional finding of Part 2 is realised at its full weight (F006-25).

The advisers leave in March 1973 with every other American, as the card said they would. What they leave is a set of counterparts who knew them, whose names are in the file.

### Enemy response and initiative

The PAVN's security troops entered Da Nang and Saigon with lists prepared in Hanoi and went first to the Special Branch offices; that was doctrine, not initiative, and the doctrine had been visible since Hue. The use of the file after April 1975 is beyond this headquarters' record, and I do not claim to know how many people it sent to the camps.

### Causal chains

**C006-3** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV006-1) Phung Hoang builds a national file to the standard of a warrant, with fingerprints and photographs, held to its rules by the American lever and the audit → when the lever lapses in 1970 the palace moves the file into the Central Intelligence Organization and uses it to vet officials and chart the opposition (EV002-6) → for the election of 1971 the provincial copies are called in and the complete file is kept in one building, which the palace can control and the provinces cannot leak → on 30 April 1975 the destruction plan for that one building needs the order of a director who has already left, and the victors take intact the most complete list of the Republic's friends the war produced, the care that made the file accurate having made it whole, and its capture by the palace having made it capturable by the enemy (EV000-98).

### Downstream

There is no later phase. The war ends on 30 April 1975 at rung (a).

### Clock

The clock had stopped before this phase began. Case–Church, the War Powers Resolution and the aid cuts run on their historical dates, and nothing in this memorandum changes a vote.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, this memorandum's cumulative force has changed the war against the infrastructure most. The Front's district administration in the held belt was broken in 1965–66, three to four years before the historical Phoenix reached that layer; about 18,000 people were neutralized on warrant by the end of 1967 and about 63,000 by the end of 1971, all audited, about one in five of them members of district and village committees, roughly twice the share I believe the historical programme reached [X-006]. Killings attributed to the programme's own operations were about 2,600 by mid-1971, against a historical claim of more than 20,000 killed from 1968, most of them in ordinary military operations credited to Phoenix [H] [X-006]. There was no ICEX, no quota and no 1969 discovery that a third of the claims were false. The word Phoenix became a scandal in 1967 rather than 1970, over one raid instead of a programme.

The American battalions fought as units: no six-month command tour, no infusion programme in 1966, no company of strangers. I put their gain at a few percent fewer American dead in 1966–69, perhaps 1,000–2,000 lives, as a judgment on a rate shown so far in one event, and at better fights at night and in the ambush; their cost was 15,000–25,000 more men in theater at the peak and expansion battalions later by two to three months [X-006]. The advisory corps put a language-trained adviser who stays eighteen months in every field post from 1965; the territorials and the battalions gained most and the divisions least.

The end is unchanged, and it is worse in one particular: the victors took the national file intact in 1975.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state stays at rung (a), collapse on the historical timeline, and this memorandum does not move it toward (b). Its effects fall where the record has already said the war is not decided: in the held belt's hamlets, which the political provisions of 1964 had already improved, and in the quality of American and advised units in the fights the enemy accepts. The binding deficit in 1974–75 is the military balance after the American withdrawal, with an ARVN dependent on American air and ammunition that it lost in 1973–74 (POS001-4, POSCP01-2). Phung Hoang deepened the government's hold where it already held; unit rotation made the American battalion better in a war it would leave; the advisers deepened influence without authority, and left. Rung (b) would need the Republic to survive while American combat forces were present, which it already does; the rung is held at (a) because the withdrawal and the aid cuts on the historical timeline are unchanged and the RVNAF that meets the offensive of 1975 has gained nothing it could keep in the conventional war.

The war's shape has not outgrown its phases. I propose no redraw.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- The Front's district government in the held belt was broken in 1965–66, three to four years before the historical Phoenix reached that layer, at Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc and across the Delta's posted districts (EV006-2, EV002-4). This is the largest effect of the memorandum and the most durable in the hamlets.
- The attack on the infrastructure was honest from its first day: no quotas, warrants for every arrest, an audit of every claim, and an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized by 1971 that the newspapers never had to correct; there was no ICEX, no padded 81,740, no 1969 scandal (EV000-54, EV000-72).
- American battalions fought as units, with stable commanders and men who had trained together, in every contact the enemy granted them from 1966, and went home whole in 1969–72 (EV006-3, EV000-60).
- Every field adviser from 1965 spoke Vietnamese and stayed eighteen months; the territorials and battalions were advised better than at any time on the historical course, and the advisers' reports were the honest record of the territorial war (EV006-1).
- At Tet the province committees sent into the Delta towns lost a third of their leaders in a fortnight, and the Can Tho uprising government never met (EV000-42).
- The Republic's offensive of 1973 in the Delta took the Front's district committees at twice the historical rate (EV000-84).

### What has not changed

- The enemy still chose every battle, regenerated in sanctuary and accepted battle with the good battalion only when he chose to; unit rotation gave him a relief window to aim at (EV006-3).
- Outside the held belt the method was the historical Phoenix whatever its rules, and Duc Thanh made it a scandal three years early (EV006-4).
- The national file built to warrant standard was captured by the palace in 1970–71 and by the enemy, intact, on 30 April 1975, with the I Corps files a month earlier (EV002-6, EV000-94, EV000-98).
- The breakdown of discipline in 1969–71, and My Lai, were not prevented.
- The advisers gained influence and no authority, made the advised units more dependent on American fire, and left in 1973; the divisions whose commanders decided 1972 and 1975 gained least.
- The American clock, the withdrawal and the aid cuts ran on their historical dates, and Saigon fell on 30 April 1975.
