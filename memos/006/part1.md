# Memorandum 006 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier
<!-- audit -->
*Audit — Memo 006, Part 1/3 · v1.0.5 · provisions: 3 (P006a, P006b, P006c) · dossier fields: 16/16, 16/16, 16/16 (floor 16) · tagged figures: 354 · untagged warnings: 0 · sources: 11 · designations: ~29 (floor 20) · variables pushed: 11 · structural deviation: none*

The order is in three sentences, and I treat it as three provisions because it changes three different institutions. The first is the Republic's own: Phung Hoang, the programme the Americans called Phoenix, is created on 1 January 1964 instead of in 1967–68, and it is built to the way a 2026 headquarters targets an insurgent network. The second and third belong to the American armed forces. The Army and the Marine Corps send their battalions to Vietnam as units, trained together and kept together for the tour, instead of feeding individuals one at a time into units that never stop changing. And the men who advise the Republic's soldiers are chosen for the work, serve eighteen months instead of twelve, and are trained to the standard of the Army's 2026 Security Force Assistance Brigades.

None of the three is a weapon, and none of them finds a regiment under canopy. They work on the war's two slowest variables: what the Republic knows about the enemy inside its population (G-3), and the quality of the men, American and Vietnamese, who lead and advise in the field (G-4, G-6, G-16). The record of this war already contains much of the ground they stand on. Memorandum 002 gave the Republic a rural police, a Special Branch with sole charge of intelligence on the infrastructure, a census and district chiefs chosen by examination from 1964 [M002]. Phung Hoang here is not built from nothing; it is built on that police, and its dossier has to say what it adds rather than what the police already does.

Two readings had to be chosen, and I state them here so that nothing below hides them. "Modernized" I read as 2026 doctrine, law, organisation and analytic method, with the small amount of 2026 equipment that method needs: biometric enrolment kits and computers for the files. It does not bring 2026 signals intelligence, because the enemy in 1964 has no telephones to exploit. "Unit rotation" names no tour length; I take 12-month unit tours, the tour the war actually used, so that the dossier measures the change of rotation method and not a change of tour length, and I note the 2026 Army's 9-month brigade deployments where they bear on it.

## §1.1 Phung Hoang (Phoenix) Programme, formalized 1964 and built to 2026 practice: national committee, PIOCC and DIOCC fusion centres, F3EAD targeting, SEEK II biometric enrolment, warrant-based targeting under the police statute — P006a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**Reading for an institutional provision: D1 is the programme's design, its historical antecedent and its organisation.**

**Historical antecedent.** Phoenix was not one programme but a sequence. The CIA's Counter-Terror teams, later renamed Provincial Reconnaissance Units (PRUs), existed from 1964, and by April 1965 there were about 140 of them of 3–12 men each [S1]. The Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme (ICEX) was created in mid-1967 from a design by Nelson Brickham under Robert Komer [S1]. The Prime Minister's decree creating Phung Hoang followed in December 1967, and President Thieu's decree giving it national standing in July 1968 [S1]. Between 1968 and 1972 the programme reported 81,740 members of the infrastructure "neutralized", of whom 26,369 were killed [S1]. About 87% of those killed died in conventional military operations and were credited to Phoenix afterwards [S1]. Quotas produced false arrests, officials took bribes to release prisoners, and interrogation included torture [S1]. A conviction required three independent sources, and sentences ran up to six years [S1]. The programme was officially ended in December 1972 [S1]. Colby, and the communist accounts written after the war, both describe 1968–72 as the infrastructure's hardest years [S1].

**This war's antecedent.** In this war the Republic already has, from 1 January 1964, a Special Branch with sole charge of intelligence on the infrastructure, a resident rural police, a family census and identity cards, and a statute requiring three independent sources and a magistrate's review within 72 hours [M002]. The record shows ICEX built in 1967 on the Special Branch's district offices, and Phung Hoang described in 1968 as "a new name over an old one" (EV000-54) [X-002]. The record also shows what the 1964 design lacked: the PRUs stayed an American instrument outside the statute, and the army, the Military Security Service and the sector S2s shared what they chose to share [X-002].

**What the provision is.** It moves the whole of Phung Hoang to 1 January 1964 and rebuilds it to 2026 practice. That means five things:

1. **A coordinating structure.** A national Phung Hoang Committee under the Prime Minister, chaired by the Director General of the National Police, with the Military Security Service, the Central Intelligence Organisation, the Joint General Staff J-2 and the Chieu Hoi ministry as members [U]. Under it, a Province Intelligence and Operations Coordinating Centre (PIOCC) in each of the 44 provinces and a District Intelligence and Operations Coordinating Centre (DIOCC) in each of about 240 districts, sitting in the Special Branch's office under the province or district chief [E: the province and district count used for P002b].
2. **A targeting method.** The F3EAD cycle of 2026 practice: find, fix, finish, exploit, analyse, disseminate [S2]. Its distinguishing claim is that exploitation and analysis, not the raid, are the main effort, and that every arrest is a source for the next [S2].
3. **A legal frame.** Every target is a person named in a file, and the finish is an arrest on a warrant from a magistrate, with capture preferred to killing in the rules, under the 1964 police statute [U]. The 2026 practice I draw on is the warrant-based targeting adopted in Iraq after 2009, which I know from the period's record rather than from a source fetched here [E: US practice under the 2009 security agreement].
4. **An instrument of identity.** SEEK II-class biometric kits that record fingerprints, iris and face, matched against an onboard watchlist of up to 120,000 people [S3].
5. **Accountability.** No numerical quotas, and an independent audit of every claimed neutralization by the Commission Against Corruption's inspectors [U] [M001].

**The PRUs.** The provision formalizes; it therefore puts the Counter-Terror teams under the National Police and the statute from their creation in 1964 [U]. They become the Provincial Reconnaissance Units of the Police Field Force, about 44 units of 40–100 men [E: the historical PRU of about 4,000–5,000 men by 1969], armed and paid by the CIA but commanded by the province police chief and bound by the warrant [U]. This is the one structural change that the record of this war says the 1964 design needed and lacked (EC002-5) [X-002].

**Quantity and build-up.** At full coverage the programme has a national centre of about 150 staff, 44 PIOCCs of 25–40 staff each and about 240 DIOCCs of 10–15 staff each [E: historical DIOCC manning of a police officer, an army S2 representative, a Military Security officer, analysts, clerks and a Chieu Hoi liaison]. That is about 4,000–5,000 Vietnamese in the centres [D]. The DIOCCs open where the Special Branch already has a district section and a police post network, which in this war is about 100 districts by the end of 1964 and all of them by the end of 1965 [E: the security-limited occupation of P002a's posts, about 600 by end 1964 and 1,500 by end 1965 per M002].

**Manufacturer of the equipment.** The biometric kit is the Cross Match (now HID Global) SEEK II, about 1,500 of them [E: one for each district station and centre and for field teams] [S3]. Case files and network analysis run on ruggedized laptops with link-analysis software of the i2 Analyst's Notebook type, about 600 [E: two or three per DIOCC, more at the PIOCC], with a national database server at the National Police headquarters in Saigon [E: 2026 practice].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Reading for an institutional provision: D2 is the staff of the programme and the US personnel it takes.**

**Vietnamese staff.** About 4,000–5,000 in the centres [E: D1 arithmetic]. Most come out of the Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the sector S2 sections that already exist; the programme's new hiring is mainly analysts and clerks, about 1,500–2,000 [E: two to five per district beyond the existing staff]. The PRUs add about 3,000–4,500 men by 1966 [E: 44 units of 70–100], and they are police, not soldiers, in this war's statute [U].

**US staff.** About 600 Americans at full coverage [E: manifest arithmetic]:

- one or two advisers in each DIOCC in contested districts, about 300 [E: about 150–200 contested districts]; the historical programme put roughly one US adviser, usually an Army captain or lieutenant, in each DIOCC [E: period accounts];
- three advisers in each PIOCC, about 130 [E: 44 × 3];
- a national cell of about 60 at the Directorate and MACV J-2 [E: committee staff, legal adviser, audit liaison];
- an exploitation and analysis detachment of about 80, document exploitation and network analysts who build the national picture [E: 2026 targeting-cell practice];
- about 40 contractor technicians for the biometric system and the database [E: one per corps plus the national centre and a repair depot].

**Spaces.** About 450 military spaces in the Republic [E: the advisers and analysts, less the CIA officers and contractors]. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so these are not foreclosed. They do compete for one scarce thing: captains who can work in Vietnamese with a police officer, which is the same pool P006c draws on.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**Reading for an institutional provision: D3 is instruments and authorities.**

The programme's instruments are legal, not physical:

- **The file.** Each target is a named person with a file that records the evidence and its sources [U]. A file needs three independent sources before an arrest warrant is sought [M002].
- **The warrant.** A magistrate issues it on the file. The arrest is reviewed within 72 hours, and detention without trial is capped at two years with six-monthly review, under the statute already in force [M002].
- **The finish force.** The arrest is made by the Special Branch, the Police Field Force, or a PRU acting on the warrant [U]. In a contested village the arrest party needs the Popular Forces or an ARVN platoon to reach the target and leave alive [E: POSTNA-2].
- **The offer.** Before the finish, the file allows a targeted Chieu Hoi offer to a named cadre through family or intermediaries [E: the 2026 practice of reconciliation offers to named fighters]. A rallier is debriefed and his knowledge enters the analysis [S2].
- **The audit.** The Commission's inspectors sample claims of neutralization and publish the unsupported share [U] [M001].

What it can compel: the attendance of the army's and the Military Security Service's representatives at the DIOCC, and their files, by decree [U]. What it cannot compel: a corps commander's intelligence staff to share what it does not want to share, or a district chief to act on a file against a relative or a patron [E: the record of the Special Branch, M002].

**Weapons.** The PRUs carry what the Police Field Force carries in this war: M1 and M2 carbines, the M1918A2 automatic rifle and M79 grenade launchers, because the rifles of P004a went to the army and the RF/PF and not to the police [M004] [X-002]. Historically the CIA armed its PRUs with whatever suited raiding, including the Swedish K submachine gun and later the M16A1 [E: period PRU accounts]. The arrest party's radio is the AN/PRC-10 until about 1966 and the AN/PRC-25 after [E: the period's police and territorial radios]. The programme brings no weapons of its own. Its directive is the decree that in history became MACV Directive 381-41 and the Prime Minister's Phung Hoang decree, here issued together on 1 January 1964 [E: the historical Phoenix directives of 1967–68].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**Reading for an institutional provision: D4 is information inputs.**

The programme learns from six inputs, in rough order of value [E: the historical ranking of sources in Phung Hoang reporting, with ralliers and prisoners first]:

1. **Ralliers.** A cadre who rallies knows his cell, his superiors, the courier routes and the tax lists. Exploitation turns one rallier into ten files [S2].
2. **Prisoners and documents.** Every captured document is read by the exploitation cell; the 2026 cycle treats it as the input to the next target, not as a souvenir of the last [S2].
3. **The census and identity cards** of P002a. A stranger or an absent son is visible [M002].
4. **Informants** of the Special Branch, paid and protected.
5. **Biometric enrolment.** Every detainee, rallier and person stopped at a checkpoint is enrolled; a later match ties a name to a face and fingerprint the enemy cannot change [S3]. SEEK II matches against its own watchlist of up to 120,000 without a network [S3]. Its use is in three cases: the cadre arrested twice under two names, the detainee released by bribe and found again, and the rallier who is not who he says he is [E: the uses reported in Iraq and Afghanistan].
6. **Military intelligence.** The sector S2's reporting and the ARVN's captured documents, which reach the DIOCC only if the decree is obeyed [E: the record of this war].

**In contested hamlets and at night** the inputs collapse to the first two. No informant survives in a village the enemy holds after dark, and no census team works there [M002]. The programme can name the cadres of such a village only from those who leave it. **Canopy and monsoon** are irrelevant to its sensors but not to its finish: an arrest party in a base area is a military operation, and the PRUs that went into base areas historically fought as raiders [E: PRU operations of 1967–71].

**Reliability.** Denunciation is the weakest input and the most abundant. A quota converts it into arrests; a file of three independent sources filters most of it [S1]. The record of this war already shows the filter working where a Special Branch section three or more years old runs the folders, with about a tenth of claims unsupported, against about a half where the PRUs and the sector S2 run them (EV000-54) [X-002].

### D5 · Performance

**Reading for an institutional provision: D5 is throughput and capacity.**

**Files.** A DIOCC with 10–15 staff can keep about 200–400 active files and develop about 10–20 targets to warrant standard a month [E: the historical DIOCC's caseload, and the 2026 targeting cell's rate of one developed target per analyst per week or two]. Across about 240 DIOCCs that is roughly 2,400–4,800 developed targets a month at full coverage [D]. That is the ceiling on the programme's rate, and it is about the scale the historical programme claimed at its peak, about 1,700 neutralizations a month [E: 81,740 over 1968–72 per S1].

**Arrests.** The finish rate is lower than the developed rate. I estimate about 30–50% of warrants executed within a month in the held belt and under 10% in the contested belt [E: target availability and the reach of the arrest party].

**Convictions.** The magistrates review each arrest; in this war's record they released about a third of the Special Branch's arrests in the held belt in 1965–66 [X-002]. I expect a similar or slightly lower release rate under the 2026 evidence standard, about 25–35% [E: better files, same courts].

**Rate limit.** The binding constraint is not the analysts but the night security behind the arrest party and the informant [E: POSTNA-2]. The second is the number of trained Vietnamese analysts, about 1,500 needed and perhaps 300 available in 1964 [E: the period's literate police and army intelligence cadres]; the national police academy can train about 600 a year in a 12-week analyst course [E: course capacity].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**Reading for an institutional provision: D6 is the reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.**

The chain runs DIOCC → PIOCC → national committee, with each level reporting to the chief at its own level: the district chief commands the DIOCC, the province chief the PIOCC [U]. The American advisers report separately through the CORDS-equivalent channel to MACV and the embassy [E: historical practice].

**Talking to the ARVN** is the programme's purpose and its weakness. The ARVN's division and regiment intelligence staffs are represented in the PIOCC by decree [U]. The record of the Special Branch shows the army sharing when the province chief is an officer the corps commander trusts, and withholding when he is not [M002]. The 2026 practice brings one new thing here: a fusion cell, where the army and police analysts sit at the same table and work the same file [S2]. It does not bring a culture of sharing, which the 2026 coalition itself built only over years in Iraq [E: the record of joint interagency task forces 2004–08].

**Data.** Files are kept on paper in the district and on laptops at the province and national level [E: power and security in the district]. Biometric records move from the district to the national server by courier on removable media, weekly [E: no data network in 1964; SATCOM is reserved for military command under CONFIG's constellation setting].

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Reading for an institutional provision: D7 is reach and security preconditions.**

The programme works in three belts, which are the belts the record of this war already uses [X-002]:

- **The held belt**, where the police posts are settled and the Popular Forces live in the hamlet: the programme works fully. In this war that is most of the Delta's populated districts around the provincial towns and the districts around Saigon by 1966 [X-002].
- **The contested belt**, where the government holds by day and the Front by night: the programme names cadres from ralliers and prisoners and finishes them only by raid. Here it is most dangerous, because a raid on a denunciation kills the wrong man [S1].
- **The Front's base areas and villages**: the programme names cadres from documents and ralliers and cannot reach them [E: the reach of a police force].

**Security precondition.** A DIOCC needs a district town that is not overrun, a Special Branch section, and police posts in the surrounding villages [M002]. The PRU needs helicopter lift for any arrest beyond the posts [E: PRU practice].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Running cost.** About $15–25 million a year in 1964 prices [E: 4,000–5,000 staff at police pay with allowances, PRU pay and equipment, centre buildings and vehicles, informant funds of about $2–4 million].

**2026 equipment.** About 1,500 biometric kits and 600 laptops need power and spares [E: D1]. Each district station needs a small generator and a battery charging station [E: 2026 kit]. Failure rates in heat and humidity mean a repair depot and a float of about 15–20% [E: field electronics in tropical service]. The national server needs air-conditioning and a protected room [E: 2026 practice].

**What the RVNAF cannot supply.** The biometric system's maintenance and its database software need contractor support for the life of the system [E: no Vietnamese electronics industry]. Everything else, the files, the analysts, the courts, is Vietnamese by design.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The programme is Vietnamese from its first day; what is transferable is the American share. The advisers can be withdrawn district by district once a DIOCC has run for two or three years [E: the record of the Special Branch, whose sections three or more years old ran the folders best per X-002]. The biometric system cannot be transferred in the sense of self-sustainment; without contractor support, about half the kits would fail within two years [E: tropical failure rates and no spares], and the system would revert to paper fingerprint cards matched by hand at the district station, which is what P002a's census already uses [M002].

**What happens to it under Vietnamization.** It becomes what the Republic's government makes it. The record of this war shows the President subordinating the Commission in 1971 and capturing the Selection Board (EV001-8, EV002-6) [X-001] [X-002]. A national targeting apparatus with files on every suspected cadre is a far more valuable prize than either, and its capture by the palace, for use against the non-communist opposition, is the most likely outcome after 1971 [E: POS002-3].

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** where the four conditions of P002a's police already hold: night security in the hamlet, a Special Branch section that has run for some years, a district chief who is not the Front's or a landlord's man, and courts that review [M002]. There, the 2026 method adds network depth: the district and province committees above the hamlet cell, whom the police alone named rarely [E: the record of the Special Branch's harvest in the held belt].

**Performance collapses** in four conditions:

- **Quotas.** If the GVN or MACV sets numerical targets, the programme reverts to the historical one, with false arrests and the killing of the wrong people [S1].
- **The contested belt.** Without night security, the finish is a raid, and raids on thin evidence kill civilians [S1].
- **A coup.** Each change of government changes the committee's chairman, and the programme can be pointed at the losing faction [E: the record of 1964–65 coups in this war].
- **Tempo.** The 2026 method was built for an enemy that regenerates; it assumes the cycle can outpace regeneration. Against an infrastructure that replaces a district cadre from the North or from a sanctuary, the rate of replacement and not the rate of arrest sets the outcome [E: POSTNA-3 applied to the infrastructure].

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **Penetration.** A fused centre holds the names of the informants as well as the targets. A single agent in a PIOCC compromises more than an agent in any one service did historically [E: the logic of fusion]. The record of this war already shows the census captured at Hue and used for the Front's lists (EV000-42) [X-002]. A fused file is a larger prize.
2. **Feud.** A file built on denunciation becomes an instrument for land disputes, debts and family quarrels [S1].
3. **Bribery.** Release for payment undoes the evidence standard at its last step [S1].
4. **Partial failure** looks like a programme that works in the held belt and becomes the historical Phoenix in the contested belt, which is the pattern the record of this war already shows for 1969 (EV000-54) [X-002].
5. **Total failure** is political: a congressional or press campaign that makes the programme untouchable in Washington, and its withdrawal of American support.

**During an outage**, the Special Branch continues its own work, as it did before the provision [M002].

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one:** one national committee, a handful of PIOCCs in the provinces around Saigon and in the Delta, and the advisers' first files [E: the build-up in D1]. The Vietnamese analysts are not yet trained; the method is in the advisers' heads [E: training lag].

**Month twelve:** about 100 DIOCCs and 20 PIOCCs, about 600 trained analysts, about 300–500 biometric kits in use [E: the build-up in D1 and D5]. The first network maps of district committees exist in the held belt.

**What declines first** is the American advisers' continuity, because they rotate on 12-month tours unless P006c's longer tours apply to them, which the provision does not state [E: the DIOCC advisers are intelligence officers, not field advisers]. Second, the biometric kits' availability, which falls from about 90% to about 70% in the first wet season without a repair depot [E: tropical electronics]. Third, the evidence standard under pressure for results, which is the historical failure [S1].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical Phoenix of 1968–72:** this programme starts four years earlier [S1], sits on a Special Branch that has kept files since 1964 rather than one rebuilt in 1967 [M002], and has no quotas [U]. The historical figure for unsupported claims was about a third across 40 districts in this war's record, a tenth where an old Special Branch section ran the folders [X-002]. I estimate that a programme built to 2026 evidence standards from 1964 lowers the unsupported share in the held belt to about a tenth everywhere it has run three years, and does not lower it in the contested belt [E: the record's split by district].

**Against P002a's Special Branch alone:** the programme adds the district-and-above cadre. I estimate it doubles the share of neutralized cadres at district level or above, from about 5–10% of all neutralized to about 10–20% [E: the historical claim that most Phoenix neutralizations were low-level, and the 2026 network method's focus on nodes].

**Against 2026 practice:** in Iraq, warrant-based targeting and fusion cells worked on a population with telephones, a coalition of about 150,000 troops and drone surveillance [E: the record of 2007–09]. Here none of those exist. The 1964 programme keeps the method and loses most of its inputs.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P006a -->
- **It does not find the main force.** It targets the infrastructure inside the population; it does nothing against a PAVN or VC regiment (A6).
- **It does not work without night security.** In the contested belt it names cadres from ralliers and prisoners and can only raid; raids on thin files kill the wrong men.
- **It is not the 2026 targeting machine.** It keeps the method and loses its inputs: no telephones to exploit, no drones over the hamlet, no data network. The biometric kits work offline against a watchlist of up to 120,000.
- **It is not a new police.** It is a coordinating layer and a method over P002a's Special Branch; without that branch it has nothing to coordinate.
- **It does not make the army share.** The decree puts the army and the Military Security Service in the room; it does not make them hand over their best files.
- **It does not stop the infrastructure regenerating.** Cadres lost in the South are replaced from the North and the sanctuaries; arrest rate is not the measure, replacement rate is.
- **It does not protect itself from capture.** A national file on every suspected cadre is the most valuable instrument a President can seize against his opposition.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy

Where night security holds, it denies the infrastructure its upper layer: the district and province committee members whom the hamlet police seldom reach, named from ralliers, prisoners and documents and arrested on warrant from 1965 rather than 1968. It denies the cadre arrested twice under two names his second identity. It denies the Front the propaganda of the historical Phoenix wherever the rules hold. It denies nothing in the villages the Front holds and nothing against the main force.

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P006a -->
In this war Phung Hoang is a coordinating layer and a method placed over a Special Branch that already exists, and its value is the difference between a police that knows the hamlet and one that knows the district committee above it. Where the police posts and the Popular Forces hold at night, it should roughly double the share of senior cadres among those neutralized and do it four years earlier than history, with fewer false arrests, because it has no quotas and its files are built to warrant. Where night security is absent it becomes the historical Phoenix, and its 2026 label will not change that. Its greatest risks are penetration of a fused centre, the press and Congress, and capture by the palace after the Americans leave. It is a real addition to the war against the infrastructure and none at all to the war against the main force.

## §1.2 US unit rotation for Southeast Asia: Army and Marine Corps battalions and brigades deployed as units for 12-month tours in place of the individual replacement system — P006b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**Reading for an institutional provision: D1 is the system's design, its historical baseline and how units are organised.**

**The historical baseline.** The Army sent its soldiers to Vietnam for 12 months and the Marines for 13, each man with his own date of return, his DEROS [S5]. The 12-month tour was tied to the two-year term of the draftee [S4]. Westmoreland set command tours of six months in 1965, so that more officers would get command [S4]. Units that deployed whole in 1965 were scheduled to go home together a year later, so in 1965–66 the Army shuffled men between units to spread their return dates, which ended unit integrity in the first year [H]. From then on the war was fought by units that were never the same for two months together. One veteran recorded five platoon leaders and four company commanders in one year [S4]. Lyndon Johnson never mobilized the reserves, so the replacement stream came from the draft and from repeat tours of the regulars [S4] [S8].

**Earlier and later practice.** Korea moved replacements in packets of four from 1952 [S5]. The Army's COHORT programme under General Edward C. Meyer, from 1979 to 1983, kept companies of about 100–180 men together for three years [S5]. From 2003 the Army deployed brigade combat teams as units, for 12 months and from 1 January 2012 for nine, with a goal of 24 months at home between deployments [S6].

**What the provision is.** From the first deployment of American ground combat units in 1965, every Army and Marine Corps maneuver battalion deploys as a unit, for a 12-month tour [U]:

- **Manning.** The battalion's men are assigned together about 6–9 months before deployment, after individual training, and are not moved out except for cause [E: the 2026 Army's stabilization window before deployment].
- **Collective training.** The battalion and its brigade train together for about 4–6 months, ending in a force-on-force exercise at a jungle training area, Fort Sherman in Panama or Schofield Barracks in Hawaii [E: the 2026 pre-deployment combat training centre rotation, with 1964 facilities].
- **Command.** Company and battalion commanders serve the whole tour [U]. Westmoreland's six-month command tour is not adopted [S4].
- **Relief in place.** A battalion is relieved in its area by a whole battalion, with an overlap of two to three weeks, the incoming unit patrolling beside the outgoing one [E: 2026 relief-in-place practice of left-seat and right-seat rides].
- **Losses during the tour.** Battle and non-battle losses are replaced in trained packets of four to ten, assigned to one platoon together [E: the Korea packet system per S5, and the 2026 practice of replacement squads].
- **Support units** rotate by company and detachment [E: the level at which they are organised].

**Which units.** The first units to arrive whole under the historical schedule are the ones that test the system: the 9th Marine Expeditionary Brigade at Da Nang in March 1965, the 173rd Airborne Brigade in May, the 1st Infantry Division's 2nd Brigade and the 1st Cavalry Division in the summer and autumn, the 3rd Marine Division and the 1st Marine Division through 1965–66, and the 25th Infantry Division, the 4th Infantry Division and the 9th Infantry Division in 1966 [H]. The 101st Airborne Division and the 23rd Infantry Division follow in 1967 [H]. Each of their battalions is relieved by a battalion of the same division formed at home, so the divisions keep their flags in Vietnam and their battalions change under them [E: the 2026 Army's practice of rotating brigades under a deployed division headquarters]. The airmobile battalions of the 1st Cavalry Division are the hardest case, because their UH-1D and CH-47A crews and air-ground skills take longest to train collectively [E: airmobile training time of the period].

**Quantity.** About 30 maneuver battalions in 1965, rising to about 105–110 at the 1968–69 peak [E: the historical Army and Marine maneuver battalions in the Republic]. Each deploys and returns once a year, so about 105–110 reliefs a year at the peak [D].

**The draftee.** A draftee serves 24 months; about 4 months of basic and advanced training, about 6–7 months of unit training, and a 12-month tour leave about 1–2 months [D]. The unit cycle fits the draft term only if the battalion is formed of men who finished training together, deploys within about seven months, and comes home to be discharged [D]. The battalion therefore lives for about 20 months and is reformed after each tour [E: the COHORT life cycle per S5, shortened to the draft term].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Reading for an institutional provision: D2 is the personnel in the system and the spaces it consumes.**

**Unit strength.** An Army infantry battalion of the period has about 800–900 men, a Marine battalion about 1,100–1,200 [E: TO&E of 1965–68]. A brigade with its support is about 4,000–5,000 [E: TO&E]. The provision changes none of these.

**Spaces in theater.** The overlap at each relief puts two battalions in one area for about two to three weeks, about 6% of the year [D]. Across the force that is about 3–5% more men in the Republic on an average day than with individual replacement [E: overlap only for maneuver battalions and part of the support]. At the 1968–69 peak of about 540,000 [H], that is about 15,000–25,000 more spaces at any one time [D]. CONFIG sets no ceiling, so this is not foreclosed, but it costs ships, aircraft and money.

**The rotation base.** The total number of soldiers needed is about the same as historically, because every man still serves one 12-month tour [D]. What changes is that the men waiting to go must be formed into units in advance, and the units returning must be broken up, so the Army's units in the United States become a three-step cycle of forming, training and deployed [E: the COHORT cycle per S5]. For each battalion in Vietnam the Army needs about two more in the cycle at home [E: a 20-month unit life with 12 months deployed]. Without reserve mobilization those come from the active Army and the draft [S8].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**Reading for an institutional provision: D3 is instruments and authorities.**

The instruments are personnel policy:

- **Assignment stabilization**: a soldier assigned to a deploying battalion stays in it from formation to return [U].
- **Command tenure**: command for the whole tour [U].
- **Packet replacement**: casualties replaced in groups to one platoon [E: S5's Korea packets].
- **Collective training standard**: a battalion is certified before it deploys [E: 2026 practice].

The authority is the Secretary of the Army's and the Commandant's over assignment, which needs no law [E: the period's personnel authority]. What it cannot compel is the draft term, which is set by law at two years, and the length of the tour, which is set by the Secretary of Defense [S4].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**Reading for an institutional provision: D4 is information inputs, which here means what the unit knows about its area.**

The individual system's one virtue was continuity of local knowledge: a battalion always had men who had been in the area for months, and the new man learned from them [S7]. Unit rotation inverts that. A whole battalion arrives knowing nothing of its area, and its knowledge leaves with it [E: the 2026 experience of unit reliefs in Iraq and Afghanistan]. The relief in place is the instrument for passing what the outgoing unit knows, and in 2026 practice it passes routes, informants, local leaders and the enemy's habits over two or three weeks [E: 2026 relief-in-place practice]. It passes perhaps half of what the unit knew [E: the period's judgement of reliefs in Afghanistan].

**At night, under canopy and in monsoon** the gain is in the unit's own skills. A platoon that has trained together moves in the dark, sets an ambush and holds fire better than one assembled from strangers [E: the cohesion literature summarised in S5 and S7]. The first relief of each year in the wet season of the Delta or the northeast monsoon of I Corps costs most, because the new battalion learns the ground in the worst weather [E: the season's effect on the unit's learning].

### D5 · Performance

**Reading for an institutional provision: D5 is throughput and capacity.**

**Unit effectiveness over the tour.** The period's estimate was that a soldier's effectiveness rose for his first few months and declined after about 180 days [S4]. Under individual rotation a battalion averages the curve over its men [D]. Under unit rotation the whole battalion moves along the curve together: slow in its first month, best in months three to eight, and tired in its last quarter [E: S4 applied to a unit].

**Turbulence.** Historically an infantry company might change most of its men in a year and its commander two or more times [S4]. Under this provision the company changes only its casualties, in packets, and its commander only if he is killed, wounded or relieved [U]. In a heavily engaged battalion, losses of about 30–50% of its riflemen over a year are possible [E: the casualty rates of the heaviest-engaged battalions of 1966–69], so the unit is still refilled, but by trained packets.

**Movement.** About 105–110 battalion reliefs a year at the peak, about two a week, each moving about 800–1,200 men and some equipment [D]. Heavy equipment stays in theater and is signed over [E: 2026 practice of theater-provided equipment]. Men move by air in C-141A Starlifter and C-130E aircraft and in commercial charters, so the load falls on Tan Son Nhut, Bien Hoa, Cam Ranh and Da Nang [E: the period's personnel terminals].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**Reading for an institutional provision: D6 is the reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.**

The chain is unchanged. The interface with the ARVN changes in one way: a US battalion partnered with an ARVN regiment or a province changes all at once each year [E: the logic of unit relief]. The ARVN commander loses the American he knew on one day and meets one he does not [D]. Under individual rotation, the change was gradual [S7]. The advisers of P006c, on 18-month tours, are the continuity across the American unit's relief [E: the two provisions' interaction].

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Reading for an institutional provision: D7 is reach and security preconditions.**

The provision applies everywhere American ground units are. It needs a secure relief: an area where two battalions can overlap for two or three weeks without inviting an attack [E: 2026 practice]. The enemy watches reliefs and attacks units in their first weeks [E: the record of attacks on newly arrived units in 1965–66]. The provision therefore bears hardest on the units in the northern provinces and on the border, where the enemy can mass on short notice [E: ENEMY.engagement_control].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Transport.** About 105–110 battalion moves a year at the peak, about 100,000–130,000 men flown out and in by unit [D]. The historical individual stream already flew about the same number of men, so the change is the lumping: charter aircraft fly full units on fixed dates [E: the period's MAC and charter airlift].

**Equipment.** Theater-provided equipment means each battalion's vehicles and heavy weapons stay; the unit trains at home on its own and fights on a set it signs for [E: 2026 practice]. This adds maintenance in theater and a second set of equipment at home [E: about 5–10% more equipment in the Army's inventory].

**Training base.** Collective training at home requires ranges, instructors and an opposing force for about 60–75 battalions a year in the cycle at the peak [E: two in the cycle for each deployed]. Fort Sherman and Hawaii cannot take more than a few battalions at a time, so most collective training is at Fort Polk, Fort Benning, Fort Lewis and Camp Pendleton [E: the period's posts].

**What RVNAF cannot supply:** nothing here is theirs to supply.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The provision is an American policy and is not transferable. The ARVN already rotated by units in one sense: its divisions stayed in their corps areas for the war, and its soldiers stayed in their units for the war, with no tour at all [E: the period's ARVN manning]. Its problem was desertion and leave, not rotation [E: G-4]. What the provision offers the ARVN is the American example of collective training and a commander kept in post, which the advisers can carry [E: P006c].

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** when a battalion is formed on time, trains for its full period, deploys whole and takes moderate losses. Then it fights as a unit that knows itself, with a commander it knows [E: the cohesion literature in S5 and S7].

**Performance collapses** in four conditions:

- **Heavy losses.** A battalion that loses half its riflemen in a few months, as some did in 1966–68, is refilled so much that it is again a unit of strangers [E: the record of the most heavily engaged battalions].
- **The draft term.** If deployment slips more than about two months after formation, draftees reach their discharge date before the tour ends [E: D1 arithmetic], and the unit must be refilled.
- **A surge.** When the force grows quickly, as in 1965–66 and early 1968, units must deploy before they are trained [E: the historical buildup].
- **The relief.** The first weeks in a new area are when the battalion is weakest [E: D4].

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **The return of individual replacement by default.** Heavy losses and the draft calendar force individual fillers, and the system quietly reverts [E: the Army's experience with COHORT per S5].
2. **The new unit's first month.** A whole battalion new to its area at once; the enemy can time attacks to it [E: D7].
3. **Leaders.** Stability does not make a bad commander good, and a bad commander kept for a whole tour does more harm than one rotated after six months [S5].
4. **The late-war collapse of discipline.** It followed the war's politics and the draft more than the rotation system [S7]. Unit rotation slows it; it does not prevent it [E: S7].

**Partial failure** is a battalion that deploys as a unit and is mostly replaced within six months. **During an outage**, the individual system is what remains.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one of a tour:** a trained, cohesive battalion that does not know its area; patrols are cautious and slow; losses to mines and booby-traps are high [E: the new man's pattern applied to a unit].

**Month six:** the battalion's best period; it knows the area and is still fresh [E: S4's curve].

**Month twelve:** fatigue, about 20–40% of its men replaced in packets if heavily engaged [E: D5], and the attention of the men turned to home [E: S4].

**What declines first** is the unit's alertness in its last two months [S4]. **Across the war**, what declines first is the quality of the rotation base: by 1968 the Army's units at home are filled with men just back from a tour and new draftees, the same strain that ruined the historical Army's home units [E: the period's readiness reports].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical individual system:** the company commander serves 12 months instead of about six [S4]. The platoon has the same lieutenant and sergeants for the tour instead of a stream [S4]. Before deployment the battalion has about 4–6 months of collective training instead of none as a unit [E: D1]. The cost is about 3–5% more men in theater on average [E: D2] and a once-a-year loss of local knowledge [E: D4].

**Against COHORT:** COHORT showed that stability alone did not produce effective units and that leadership failures persisted [S5]. The provision adds collective training and a command tour, which COHORT lacked in practice [E: S5].

**Against the 2026 Army:** the 2026 brigade deploys for nine months with a goal of 24 at home [S6]. This provision deploys for 12 with a much shorter home period, because there is no reserve [S8].

**Against the cohesion literature:** the best study argues that individual rotation was a "bum rap" and that cohesion in Vietnam failed for other reasons [S7]. I take that seriously. The provision's gain is real but smaller than the war's veterans and critics believed: I estimate it at one band of US unit quality plus one for the command tour, against a band of loss for the yearly relief [E: S5 and S7 weighed].

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P006b -->
- **It does not change the tour.** Each man still serves 12 months, and the total number of soldiers needed is about the same [D].
- **It does not keep local knowledge.** A whole battalion arrives new to its area once a year, and a relief in place passes perhaps half of what the outgoing unit knew.
- **It does not survive heavy losses.** A battalion that loses half its riflemen is refilled until it is again a unit of strangers.
- **It does not make bad leaders good.** It keeps commanders for the whole tour, good and bad.
- **It does not stop the late-war breakdown of discipline.** That followed the war's politics and the draft more than the replacement system.
- **It does not fit the draft term easily.** A battalion must deploy within about seven months of forming or its draftees leave before the tour ends.
- **It does not touch the ARVN.** It is an American personnel policy.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P006b -->
It denies the enemy the American unit of strangers with a new commander every six months: the platoon that does not know its own men, the company that forgets its lessons, the ambush sprung by the newest man. It gives him back one thing: a whole battalion new to its area once a year, at a time he can predict.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P006b -->
Unit rotation trades a constant trickle of strangers for a yearly flood of them. Inside the tour it gives the American battalion stable leaders, collective training before it deploys, and men who know each other, and that should make it fight better at night, in the ambush and under fire, and lose fewer men in its middle months. It costs a few percent more men in theater, a larger formed rotation base without the reserves, and a battalion that knows nothing of its area once a year. The best study of the war doubts that individual rotation was the cause of the late-war breakdown, and this provision will not prevent it. The gain is real and moderate: it improves American units, it does not change which battles the enemy accepts.
<!-- /card -->

## §1.3 Selected US Advisory Corps: volunteers chosen by board and assessment for the field advisory posts, 18-month tours, 2026 Security Force Assistance Brigade training — P006c

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**Reading for an institutional provision: D1 is the corps' design, its historical baseline and its organisation.**

**The historical baseline.** American advisers served with the RVNAF from the 1950s in the Military Assistance Advisory Group and from 1962 under MACV [H]. Most served 12-month tours, as everyone did [S4]. Selection was by assignment, not choice, and the advisory post was widely seen as less valuable to a career than command of an American unit [E: the period's officer assessments]. Training was short: an in-country adviser school at Di An of about two weeks, and from 1969 a six-week Military Assistance Training Advisor (MATA) course at Fort Bragg that included Vietnamese language [S9]. Some advisers attended a 12-week Defense Language Institute course [S9]. From late 1967, mobile advisory teams (MATs) of five men worked with the Regional and Popular Forces; 354 were authorized and more than 300 operating [S9]. Advisers who failed to adapt were sent to the rear [S9].

**The 2026 model.** The Army created its Security Force Assistance Brigades from 2017 [S11]. A brigade has about 800 soldiers in 36 advisor teams, each of 12 advisers and eight security personnel [S10]. Its soldiers were meant to be volunteers, its leaders men who had already succeeded in the same post in a brigade combat team [S10]. They take a six-week course at the Military Advisor Training Academy, as much as 16 weeks of language, and training in foreign weapons, combat medicine, driving and survival [S10]. The first course began on 7 August 2017 and trained about 525 soldiers [S11]. The first brigade suffered attrition up to 70% and filled billets to the day it left, because advising was seen as a poor career move [S10].

**What the provision is.** From 1 January 1964 every US field advisory post below corps is filled by a selected adviser on an 18-month tour, trained to the 2026 SFAB standard [U]:

- **Posts.** Battalion, regiment and division advisers to the ARVN; sector (province) and subsector (district) advisers; Special Forces with the CIDG remain outside the provision [U]; and the mobile advisory teams from whatever date they are created [U].
- **Selection.** Volunteers, chosen by a board and a three-day assessment of temperament, judgement and aptitude for language [E: the SFAB's assessment and selection practice]. Officers must have succeeded in the equivalent American post: a battalion adviser has commanded a company, a regimental adviser a battalion [S10].
- **Training.** About 32–36 weeks: a MATA course of six weeks [S10], Vietnamese language of about 24 weeks to working level [E: more than the SFAB's 16 weeks per S10 because Vietnamese is harder than most SFAB languages], and two to four weeks of weapons, medicine and fire-support training, including the qualification to call and control American air and artillery [E: the SFAB's joint fires training].
- **Tour.** 18 months, with one home leave at nine months [U] [E: the leave the period's extended tours carried].
- **Career.** Advisory service counts as command for promotion, and promotion boards are instructed to treat it so [U]. That is the incentive the 2026 SFAB lacked [S10].

**Quantity.** About 3,500 field advisory posts in 1964, rising to about 8,000–9,000 in 1968–69 including the mobile teams [E: MACV field advisory strength of the period]. On 18-month tours about 2,300 new advisers a year are needed in 1964 and about 5,500–6,000 a year at the peak [E: posts divided by 1.5, plus about 10% losses].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Reading for an institutional provision: D2 is the advisory complement and the spaces it consumes.**

**Team sizes** are the historical ones [E: the period's adviser team strengths]. A team with an ARVN battalion has about 3–5 officers and NCOs, with a regiment about 10–15, and with a division about 40–80 [E: the period's adviser team strengths]. A sector team has about 8–20 and a subsector team 3–6 [E: the period's sector and subsector teams]. A mobile team has five [S9].

**Spaces in theater** are unchanged, because the posts exist historically [E: the provision changes who fills them]. **Outside the Republic** the pipeline is the cost: with about 32–36 weeks of training, about 1,500–3,000 officers and NCOs are in training at any time [E: the annual intake times the training period]. CONFIG sets no ceiling.

**The pool.** The cost that matters is not spaces but people. At the peak the corps needs about 5,500–6,000 officers and senior NCOs a year of whom about 2,000–2,500 are captains and majors who have commanded [E: the officer share of the teams]. The Army's whole intake of company commanders in Vietnam ran to a few thousand a year [E: about 300 combat battalions Army-wide, four or five companies each, a commander a year]. The advisers will be taken from the same pool, and the American battalions will lose some of their best company commanders [E: the SFAB's documented effect on BCT leadership per S10].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

**Reading for an institutional provision: D3 is instruments and authorities.**

The adviser's instruments are four:

1. **Knowledge**, of his counterpart, the unit, the province and the language [E: the provision's design].
2. **Access** to American fire support, medical evacuation, helicopters and supplies, which he alone can call [E: the period's adviser's standing].
3. **Reporting**, up the American chain, which can reach the Aid Compliance Board of memorandum 001 [M001].
4. **Example**, in the field beside the counterpart.

What he cannot compel: any Vietnamese decision. He has no command authority [E: TNA G-6]. The 18-month tour and the language lengthen and deepen his influence; they do not add authority [E: POSTNA-6].

**Weapons.** The adviser carries what the advised unit carries, and his radio, the AN/PRC-10 in 1964 and the AN/PRC-25 from 1965, is the most important thing he carries [E: the period's adviser's kit]. His counterpart's battalion carries the M1 rifle, the M1918A2 automatic rifle and the M60 machine gun or, in this war, the M4A1 and M249 of memorandum 004 [M004]. The adviser must know them, and also the enemy's AK-47, SKS and RPD, which is what the SFAB's foreign-weapons training is for [S10].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**Reading for an institutional provision: D4 is information inputs.**

The adviser learns from the counterpart, from the unit's soldiers and from the province. The language decides how much: an adviser who works through an interpreter hears what the interpreter decides to tell him [E: the period's adviser memoirs]. With about 24 weeks of language he can follow a staff meeting and talk to a sergeant [E: working proficiency after six months of intensive study]. He does not become fluent [E: DLI category of Vietnamese].

**At night and in the field** the adviser who lives with the unit sees whether the outposts are manned, whether the Popular Forces sleep in the hamlet, and whether the battalion's casualty and strength reports are true [E: the subsector adviser's role]. That is the information the war most lacked in Washington [E: G-6 and US.credibility_gap].

**The 18-month tour** gives him about six months of learning and twelve of knowing [E: the period's estimate that an adviser took about six months to be effective].

### D5 · Performance

**Reading for an institutional provision: D5 is throughput and capacity.**

**Pipeline.** The MATA school must train about 2,300 a year in 1964 and about 5,500–6,000 at the peak [E: D1]. With six-week courses of about 300–400 students, that is about 8–17 courses a year [E: class sizes of the SFAB's MATA per S11]. The language school must teach Vietnamese to about the same numbers for about 24 weeks, about 1,200–3,000 students at any one time [E: intake times course length]; the historical Defense Language Institute never taught Vietnamese at that scale until 1966–67 [E: the period's DLI expansion]. **The language school is the rate limit** in 1964–65 [E: instructor supply].

**Selection.** If about 40–60% of volunteers pass the assessment [E: SFAB and Special Forces assessment pass rates], the corps needs about 4,000–5,000 volunteers in 1964 and about 10,000–12,000 a year at the peak [D]. The career incentive must be strong enough to produce them [E: the SFAB's experience per S10].

**Effect on the advised unit.** One adviser in a battalion does not change its effectiveness by a measured multiple. The period's evaluations found that good advisers mattered most in small units and in the territorial forces, where the counterpart was willing [E: the MAT experience per S9].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**Reading for an institutional provision: D6 is the reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.**

The adviser reports to his senior adviser and up to MACV, and in the provinces through the province senior adviser [E: the period's chain]. The provision changes the reports in one way: an adviser whose career is protected, who has been in post 18 months and who speaks the language can report what he sees [U]. Whether his reports change MACV's picture depends on MACV [E: US.credibility_gap].

**Talking to the ARVN** is the provision's core. The language training removes the interpreter for working conversation [E: D4]. The 18-month tour outlasts the ARVN's battalion commanders, many of whom served less [E: the period's command turnover].

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Reading for an institutional provision: D7 is reach and security preconditions.**

The corps works wherever the advised units are: every ARVN battalion, every province and district, and with the territorial forces through the mobile teams [E: the posts in D1]. It needs no security of its own beyond what the advised unit provides; the adviser shares the unit's risk [E: the period's adviser casualties].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Cost.** The selected adviser costs more to make than the historical one: about 32–36 weeks of training against about 2–8 weeks [E: D1 and S9]. At about $10,000–15,000 a trainee in 1964 prices [E: pay and school costs for the training period], the programme costs about $25–35 million a year in 1964 and about $60–90 million at the peak [D].

**What the RVNAF cannot supply:** nothing; the corps is American by definition.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The corps is not transferable; it is the American side of the relationship. Under Vietnamization it is withdrawn, and in this war that withdrawal is the moment its effect is tested [E: the drawdown of 1969–72]. What it can leave behind is the ARVN officers it helped make, and an ARVN training system that teaches what it learned [E: the adviser's purpose].

### D10 · Employment envelope

**It performs to specification** where the counterpart is willing to listen, the adviser has the language and the time, and the American lever is behind him [E: POSTNA-6].

**Performance collapses** in four conditions:

- **An unwilling counterpart** whom the adviser cannot have removed [E: G-6].
- **A coup** that replaces the counterpart [E: GVN.political_stability].
- **The adviser as fire-support officer.** When the adviser's value is the air and artillery he can call, the advised unit fights with American firepower and not its own, and its dependence deepens [E: POS005-2].
- **The career incentive failing.** If the Army's promotion boards do not honour the instruction, volunteers fall and selection becomes assignment again [S10].

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **The pool runs dry.** The corps takes good captains from American units at the peak [E: D2].
2. **Volunteers fall.** The SFAB's attrition of up to 70% shows how fast an advisory organization loses men if the career incentive fails [S10].
3. **Advice becomes command.** The 2026 adviser is trained to accompany and enable; in a war where Americans fight beside the ARVN, the adviser becomes the man who calls the fire and the ARVN commander stops leading [E: the period's critique of advisers].
4. **Reports ignored.** Honest reporting changes nothing if MACV and Washington do not want it [E: US.credibility_gap].

**Partial failure** is a corps that is selected and trained but on 12-month tours, or on 18-month tours without language. **During an outage** the historical advisory system is what remains.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one (1964):** the first selected advisers arrive in the second half of 1964, because the training takes about eight months [E: D1]. In the first months the posts are filled by the historical advisers [E: the pipeline lag].

**Month twelve:** about a third of advisers in post are selected and trained [E: the first year's intake against about 3,500 posts].

**What declines first** is the quality of selection at the peak, when the corps needs about 10,000–12,000 volunteers a year [E: D5]. Second is the language standard, because the school cannot keep up with the 1966–68 expansion [E: D5].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the historical adviser:** about 32–36 weeks of training against about 2–8 [E: D1, S9]; 18 months in post against 12 [S4]; selection by board against assignment [U]; working Vietnamese against an interpreter [E: D4]. An adviser on an 18-month tour spends about twelve months effective against about six [E: D4].

**Against the 2026 SFAB:** the same course and more language, plus a career incentive the SFAB lacked [S10].

**Against the historical MATs:** the mobile teams showed what selected, trained advisers could do with the territorial forces [S9]. The provision gives the MATs' standard to every field adviser from 1964.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P006c -->
- **It does not add authority.** The adviser still has no command over his counterpart; longer tours and language deepen influence, not power.
- **It does not create Vietnamese leaders.** Advice changes a commander who will listen, not one who will not.
- **It does not make advisers fluent.** About 24 weeks of Vietnamese gives working conversation, not native ease [E: DLI course lengths].
- **It does not come free.** The good captains and NCOs it takes are taken from American units.
- **It does not arrive at once.** The first selected advisers reach the field in the second half of 1964; about a third are in post by the end of the first year.
- **It does not end dependence on American fire.** An adviser who can call air and artillery deepens the advised unit's reliance on it unless he is told not to.
- **It does not survive the withdrawal.** It is American and leaves with the Americans.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P006c -->
It denies the enemy the adviser who cannot speak to his counterpart and leaves just as he learns, and the American report that says what the career needs rather than what the adviser saw. It denies him nothing at the regiment level and nothing once the Americans are gone.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P006c -->
A selected advisory corps on 18-month tours, trained in language and advising to the 2026 standard, is the most direct answer this war can give to the advisory problem the record names: advisers without authority, of falling quality, who leave as they learn. It buys an adviser who is effective for about twelve months instead of six, speaks to his counterpart without an interpreter, and can report honestly. It does not buy authority, it does not make a bad commander good, and it takes good officers from American units. Its effect is a band in advisory leverage, ARVN effectiveness and the territorial forces, and a band against the credibility gap; it is lost when the Americans leave.
<!-- /card -->
