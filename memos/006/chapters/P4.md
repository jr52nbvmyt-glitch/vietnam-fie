# Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971 [H40]. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 [D] to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971 [H40]. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest [H62]. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The revelation of My Lai, the Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the trial of Calley and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win [H]. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington [X-001]. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001]. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle (EV001-8) [X-001].

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses (EV002-6). The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective (EV000-66); Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season (EV000-68); and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically (EV000-71).

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open (EV000-58). Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy (EV000-68). In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home (EV000-71).

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless (EV000-60). The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election (EV002-6). And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute (EV000-72). The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

## EV000-56 · Nixon takes office; Vietnamization · 20 Jan – Mar 1969 · Washington and Saigon · HIST · 000
Richard Nixon was inaugurated on 20 January 1969 with a promise to end the war with honour and no published plan to do it [H]. His national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, commissioned a survey of the agencies' views of the war, which showed deep disagreement about almost everything except that the South could not yet stand alone [H]. At a National Security Council meeting on 28 January the Secretary of Defense, Melvin Laird, proposed calling the policy "Vietnamizing" the war; its two parts were to strengthen the RVNAF to take over the fighting and to extend pacification [H58]. Henry Cabot Lodge replaced Harriman in Paris [H51]. Laird, who visited Saigon in March, pressed for withdrawals sooner and larger than the military wanted, and made "Vietnamization" the measure of the South's progress [H] [H58]. The Improvement and Modernization programme behind it re-equipped the RVNAF from top to bottom: M16 rifles for the regulars and then the territorial forces, M60 machine guns, artillery, helicopters, aircraft, and the training base to use them [H].

## EV000-57 · The post-Tet offensive · 23 Feb – Mar 1969 · Saigon, Da Nang and installations throughout the South · HIST · 000
On the night of 22–23 February 1969 the PAVN and VC opened a countrywide offensive of attacks by fire and sapper raids against more than a hundred targets, including Saigon, Da Nang, Hue and US bases, the heaviest since 1968 [H]. It was smaller in scale and ambition than Tet: the ground attacks were mainly against bases and firebases, not into the cities, and the population did not rise [H]. It cost the Americans heavily in the first weeks, and it was taken in Washington as a breach of the understanding that had accompanied the bombing halt [H]. Nixon's answer, in the third week of March, was the secret bombing of the Cambodian base areas [H57].

## EV000-58 · Operation Menu · 18 Mar 1969 – 26 May 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: allied*

On the night of 18 March 1969, 60 B-52s took off from Guam on a mission ostensibly against targets in South Vietnam; 48 were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, where COSVN was believed to be, and dropped about 2,400 tons that night [H57]. In this war the 48 were Js, and their unguided carpet was half as dense; about 1,600 tons fell in the boxes, and twelve of the aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s aimed at the coordinates the U-2S had measured over five years in the Fishhook: storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop with a generator, and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload by night (EV003-4) [X-005]. The campaign that followed, codenamed Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner, Supper and Dessert after the base areas it struck, flew about 3,800 B-52 sorties and dropped 108,823 tons until 26 May 1970 [H57]. Here it dropped about 75,000 tons, a fifth of the sorties carrying JDAM against measured structures [X-005]. It was concealed by dual reporting: an overt channel ordered strikes near the border inside South Vietnam, while a back channel ordered the strikes in Cambodia, whose records were destroyed after each mission; neither the Secretary of the Air Force nor its chief of staff was told [H57].

The measured structures died; the base areas did not. In the first six weeks of Breakfast and Lunch about 300 structures that the U-2S had photographed were destroyed, most of them sheds and workshops in clearings [X-005]. By June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, and the clearings were empty when the U-2S next imaged them; the J's precision had a season's targets and then none (F005-16) [X-005]. The rear services cadres, by the account of a COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970, blamed spies in the base areas and searched for beacons in the forest; they found neither, and did the right thing anyway, which was to own nothing that stood in the open [X-005]. COSVN itself, a mobile headquarters of a few hundred people under the canopy, was never located and never struck, as it was not historically (POS005-1) [H] [X-005].

Sihanouk, whose territory it was and whose government had tolerated the base areas and the supply line from Sihanoukville, did not protest publicly [H]. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969; Nixon ordered the leak traced, and the telephones of 13 officials and four journalists were tapped, the first of the abuses that led to Watergate [H57]. The bombing forced the PAVN and VC to disperse and camouflage their Cambodian bases, here a few months earlier and more completely, but did not stop their offensives, and its effect on the war in the South is disputed [H57] [X-005].

## EV000-59 · Hamburger Hill · 13–20 May 1969 · Ap Bia Mountain (Hill 937), A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · HIST · 000
In May 1969 the 3rd Brigade of the 101st Airborne Division (Airmobile), under the division commander Major General Melvin Zais, went into the A Shau valley in Operation Apache Snow to destroy the PAVN base areas there [H54]. On Ap Bia Mountain, Hill 937, about 1.9 km from the Laotian border, the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry under Lieutenant Colonel Weldon Honeycutt met the 7th and 8th Battalions of the PAVN 29th Regiment, dug in [H54]. Ten days of frontal assaults followed, with the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry, the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry and the ARVN 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment joining for the final attack on 20 May [H54]. The Americans lost 56 killed, 367 wounded and 7 missing; they counted 630 PAVN dead on and around the hill, many in tunnels, and captured 89 individual and 22 crew-served weapons and 3 prisoners [H54]. On 5 June the hill was abandoned [H54].

"That hill had no military value whatsoever," Zais said [H54]. Senators Edward Kennedy, George McGovern and Stephen Young attacked the battle as senseless; on 27 June *Life* published the photographs of 242 Americans killed in one week [H54]. Abrams turned from "maximum pressure" to a posture designed to keep American casualties down, and Nixon announced the first troop withdrawal within weeks [H54].

## EV000-60 · Midway, the first withdrawals and the Nixon Doctrine · 8 Jun – Dec 1969 · Midway Island; Guam; South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: allied*
On 8 June 1969 Nixon met Thieu on Midway Island and announced the first withdrawal of US troops, 25,000 men, to be completed by the end of August [H]. Elements of the 9th Infantry Division left the Mekong Delta in July, and the whole 3rd Marine Division had left I Corps by the end of the year [H] [H58]. On 25 July, at Guam, Nixon set out what became the Nixon Doctrine: the United States would honour its treaty commitments and provide a nuclear shield, but would look to its Asian allies to provide the manpower for their own defence [H]. Further withdrawals were announced in September and December [H]. For the troops still in Vietnam, the withdrawals made every American death harder to justify. Discipline in the US Army declined through 1969–71; drug use, racial violence and "fragging", attacks on officers and NCOs with grenades, became endemic in rear areas and in some combat units [H]. The withdrawals were faster than the RVNAF's improvement, and they ran on a timetable set by American politics, not by the war.

In this war the withdrawals are of units, and they are cleaner for it. Under the individual system the first increment, Keystone Eagle, took the 9th Division's units home with whichever men had the least time left, and filled the units that stayed with the rest, so that the withdrawal broke up every company it touched [H]. Here the 9th Division's battalions and the 3rd Marine Division's had each arrived as units and left as units, whole, on the dates their tours ended; no company that stayed received the short-timers of one that went, and the commanders of the battalions that stayed kept men they had trained [X-006]. The cost was arithmetic: an increment had to be made of units whose tours ended in the window, so the withdrawal schedule set by Washington had to be met by shortening some tours and lengthening none, and about 12,000 men in 1969–70 went home one to four months early with their battalions [X-006]. The breakdown of discipline was not prevented. Fragging, drugs and racial violence rose in 1970–71 in battalions that had trained together, as the best study of the war had led this headquarters to expect; they rose somewhat less inside the rifle companies and as much in the rear (F006-16) [X-006]. Unit rotation preserved the unit and could not preserve the war's purpose in the minds of men who knew it was ending.

## EV000-61 · COSVN Resolution 9 and the death of Ho Chi Minh · Jul – 2 Sep 1969 · COSVN headquarters and Hanoi · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002
In July 1969 COSVN issued Resolution 9, which accepted that the 1968 offensives had not produced the uprising and directed a return to protracted war: small-unit and sapper attacks, economy of force, the rebuilding of the infrastructure, and a campaign against Vietnamization and pacification, while main forces were preserved in the sanctuaries [H]. It was a decision taken in the light of losses and on the movement's own reading of its position, and it set the pattern of the enemy's war in the South until 1971. On 2 September 1969 Ho Chi Minh died in Hanoi [H]. His testament called for the war to continue until the Americans had left and the country was reunified [H]. Power passed to a collective leadership in which Le Duan, as First Secretary, was the strongest figure, with Le Duc Tho, Truong Chinh and Pham Van Dong [H]. Ho's death did not change the policy; it removed the one figure whose authority stood above the factions, and left the militants who had planned Tet in charge.

In this war Resolution 9 is issued on the same date with the same direction [X-001]. Its section on the countryside is different in one respect. On the historical course the Front could still campaign in 1969 against a government that had not given the peasants their land. Here the government had been titling since 1964, and the resolution's instruction to the Delta's party committees was to stop contesting the titles, to treat a titled hamlet the government held at night as lost for the present, and to concentrate cadres and the reduced tax effort on the hamlets the government held only by day (F001-10, F001-13) [X-001]. That was a realistic instruction and a costly admission: it surrendered the issue on which the Front had been built in the Delta, and it left the movement there with nothing to offer the peasant but reunification and the end of the American war [X-001].

The resolution's instruction on the infrastructure was the Front's admission of what the police had done to it. It told the party committees of the Delta and the provinces around Saigon to stop keeping cadres resident in villages with settled police posts, to govern them by visiting cadres who slept elsewhere, to concentrate the reduced effort on the hamlets the government held only by day, and to put the movement's best security cadres into penetration of the Special Branch and of the provincial administration rather than into the villages (F002-2, F002-13) [X-002]. A movement that had lived among its people since 1960 now visited them, and visiting cadres taxed less, recruited less and governed less [X-002].

## EV000-62 · The secret Paris channel opens · 4 Aug 1969 – Feb 1970 · Paris · HIST · 000
In June 1969 the National Liberation Front formed the Provisional Revolutionary Government of the Republic of South Vietnam, to claim governmental status at the talks [H51] [H]. On 4 August Kissinger began secret talks in Paris with Hanoi's delegation, first with Xuan Thuy, and from February 1970 with Le Duc Tho, a member of the Politburo [H51] [H]. The formal sessions at the Hotel Majestic became a forum for public statements; the substance moved to the secret channel. The core issues were fixed from the start and remained so for three years: Hanoi demanded the withdrawal of all US forces and the removal of Thieu as the price of a settlement, and would not discuss the withdrawal of its own forces from the South; Washington would not overthrow its ally [H51].

## EV000-63 · My Lai revealed; the Moratorium · 15 Oct – 24 Nov 1969 · Washington and the United States · HIST · 000
On 15 October 1969 the Moratorium brought hundreds of thousands of Americans out against the war in cities across the country, and on 15 November the Mobilization brought a march of several hundred thousand to Washington [H]. On 3 November Nixon appealed on television to "the great silent majority" to support his policy of withdrawal on his terms, and the speech was well received [H]. On 13 November Seymour Hersh published the story of the My Lai massacre, and on 20 November the photographs taken by the Army photographer Ronald Haeberle appeared [H47]. On 24 November the Army appointed Lieutenant General William Peers to investigate the cover-up; his report of 17 March 1970 found that at least 175–200 Vietnamese had been killed and condemned the concealment [H47]. My Lai became, in American opinion, the symbol of the war in the countryside, and in South Vietnam a weapon for the Front's propaganda in a province that had suffered it.

## EV002-6 · The Board and the Directorate in the palace · Jan 1970 – Sep 1971 · Saigon and the provinces · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
The Provincial Administration Selection Board had outlived four governments because each had found it cheaper to keep than to break, and because a province chief appointed outside the list could not sign a budget, a title or a counterpart-fund disbursement while the Aid Compliance Board stood behind the rule [M002]. By 1970 the American lever was lapsing with the withdrawal (EV001-8), and Thieu, preparing for the election of 1971, wanted the provinces [X-002]. He did not abolish the Board. As the terms of its two general officers expired in January 1970 he replaced them with officers of his own staff, and from that spring his Prime Minister, General Tran Thien Khiem, appointed the third name on the list for about half the province vacancies and for most of those in the provinces with the largest electorates [X-002]. The list still excluded the incompetent and the bought; it no longer excluded the loyal mediocre [X-002].

The Inspectorate's reports, which had gone to the Board and the Commission since 1964, were routed through the presidential Inspectorate General from July 1971 under the decree that subordinated the Commission (EV001-8) [X-002]. The National Police had followed the same road earlier: its Director General had been the President's appointee in fact since 1966 (EV000-26), and in 1970–71 the Special Branch's Saigon headquarters spent a growing share of its effort on the opposition, the An Quang Buddhists and the Ky camp, as historical police chiefs always had (F002-16) [X-002]. The rural posts, the census and the district sections went on much as before, because they were too many and too local for the palace to want them [X-002]. In September 1971 province chiefs campaigned for Thieu as they did on the historical course; the difference was that about two-thirds of them were men who had passed an examination and served a district first, and the villages they governed were governed better than they were represented [X-002].

The palace took Phung Hoang's national file the same way, without abolishing anything. In February 1970 Khiem, who was Minister of the Interior as well as Prime Minister, moved the national centre from the Directorate of National Police to the Central Intelligence Organization's building and replaced Hieu, who resigned rather than serve under the new chairman, with a Military Security colonel of the President's staff [X-006]. The file held about 210,000 names by then, every one entered to the three-source standard and many with fingerprints and photographs, and it now served a second purpose: from the spring of 1970 the national centre vetted every candidate for province and district office and every officer above major against it, and its analysts were asked to chart the An Quang Buddhists' committees and the Ky camp's officers by the same method they had used on the Front [X-006]. For the election of 1971 the provincial copies of the file were called in to Saigon "for consolidation", and about two-thirds of the PIOCCs were left with district extracts; from September 1971 the complete national file existed in one building (F006-25) [X-006]. Ashby's successor protested in writing in October 1970 and was told, correctly, that the decree of 1964 placed the committee under the Minister of the Interior [X-006]. The district centres went on working the Front as before, and their audited count did not fall; what had changed was who else the file was for.

## EV000-64 · Sihanouk falls; the PAVN turns on Cambodia · 18 Mar – Apr 1970 · Phnom Penh and eastern Cambodia · HIST · 000
On 18 March 1970, while Sihanouk was abroad, the Cambodian National Assembly deposed him, and General Lon Nol became the head of a government hostile to the Vietnamese communists [H55]. Lon Nol closed the port of Sihanoukville to the supplies that had reached the PAVN and VC base areas through it [H55]. From 29 March the PAVN attacked the Cambodian army and within weeks overran much of eastern and northeastern Cambodia, protecting its bases and opening the country to the Khmer Rouge, whom it armed and trained [H55]. Cambodia, a sanctuary since 1965, became a battlefield. The loss of Sihanoukville threw the whole burden of supplying the southern half of South Vietnam onto the Trail, which in turn was extended through Laos and northeastern Cambodia.

## EV000-65 · Land to the Tiller · 26 Mar 1970 · Republic of Vietnam · CANCELLED · 000,001
The Land to the Tiller law of 26 March 1970 is not enacted in this war, because there is nothing left for it to do (F001-7) [X-001]. On the historical course it limited holdings of rice land to 15 hectares, gave tenanted land to the tenants who tilled it, confirmed title for peasants who had received land from the Viet Cong, and cost 441 million dollars, 339 million of them American [H63]; historians debate whether it came "too little, too late" [H63]. Here the decree of 1 January 1964 had done all of that six years earlier, with a lower ceiling, and by March 1970 about 1,050,000 hectares had been titled to about 800,000 families [M001] [X-001]. What happens instead on 26 March 1970 is a smaller act: Thieu signs a decree converting the remaining provisional titles to final ones and extending the programme to the communal rice lands of the villages, the last large category of tenanted land, about 200,000 hectares let each year by village councils [X-001]. It draws no headlines in Washington, because the programme it completes had been reporting monthly for six years. Its effects had accrued over those years, not over the last five of the war, in the attitude of the rural population of the Delta towards a government that had given them something before it asked them for anything (F001-17).

## EV000-66 · The Cambodian incursion · 29 Apr – 30 Jun 1970 · The Parrot's Beak and the Fishhook, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 29 April 1970 ARVN forces of III and IV Corps under Lieutenant General Do Cao Tri attacked into the Parrot's Beak west of Saigon, and on 1 May the US 1st Cavalry Division and 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment, with ARVN airborne units, attacked into the Fishhook north of it; the 25th and 4th Infantry Divisions followed [H55]. In this war the attack went in on a map. Five years of the Sihanoukville count and the U-2S's radar change detection had located the depots where the convoys stopped, the road net that joined them and the clearings they hid in, and the 1st Cavalry's brigades were landed on the largest of them on the first two days rather than finding them by chance [X-003]. The great complex that the 1st Cavalry found on 4 May historically, and that the troops called "the City", was its objective on 1 May here [H] [X-003].

Nixon announced the incursion on television on 30 April as an attack on COSVN [H] [H55]. MACV had told CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs on 24 April that the U-2S had heard COSVN's transmitters move west and north out of the Fishhook from about 19 April, and advised against naming it as an objective [X-003]. The speech was written in Washington, and it named it [X-003]. COSVN had moved before the attack [H55]. The PAVN and VC mostly avoided battle, leaving rearguards, and withdrew westward [H55].

In the base areas the allies found the logistics of the war in the South: about 22,900 individual and 2,500 crew-served weapons, 7,000–8,000 tons of rice, about 1,800 tons of ammunition, 431 vehicles and 55 tons of medical supplies [H55]. Here most of it was found in the first ten days rather than through May and June, and the total was larger by about a tenth, because the depots on the map were searched first and the time left over went to the ones that were not [X-003]. The allies claimed 11,369 PAVN and VC killed and 2,509 captured [H55]; I make no adjustment to those claims, which carry the usual inflation (A13). US forces were out by 30 June; the ARVN stayed on [H55].

On 4 May Ohio National Guardsmen shot dead four students at Kent State University, and protests closed hundreds of campuses [H55] [H]. On 30 June the Senate passed the Cooper–Church amendment barring US ground forces from Cambodia [H55]. The incursion bought time: the loss of the caches and of Sihanoukville delayed the PAVN's recovery in III and IV Corps, and the southern half of the country was quieter for more than a year. It did not destroy the sanctuary. The PAVN rebuilt its supply routes, seized Attapeu and Saravane in Laos, opened a "Liberation Route" through northeastern Cambodia to the Mekong, and in 1972 would attack An Loc from Cambodia [H55]. The radar watched the Liberation Route being built from the summer of 1970, as it had watched Sihanoukville; it could not stop it either.

## EV000-67 · Fire Support Base Ripcord · 1–23 Jul 1970 · Fire Support Base Ripcord, near the A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · HIST · 000
In the spring of 1970 the 101st Airborne Division reopened Fire Support Base Ripcord on the eastern edge of the A Shau valley for an offensive against the PAVN base areas [H61]. From 1 July the PAVN 324B Division, with its 803rd Regiment at Ripcord, its 29th Regiment on the nearby hills and a battalion of the 6th Regiment of the Tri-Thien command in support, besieged the base, held by the 2nd Battalion, 506th Infantry of the 3rd Brigade under Colonel Ben Harrison [H61]. On 18 July a CH-47 hit by small-arms fire crashed into the ammunition storage area, killing four and destroying six M102 howitzers and 2,238 rounds of 105mm ammunition [H61]. The command judged the base indefensible and evacuated it on 23 July under fire; the battalion commander, Lieutenant Colonel Andre Lucas, was among the last killed and received the Medal of Honor posthumously [H61]. The Americans lost 75 killed and claimed 422 PAVN dead [H61]. It was the last major battle between US ground forces and the PAVN [H61]. Whether it crippled the PAVN in the region, as Harrison believed, or achieved the PAVN's purpose by forcing the Americans out, is disputed [H61].

## EV000-68 · Commando Hunt and the growth of the Trail · 10 Oct 1970 – Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle and northeastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: both*

The dry-season phases of Commando Hunt were the main US effort against the North's logistics after 1968 [H53]. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks destroyed, a figure Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; in Phase VII the AC-130E gunships were credited with 7,335 of 10,689 trucks claimed [H53]. The CIA estimated that the entire North Vietnamese truck inventory was about 6,000, and a Defense Intelligence Agency model reached zero trucks remaining fourteen times in 1969 [H53]. Over the campaign the Air Force claimed 51,000 trucks and 3,400 anti-aircraft guns destroyed [H53].

In this war a second ledger stood beside the claims from 1969, and it was kept by the radar (EV000-55). Its Phase V figure was about 4,500–5,500 trucks destroyed or disabled, and its estimate of net loss to the throughput was about 8–10 percent, against the phases' own historical estimate falling to about 2 percent [H53] [X-003]. The DIA model's zero did not reach the Secretary of Defense's desk in 1969 because the radar showed, every night, the trucks that the model said did not exist [X-003]. The claims went on being made; the AC-130E crews were not lying, and a burning truck at night is easy to count twice. What changed was that no one in Washington or Saigon could believe them for long [X-003].

The J and its weapons were a smaller part of this war than its planners had expected, and the reason is in the panhandle's geography. By 1970 the cell's folders for the panhandle held about 4,000 crossings, of which on any night perhaps a third were in use; the J cut what it could measure, and the Quickstrike fields on the Xe Kong and the Se San below Attopeu kept the steel pontoons and motor barges off the rivers, so the fuel drums floated down them on bamboo rafts instead (F005-4, F005-5) [X-005]. The petroleum pipeline from Vinh toward the A Shau was the one target in the panhandle that could not move; each of its pumping stations was measured and cut with GBU-31s, about eighty times between October 1970 and the end of 1971, and each was bypassed within two to four days by a length of flexible pipe laid around the crater [X-005]. The radar's estimate is that the J's work raised the net loss to the throughput in Phase V by about one or two points, from about 8 to about 10 percent at the season's peak [X-005].

The Trail grew regardless. After the PAVN's offensives in Laos in 1970 its corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles; the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads and paths with bulldozers and graders, and floated fuel drums down the rivers [H53]. The U-2S's radar mapped each new road within days of its cutting, and the Global Hawk counted its traffic; the count rose through 1971 to about 150–200 southbound truck movements a night at the dry season's peak [X-003]. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving (A13): the trucks destroyed and the crossings cut rose, and what arrived in the South rose with them. In this war that sentence was written in 1969 by this headquarters, from its own counts, and not by historians. Over the war more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos [H53].

## EV000-69 · The Son Tay raid · 21 Nov 1970 · Son Tay prison camp, about 37 km west of Hanoi · HIST · 000
On the night of 20–21 November 1970 a joint task force under Brigadier General LeRoy J. Manor and Colonel Arthur D. Simons landed 56 Special Forces soldiers by HH-53 and HH-3E helicopters, guided by C-130 Combat Talons, at the prison camp at Son Tay to rescue about 61 American prisoners [H59]. Navy aircraft from USS *Oriskany* and USS *Ranger* flew a diversion over Haiphong, and F-105G Wild Weasels suppressed the SA-2 sites [H59]. The camp was empty: the prisoners had been moved in July, which intelligence learned only days before [H59]. The raiders lost two wounded, an HH-3E deliberately crash-landed inside the compound and an F-105G to a SAM; they reported 42 guards killed, a Vietnamese source six guards and seven civilians [H59]. The raid failed in its purpose but succeeded in another: Hanoi consolidated its prisoners in central prisons in Hanoi, and many returning prisoners said their treatment and morale improved [H59].

## EV000-70 · The Cooper–Church amendment · 29 Dec 1970 · Washington · HIST · 000
After the House had rejected the version passed by the Senate in June, the Cooper–Church amendment was enacted in December 1970 as part of the Supplementary Foreign Assistance Act and signed on 29 December [H55] [H56]. It barred US ground troops and advisers from operations in Laos and Cambodia [H55] [H56]. It wrote into law the sanctuary constraint that had been policy since 1965, at the moment the administration was planning to attack the Trail on the ground. Any operation into Laos would now be the ARVN's alone, without the American advisers who called in the air support and coordinated the helicopters on which ARVN operations depended.

## EV000-71 · Lam Son 719 · 8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam [H56]. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak [H56]. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons [H56]; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units [H56]. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery [H56]. MACV J-2's estimate on 4 February, built from the U-2S's signals and the panhandle watch, put the enemy's strength within reach of Tchepone at about 35,000–40,000 men and rising, about twice the historical estimate given to the planners; the operation went ahead on its date [H] [X-003].

In this war the operation carried a siege box with it. Sorrell's detachment at Quang Tri, a dozen Americans who could not cross the border, received the Global Hawk's imagery of every firebase north and south of Route 9 each night and passed measured positions to the Js on Guam and at U-Tapao; the ARVN battalions on the hills passed nothing, because they had no means to give a coordinate the bombers could use, and no American advisers were there to give it for them (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. The box worked where the enemy dug and failed where he closed. At Fire Support Base Lolo and at Sophia, south of the road, the PAVN 2nd Division's regiments dug in around the hills from late February, their trenches were imaged and struck each night at 400–700 metres, and the bases held until they were evacuated in the withdrawal [X-005]. North of the road, Ranger North and Ranger South fell on 20–21 February and Fire Support Base 31 on 25 February much as they fell historically, to infantry and tanks of the 308th and 320th Divisions that came straight at the wire in daylight and were inside 400 metres before they could be measured; on 25 February the radar tracked about twenty tanks forming up north of the hill at about 0900, strike aircraft destroyed six before they reached it, and the base fell that afternoon all the same (F005-6) [H] [X-003] [X-005].

On 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began [H56]. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world [H56] [H]. Along Route 9 the radar showed the PAVN columns closing on the retreating units, and the Js with GBU-38s were put on the anti-aircraft guns the PAVN had dug in around the landing zones, which the cell measured each night; about 110 gun positions were destroyed in the last two weeks, and I estimate that the US Army lost about a sixth fewer helicopters than the historical 108 destroyed [H56] [X-005].

The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April [H56]. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing [H56]. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded [H56]. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the landing zones and the command, not by what anyone could see or strike [X-005]. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded" [H56]. Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after [H56]. The ARVN's I Corps read the operation in a way history's did not: the bases it had held were the bases where the American bombs had come every two hours, and it began to build its plans for the defence of the North around them (F005-20) [X-005].

## EV000-72 · The war on trial at home · 29 Mar – 30 Jun 1971 · Fort Benning, Washington and New York · HIST-ALT · 000,002,006
*Initiative: neither*
On 29 March 1971 a court-martial at Fort Benning convicted Lieutenant Calley of the premeditated murder of at least 20 people at My Lai and sentenced him to life imprisonment; two days later Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest pending appeal [H47]. His sentence was later reduced, and he was paroled in 1974 [H47]. In April veterans opposed to the war demonstrated in Washington and threw their medals onto the Capitol steps [H]. On 13 June the *New York Times* began publishing the Pentagon Papers, the Defense Department's secret history of the war's decisions to 1968, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg; on 30 June the Supreme Court refused to stop publication [H]. The administration's response, the "plumbers" unit formed to stop leaks, led to Watergate [H]. By mid-1971 a majority of Americans told pollsters the war had been a mistake [H].

In this war the Phoenix programme comes before Congress on the historical date, in the hearings of the House Government Operations subcommittee in July 1971, at which, on the historical course, William Colby testified that more than 20,000 people had been killed under Phoenix since 1968 [H]. Here Colby gave the audited figures instead: since January 1965 about 58,000 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 36,000 tried or held under review, about 15,000 rallied and about 7,000 killed, about 2,600 of them in the programme's own operations and the rest in military operations against men whom the centres had named (F006-13) [X-006]. The hearings were damaging and less damaging than they were historically. The story had been told since Duc Thanh in 1967 (EV006-4); the Commission's quarterly samples since 1965 gave the administration an independent count of unsupported neutralizations, about a tenth in the held belt and a quarter in the contested belt across six years, and the magistrate review and capped detention gave it a legal frame to describe (F002-14) [X-006]. The witnesses who hurt the programme most were former Americans of the PRUs' contested-belt advisory teams of 1967–68, and the subcommittee's report singled out the eleven districts the Commission had already named [X-006]. The Con Son "tiger cages" of July 1970 had happened as they did historically, in a prison the statute did not reach [H] [X-002]. The Public Safety appropriation for 1972 passed with a cut of about a tenth rather than the larger cuts its critics proposed, and the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers were kept [X-002].

## EV001-8 · The Commission placed under the Inspectorate · 14 May – 9 Jul 1971 · Saigon · NEW · 001
On 14 May 1971 the Commission Against Corruption opened an inquiry into the sale of import licences for fertilizer and motor vehicles under the Commercial Import Program, which its auditors had traced through three Saigon firms to a relative of a member of the President's staff [X-001]. It was the first inquiry in seven years to reach the presidential palace, and it reached it in the year Thieu stood for re-election [X-001]. The American lever that had protected the Commission since 1964, the Aid Compliance Board's power to suspend counterpart funds to a named command, importer or province, had not been used since 1969: the Mission was managing a withdrawal, and Washington would not risk a quarrel with the government it was leaving over a licence case (EC001-8, F001-15) [X-001]. Commissioner Khoa's second term, from January 1970, had been confirmed with the American co-chair's concurrence; his removal still required it [M001] [X-001].

Thieu did not remove him. On 9 July 1971 he issued a decree placing the Commission, the Special Court's prosecutors and the Pay and Muster Verification Service under a new Inspectorate General of the Presidency, with authority to approve or suspend any inquiry touching an official of the rank of minister, general officer or province chief and above [X-001]. The licence inquiry was suspended the same day. The Deputy Chief of Mission protested in writing; the Embassy did not invoke the Board [X-001]. Khoa stayed in office and the Commission's work below that rank went on: the musters, the pay at the parade, the land court cases and the Phoenix audits continued through 1971 at about their earlier rate [X-001]. What ended was the reach upward that had convicted a general in 1967 (EV001-7). An anti-corruption body that had survived seven years with a patron and an American lever lost the lever first, and the patron followed his own interest [X-001].

## EV000-73 · Thieu's unopposed re-election · Oct 1971 · Republic of Vietnam · HIST · 000,001
In the presidential election of October 1971 Thieu ran alone. Ky was at first disqualified under an election law requiring endorsements from deputies or provincial councillors, then reinstated by the Supreme Court, and withdrew; Duong Van Minh withdrew, charging that the election was rigged [H60] [H]. Thieu received all the valid votes, 5,971,114, and the reported turnout was 87.97 percent of 7,192,660 registered voters; valid ballots were 94.37 percent of those cast [H60]. A government that had won with a third of the vote in a contested election in 1967 was now returned in a referendum on itself. The army and administration remained Thieu's, and the regime was more stable than at any time since 1963; but its claim to stand for the whole non-communist South had narrowed, at the moment American forces were leaving it to stand alone.

In this war the election runs as it did historically, with the same withdrawals, the same single candidate and the same returns [X-001]. Its context is different in one respect and the same in another. Thieu's government could claim in the countryside what it could not claim on the historical course, that it had given about 800,000 families their land seven years before the election, and its province chiefs campaigned on it [X-001]. But the subordination of the Commission in July (EV001-8) had removed the one body with the standing to hear the opposition's complaints about the endorsement law, and Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard in Saigon as a description of a government that had shut down its own inspector in the year it chose to run alone [X-001].

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving [H40]. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before [H62]. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed (EV000-65) [X-001]. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it (EV001-8) [X-001]. These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal [H56] [H]. The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent (EV002-6) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

The radar's ledger has made the interdiction debate honest and changed nothing it was about: the Trail is larger at the end of 1971 than ever, the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia is mapped and open, and the aircraft that count both will leave with the Americans (EV000-68) [X-003].

The ARVN's I Corps ends the phase believing, on the evidence of Lolo and Sophia, that a besieged base under American bombers holds (EV000-71). It is true, and it is the belief that will cost it most when the bombers go [X-005].

Phung Hoang ends 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon, serving the President as well as the war (EV000-72, EV002-6) [X-006]. About 3,500 selected advisers remain, with the territorials and the province teams more than with the divisions; the American battalions have gone home whole and their discipline has decayed on its historical course (EV000-60) [X-006].
