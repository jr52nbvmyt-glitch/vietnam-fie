*COMUSMACV to CINCPAC · Saigon*

The provision names two things, and I have filed them as two: an accelerated and enhanced land reform, and an anti-corruption programme for the Government of Vietnam with a mechanism to enforce it. Both are institutional provisions, so the sixteen fields are read in the institutional sense the procedure gives them, and the reading is stated at the head of each field where it differs from the literal one. Neither message gives a date, a scale or a configuration. I have taken the campaign's opening date, 1 January 1964, as the arrival of each [U: arrival defaults to campaign_opens]. I have taken "granted" to mean that the law is promulgated, the money is appropriated and the organisations exist on that date [U]. I have not taken it to mean that the junta in Saigon will execute either programme faithfully, that the landlords and generals they injure will accept them, or that the Viet Cong will leave them alone; those are the analysis, not the premise. The 2026 configuration of a programme is the design a 2026 practitioner would bring, informed by sixty years of land reforms and anti-corruption agencies that succeeded and failed, together with the equipment of 2026 that works in 1964. Much 2026 equipment does not work in 1964, because the satellites, networks and databases it assumes do not yet exist, and I say so where it matters.

## §1.1 Land-to-the-Tiller Programme, 1964 (accelerated and enhanced) — P001a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read for an institutional provision: the law, the organisation that executes it, its target and its structure in the provinces.*

**Designation.** The Land-to-the-Tiller Programme of 1964: a decree-law of the Republic of Vietnam promulgated on the arrival date [U], with the Directorate General of Land Affairs expanded into an executing agency, a compensation fund financed by the United States, and a titling apparatus built on aerial photography and community demarcation. Its substantive terms are those of the Land to the Tiller law that President Thieu signed at Can Tho on 26 March 1970 [S2], brought forward six years and changed in six respects that the record of that law, and of land reforms since, shows to matter.

The terms it carries over unchanged from 1970 are these. No landlord retains any tenanted rice land: the retention limit for land worked by tenants is zero [S2]. An owner who cultivates his own land keeps up to 15 hectares of it [S2]. Ancestral-worship land is exempt up to 5 hectares a family if it was registered before promulgation [S2]. The present tiller has first claim, up to 3 hectares a family in the Delta and 1 hectare in the Central Lowlands [S2]. The land is transferred free; the new owner pays nothing for it and no tax on it in the first year [S2]. He may not sell it for 15 years [S2]. The landlord is compensated at 2.5 times the land's average annual paddy yield, 20% in cash and 80% in bonds maturing over eight years at 10% interest [S2] [S3]. Interference with the programme is punishable by fines of up to 200,000 piastres, and a false claim of self-cultivation is punished by expropriation without compensation [S2].

The six enhancements are the provision's own design [U: "acceleration and enhancement" read as the improvements the record shows the 1970 law needed]:

1. **Immediate provisional transfer.** On promulgation every present tiller of tenanted rice land becomes its provisional owner, and the collection of rent on such land becomes an offence, from the main-season harvest of 1964. Titles follow. Prosterman described this blanket preliminary distribution as the fallback available to Thieu in 1970 [S2]; here it is the opening move, because the thing a tenant notices first is the rent he no longer pays, not the paper he later receives.
2. **Explicit confirmation of every Viet Minh and Viet Cong distribution.** Land given out by the Viet Minh before 1954 or by the Viet Cong since 1960, and in the hands of the present tiller, is confirmed to him by name. The 1970 law did this [S4]; the provision makes it the first article of the decree and the first sentence of every broadcast about it, because it is the article that answers the enemy's argument.
3. **Inflation-protected bonds.** The landlord's bonds are indexed to the paddy price. The 1970 bill had this protection and lost it in the legislature [S2]. Japan's reform shows what happens without it: by 1950 riceland sold for about 20,000 yen a tan while landlords had been paid under 1,000 [S7]. That was survivable under occupation; in a Saigon whose generals come from landed families it is a coup grievance (D11).
4. **Grievance machinery from the first day.** Mobile land courts with summary jurisdiction over boundary disputes, eviction attempts, false worship-land claims and fees demanded by officials. The General Accounting Office judged the absence of adequate grievance and conflict-resolution machinery perhaps the most important remaining problem of the 1970 programme [S3].
5. **Communal land in the Centre.** Communal rice land in the Central Lowlands is distributed to the families working it within the 1-hectare limit. The 1970 programme made little progress there and did not attempt to distribute shared communal fields [S3].
6. **A fit-for-purpose titling apparatus.** Titles are produced from aerial photographs on which the community itself marks the boundaries, the method Rwanda used to demarcate 10.3 million parcels between February 2010 and June 2012 [S8], rather than from instrument surveys of every plot. Where the 1970 programme matched landlord declarations against maps, village registers and aerial photographs and printed titles by computer [S3], this one starts from the photograph.

**Manufacturer.** The Government of Vietnam's Ministry of Land Reform and its Directorate General of Land Affairs, with the United States Operations Mission (the aid mission) supplying advisers, finance and the photogrammetric and computing components, and a US Army engineer topographic detachment supplying ground control (D2).

**In-service date.** No programme of these terms existed in the Republic in 1964. The real-world reference is the 1970 Land to the Tiller law [S1], which by 26 March 1973 had issued titles for 1,007,217 hectares to about 650,000 farmers [S3], and by the end of 1973, 953,000 titles covering 1,198,000 hectares, nearly 40% of the South's cultivated land [S4]. The design references are Japan's reform of 1946–50 [S7] and Rwanda's regularisation of 2010–13 [S8] [S9]. Ordinance 57 of 1956, the reform this one replaces, remained in force but had been largely unused since 1960 [S1].

**Quantity.** One national programme. Its target is about 1.0–1.2 million hectares of tenanted land transferred to about 850,000–950,000 families [E: Prosterman's 1960–61 tenancy census and the GAO's 1970 target of 1 million ha to 800,000 tenants]. The arithmetic behind it: of the Delta's 1,175,000 farm families in the census of 1960–61, about 334,000 rented part of their land and about 521,000 rented all of it [S2], so about 855,000 Delta families farmed tenanted land [D]. In the Central Lowlands 403,000 of 695,000 families farmed a two-acre holding of which one acre was rented, and about 74,000 rented everything [S2]. The 1970 programme, reaching most but not all of this population over three to four years, titled 1,198,000 hectares [S4].

**Organisation in theater.**

| Element | Number | Where | Task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Directorate General of Land Affairs, expanded | One, about 400 staff [E: headquarters for a national programme of this size] | Saigon | Law, policy, the register, compensation, titles |
| Province land services | One in each province, about 40 staff each [E: a land officer, registrars, a photo-interpretation cell, clerks, a grievance officer] | The 43–45 provinces of 1964 [E: province boundaries changed during the year] | Run the programme in the province; hold the parcel map and the register |

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

*Read as staffing: Vietnamese and American.*

**Vietnamese.** About 9,000 full-time staff at full strength [E: the organisation in D1 plus locally hired para-surveyors]: about 400 in the Directorate [E: a national directorate's headquarters], about 1,760 in province land services [D], about 3,000 in mobile titling teams [D], about 120 in the mapping and computing cells [D], about 80 in the land courts [E: twelve courts with clerks and bailiffs], and about 3,500 para-surveyors hired in the villages being titled and paid by the day [E: Rwanda hired about 110,000 local people over its programme, 99% from the communities being demarcated, and trained para-surveyors through para-surveyors [S8]]. The village committees add about 12,500 part-time members [D]. The binding shortage is not numbers but literate, honest men who will go to the villages: the Republic has a few hundred French-trained cadastral surveyors at most [E: the size of the colonial cadastral service], and most of the programme's field staff must be trained from school-leavers in three to four weeks [E: the para-surveyor model]. Every one of them is of military age and needs a draft deferment, which is itself a thing that can be sold (P001b).

**American.** About 260 [E: the components below]. The aid mission provides about 90 province land advisers, two to a province [E: two advisers to each province], and about 40 in Saigon in finance, law, the register and the computing cell [E: central staff in the functions named]. A contractor provides about 70 photogrammetrists, aircrew, camera and computer technicians [E: crews and technicians for two aircraft and two cells on two shifts]. A US Army engineer topographic detachment of about 60 provides geodetic ground control [E: a survey and mapping detachment of the period]. For comparison, the aid mission's land-reform technical staff in July 1972, when the 1970 programme was two years old and the records existed, was 31 [S3]. The provision needs about eight times that [D], because it is faster, because the records do not exist, and because the Americans are in the provinces to watch for fees, collusion and reversal as much as to advise.

**Personnel spaces.** About 60 military spaces, the topographic detachment [D]. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so nothing is foreclosed by number. The roughly 200 civilians [D] do not count against any military ceiling, but they cost what every American space costs (G-16): visibility, protection, rotation, and the charge that the programme is American. The last of these is the most expensive, and it is why the American presence is in the aid mission's offices and the photo laboratory, not on the titling teams in the villages.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities: the law, the money, the powers, and what they can and cannot compel.*

**The law.** The decree-law in D1, with four powers that matter in practice. Expropriation of all tenanted rice land without the landlord's consent, compensated on a fixed formula, not a negotiated price [S2]. Provisional transfer on promulgation, which makes every tenant an owner in law before any official has visited him [U]. Criminal sanctions: fines of up to 200,000 piastres for interference and confiscation without compensation for false self-cultivation claims [S2], to which the provision adds imprisonment for collecting rent on transferred land or evicting a present tiller [U]. Summary jurisdiction for the mobile land courts, so that a dispute is heard in the village in weeks, not in a provincial court in years.

**The money.** Compensation at 2.5 times annual yield cost the 1970 programme an average of about $282 a hectare and an estimated $537 million through 1981 [S3]. The piaster cash is drawn from the counterpart funds generated by Commercial Import Program and PL-480 commodity sales [H]. For 1.0–1.2 million hectares at 1964 prices the bill is about $200–280 million [E: the 1970s average per hectare, reduced for lower paddy prices and piaster values in 1964], of which the 20% cash share, about $40–56 million [D], falls in the first two years and the bonds, about $20–28 million a year [D], fall over the next eight. The United States financed $339 million of the $441 million the 1970 programme cost [S1], about 77% [D], and finances the same share here [U]. Against the Commercial Import Program, which obligated $397 million in 1966 alone [S21], the compensation is a small sum: Prosterman called it a minutely fractional cost of the war [S2]. Administration adds about $20–45 million over three years [E: Rwanda's cost of under $6 a parcel [S9] multiplied three to five times for war, helicopters and the absence of records, over about 1.3–1.5 million parcels].

**What it can compel.** The landlord's legal claim to rent ends on promulgation, and the state no longer collects it for him. The provincial courts can no longer enforce a lease on tenanted rice land. The tiller's claim to the land becomes one the state is bound to defend.

**What it cannot compel.** It cannot stop a landlord's agent from collecting rent by private intimidation where the state's writ does not run at night, nor stop a district chief who is a landlord's cousin from looking away. It cannot make the Viet Cong stop taxing the peasant, or stop them conscripting his son. It cannot make a future junta keep it. Every one of these is a failure mode (D11), and each is addressed by something other than the law itself: security (POP.night_control), the anti-corruption programme (P001b), and American pressure (ADVISE.leverage).

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what the programme learns, how, and how reliably in contested hamlets and at night.*

**What it needs to know.** Who tills which parcel, who owns it, and where its boundaries run. Of these, the first is the most public fact in any hamlet: everyone knows who works which field. The second is often unknown to the state, because the French cadastre was incomplete and much of it is lost; in one region the GAO found 235 of 295 villages without land registers, and many claims rested on French records that could not be validated [S3]. The third is visible from the air.

**How it learns it.**

- *Aerial photography.* A frame camera of UltraCam Eagle Mark 3 class produces a panchromatic frame of 26,460 × 17,004 pixels at 4.0 µm [S10]. At 10 cm ground sample distance it flies at about 2,000 m with the 80 mm lens [S10]; at 25 cm, ample to show every paddy bund, it flies at about 5,000 m [D] (ground sample distance scales linearly with height), where it is above 12.7mm and small-arms fire. Each frame then covers about 6.6 km across track [D]. The 2026 camera is designed to work with a satellite navigation and inertial system for direct georeferencing [S10], and **in 1964 there are no navigation satellites to use**: the first GPS satellite is fourteen years away [H]. Every block of photography must therefore be tied to ground control points surveyed by optical total station from the French triangulation network [E: the standard pre-satellite method], which is why the provision carries an Army topographic detachment. The photography already in theater, the USAF RF-101C detachment at Tan Son Nhut and the Army's OV-1 Mohawks [H], is flown on film for targets and routes, not in cadastral blocks; its prints are a useful cross-check of old boundaries, not a map.
- *Community demarcation on the photograph.* The village committee and the tillers walk the fields with an enlarged print and mark the boundaries they agree; disputed boundaries are marked as disputed. Rwanda used this general-boundary method, with natural features rather than instrument survey, and a public objection period [S8].
- *Claims and declarations.* Tenants claim; landlords declare. In 1970 false landlord declarations were a problem [S3].
- *Public posting.* The list of parcels, tillers and owners is posted in the village. The 1970 programme posted for seven days [S3]; Rwanda allowed 60 [S8]. This programme posts for 30 [U: a compromise between speed and contest], and the posting is the check: a false claim must survive the scrutiny of every neighbour.

**Triple canopy.** Largely irrelevant. Rice land is open, and paddy bunds are among the clearest features on an aerial photograph. Hamlets sit under tree lines and orchards, and house plots and orchards are not part of the transfer. The canopy matters only in the highland and rubber areas the programme does not touch (D7).

**Night.** The programme does nothing at night and learns nothing at night. That is its central weakness, and it is the reverse of the enemy's: the Viet Cong's land committee works in the hamlet after dark; the government's works by day and leaves.

**Monsoon.** Photography is limited by cloud, not by rain on the ground. The southwest monsoon brings afternoon convection across the South from about May to October [E: the regional climate], so most photography is flown in the dry season from December to April, with morning flights in the wet season. Demarcation on the ground is easier in the dry season and harder but not impossible when the paddies are flooded; the mobile teams move by boat in the Delta.

**Reliability in contested hamlets.** The information is public and hard to falsify in the hamlet's presence, but it can be coerced. In a hamlet the Viet Cong control by night, a tenant may decline to claim for fear of reprisal, or may claim exactly as the local Party cell tells him. Tenant intimidation reduced claims in 1970 [S3]. The programme therefore gathers good information where the government holds the hamlet, and information shaped by the enemy where it does not.

**Which half of find-and-fix (A6).** Neither directly, but a by-product falls on the infrastructure side. The parcel register, the claims and the posted lists are the first household-by-household record the state has made of the hamlet, and a peasant who has received a title has a reason to tell the government who is threatening it. Neither of these identifies a cadre by itself.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity: hectares, parcels and families per month, and the rate limit.*

**Provisional transfer.** Instantaneous in law across the whole country on the arrival date [U]. Its effect on the ground is not instantaneous: it ends rent where the state's courts, district chiefs and Regional Forces stand behind it, and has no physical effect in hamlets the government does not enter.

**Photography.** At 25 cm and about 6.6 km across track, with 30% sidelap the effective swath is about 4.6 km [D]; at a ground speed of about 300 km/h one aircraft images about 1,400 km² an hour [D]. The South's 7.5 million cultivated acres [S1], about 3.0 million hectares or 30,000 km² [D], need about 22 hours of photographic flying [D]. Allowing for cloud, ferry, repeat lines and the dry-season window, two aircraft complete the rice lands in about three months [E: weather typically limits a photographic aircraft to a fraction of available days]. Imaging is not the bottleneck.

**Demarcation and titling.** A 25-man mobile team demarcates and posts a village of 400–600 tenanted parcels in two to three weeks [E: the Rwanda rate per para-surveyor scaled to a war, with escort and boat movement]. At about two villages a team-month, 120 teams process about 240 villages a month [D], and the 2,500 villages in roughly ten to eleven months [D] if every village were accessible, about 40,000–60,000 hectares a month [E: average tenanted land per village]. The 1970 programme averaged about 28,000 hectares a month over its first three years [D] (1,007,217 ha in 36 months [S3]). Rwanda demarcated 11.4 of an estimated 11.5 million parcels in under three years [S9]. The apparatus can therefore run at roughly one and a half to two times the 1970 rate [D].

**The rate limit is security, not cadastre.** In January 1964 the government holds by night perhaps a fifth of the Delta's hamlets [E: the record's assessment on the opening date, from the Strategic Hamlet reporting]. A titling team can work by day, under escort, in hamlets that are contested at night, but cannot work at all in hamlets the Viet Cong hold. In 1964, therefore, I expect titles for about 15,000–30,000 hectares a month [E: the apparatus rate limited to accessible villages] and a cumulative figure by the end of 1964 of about 150,000–300,000 hectares [D], about a sixth to a quarter of the target, concentrated in the provinces around Saigon, along the Bassac and in the coastal Centre. The rest of the target is reached only as fast as security spreads.

**Compensation.** Cheques and bonds are printed by the computing cell; in 1970 the aid mission ran the computer operation that printed titles, cheques and bonds [S3]. In 2026 configuration that is ruggedised laptops and printers on generator power, with the register replicated by courier on encrypted drives between province and Saigon [E: no data network exists]. Throughput is several thousand titles a day [E: current office printers working in batches], far ahead of demarcation.

**Grievances.** Twelve courts hearing perhaps 15–25 cases a day each [E: summary hearings in the village] can hear about 4,000–6,500 a month [D]. Rwanda left only 0.1% of parcels with unresolved disputes, but in peacetime and after mediation by local bodies [S8]. In a war, with landlords contesting and officials colluding, I expect disputes on 3–8% of parcels [E: the 1970 complaints of collusion and reclassification [S3] in a contested countryside], or 40,000–120,000 cases [D]. The courts are a rate limit in the second year.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** Minister of Land Reform → Directorate General → province land service → Village Land Distribution Committee, as in 1970 [S3]. The province land officer serves the province chief, who in 1964 is almost always an army officer commanding the Regional and Popular Forces of his province [H]; the provision gives the land officer a second, direct line to the Directorate and a monthly return of claims, titles, disputes and complaints of fees that the province chief does not sign. The American province land advisers report to the aid mission, and a monthly abstract comes to this headquarters. There is no single manager of pacification in 1964 (G-13), and this programme adds one more line to a country team that is not working to a common plan.

**Physical communications.** Province land services use the province radio net, AN/GRC-9 and AN/PRC-10 sets until the AN/PRC-25 reaches the advisory effort from 1965 [H], and courier; the mobile teams carry an HF set for calling the district and, under escort, the escort's radio. The register moves by courier.

**Talking to ARVN.** It is a Vietnamese programme in Vietnamese, executed by Vietnamese officials, so it talks to the RVNAF through the province and district chiefs without interpreters or American intermediaries. That is its strength. The weakness is the same fact seen from the other side: the province and district chiefs who must protect the teams, enforce the courts' orders and stop rent collection are officers, often of landed families, chosen for loyalty to whichever general appointed them (G-4), and changed with each coup.

**Security of the information.** The list of new owners is a list of people who have accepted something from the government. In a hamlet the Viet Cong control at night, that list is a target list. The posted lists in contested villages show parcels and tillers by parcel number, and names are posted only where the village is held at night [U: a design choice to protect claimants].

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions: which provinces, districts and hamlets it covers, and what security it needs.*

**Where the land is.** The Delta provinces of the present IV Corps area hold most of the tenanted land and most of the tenant families: about 855,000 of the Delta's 1,175,000 farm families [D]. Average Delta rents were 34% of the crop [S2], against a legal ceiling of 25% set by Ordinance 2 in January 1955 [S1]. The coastal provinces of the Centre, from Quang Nam to Binh Thuan, have smaller plots, rents generally half the crop [S2], village officials who are themselves landowners [S3], and communal land. The provinces around Saigon carry tenancy on a smaller scale.

**What it excludes.** Rubber and other plantations, which are estates worked for wages rather than tenanted rice land [U]. The highlands, where Montagnard land rights are a separate and politically different problem that this programme does not touch [U]. Orchards and house plots.

**Security categories and what the programme does in each.**

| Hamlet category | 1964 share, Delta | What the programme does |
|---|---|---|
| Held by the government by day and night | About a fifth [E: the record's opening-date assessment] | Full titling, posted names, courts in session, rent collection prosecuted |
| Held by day, contested at night | About a third [E: the remainder not under VC control] | Provisional transfer in force; demarcation by day under escort; titles issued and held at the district until the hamlet is secured, or delivered at the tiller's choice; names not posted |
| Held by the Viet Cong | About two-fifths or more [E: the Delta situation at the end of 1963] | No teams. The decree's confirmation of VC distributions is broadcast and leafleted; the tiller's claim is registered from the photograph and the district's information, to be redeemed when the government arrives |

**Security needed.** A team of 25 working by day in a contested village needs an escort of about a Regional Forces company [E: enough to deter a guerrilla platoon], must leave before dark, and must not return on a predictable schedule. The team itself is unarmed by design, because an armed land team is a target and a militia; its escort carries the Regional Forces' weapons of 1964, M1 and M2 carbines and M1918A2 Browning Automatic Rifles [H], and calls for help on the escort's radio. A titled hamlet needs Popular Forces resident at night (POSTNA-2), or its new owners will be visited by the enemy's security section. The programme does not provide that security; it depends on it.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

*Read as what the programme consumes and what RVNAF cannot supply.*

**Fuel and flying.** Two aircraft at about 100 gallons an hour [E: King Air 350-class consumption], flying about 60 hours a month each in season [E: a typical photographic season], need about 12,000 gallons a month of turbine fuel [D] from Tan Son Nhut, a negligible draw. The mobile teams need lift into villages off the roads: about one helicopter sortie a team-week [E: the share of villages beyond road or waterway], or about 500 sorties a month for 120 teams [D], from the CH-21C and UH-1B companies, and in I Corps the Marine UH-34D squadron, whose hours are already the scarcest resource in the advisory effort (G-15) [H]. In the Delta most movement is by boat, in the LCM-6 and LCVP craft of the ARVN River Assault Groups or by hired sampan [H], and the requirement falls; in the Centre it is by road where the road is open. Title stock, generators and cash move between Saigon and the province capitals by C-47 and C-123B [H], and the American province advisers move in U-6A, U-1A, U-10B and O-1 light aircraft when seats can be had [H]. I estimate the programme's real draw at 200–300 helicopter sorties a month [E: the share of villages beyond road or waterway], which must be bid against operations.

**Spares and contractor support.** Cameras, aircraft and computers are maintained by the contractor in Saigon; none of it is supportable by the RVNAF. Paper for about one million titles printed on security stock with a parcel extract and a photograph of the holder [E: a title bearing parcel and holder], ink, laminating material, generators and fuel.

**Specialist personnel.** Photogrammetrists and computer staff, American, for about three years [E: the photography and its first revision]. Vietnamese cadastral surveyors, scarce (D2). Judges for the land courts, drawn from a judiciary that is small and mostly in Saigon [E: a judiciary concentrated in Saigon in the period].

**Money.** The piaster cash for the 20% compensation [S2], about $40–56 million equivalent in the first two years [D], delivered to landlords who mostly live in Saigon and the provincial capitals. This is the programme's largest single logistic task and its most corruptible (P001b).

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Photogrammetry, computing, bond finance, and the political protection of the teams against their own province chiefs. What only the RVNAF can supply: escort, night security, and the authority of a district chief who wants the programme to work.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

*Read as transfer to the Government of Vietnam, which owns it from the first day.*

It is Vietnamese law executed by Vietnamese officials, so there is nothing to transfer in the Vietnamization sense. The American components transfer as follows. The aerial survey and photogrammetry: within three to four years [E: training Vietnamese photogrammetrists from a standing start], although by then the photography is done and only revision flights remain. The computing cell: within two to three years [E: training Vietnamese operators on current software]. The bonds: not transferable, because they are American money, and the landlords' confidence that they will be paid over eight years is confidence in Washington, not in Saigon.

What happens when it is transferred is the question Rwanda raises: after the first registration, the register must record every later inheritance, sale and division, or the land slides back into informality; Rwanda's case study found the long-term sustainability of the land administration system not secured [S8]. The 15-year transfer ban [S2] postpones the problem and creates another, an informal market in titled land that the register does not see. A land register maintained by a corrupt province service becomes, within a decade, a list of who used to own what. The programme's durable product is not the register; it is the fact that a million families once received land from the Republic, and that cannot be transferred back.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**Performs to specification when:** the hamlet is held at night; the province chief wants the programme to work, or fears the consequences if it does not; compensation reaches the landlord on time, so that he has something to lose by resisting; the courts hear grievances in weeks; the photography is flown in the dry season; and the government that promulgated the decree is still the government a year later.

**Performance collapses when:** troops escort the landlord back. That is the historical pattern, in which land the insurgents distributed was often reclaimed by landlords when government forces cleared an area [S4], and it is the single act that would turn the programme into the enemy's propaganda. It collapses when officials charge fees to accept applications or take discounts on compensation cheques [S3]; when landlords reclassify rented land as worship land [S3]; when the bonds lose their value; when the Viet Cong kill the first title holders in a district and nobody comes; and when a new junta suspends it.

**Tempo over weeks, not once.** A show village titled in the presence of the Chief of State proves nothing. The test is the hundredth contested village in the second monsoon, with the escort company withdrawn for an operation and the land officer's pay three months late.

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **Reversal by coup.** The decree is promulgated by one junta and must survive the next. Partial failure: a successor suspends titling or compensation and leaves the rent ban in place, which keeps the tenant's gain and turns the landlords against the state. Total failure: a successor restores rents. Total failure would be worse than never having started, because it would prove the enemy's argument in the most personal way available. I judge partial suspension a real risk in 1964 and total reversal unlikely once more than about a quarter of the target is titled [E: titled families become a constituency no junta wishes to provoke].
2. **Capture by officials.** Fees, collusion with landlords, false worship-land claims and discounted cheques, all of which occurred in 1970 [S3]. Partial failure is a programme that works at a price; the peasant pays a bribe for his title and still owns his land. That is a smaller gain than intended, not a loss.
3. **Landlord resistance.** Many officers and officials come from landed families or have married into them [H]. Their resistance comes through the province chief, through the junta, and through the legal fiction of self-cultivation.
4. **Enemy retaliation and co-optation.** The Viet Cong can kill title holders in contested hamlets, and can tell the peasants that the land was the revolution's gift and the puppets have merely been forced to confirm it. Both are likely (Part 2).
5. **Records failure.** Lost registers, disputed boundaries, and claims the courts cannot hear in time.
6. **Bond failure.** If the bonds are not indexed, or are not paid, inflation transfers the cost to the landlord, and the landlord is the officer corps' family.

**During the window of failure.** The provisional transfer stands unless it is repealed, so a halt in titling does not reopen rent collection by itself. The force's task in such a window is to stop the escort of landlords, which is in my hands, and to make the case to Washington, which is not.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** The decree; the broadcasts; rents stop in the towns and along the main roads; photography begins; the province services and teams are recruiting and training. Almost no titles. The landlords' lobby in Saigon forms in the first week.

**Month six.** Photography of the Delta complete [D]. Teams in the field in the government-held and contested-by-day villages of perhaps a dozen provinces [E: the provinces with accessible villages near main roads and waterways]. Titles for perhaps 60,000–120,000 hectares [E: half the 1964 rate in D5 while teams work up]. The first killings of title holders in contested hamlets [E: the enemy's predictable response], and the first fee scandals.

**Month twelve.** Titles for about 150,000–300,000 hectares [D]. The para-surveyors are proficient; the accessible villages are running out, and the remaining target lies in hamlets the government does not hold at night. Courts are backed up. The programme's pace now depends almost entirely on security.

**What declines first.** Not the equipment or the staff's skill, which improve. The first thing to decline is honesty in the province services as volume and money rise; the second is the junta's will after its first coup scare in which landed officers are involved. The third is the escort: every operation that pulls a Regional Forces company away stops a team.

The margin that matters is the six years. ### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P001a -->
It does not make a single hamlet secure at night, and it cannot be delivered in a hamlet the government does not hold: there it is a promise and a registered claim, not a title. It does not stop Viet Cong conscription or coercive taxation, which do not depend on persuasion. It does not reach the main force or Hanoi's will. It does not produce gratitude on any schedule; its effect accrues over seasons and years in what the rural population believes the government's return means. It does not touch the highlands, the plantations or the cities. It is not a guarantee against reversal by the next junta, and it injures the landed families from which much of the officer corps comes. The claim that the 1970 programme cut Viet Cong recruitment by 80% [S11] is the designers' own organisation's claim and is not adopted here. 2026 survey practice that depends on satellite navigation or data networks does not work in 1964; the photography is tied to ground control by optical survey [H].
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P001a -->
The land argument: the claim that the government's return to a hamlet means the landlord's return, and with it the logic that a family must send its sons to the revolution because only the revolution's victory keeps the land [S5]. Where titles are delivered and defended, it denies the infrastructure the consent it taxes by; where they cannot be delivered, it denies the enemy the certainty that the peasant has nothing to gain from the government's arrival.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P001a -->
This is the provision the theater needs assessment said could touch its first-ranked deficit, and it does: it changes, at a stroke and on paper, the answer to the question that defines the Republic in the countryside, which is what the government's arrival means to a tenant. Six years earlier than history, with the 1970 law's terms and the repairs that law needed, it is a potentially war-shaping political instrument at a negligible cost in money and men. It is not a security measure and it cannot outrun security: in 1964 the government can deliver titles in perhaps a fifth to a half of the Delta's hamlets, and a title in a hamlet the enemy holds at night is a list of targets. Its value therefore depends on what holds the hamlets, on whether the juntas keep it and keep their troops from escorting landlords back, and on whether the officials executing it are honest. Its great virtue is that once a million families have received land, no later government or enemy can easily take that fact back. Its impact on 1964 is modest; its impact on the war, if it survives, is large and slow, and I will say so at full weight.

## §1.2 GVN Anti-Corruption Programme with Enforcement Mechanism — P001b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

*Read for an institutional provision: the law, the bodies it creates, their scale and their structure.*

**Designation.** The Anti-Corruption Programme of the Government of Vietnam, 1964: a decree-law promulgated on the arrival date [U] creating five bodies and one obligation. Its design is that of the Hong Kong Independent Commission Against Corruption, founded on 15 February 1974 [S12], adapted to a state at war whose army is its government, with an enforcement mechanism that the Republic cannot supply for itself and that only the United States can.

1. **The Commission Against Corruption.** An independent statutory body under a Commissioner appointed for a fixed six-year term and removable only for cause by a two-thirds vote of the ruling military council (on the arrival date, the Military Revolutionary Council) with the concurrence of the US co-chair of the Aid Compliance Board [U: the provision's enforcement mechanism read as an American guarantee of the Commission's independence]. Three departments on the Hong Kong model [S12]: Operations, which investigates and in Hong Kong holds about 72% of the staff [S12]; Corruption Prevention, which reviews the procedures of ministries and commands for opportunities to steal; and Community Relations, which takes complaints and tells the public what the Commission does [S12]. Powers to arrest, search and seize [S12], to compel asset declarations, to freeze accounts, and to grant immunity to witnesses.
2. **The Special Court for Corruption.** Panels of three judges with an appellate chamber, trying the Commission's cases in public under ordinary rules of evidence. The provision rejects the existing model: in March 1966, on the historical course, a special court for economic crimes would convict the businessman Ta Vinh of speculation, hoarding and bribery in a one-day trial and have him shot by a military firing squad in Saigon before a crowd [S18]. That is spectacle, not enforcement, and it fell on a Chinese merchant, not on an officer.
3. **The Pay and Muster Verification Service.** A body independent of the RVNAF chain of command that musters any unit of the regular, Regional or Popular Forces without notice, issues every serviceman an identity card bearing his photograph and fingerprints, and delivers his pay to him in person through its own paymasters on presentation of the card. Its 2026 equipment is the part of 2026 that works in 1964: biometric enrolment kits with fingerprint scanners and cameras, ruggedised laptops holding an offline database, and generators [E: current field biometric kits]. There is no network to connect them; the database moves by courier on encrypted drives.
4. **The Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board.** The enforcement mechanism. A Vietnamese chairman and an American co-chair, the Deputy Chief of Mission, with the Director of the aid mission and my chief of staff as American members. On a finding by the Commission or a muster report, the Board may suspend Military Assistance Program deliveries to a named command, Commercial Import Program licences to a named importer or ministry, or the release of counterpart piasters to a named province, until the government acts on the finding. Resumption needs the American co-chair's agreement [U].
5. **The asset declaration.** Every officer of the rank of major and above, every province and district chief, every minister and director-general declares his and his family's assets on appointment and annually; wealth beyond lawful income that he cannot explain is itself an offence [U: an unexplained-wealth provision on the Hong Kong model].
6. **A limited amnesty.** Petty corruption completed before promulgation is not prosecuted unless the offender continues it [U]. Hong Kong came to the same answer the hard way in 1977, with a partial amnesty for minor offences before that year after police officers stormed the Commission's offices [S12]; this provision begins with it, because a programme that threatens every official for the past fights every official at once.

**Manufacturer.** The Government of Vietnam, with the US Mission providing the American members of the Board, investigators and auditors, the biometric equipment, and training.

**In-service date.** No anti-corruption body of this kind existed in the Republic. Its real-world references are the Hong Kong ICAC (1974) [S12] and Indonesia's Corruption Eradication Commission, the KPK (2003) [S13]; its cautionary reference is the Afghan Personnel and Pay System of the 2010s [S14].

**Quantity.** One programme. About 2,000 Vietnamese staff at full strength and about 320 Americans [E: ICAC's 1,213 staff in 2007 for a city of about 7 million [S12], scaled for a country of about 15 million with an armed force of about 514,000 in 1964 [S24]].

**Organisation in theater.**

| Element | Strength | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Commission headquarters | About 300 [E: a national headquarters for investigations, prevention and community relations] | Saigon |
| Regional offices | Four, about 150 each [E: one per corps area] | Da Nang, Pleiku or Nha Trang, Bien Hoa, Can Tho |
| Pay and Muster Verification Service | About 700, including 60 muster teams of eight [E: enough to muster the force twice a year] | Headquarters in Saigon, teams by corps area |
| Special Court for Corruption | Ten panels and an appellate chamber, about 150 with staff [E: ten three-judge panels and an appellate chamber with clerks] | Saigon, sitting on circuit in the corps headquarters |
| Commission protective detail | About 100 [E: armed guards for witnesses, archives and investigators] | Saigon and regional offices |
| Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board | A board with a secretariat of about 30 [E: a joint secretariat for case tracking] | The US Embassy and the Ministry of Finance |
| US Anti-Corruption Advisory Group | About 320 Americans (D2) | With the Commission, the Service and the Board |

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

*Read as staffing: Vietnamese and American.*

**Vietnamese.** About 2,000 [D], recruited outside the networks they will investigate. That is the hardest thing in the provision. Hong Kong created its Commission precisely because the police's own anti-corruption branch had failed: a senior police officer under its inquiry, Peter Godber, simply flew home [S12]. A Saigon commission staffed by transfers from the Sûreté, the ministries or the army would bring its networks with it. The provision recruits from younger university graduates, from the magistracy, and from northern refugees in the civil service who have no family in the southern patronage networks [E: the pools available in 1964], vets them through the American advisers, and pays them two to three times the civil service scale [E: enough that the bribe offered must be large], with draft deferment for service in the Commission.

**American.** About 320 [E: the components named in this paragraph]: about 140 military, Army Criminal Investigation Division agents, finance and audit officers, and muster-team advisers [E: about 60 CID agents, 40 finance and audit officers and 40 muster-team advisers]; about 180 civilians, auditors from the aid mission, Treasury financial investigators, lawyers, interpreters, and biometric-system technicians [E: the functions named, at roughly one civilian per eleven Vietnamese staff]. Americans who can read Vietnamese and French financial records are scarce [E: the small pool of Army and Treasury linguists of the period], and this is the provision's American bottleneck.

**Personnel spaces.** About 140 military spaces [D]. CONFIG sets no troop ceiling. The cost of the spaces is not their number but their visibility: American investigators inside the Vietnamese state are the most direct form of the leverage that costs the Republic its claim to be its own government (POSTNA-6), and the provision keeps them in advisory and audit roles, never as the arresting officers.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as instruments and authorities.*

**Powers of the Commission.** Arrest, search and seizure [S12]; compelled asset declarations; freezing of bank accounts in the Republic; the unexplained-wealth offence; witness immunity and protection; the right of entry to any ministry, province office or military headquarters with the Commissioner's warrant [U].

**Powers of the Pay and Muster Verification Service.** Unannounced muster of any unit; the identity card as the only document on which pay is drawn; payment in person [U]. A name on the roll with no man to answer it is struck off, and the unit's commander must explain it to the Commission.

**Powers of the Board.** Suspension of military aid to a command, of import licences to an importer or ministry, and of counterpart piasters to a province [U]. These are the levers that matter, because the money that pays the RVNAF and the civil service runs through them: the Commercial Import Program sold aid dollars to licensed importers for piasters, which went into a fund that paid the army, the police and the civil service [S20]. Licences were withheld until bribes were paid [S20]; on the historical course importers would be paying 4–5 piastres per dollar for a licence by 1965 [S17], and a 1966 US report would find a 100% domestic profit on piasters possible [S20]. The Board can suspend a licence; it can stop a province's piasters until the province chief explains where last quarter's went.

**What it targets.** In order of priority [U: the design ranks targets by their effect on the war]: ghost soldiers and pay theft, because they rob the fighting units and falsify the army's size; the sale of commands and offices, because it selects the wrong men for the posts that decide the countryside (G-4); diversion of aid commodities, rice and fuel; and fees extorted from the population by officials, including those on the land programme (P001a).

**Desertion.** Desertion across all components stood at 73,000 in 1964 and would reach 113,000 in 1965 [S23]. The record attributes it to family and village loyalty and weak punishment, not to pay [S23]; the pay delivery touches it only where a soldier leaves because what reaches his family will not feed them [E: the share is unknown and probably a minority of cases], which is why the band claimed for desertion is small.

**What it cannot compel.** It cannot compel the ruling military council, which is the government. Its warrant against a corps commander is worth what the faction protecting that commander is worth against the faction protecting the Commission. It cannot make officers competent, only remove some who are both corrupt and exposed. It cannot pay a soldier enough to live on; with Saigon living costs rising 40% in 1965 and 68% in 1966 on the historical course [S21], petty corruption that is survival will not yield to prosecution.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what it learns, how, and how reliably.*

**Physical counts.** The muster is the Commission's best instrument because it produces a fact the reporting chain cannot write: the number of men who answer their names. It is the indicator the theater needs assessment asked for, the share of a battalion's authorised strength present for duty, counted by someone outside the chain [E: the record's opening-date measures for G-4 and G-10].

**Documents.** Payrolls, ration strength returns, Commercial Import Program licences and invoices, province budget accounts, bank records in the Republic, and the asset declarations. The aid programmes' own controls were weak: on the historical course the GAO would find end-use checks limited, theft at off-loading estimated at 2–5% and unverifiable, and licences split below $10,000 to avoid competitive bidding [S21].

**Complaints and informants.** Complaint boxes in every district office and post office, letters, walk-ins at the regional offices, and a Commission representative on the circuit of the land courts [U]. There are no telephones in the hamlets. Informants inside the networks, who come forward only once someone has been convicted.

**American advisers.** The advisers already know a good deal of what the Commission needs, and report little of it, because reporting a counterpart's theft destroys the relationship that is the adviser's only instrument (G-6). The provision gives them a channel that does not run through the counterpart: confidential reports to the Commission's American advisory group.

**Triple canopy, night, monsoon.** Not applicable in the literal sense. In the institutional sense: the Commission sees well in Saigon and the province capitals, adequately in garrisons and district towns, and almost not at all in the hamlets and the isolated posts, where the Popular Forces' pay is most easily stolen. Musters of remote posts are flown in by helicopter in the dry season and are irregular in the monsoon, when roads close.

**Reliability.** Physical counts and documents are reliable; complaints are a mixture of truth and denunciation, including denunciations planted by rival factions and by the enemy. A commission that acts on denunciations without corroboration becomes the instrument of whoever denounces most.

**Which half of find-and-fix (A6).** Neither. The Commission's investigations of officials who sell offices, documents and information will turn up enemy agents among them, because the enemy buys what is for sale [E: corrupt officials are the easiest point of penetration]; that is a by-product, not a capability.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

**Musters.** Sixty teams at about eight battalion-equivalents a team-month [E: two days per unit with travel] muster about 480 units a month [D], enough to muster the whole RVNAF of about 514,000 in 1964 [S24], regular and territorial, about twice a year [E: roughly 1,200 battalion-equivalents in the force]. Biometric enrolment runs ahead of the musters at about 2,000 men a day [E: 600 kits at a few dozen enrolments a day each, reduced for movement], so the force is enrolled in about nine months [D].

**Investigations and prosecutions.** About 1,100 Operations staff [D] (about 55% of 2,000, below Hong Kong's 72% [S12], because the Muster Service is separate) open about 1,500–2,500 cases a year and bring about 300–500 to court [E: a new commission's caseload, weighted toward documentary cases]. The court's ten panels try about 300–400 cases a year [E: a public trial of a corruption case takes weeks, not a day]. The court is the bottleneck by the end of the first year.

**The rate limit is political.** The Commission can investigate a district chief in weeks; it can arrest him only if his province chief does not protect him, and the province chief only if his corps commander does not. How many networks the Commission can engage at once is set not by its staff but by how many enemies its sponsors can afford. Indonesia's KPK prosecuted the allies of a president and the son of the ruling party's chair, and in 2019 rival political leaders converged to put a supervisory board over it and its surveillance [S13]. A Saigon commission reaches that point faster, because its sponsors are generals.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as reporting chain and interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

**Chain.** The Commissioner reports to the Chief of State and to the Aid Compliance Board, and to no minister. The Muster Service reports its counts to the Commission, to the Joint General Staff and to this headquarters' personnel staff at the same time, so that no one in the chain can edit the number on its way up [U]. The Board's American co-chair reports to the Ambassador.

**Interface with the RVNAF.** By order of the Joint General Staff, the Muster Service has right of entry to any unit and the unit's commander must parade his men [U]. The order is only as good as the Joint General Staff's will to enforce it, and the Joint General Staff's chief is a member of the junta. Commission investigators interviewing officers will meet the RVNAF's own military security service, which has its own interests.

**Communications.** The Commission must not use RVNAF or police channels for anything that names a target, or the target is warned by his friends and, through them, perhaps by the enemy's agents. It has its own HF net with American-supplied encryption [E: a small set of HF stations with modern encryption] and couriers; muster teams carry an AN/PRC-10 for movement control only [H].

**Talking to ARVN.** It speaks the language and works through Vietnamese law. The danger is the reverse of a communications problem: the RVNAF hears the Commission very clearly, and part of what it hears is that an American-backed body is counting its men and reading its accounts.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

**Reach.** Strong in Saigon, the corps headquarters and the province capitals, where the money, the licences and the offices are. Moderate in division and regimental garrisons and district towns. Weak in the hamlets and isolated posts. It reaches the Popular Forces' pay only through the Muster Service, and only where a team can land.

**Security preconditions.** The investigators need protection from the people they investigate before they need it from the Viet Cong. Witnesses need protection from both. In the historical record a junior civil servant who spat on the Minister of Economics over corruption was dismissed for it [S17]; under this provision he is a witness, and the Commission must keep him alive. The protective detail of about 100 [D] is a floor. The regional offices sit inside the corps headquarters compounds for physical security, which places them inside the commands they investigate; that is a contradiction the provision cannot resolve in 1964.

**Political preconditions.** It needs a faction in Saigon strong enough to protect it and interested enough in clean government, or in its rivals' exposure, to do so; and an American mission willing to pull the Board's lever against a command at war.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Vehicles and flying.** About 150 vehicles [E: muster teams, investigators, couriers]; about 40 helicopter sorties a month for musters of posts off the roads [E: a share of the 480 monthly musters in D5], which must be bid against operations.

**Equipment.** About 600 biometric enrolment kits [E: D5 enrolment rate], laptops, printers for identity cards on security stock, generators, encrypted drives, a secure archive in Saigon for the asset declarations and case files.

**Cash.** The Muster Service's paymasters deliver the RVNAF's monthly pay, a very large sum in piasters [E: the pay of over half a million men], under guard, to the soldier. Carrying the payroll is the programme's largest logistic task and makes its convoys a target for the enemy and for bandits. In 2026 the answer would be payment to a bank account or a telephone; in 1964 neither exists in the countryside, and Afghanistan's experience of paying through trusted agents shows the leakage [S14]. The payroll moves between the Saigon treasury and the corps areas by C-47 under guard [H], and by road only under escort, where the road needs it by ARVN armoured cavalry in M113 armoured personnel carriers [H].

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Independence. Every other item could in principle be Vietnamese; the one thing the RVNAF cannot supply to a body that investigates it is freedom from it.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

*Read as transfer to the Government of Vietnam.*

The Commission, the Court and the Muster Service are Vietnamese from the first day. The American components divide. The investigators, auditors and biometric technicians can hand over within five to eight years [E: the time to train a generation of Vietnamese investigators and to build a record of convictions they can stand on]. The Aid Compliance Board cannot be handed over at all, because it is American leverage, and leverage cannot be Vietnamized: when the American aid flows diminish, so does the lever. On transfer, the Commission survives only if a Vietnamese political sponsor wants it, and its independence lasts only as long as no coalition of the powerful decides to end it; Indonesia's KPK shows how fast that can happen in a far more stable state [S13]. The programme's durable products are the identity-card roll of the RVNAF, a body of convicted cases that changes what officers expect, and a generation of investigators; none of these is guaranteed to survive the end of the American lever.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**Performs to specification when:** a faction in Saigon protects it; the American mission backs the Board's suspensions even when a suspended command is in combat; it begins with cases that are documentary, physical and undeniable, ghost soldiers and stolen pay, before it moves to the sale of offices; it is seen to convict on evidence and to acquit the innocent; and it does not reach the ruling military council before it has a constituency outside it.

**Performance collapses when:** it reaches a member of the junta without a stronger patron; it becomes the weapon of one faction against another, as charges of "lax morality" became the instrument of Khanh's coup against the Dalat generals in January–May 1964 on the historical course [H]; the Americans flinch from suspending aid to a unit in contact; investigators are killed or bought; or a coup installs a junta that wants it gone.

**Tempo over weeks, not once.** The first wave of musters will show the size of the ghost problem in a single month, as Afghanistan's reported force fell by about 42,000 when reporting moved to biometrically validated personnel [S15]. The test is the second year, when the easy cases are finished and the remaining ones are protected.

### D11 · Failure modes

1. **Factional capture.** The commonest failure of anti-corruption bodies in weak states: the Commission prosecutes the junta's rivals and spares its friends. Partial capture is a commission that is honest at the district level and selective at the top; total capture is a political police with an anti-corruption name. In a junta that has already used corruption and morals charges against fellow generals [H], this is the most likely failure.
2. **Corruption of the Commission.** Its investigators are offered the price of their silence. Hong Kong built its Commission because the previous body had been corrupted [S12]. The defences are pay, vetting, rotation, and the American advisory group's audit of the Commission itself.
3. **Coup.** The generals whose incomes the Commission threatens are the generals who make coups. A coup removes the Commission's patron and, very possibly, the Commission.
4. **Biometric circumvention.** Commanders enrol real men as ghost soldiers, drawing pay for men who live at home and share it, or bribe the enrollers. Afghanistan's pay system never connected to its biometric database, which left 7,714 payroll records absent from it and at least $11.3 million paid to them [S14], and its records held 7,100 duplicate and 1,009 invalid identity numbers among 175,195 [S14]. The equipment is not the control; the independent, physical muster is.
5. **The American flinch.** The Board suspends aid to a division whose province is under attack, and Washington overrules it within a week. After the first such reversal the Board is not believed.
6. **Paralysis.** Officials who fear prosecution stop signing anything, and the war's administration slows. This is a real cost of a successful start and must be managed by the Prevention department's work on procedures.
7. **Penetration.** A commission that holds the regime's secrets is a target for the enemy's agents.

**During the window of failure.** If the Commission is suspended, the Muster Service's identity-card roll and the Board remain American-backed facts. The minimum I would fight to keep through any coup is the independent muster and pay delivery, because it is the part that most directly puts money and food in the soldier's hands and the true number of soldiers in mine.

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** Recruitment and vetting; the first muster teams trained; the first complaints, thousands of them, most unusable [E: the experience of new anti-corruption agencies]. No prosecutions. The networks are watching to see whom the Commission touches first.

**Month six.** Enrolment about two-thirds complete [D]; the first musters show the ghost rate by corps area. The first hundred or so cases in court [E: from D5], almost all of them below the rank of colonel. Pay reaching the soldier where the Service delivers it. The first attempt to protect a senior officer.

**Month twelve.** Enrolment complete; the force mustered about once [D]. The Commission has either reached a general officer or been contained; which of these happens is the most important fact about it, and no figure will show it in advance.

**What declines first.** Political backing, after the first case that touches a protected man. Then the morale and safety of the Vietnamese investigators. The equipment and the American staff are the last things to fail.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P001b -->
It does not end corruption in the Republic, and it does not reach the junta itself without a patron stronger than the generals it would accuse. It does not make officers competent; it removes some who are corrupt and exposed, and only where a replacement exists. Biometric identity cards do not stop a commander who controls who is enrolled; only the independent, physical muster does that, and only where a team can reach. It does not pay a soldier enough to live on in an inflating economy, so petty corruption born of need survives it. It is not an instrument against the Viet Cong. It is likely to be used by factions against each other. Its enforcement lever, the Aid Compliance Board, is American and cannot be transferred; it weakens as American aid falls, and every suspension costs the Republic part of its claim to be its own government (POSTNA-6).
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P001b -->
One of the Front's strongest themes, a corrupt regime that robs its own soldiers and people, where convictions are visible and reach above the district. Some of the black market through which American goods reach the enemy, and some of the bribes by which his agents buy documents and posts. Above all it denies him the hollow battalion: a unit carried at a strength it does not have, whose commander and whose advisers do not know how weak it is until it breaks.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P001b -->
This is a smaller provision than it sounds and a more important one than its figures will show. It will not clean the Saigon government, and in a junta that makes coups its most likely failure is to become one faction's weapon against another. What it can do reliably is the physical part: count the army, put the pay in the soldier's hand, and end the ghost battalion, which strengthens the RVNAF's real fighting power, reduces one cause of desertion, and gives this headquarters a true strength return for the first time (G-10). What it may do, if it is protected and the American lever is used and seen to be used, is change what an officer expects to happen to him if he buys a command or sells his men's rice, which reaches the officer corps (G-4) where nothing in an arsenal can. Its greatest value may be to the land programme (P001a), whose officials it watches. Its greatest cost is political: it threatens the incomes of the men who make governments, and it gives the Americans a hand on the Republic's throat that the enemy will name for what it is.
