# Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war [H40], and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end [H40].

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp [H39]. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone [H39].

## EV000-41 · The siege of Khe Sanh · 21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province · HIST · 000
In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region [H46]. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion [H46]. The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump [H]. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", answered with Operation Niagara: intelligence collection by sensors and reconnaissance, followed by a campaign in which about 60 B-52s attacked on an average day and more than 100,000 tons of bombs fell by mid-April, with more than 158,000 artillery rounds fired in the first three months [H46].

On 7 February twelve PT-76 amphibious tanks of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, overran the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei, the first PAVN tank attack of the war; at least five tanks were knocked out, but of about 500 CIDG some 200 were killed or missing, and of 24 Americans ten were killed and eleven wounded [H46]. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, much of it by C-130s using the Low Altitude Parachute Extraction System, and from late February the "Super Gaggle" of A-4s suppressing flak let helicopters supply the hill outposts [H46]. The air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters [H46].

Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April; it cost 92 Americans and 33 ARVN killed [H46]. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July; US intelligence counted 1,602 bodies and estimated 10,000–15,000 dead, while a secret MACV report estimated 5,550 to the end of March [H46]. Whether Hanoi ever meant to take the base, or used it to fix American attention on the border while the cities were attacked, is disputed to this day; a captured PAVN study of 1974 said the PAVN would have taken it if it could, at a price it was not willing to pay [H46].

## EV000-42 · The Tet Offensive: the first wave · 30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · HIST · 000
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps [H39]. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet [H]. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country [H39]. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital [H39].

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns [H]. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed [H39]. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing [H39]. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed [H39].

## EV000-43 · The battle for Saigon and the attack on the US embassy · 31 Jan – 7 Mar 1968 · Saigon, Cholon and Tan Son Nhut · HIST · 000
At 02:45 on 31 January a 19-man team of the VC C-10 Sapper Battalion blew a hole in the wall of the new US embassy compound in Saigon and fought inside the grounds for more than six hours without entering the chancery; the compound was secured by 09:20, and five Americans were killed [H39] [H]. The sappers were all killed or captured [H]. Other VC units attacked Tan Son Nhut air base with three battalions, the Joint General Staff compound, the Presidential Palace and the naval headquarters, and held the national radio station for about six hours, though they could not broadcast [H39]. The image of the embassy under attack, broadcast in the United States hours after Westmoreland had declared the enemy's offensive capacity broken, did more political damage than any battle of the war [H].

The fighting in Saigon lasted weeks in Cholon, the Chinese quarter, where VC battalions held out around the Phu Tho racetrack. On 4 February residents were ordered out and the area declared a free-fire zone; the last battle in Cholon was fought on 7 March [H39]. On 1 February the national police chief, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shot a captured VC officer in the head in a Saigon street before a photographer and a film camera [H]. The image became, with the embassy, the American public's picture of Tet.

## EV000-44 · The battle of Hue · 31 Jan – 2 Mar 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · HIST · 000
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River [H45]. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions [H45]. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division [H45].

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle [H45] [H]. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west and did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February [H45]. The ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel; the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March [H].

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded [H45]. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source [H45]. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed [H45]. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800 [H45] [H]. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war.

## EV000-45 · The reinforcement request and the decision of 31 March · 20 Feb – 31 Mar 1968 · Saigon and Washington · HIST · 000
On 12 February Westmoreland asked for 10,500 more troops; on 13 February a brigade of the 82nd Airborne Division and a Marine regimental landing team, already authorized, were sent [H39] [H]. On 20 February the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Earle Wheeler, went to Saigon, and returned with a request for 206,000 more men, framed by the Chiefs to force a mobilization of the reserves [H]. On 28 February McNamara left office and on 1 March Clark Clifford succeeded him, charged with reviewing the request [H39] [H]. On 10 March the *New York Times* reported it [H]. On 12 March Senator Eugene McCarthy, running against the war, took 42 percent of the vote in the New Hampshire primary; on 16 March Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race [H]. On 25–26 March the senior outside advisers, the "Wise Men", told the President that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost [H].

On 31 March Johnson announced in a televised address that he was stopping the bombing of North Vietnam except in the area just north of the DMZ, that he would send only about 13,500 more troops, that the United States was ready to talk, and that he would not seek or accept his party's nomination for another term [H] [H9]. The speech capped the American commitment. Westmoreland's strategy of attrition by American forces had been judged by the President's own advisers to have no end that the country would accept, and from this point the questions were how to get out, and how to leave the South able to stand.

## EV000-46 · The My Lai massacre · 16 Mar 1968 · Son My village (My Lai 4 and My Khe), Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · HIST · 000
On the morning of 16 March 1968 Company C, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Brigade, Americal Division, operating under Task Force Barker, was landed by helicopter at the hamlet of My Lai 4 in Son My village, in an area of Quang Ngai long held by the VC 48th Local Force Battalion [H47] [H]. It met no resistance. Its men, led among others by Second Lieutenant William L. Calley, killed the unarmed villagers they found, mostly old men, women and children, in their homes, on the paths and in an irrigation ditch; Company B, 4th Battalion, 3rd Infantry killed civilians at My Khe nearby [H47]. The dead number from 347, the US Army figure, to 504, the Vietnamese figure [H47]. Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson, flying an OH-23 scout helicopter overhead, landed between the soldiers and a group of villagers, threatened to fire on his own side, and evacuated survivors; his crewman Glenn Andreotta pulled a wounded child from the ditch [H47].

The Army reported the operation as a victory over 128 Viet Cong, and the brigade's investigation conceded about 20 accidental civilian deaths [H47]. The cover-up held for a year and a half, until Ronald Ridenhour's letters to Congress in March 1969 and Seymour Hersh's report of 13 November 1969 [H47]. My Lai belongs to this phase as an event of the war in the countryside: a company of a division assembled hastily, under a command climate that measured success by bodies, in a province where the population was treated as the enemy's. Its effect on the war came later, when it became known.

## EV000-47 · The battle of Dai Do · 30 Apr – 3 May 1968 · Dai Do and the hamlets north of Dong Ha on the Cua Viet, Quang Tri Province · HIST · 000
At the end of April, as the second wave of the offensive began, battalions of the PAVN 320th Division, two of them identified as belonging to the 48th and 52nd Regiments, moved into the hamlets of Dai Do, Dinh To and their neighbours north of Dong Ha, threatening the Marine base and the Cua Viet supply route [H49]. The 2nd Battalion, 4th Marines under Lieutenant Colonel William Weise attacked across open paddies from amphibian tractors and boats, with the 1st Battalion, 3rd Marines, ARVN units of the 2nd Regiment and a mechanized battalion, and from 1 May the US 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry [H49]. The Marines lost 81 killed and the ARVN 5; the Americans claimed more than 600 PAVN killed, and the PAVN has not released its losses [H49]. By 3 May the PAVN had gone. Whether Dai Do was a main thrust against Dong Ha or a diversion is not settled; the frontal assaults of 1 and 2 May were costly enough to be criticized by later historians [H49].

## EV000-48 · The Paris talks open · May 1968 · Paris · HIST · 000
After weeks of argument over a site, talks between the United States and North Vietnam opened in Paris in May 1968, with W. Averell Harriman leading for the United States and Xuan Thuy for Hanoi [H51]. They stalled at once: Hanoi demanded a complete and unconditional end to the bombing of the North before substantive talks, and refused to recognise the Saigon government; Saigon refused to sit with the Front [H51]. Through the summer the talks were a forum for statements, while both sides fought for position on the ground. Their opening nonetheless changed the war's logic. From May 1968 both governments were fighting to shape a settlement as well as to win, and every offensive that followed had a negotiating purpose.

## EV000-49 · The May offensive in Saigon · 5 May – 15 Jun 1968 · Saigon and its outskirts, and towns throughout the South · HIST · 000
On 5 May the PAVN and VC launched the second phase of the general offensive, which the Americans called "mini-Tet", with attacks by fire on more than 100 towns and installations and a renewed assault on Saigon [H39] [H]. VC battalions fought their way into the southern and western districts of the capital and into Cholon, where they were destroyed or driven out by the ARVN and US forces over several weeks, with air strikes and artillery used inside the city; much of District 8 was destroyed and tens of thousands were made homeless [H]. The phase lasted until 15 June [H39]. Its losses fell heavily on the same local forces and cadres who had survived February, and many of the units that fought in Saigon in May were filled out with northern replacements [H]. American deaths in May 1968 were the highest of any month of the war [H].

## EV000-50 · The evacuation of Kham Duc · 10–12 May 1968 · Kham Duc Special Forces camp and Ngok Tavak, Quang Tin Province · HIST · 000
On 10 May the 40th Battalion of the PAVN 2nd Division's 1st Regiment overran the outpost of Ngok Tavak, held by an Australian-led company of the 11th MIKE Force and a battery of the 2nd Battalion, 13th Marines [H48]. The PAVN 1st and 21st Regiments then closed on the camp at Kham Duc, held by Special Forces Detachment A-105, CIDG and a SOG detachment, reinforced by the 2nd Battalion, 1st Infantry of the Americal Division [H48]. On 11 May Westmoreland approved evacuation, and on 12 May, under about 350 air sorties, roughly 1,500 soldiers and civilians were flown out [H48]. A C-130 flown by Major Bernard L. Bucher was shot down just after take-off, killing all 183 Vietnamese civilians aboard, a Special Forces officer and the five crewmen; a second C-130 was wrecked on the runway [H48]. Lieutenant Colonel Joe M. Jackson landed a C-123 on the abandoned strip to recover a three-man combat control team left behind, and received the Medal of Honor [H48]. The Americans lost about 42–46 killed and 31–33 missing and twelve aircraft; about 220 CIDG were killed or missing; the US claimed about 345 PAVN killed, and a later study estimates 1,500–2,000 [H48]. The abandonment of the camp removed one of the few allied positions between the Laotian border and the lowlands of Quang Tin and Quang Nam, and its loss was one of the few clear PAVN victories over a US-defended position in 1968 [H48].

## EV000-51 · Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned · 10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · HIST · 000
On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff [H50] [H]. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces [H50]. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy [H50].

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended [H62]. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969 [H62]. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh, which Westmoreland had held through the siege; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July [H46]. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ, but it was read by both sides as an admission of what the base had cost.

## EV000-52 · The third offensive · 17 Aug – 23 Sep 1968 · Provincial towns and bases, especially in III Corps and the Central Highlands · HIST · 000
The third phase of the general offensive began in mid-August with attacks by fire and ground assaults on towns and bases, including attacks around Tay Ninh in III Corps [H39] [H]. It was weaker than its predecessors: the local forces that had carried the attacks of February and May were largely gone, and the main forces would not commit themselves against American firepower in the same way again [H39]. It ended in late September without lasting gains [H39]. Taken together, the three offensives of 1968 cost the VC about 50,000 dead by one estimate against about 4,000 US and 6,000 ARVN [H39]. Their recruitment in the South collapsed: in Region 5, 1,700 recruits in 1969 against 8,000 in 1968, in the Mekong lowlands 100 against 16,000 [H39]. By the end of the year about 70 percent of the main-force troops in the South are reported to have been northerners [H39]. MACV estimated total PAVN and VC strength at 287,000 in January 1968 and 251,000 at its end [H39]. The losses forced a reassessment in Hanoi that would carry into 1969 [H].

## EV000-53 · The bombing halt and the American election · 31 Oct – 5 Nov 1968 · Washington, Paris and Saigon · HIST · 000
In October Hanoi signalled that it would accept the Saigon government at the table if the bombing stopped [H]. On 31 October Johnson announced the end of all bombing of North Vietnam from 1 November, ending Rolling Thunder [H9] [H51]. Thieu refused to join the talks on the terms agreed [H51]. Through Anna Chennault the Nixon campaign had encouraged him to wait for a better deal, and on 2 November she told the South Vietnamese ambassador to "hold on a while longer"; Johnson learned of it through intercepts and ordered the campaign watched, but did not make it public [H51]. Historians disagree on whether the channel changed anything, since Thieu had his own reasons to refuse [H51]. On 5 November Richard M. Nixon was elected president by a narrow popular margin [H]. Talks in Paris did not resume in substance until the new year, after a dispute over the shape of the table was settled with a round table for the governments [H51].

Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder had flown 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine attack sorties [H9], and lost 506 USAF, 397 Navy and 19 Marine aircraft over or near the North [H9]. It ended without having achieved its purposes of breaking Hanoi's will or stopping the flow of men and supplies south.

## EV000-54 · The Accelerated Pacification Campaign and Phoenix · 1 Nov 1968 – Jan 1969 · The countryside of South Vietnam · HIST · 000
Phung Hoang, the Vietnamese programme to coordinate the attack on the VC infrastructure, had been established by decree in December 1967 out of the American ICEX programme of June 1967, and Thieu put his own decree behind it in July 1968 [H52]. Its instruments were district intelligence centres, the CIA-run Provincial Reconnaissance Units and the police; its method was to identify, capture, turn or kill the cadres of the parallel administration [H52]. On 1 November 1968, at the urging of Komer and his successor William E. Colby, the government launched the Accelerated Pacification Campaign, a three-month drive to raise about 1,000 contested hamlets to relative security before any ceasefire could freeze the map, using RF and PF, Revolutionary Development cadre and the new People's Self-Defence Force, with regular forces screening [H].

The campaign met its targets by the Hamlet Evaluation System, a scale filled in by American district advisers under pressure to show progress (A13), and in many places those hamlets did become more secure after dark, because the VC local forces that had protected the infrastructure had died in the offensives [H]. Phoenix's own figures, 81,740 people "neutralized" between 1968 and 1972, 26,369 of them killed, were produced against monthly quotas that encouraged false arrests and padding, and about 87 percent of the killings credited to it were by ordinary military operations [H52]. Communist cadres later described 1968–72 as their hardest period, and some American advisers concluded that Phoenix had been a mortal threat in some provinces and a paper programme in others [H52].

## EV000-55 · Commando Hunt begins · 15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · HIST · 000
On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads [H53]. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night [H53]. Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons [H53]. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000 [H53]. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it.

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent [H40].

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.
