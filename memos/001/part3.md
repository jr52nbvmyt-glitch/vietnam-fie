## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I inherited on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war being lost, two months after the coup that killed Diem, with an administration gutted and a countryside in which the government's arrival meant the return of the landlord. On that day the Military Revolutionary Council promulgated two decree-laws, drafted with the US Mission: one ended rent on tenanted rice land, made the tiller the owner and confirmed to him whatever the Viet Minh or the Front had given him; the other created an independent Commission Against Corruption with a Special Court, a muster service and an American power to suspend aid. Neither touched the enemy's main force. Both were aimed at the question the war was being fought over, whose government the peasant would accept, and that is the thread this history follows. Where it bends the war and where the war runs straight past it are both part of the record.

**1964–65: the reform behind the security line.** The first year is the story of a reform discovering its limit. Survey aircraft photographed the Delta from January; the first 40 titling teams took the field in March and 70 by July, each behind a Regional Forces company, and the first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen in April [X-001]. The teams could have titled faster than the provinces could escort them, and they titled where the government held the hamlets at night: the Hoa Hao country, the riverine districts along the Bassac, the inner ring of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa that Hop Tac half-secured, and the coastal plain of Quang Nam. Where they went further, the enemy taught them the rule. At Can Giuoc in June and July 1964 the Front's security sections read out the names posted in the village offices and killed 37 title holders and 9 committee members [X-001]. A COSVN directive captured in August told the cadres not to obstruct the titling but to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper" and keep paying the Front's tax [X-001]. From then on the land programme was exactly as valuable, in any hamlet, as the security that stayed there after dark, and its headline figure, titles issued, measured the government's paperwork and not the peasant's allegiance.

The Commission's first year ran on the same principle. The Pay and Muster Verification Service mustered units at dawn from April and struck about 41,000 names from a force carried at about 514,000 by the end of the year [X-001]. It was the first honest count of the army we advised, and in Washington it read as a smaller army in the year the ARVN was losing battalions. The Commission refused to begin at the top, refused Khanh's invitation to charge the Dalat generals without evidence, and built up from battalion and province cases. Its one use of the American lever in these years, the suspension of counterpart funds to Vinh Long in April 1965 after landlords' agents had collected rent under escort, worked because it was used once, on a documented case.

Saigon's coups came on their historical dates. Khanh took power on 30 January, and in September Generals Phat and Duc tried to remove him. The reforms gave both the support of officers who expected a new government to let the decrees lapse, and in September the coup-makers said so on the radio. That made 1964 more dangerous than it was historically, and it was survived because Johnson's note of 2 February had made the reforms a condition of American support, and because the September coup failed within a day. The Young Turks who saved Khanh took up the land decree as their own. When the Thieu–Ky government came to power in June 1965 it inherited the reforms as its faction's cause rather than as an American imposition.

Hanoi's course did not bend. The Ninth Plenum had decided in December 1963, before the decree, to seek victory before the Americans came, and the PAVN's regular regiments came down the Trail in the autumn of 1964 on the schedule their training and the Trail allowed. The decree gave Le Duan a second argument for haste, that the base would erode if victory waited. The main-force war of 1965, Binh Gia, Song Be, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, ran as it had run, and in July the President committed American ground forces in strength on the historical date. By then about 330,000 hectares had been titled to about 270,000 families, and in the districts the government held the rent stop had held [X-001]. Where it did not hold the night, nothing had changed but the promise.

**1965–67: the war beneath the main-force war.** The American divisions fought the war of the borders and the Highlands, from the Ia Drang to Junction City and Dak To, and none of it changed. The provisions worked in the part of the country the main-force war did not reach. As the American units took the main-force battle, the ARVN divisions of the Delta were turned to territorial security, and the titling followed them. GOLDEN SHEAF, in the western Delta in the first half of 1966, was the first operation of the war planned around the land rather than around an enemy unit: the 21st Division cleared blocks of villages along the Xa No canal by day, RF companies stayed in the hamlets at night, the titling teams followed, and each titled hamlet raised its own PF platoon from the families who had just received their land. The enemy declined battle and fought only where he could catch an RF company alone. By June about 46,000 hectares had been titled and 74 hamlets were held at night by their own people; the teams stopped not because they had run out of capacity but because the territorial forces could hold no further [X-001]. In those 74 hamlets the Front's tax collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn the villages sold rice through the government's market at Phung Hiep. That is the reform at its best, and the line it stopped at is the line it stopped at for the rest of the war.

Elsewhere the change was quieter. The refugees driven from the Bong Son plain by Masher/White Wing found, when they returned, that their parcels had been held in their names. CORDS was founded on its historical date and inherited the land register, the first household record of the titled hamlets the American pacification effort ever had, and over my written objection took titles issued into the Hamlet Evaluation System as a measure of security. In April 1967 the Special Court convicted the IV Corps deputy for logistics, Brigadier General Khiem, of stealing his troops' rations and fuel; the graft had moved there when the musters took away ghost pay, and the case reached a general because the American co-chair threatened to suspend aid if it was moved to a military court. Thieu, a candidate who needed the Embassy, let it stand. The Commission's reach stopped one rank below the Directory.

In Hanoi the decision for a general offensive was taken on its historical dates by the same men, for the same reasons. COSVN reported that the Delta tax was falling and the "land trick" had not been exposed, and that argued for haste; its Military Region 9 appreciation said the government was not unpopular in the Delta, and was overruled. By the end of 1967 about 820,000 hectares had been titled to about 640,000 families [X-001]. The Front was smaller in the Delta and intact in every hamlet the government did not hold at night.

**1968: Tet.** The General Offensive struck on the historical night, and the year turned as it turned historically: there was no uprising, the Front's local forces and cadres were destroyed, and in Washington the scale of the offensive broke the President's policy. None of that changed. In the western Delta it went differently. At Can Tho the attacking battalions marched through titled villages whose PF platoons reported them by radio, and two fought; the town was cleared in about four days, and Vinh Long in about three [X-001]. At Ben Tre, where the government had titled little because it held little, the town was destroyed as it was historically. Front recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968 was about 11,000 rather than 16,000, and COSVN began filling its Delta battalions with northerners months earlier than historically [X-001]. The reform that had confirmed the Front's own land gift ended by making the Delta's war a northern army's war among southern landowners. When the government and CORDS moved into the emptied countryside in November, the Commission audited Phoenix against the land register in 40 districts and found a third of the claimed neutralizations unsupported [X-001]. The precaution forced on the land programme by the murders at Can Giuoc became, four years later, the check on false arrests in the war's most abused programme.

The clock did not move. US deaths by hostile action were 14,592 in 1968 [H40], incurred at Khe Sanh, Hue, Saigon and in I Corps, beyond the reach of land titles and audits. The faster clearing of Can Tho was invisible from Washington.

**1969–71: the best years, earlier.** The Vietnamization years were historically the government's best in the countryside, and here they began from a higher base. The Front's Resolution 9 of July 1969 told the Delta's committees to stop contesting titles and to treat a titled, night-held hamlet as lost for the present [X-001]. In March 1970 there was no Land to the Tiller law, because there was nothing left for it to do; Thieu signed instead a decree converting provisional titles to final and extending the programme to the communal lands. By the end of 1971 about 1,150,000 hectares had been titled [X-001], against 1,007,217 by March 1973 under the historical law [S3].

The Commission did not last as well. The American lever that had protected it was not used after 1969, because the Mission was managing a withdrawal. When the Commission's import-licence inquiry reached the palace in May 1971, Thieu placed it under a presidential inspectorate, and the Embassy protested in writing and did nothing more. The Commission's work below the top went on; its reach above it ended. Thieu ran alone in October, with a claim to have given the peasants their land seven years before and a record of having shut down his own inspector the same year. On the frontiers the war was the historical one: Menu, the Cambodian incursion, Lam Son 719, and a Politburo that decided after Laos to stake 1972 on invasion.

**1972–75: the end the provisions could not reach.** The Easter Offensive was a conventional invasion on three fronts, at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc, decided by armour, B-52s, American advisers and ARVN leadership [H64]. The Delta was not one of its fronts, and by the end of 1972 the historical course had closed most of the countryside gap this war had opened. The Paris Agreement left the PAVN in the South; Congress ended American combat and cut aid; Thieu's order to abandon the Highlands turned the defeat at Ban Me Thuot into a rout. A titled peasantry in the Delta did not stop the PAVN corps at Phuoc Long, Ban Me Thuot or Xuan Loc, and a Commission curbed in 1971 could not restore the fuel and ammunition the ARVN lacked in 1974. The Republic of Vietnam fell on 30 April 1975, on the historical timeline.

**Where the line bends and where it holds.** The provisions bent the war where it was a war for the population, in the rice districts of the Delta and the coastal plain of the Centre, and nowhere else. The land reform came six years early and did what the historical reform did, before the main-force war rather than after the Front's base had already been gutted. The Commission counted the army honestly, convicted a general and audited Phoenix, and lasted exactly as long as an American lever stood behind it. Neither changed a single battle between armies, the American clock or the end. That is the lesson of this memorandum: the government's standing in the countryside was the binding deficit on the opening date, and moving it was necessary. It was not sufficient. A South with more to hold still had to be able to hold it, against a conventional army, after the Americans had gone.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

Phase I changes in the countryside and in the coup politics of Saigon, and not on the battlefield. The land decree and the Commission arrive on the same day, and the first eighteen months prove that each is the other's precondition: the land programme gives the Commission its first cases, cheap and documentary (a fee demanded, an illegal escort, a false rent claim), and the Commission guards the land programme against the officials who would have turned it into a new racket (F001-1). The Aid Compliance Board uses its lever against a province once in the phase, at Vinh Long in April 1965, and it works because it is used once, on a documented case, after thirty days' notice [X-001].

The titling moves at the rate of the security line, not the rate of the apparatus. The teams could title 40,000–60,000 hectares a month if escorted [M001]; they title about 210,000 hectares in the last nine and a half months of 1964 and about 330,000 by the end of July 1965 [X-001]. One Regional Forces company per team is all the provinces can spare, and the teams stop where the government stops holding the hamlets after dark. Can Giuoc is where that edge was found, at the cost of 46 lives, and it is narrated at length in the chronicle because it shows what the reform did not change [X-001]. The enemy did not obstruct the titling. He let the peasants take the paper, and he killed the people whose names were posted where the government could not protect them (F001-8).

The strongest positive effect is real in this phase and small. By the end of July 1965 about 270,000 families hold titles, and in the districts the government holds, a government official no longer arrives with the landlord's agent behind him [X-001]. It changes no battle. Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are lost as they were lost historically, because they were decided by VC and PAVN regiments against ARVN battalions in Quang Ngai and Phuoc Long, main-force battles in which titles played no part (F001-17).

The reforms also made 1964 more dangerous, and that is why they needed an American guarantee. Khanh's coup of 30 January and the Phat–Duc attempt of 13 September both drew on officers who expected a new government to let the decrees lapse, and in September the coup-makers said so on the radio (F001-16). Johnson's note of 2 February and the American co-chair's refusal to let Khanh remove the Commissioner made the reforms a condition of American support [X-001]. The coups happened on their historical dates; what they were for changed, and the September failure handed the reforms to the faction that won.

The musters are the phase's other new fact. About 41,000 names are struck from a force carried at about 514,000 by the end of 1964 [X-001]. That is not a smaller army; it is the same army counted honestly. In Washington it reads as a smaller army, in the months when the ARVN is losing battalions at Binh Gia and Ba Gia (F001-9).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's reaction to the land decree was quick, cheap and correct. The Can Giuoc killings of June–July 1964 were the district committee's own initiative; the COSVN directive captured near Duc Hoa in August, telling cadres to let the peasants take titles, keep collecting the agricultural tax and make examples only of informers, was the movement's settled answer [X-001]. It cost the Front little in the hamlets it held at night and conceded the ones it did not (F001-10). Liberation Radio's month of broadcasts about the "American masters" who dismissed the Vinh Long province chief showed that the Front also understood the Commission's weak point, which was the American hand on its lever.

<!-- initiative --> The decisive enemy decision of the phase was not a response to anything this headquarters did. The Ninth Plenum of December 1963 had resolved to seek decisive victory before the United States intervened in strength, and through 1964 Le Duan's Politburo acted on it, sending the first complete PAVN regular regiments south in the autumn: the 95th Regiment of the 325th Division reached Kontum in December 1964 and the 101st followed in February 1965 [H22]. It was driven by the factional victory of the militants at the Plenum, by a reading of Saigon's chaos after Diem's fall, and by the fear that the Americans would come; it was fixed before the land decree existed. The decree added a second argument to Le Duan's case, that the base would erode if victory waited, and did not change the date, the formations or the route by which they came (F001-12) [X-001].

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a land programme that has found its rate and its limit, about 330,000 hectares titled and a security line it cannot cross without the Regional and Popular Forces [X-001]. It inherits a Commission that has convicted at battalion and province level and never above, by its own choice; a coup politics in which the reforms belong to the Young Turks; and an enemy in the Delta that has stopped contesting titles and kept its tax. Phase III inherits the register of parcel numbers that Can Giuoc forced on the programme, which becomes in 1968 a household record (C001-4). The Land to the Tiller law of 1970 is already, in July 1965, a third done [X-001].

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is when the land programme does most of its work, and the main-force war does not notice. From about 330,000 hectares titled in July 1965 the programme reaches about 820,000 by the end of 1967, about 640,000 families [X-001]. The growth comes not from new teams but from new security. The American divisions that arrive from the summer of 1965 fight on the borders and in the Highlands, the ARVN divisions they relieve of the main-force battle in the Delta are turned to territorial security, and the titling follows them. GOLDEN SHEAF is that mechanism in a single operation, and it shows the rule that governs the programme for the rest of the war: the bottleneck has moved from the government's offer to its delivery. In June 1966 the teams were idle because the territorial forces could hold no further (F001-4) [X-001].

GOLDEN SHEAF is also where the strongest positive effect can be seen on the ground, in a named place on named dates. In 74 hamlets along the Xa No canal the families who received titles in the spring of 1966 formed their own PF platoons, the Front's tax collectors stopped coming, and by the autumn those villages were selling rice through the government's market at Phung Hiep [X-001]. That is a government arriving as a guarantee rather than a threat (F001-17). It is also exactly bounded. The other 38 hamlets of the operation were held by day only, and their titles stayed in the district office [X-001].

The Commission's phase turns on one trial. Its conviction of Brigadier General Khiem in April 1967 rested on three things this memorandum predicted. The musters had driven graft from ghost pay to rations and fuel, where the auditors could follow it; the Commission had built up from documentary cases rather than beginning at the top; and the Board's lever, threatened rather than used, stopped the case being moved to a military court [X-001]. It was also bounded. Thieu let it stand because he was a candidate who needed the Embassy, and the Commission's reach stopped at the rank below the Directory (F001-3).

Two historical events run on their historical lines and mean something different. The An Lao valley's 4,500 refugees still left in 1966, but the Bong Son plain's returning families found their parcels held for them, and the Front lost its best sentence in Binh Dinh [X-001]. CORDS inherited the land register and, over this headquarters' written objection, took titles issued into the Hamlet Evaluation System as a measure of security (F001-10). That is the phase's most important mistake, and it was made because the figure was available.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy in the Delta declined battle where the titling came behind regular troops, and fought where he could catch an RF company alone at night, as at kilometre 28 on the night of 22–23 March 1966 [X-001]. Waiting and coercion were cheap; competing for the peasant's loyalty on the government's terms was expensive; and the Front chose the cheap course (F001-13). Its cost was that the Front's tax and recruitment base in the titled districts shrank, and COSVN reported that honestly to Hanoi.

Hanoi's decision of 1967 for a general offensive is the historical one, taken for its historical reasons: the stalemate, the militants' victory in the Revisionist Anti-Party Affair, and a reading of the South that was wrong. The provisions added urgency and made the reading more wrong in the Delta, where COSVN's Military Region 9 appreciation said the government was not unpopular and was overruled [X-001]. In effect the reform strengthened the militants' argument for haste and weakened the premise on which the haste was to pay off (F001-12).

### Causal chains

The chains that begin in this phase land later: the confirmation of the Front's land gifts and the register's use for Phoenix in Phase III, and the Commission's reach toward the top in Phase IV.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits about 820,000 titled hectares, a Front in the Delta with a smaller base and an urgent leadership, a register of titled households in CORDS's hands, and a Hamlet Evaluation System that counts titles as security [X-001]. Phase IV inherits a Commission that has convicted a general with the American lever behind it and will need the lever again the first time it reaches higher. The Masher refugees' parcels, held in their names, are waiting for them when the camps empty.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet is the hinge of the historical war, and the provisions do not move the hinge. The offensive is planned and launched on the same dates with the same forces; Saigon, Hue and Khe Sanh are fought as they were fought; and the American decision of 31 March is taken for the same reasons. What changes is the western Delta. At Can Tho the attackers marched through titled villages whose PF platoons reported them by radio and in two cases fought them, and the town was cleared in about four days with far less of it destroyed; Vinh Long was cleared in about three [X-001]. Ben Tre, where the government held little outside the town and had titled little, was destroyed as it was historically. The line of difference is the line of night security the land programme followed since 1964. It is the clearest sign in the war that the government's arrival as a guarantee had become something the enemy's own soldiers met on the ground (F001-17).

The second change is in the arithmetic of the Front's manpower. Mekong lowlands recruitment in 1968 is about 11,000 against 16,000 on the historical course, because about a third of the families who would have supplied the recruits held their land from the government in districts it held at night [X-001]. The third is in Phoenix. The district intelligence centres start from a register compiled for land, not for a quota, and the Commission's audit of 40 districts finds about a third of claimed neutralizations unsupported [X-001]. In the sampled districts padding falls and false arrests become cases before the Special Court (F001-1).

### Enemy response and initiative

The General Offensive was Hanoi's initiative, and the provisions did not provoke it beyond adding to the haste. COSVN's answer to the Delta's shrinking recruitment was to fill its Delta main-force battalions with northern replacements from the summer of 1968, months earlier than historically [X-001]. That kept the battalions in the field and changed what they were: northern soldiers among Delta peasants who now owned their land. The Front's propaganda turned on the one feature of the reform that had become a weapon against it, the use of the land register as a list of persons for Phoenix, and said so; this headquarters judged the charge partly true [X-001].

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits a Delta in which the Front's base was smaller before Tet and is gutted after it, as historically, but from a lower level and with its main forces already more northern [X-001]. It inherits a Phoenix programme with an outside auditor in 40 districts, and about 900,000 titled hectares, so that the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 has little left to do [X-001]. The American calendar it inherits is the historical one.

### Clock

The provisions do not move the American clock in 1968. US deaths by hostile action are 14,592 in the year on the historical course [H40], and nothing here changes the battles in which they were incurred: Khe Sanh, Hue, Saigon and the I Corps fighting were beyond the reach of land titles and audits. The appearance of stalemate that broke the President's policy in March came from the scale and surprise of the offensive in the cities and its distance from the progress campaign of 1967, which in this war is the same distance. The faster clearing of Can Tho was not visible from Washington [X-001]. If anything, the Hamlet Evaluation System's inclusion of titles issued made the progress campaign's figures a little more flattering and the shock a little worse. That is the old warning about reported progress that proves false, in a small form (POSTNA-5) [E: titles carried half the weight Komer proposed, and the HES was one figure among several]. The Commission's audit of Phoenix from December is the opposite effect, a measure that would have made reported progress more believable if it had been published. In 1968 it was not.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

Phase IV is where the historical war and this one look most alike on the surface and differ most underneath. The Land to the Tiller law of 26 March 1970 does not happen. The decree of 1964 did its work six years earlier, and the act of March 1970 is a smaller one, converting provisional titles to final and extending the programme to about 200,000 hectares of communal land [X-001]. By the end of 1971 about 1,150,000 hectares are titled [X-001], against 1,007,217 hectares by March 1973 under the historical law [S3]. The years 1969–71, historically the government's best in the countryside, begin here from a base the historical government reached only at their end (F001-17).

The Front's own reading confirms it. COSVN Resolution 9 of July 1969 tells the Delta's committees to stop contesting titles and to treat a titled, night-held hamlet as lost for the present [X-001]. That is the enemy admitting, in his own instruction, the limit of letting the peasants take the paper and keep paying: it worked only where the government did not stay (F001-10).

The Commission's phase ends in its curbing. Its inquiry into import licences reaches the palace in May 1971; the Aid Compliance Board's lever, unused since 1969 as the Mission manages a withdrawal, is not invoked; and Thieu places the Commission under a presidential inspectorate in July [X-001]. This is the American guarantee of 1964 run backwards. It had made the reforms a condition of support, and when support became a withdrawal the condition lapsed (F001-15). The Commission's work below the top, the musters, pay at the parade, land court cases and the Phoenix audits, went on [X-001].

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's adaptations in this phase were his own and largely historical. Resolution 9's return to protracted war was driven by the losses of 1968; its Delta instruction was a response to the land programme and was realistic. In Hanoi the decision after Lam Son 719 to stake 1972 on a conventional invasion was taken for Hanoi's reasons, the American withdrawal, the American election and the ARVN's failure in Laos, and the provisions did nothing to change it [X-001]. The enemy also gained from the provisions' end. The subordination of the Commission in the year of Thieu's single-candidate election gave the Front's propaganda, and the non-communist opposition, a fact to point to (EV000-73).

### Causal chains

**C001-5** [war-shaping] The Pay and Muster Verification Service pays IV Corps at the parade from August 1964 (EV001-4) [X-001] → graft moves from ghost pay to rations and fuel (F001-3) → the Service's ration audit, matching draws against mustered strength, convicts the IV Corps deputy for logistics in April 1967 with the Board's lever behind the case (EV001-7) → the Commission's proven ability to reach a general is the reason Thieu subordinates it in July 1971, the first time it reaches toward his own staff and the first year the American lever is not there to protect it (EV001-8) [X-001].

**C001-6** [months] The Commission and the land programme are founded on the same day and guard each other (EV001-1, F001-1) → by 1971 the land programme's work is done and its constituency of about 800,000 titled families has no further claim on the Commission [X-001] → the Commission's remaining cases are against officials, importers and the palace, and it has no peasant constituency left to defend it → when the licence inquiry of May 1971 reaches the palace, the Commission is subordinated with no one in the countryside objecting, because the programme that had made it popular had succeeded.

### Downstream

Phase V inherits a countryside the government holds more securely than it held it historically at the end of 1971, a Front in the Delta that has conceded the land issue and fills its main forces with northerners, and a Commission that works below the top and not above [X-001]. It also inherits the historical frontier war unchanged: a rebuilt PAVN, a Trail extended through Laos and Cambodia, and a Politburo decision to invade in 1972. Phase VI inherits a government that can claim to have given the peasants their land seven years before its last election, and a government that shut down its own inspector the same year.

### Clock

The provisions' effect on American political tolerance in this phase is small and mixed. Casualties fall on the historical schedule, from about 9,414 in 1969 to 1,380 in 1971 [H40], because the withdrawal's pace was set in Washington and not by the countryside. The Khiem conviction of 1967 and the Phoenix audits gave the administration a few true stories of a cleaner ally to tell (F001-14) [X-001]. They were outweighed by the curbing of the Commission in July 1971, which the American press reported in the months of the Pentagon Papers and the single-candidate election as one more sign that Saigon would not reform, and which cost the drawdown a little of the support it had in Congress for continued aid [X-001]. The net effect on the appropriation cycle of 1972–74 is below one band, and I record it as none [E: the curbing was one story among many in 1971, and the aid votes of 1973–74 turned on Watergate, the Paris Agreement and the end of the draft].

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- null P5 --> <!-- ripple-addressed -->
Phase IV ends with legitimacy, night control and the attack on the infrastructure each one band better and the VCI one band weaker. None of that reaches 1972. The Easter Offensive was a conventional invasion by about fourteen PAVN divisions on three fronts, decided at Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc by armour, B-52s, American advisers and ARVN leadership [H64]; the Delta, where the provisions bit hardest, was not one of its fronts. By the end of 1972 the historical course had closed most of the gap: the 1970 law had titled about a million hectares by March 1973 [S3], and the margin in the countryside falls below one band.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- null P6 -->
The provisions do not alter the end. The Republic fell in 1975 because the Paris Agreement left the PAVN in the South, Congress ended American combat and cut aid, and Thieu's order to abandon the Highlands turned Ban Me Thuot into a rout [H]. A titled peasantry in the Delta did not stop PAVN corps at Phuoc Long, Ban Me Thuot or Xuan Loc, and the Commission curbed in 1971 did not restore the ARVN's fuel and ammunition in 1974. The war ends on 30 April 1975.

## §4.H Historical comparison

The provisions move the land reform of the Republic of Vietnam six years earlier and make it more complete. On the historical course the Land to the Tiller law of March 1970 titled 1,007,217 hectares by March 1973 [S3] and about 1,198,000 hectares by the end of 1973 [S4]. In this war about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001], and the 1970 law never happens (EV000-65). More important than the hectares is the date: about 270,000 families hold titles by the July decision of 1965 that commits American ground forces in strength, about 640,000 by the end of 1967 and about 800,000 by March 1970 [X-001]. The land issue, the Front's strongest appeal in the Delta, is taken from it before the main-force war begins rather than after the Front's southern base had already been gutted in 1968.

Concretely, against history: the battle of Can Tho at Tet is shorter, about four days, and Vinh Long about three, with less of either town destroyed [X-001]. Front recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968 is about 11,000 rather than 16,000 [X-001], and COSVN's Delta battalions fill with northerners months earlier. A new operation, GOLDEN SHEAF, adds about 46,000 hectares and 74 night-held hamlets along the Xa No canal at a cost of about 214 RVNAF dead [X-001]. The An Lao and Bong Son refugees of 1966 return to parcels held in their names. Phoenix has an outside auditor in 40 districts from December 1968, which finds a third of its claims unsupported [X-001].

On the Commission's side: about 41,000 ghost names are struck from the RVNAF's rolls in 1964, about 8% of its carried strength [X-001]; a general officer is convicted by an independent court of stealing from his troops' rations in 1967 [X-001]; and a body exists from 1964 to 1971 with the standing to audit the government's own figures. It is curbed in 1971, when the American lever lapses with the drawdown.

The margin is substantial where the war was fought over the population: the Delta's rice districts and the coastal plain of the Centre. It is zero where the war was fought between armies: the Highlands, the DMZ, the border base areas, Hue, Khe Sanh, and every battle of 1972 and 1975. No city that fell historically is held; no offensive is defeated that was not defeated historically; no month is gained in the end.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state is unchanged at rung (a): the Republic of Vietnam falls on 30 April 1975 on the historical timeline. The provisions do not make rung (b) reachable, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of degree.

The assessment on the opening date said that the binding deficit was political, and that no provision leaving the government's standing in the countryside unchanged could move the end state above rung (b) (POSTNA-1). This memorandum is the first to change that standing, and it changes it by a band through 1971 [X-001]. But that judgment named a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. The war was ended by a conventional invasion in 1975 against an army stripped of American air power and short of fuel and ammunition, after a peace agreement that left the PAVN in the South. A peasantry that owns its land and is less afraid of the government's arrival does not stop a PAVN corps, and the Commission, curbed in 1971, could not repair a supply system cut by Congress in 1974. The provisions move capability inside the rung: a countryside held more cheaply, a Front in the Delta smaller and more northern, a measure of honest counting in the RVNAF's rolls. They make a later provision aimed at the 1973–75 military balance more likely to matter, because it would defend a South that has more to hold. By themselves they do not move the end.

No redraw of the phases is proposed. The war's shape is unchanged; it has a different interior.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- The land reform arrives six years early: about 1,150,000 hectares titled by the end of 1971, against 1,007,217 by March 1973 historically [S3], and about 270,000 families titled before American ground forces arrive in strength [X-001]. The Front's strongest appeal in the Delta is taken from it before the main-force war, not after.
- Where the government holds the night, its arrival is a guarantee rather than a threat. GOLDEN SHEAF's 74 night-held hamlets, and Can Tho's warning at Tet, are that change on the ground [X-001].
- The Front's Delta base is smaller: 1968 recruitment in the Mekong lowlands about 11,000 instead of 16,000, COSVN's Delta battalions northern sooner, and a 1969 resolution that concedes the land issue [X-001].
- The RVNAF is counted honestly for the first time: about 41,000 ghost names struck in 1964, pay at the parade, and a general convicted in 1967 for stealing his troops' rations [X-001].
- Phoenix has an outside auditor from December 1968, working from a register compiled for land, and a third of its claims are shown to be unsupported [X-001].
- The 1970 Land to the Tiller law does not happen, because it has nothing left to do.
- The reforms raise the stakes of Saigon's coups in 1964 and give Hanoi a reason to hurry; both were survived because of an American guarantee.

### What has not changed

- The main-force war on the borders, in the Highlands and along the DMZ runs exactly as historically; no battle from the Ia Drang to Dak To changes.
- Tet is decided in Saigon, Hue, Khe Sanh and Washington as it was; the American clock is not moved in any phase.
- Ben Tre and every district the government did not hold at night are unchanged: titles there stayed in district offices and the Front kept its tax.
- The Commission's reach stopped at the rank below the head of government, and in 1971, when the American lever lapsed with the drawdown, at the rank below minister.
- The Easter Offensive and the fall of the Republic in 1975 were decided by PAVN conventional force, American aid and Saigon's command errors, none of which these provisions touched.
- Titles issued entered the Hamlet Evaluation System as a measure of security, over this headquarters' objection, and flattered the progress campaign.
