## P1 · Overview

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964 [H40], none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year [H40]. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon; at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve and its regiments in set-piece battles, and at Song Be the government holds a province capital only narrowly and with American firepower. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

Five things in this phase are new, and all of them begin on its first day. Neither the coups in Saigon nor the summer offensive turns on any of them.

The first two belong to the Republic itself. On 1 January 1964 the junta decrees land to the tiller, confirming by name what the Viet Minh and the Front had already given, and an independent Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it (EV001-1); the same session gives the Republic a resident rural police with a Special Branch, and province and district chiefs chosen by examination from a ranked list (EV002-1). Khanh's coup tests both within a month and both survive it (EV000-1). By the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the musters have shown the RVNAF to be about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls [X-001]; about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the Front has killed about 410 constables, nearly all in posts settled ahead of night security (EV002-2) [X-002]. The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

The third is what this command can see. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks fly from January 1964. The coastal picket closes the trawler route within a year, and Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months before it did historically (EV003-1, EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats near the destroyers, Pierce Arrow is not flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the real attack of 2 August alone (EV000-3).

The fourth is in the hands of the men who fight: modern carbines for the whole RVNAF and the territorials, and a belt-fed gun and a marksman in every regular squad (EV004-1). In the held belt a post with its Popular Forces platoon now holds about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2). Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy still chooses where and when to fight, but they are lost at a smaller price and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out (EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18). The outposts that fall give the Front the same carbines.

The fifth waits on Guam: B-52Js carrying GPS-guided bombs and, from March 1964, a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut that measures the U-2S's imagery into targets for them (EV005-1). Washington refuses every request to use them over the North (EV000-15), and the first Arc Light strike of June 1965 flies without tankers and finds as little as history's did (EV000-20).

A sixth thing begins on the same first day and does not show until the second year. The decree of 1 January 1964 puts a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch and orders a coordinating centre in every province and district, built on the posts and files the police decree creates; the centres are staffed by the Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the sector S2, forbidden quotas, held to warrants under the police statute, and audited by the Commission (EV006-1). About 100 district centres are open by the end of 1964 and all of them by the end of 1965. In the same month the Army begins to select its field advisers by board, to train them for eight months in Vietnamese and in advising, and to keep them eighteen months; the first of them reach the subsectors and battalions in October. In March 1965 the centre at Hoc Mon breaks the district committee above the hamlets the police had already cleared, and the Front learns that leaving a village no longer puts its leaders out of reach (EV006-2).

A seventh thing began before the first day, and it is the only one that reaches back past it. Since 1957 every new officer of the ARVN, and every new American officer, has passed a branch course after commissioning and the courses of the 2026 continuum before each promotion, so that on 1 January 1964 seven such classes, about 10,000–14,000 ARVN officers, hold most of the platoons and companies of the army [M007]. The session of 1 January gives that army an Officer Selection and Promotion Board with written criteria, a rated evaluation report, central command lists for battalions and regiments, relief for cause, a path to commission for proven sergeants, and a ranked list from which generals and division commanders must be chosen, behind an American aid condition (EV007-1). Khanh's coup tests it in its first month (EV000-1). Its effect in this phase is in the companies, where the educated officers already stand, and in the first battalions the board fills; Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy chose them and the generals planned the relief (EV000-17, EV000-18).

## EV007-1 · The Officer Selection Board and the first board season · 1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Joint General Staff at Tan Son Nhut; the Thu Duc Reserve Officers School and the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The officer decree was the fourth signed in the session of 1 January 1964, and the generals who signed it understood it least. It made every promotion from first lieutenant to colonel, and every command of a battalion or regiment, the decision of an Officer Selection and Promotion Board under the Joint General Staff, sitting in panels of five senior officers on written criteria; it required that every officer be rated twice a year by his commander and by his commander's commander on a form that allowed no more than a third of any senior rater's officers in the top block; it gave the board the power to relieve a commander for cause after a thirty-day review, opened a commission to sergeants with a combat record and a recommendation, abolished the ninth-grade diploma as a bar to the rank of major, and required that the President choose each new general and each division commander from a ranked list of three [M007]. Article 14, the one this headquarters and the US Mission fought for, put the general-officer list under the Aid Compliance Board's condition: a division whose commander was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds for its operations [M007].

The board's first chairman was Brigadier General Phan Khac Minh, a French-trained infantry officer of fifty-one who had run the personnel directorate of the General Staff in 1955–57 and been sent to command a training camp by Diem for refusing to promote the Can Lao's men ahead of the list [X-007]. Beside the board sat an American cell of about 40 under Colonel Robert E. Lindqvist, who had built the evaluation system's trainers' course at Fort Benning and had never served in Asia; his first report to this headquarters said that the hard part of the reform was not the form but persuading a colonel to give a nephew a middle rating, and that he had no idea how it would be done [X-007].

The board inherited the officers that seven years of the education had made. The classes of 1957–63 had passed the branch basic course after Thu Duc or Dalat, and the first four of them the career course as well, so that in January 1964 most of the ARVN's rifle companies and many of its artillery batteries were commanded by lieutenants and captains who had been taught to make a fire plan, to scout a route, to run a range and to write an order, in Vietnamese, to the American standard of 2026 [M007]. Above them the army was what it had been: about two-thirds of the battalion commanders had been appointed by a corps or division commander for reasons of his own, about a third of the battalions were commanded by captains because there were not enough majors, and the generals were the generals of the November coup [X-007]. One of the captains was Bui Quang Trieu, commissioned from Thu Duc in 1957 in the first educated class, who had commanded a company of the 5th Division's 9th Regiment for three years and was rated in the top block by both his raters in the first season [X-007].

The first season ran from April to September. The panels read about 9,000 evaluation reports, promoted about 6,500 officers, nearly all of them on the old seniority with the new reports beside it, and published on 30 June the army's first central command list: 61 battalion commands and 9 regimental commands, about a third of those falling vacant in the year [X-007]. The corps commanders were asked to nominate and did; about half of their nominees were selected, and the rest of the list came from officers no corps commander had put forward [X-007]. The career-course gate held the majors' grade to the officers already through the course, and the shortage of majors that the historical army met with captains in command was met here with captains as well, for another year and a half (F007-8) [X-007]. The schools took their price at the same time: the career course at Thu Duc and the staff course that opened at Dalat in September drew about one officer in fifteen out of the units and about 120 of the army's best captains and majors as instructors, in the year the summer offensive was being prepared (F007-10) [X-007].

The coups tested the board twice before the year ended. Khanh's coup came thirty days after the decree (EV000-1). In September, when Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc moved on Saigon, two of the battalions they brought were commanded by officers the board had refused for the June list, and none by an officer it had chosen [X-007]. Minh told Lindqvist that he had not expected the list to show its worth so soon, and that he expected the generals to have noticed as well [X-007].

## EV000-1 · Khanh's coup · 30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,CP01
*Initiative: neither*

Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh without firing a shot [H8]. He acted with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau [H8]. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were put under house arrest on a charge of plotting a French-backed neutralist settlement, and Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho was detained [H8]. The one death was Major Nguyen Van Nhung, Minh's aide, executed after the takeover [H8]. Khanh kept Minh as a figurehead chief of state [H8]. The generals were tried in closed session on 28–29 May and found guilty only of "lax morality" [H8].

The coup was not caused by the decrees of 1 January; it was the product of the generals' rivalries and Khanh's ambition, and it came on the historical date [X-001]. But the reforms shaped what followed. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay gave Khanh their quiet support in the hope of a government that would let the decrees lapse (F001-16) [X-001]. In February Khanh asked Commissioner Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals; Khoa replied that the Commission had no evidence against them and would bring no case without it [X-001]. Khanh moved to replace him, and the American co-chair of the Aid Compliance Board refused the concurrence the decree required [X-001]. The generals went before a military court instead, on the "lax morality" charge of the historical record.

Washington was surprised but quick to back the new man; President Johnson sent Khanh a personal note on 2 February [H8], which in this war carried the condition, at the urging of the US Mission and this headquarters, that the United States would support no government that repealed either decree (F001-15) [X-001]. Khanh, who needed American backing more than he needed the landlords, accepted it and kept both. The coup mattered less for what Khanh did than for the precedent it set: a second violent change of government within three months, carried out by officers against officers, and accepted by the United States. Khanh moved, as every coup-maker had, to place his men in the provinces, and the decree of 1 January now stood in his way (EV002-1). In March, Secretary of Defense McNamara visited and endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 of 17 March 1964 committed the United States to the survival of an independent, non-communist South Vietnam [H]. In June 1964 General William C. Westmoreland, Harkins's deputy since January, took command of MACV, and General Maxwell D. Taylor replaced Henry Cabot Lodge as ambassador in July [H].

In this war Khanh's coup was the first test of the police and provincial decrees, and it came thirty days after them, before the Board had published a list. On the morning of 30 January Khanh's officers detained the new Director General of National Police, Vo Dinh Cuong, as they had detained the police chief on the historical course, and in the first week of February Khanh named "acting" chiefs in eleven provinces and districts [X-002]. The US Mission and this headquarters treated the two decrees as part of the condition Johnson's note attached to the land and anti-corruption decrees: the Aid Compliance Board gave notice that a province whose chief was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds once the first list was published, and Cuong was released on 2 February and restored to his office (F002-16) [X-002]. Khanh kept the Board, because he had no candidate for its chairmanship and saw a reputation for reform in it, and kept his acting chiefs until the list replaced nine of the eleven in April and May; the other two placed well enough to be appointed from it [X-002]. It was a partial failure of the reform's first test and a partial success, and it set the pattern that held until 1970: the Board survived each government because the government that inherited it found it cheaper to keep than to break.

The officer decree was tested in the same month, and in the same way. On 3 February Khanh's staff sent the Joint General Staff a list of 23 promotions and six regimental commands for officers who had served the coup; the board, which had existed for 34 days and had not yet read a single evaluation report, had no list of its own to set against it [X-007]. The Mission added the officer decree to the condition of Johnson's note, and Minh offered Khanh a bargain that set the pattern for the next six years: the board confirmed the eleven promotions that met its written criteria and refused the twelve that did not, and Khanh's regimental commanders kept their commands until the first command list in June, which confirmed four of the six (EV007-1) [X-007]. The generals were the exception. Until the first ranked list in January 1965 Khanh made the generals he needed, and after it every junta learned to place its partners among the three names rather than outside them. The reform's first lesson to Saigon was that the board could be bargained with and could not be ignored; its first lesson to this headquarters was that the list for division command was exactly as strong as the aid condition behind it (F007-1).

## EV000-17 · The battle of Ba Gia · 28–31 May 1965 · Ba Gia, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

On 29 May 1965 the VC 1st Regiment of the forming 2nd Division (40th, 60th and 90th Battalions, with the 45th Independent Battalion and local force battalions), directed by PAVN Major General Chu Huy Man, overran the outpost at Loc Tho near Ba Gia, about 10 km from Quang Ngai, and ambushed the ARVN 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry Regiment as it came to help [H11]. On the historical course the battalion was destroyed. On this one it was cut in two on the road from Ba Gia and lost its headquarters company and most of one rifle company in the first half hour; the other three companies, each with a squad gun in every squad and a marksman in most, formed a perimeter on the low ridge north of the road and held it until dark, and about 280 of the battalion's men came back to Quang Ngai over the next two days [X-004]. Major General Nguyen Chanh Thi, commanding I Corps, sent a task force under the 51st Regiment: its 2nd Battalion, the 3rd Marine Battalion, the 37th and 39th Ranger Battalions, two 105mm artillery battalions and an M113 squadron [H11]. On 30 May the 39th Rangers fought the VC 45th Battalion on Mount Chop Non, and the 2nd Battalion, 51st and the Marines were hit near Phuoc Loc; US fighter-bombers and UH-1 gunships, delayed because forward air controllers could not find the fight, prevented a rout [H11]. On the night of 30–31 May the 3rd Marines held a moat line through repeated assaults, with nine M249s across the line firing under illumination from the 105s, and their marksmen picking off the 40th Battalion's machine-gun teams setting up in the paddy dikes beyond the moat at 300–400 metres (EC004-6) [X-004]. The 39th Rangers were reduced to about two companies, against one historically [X-004]. The VC withdrew at dawn on 31 May.

I put ARVN losses at about 75 killed, 110 wounded and 190 captured or missing, against the US Army's historical figures of 107 killed, 123 wounded and 367 captured or missing; the individual weapons lost, 384 historically, I put at about 230, most of them M4A1 carbines with their sights [E: the historical return less the 1st Battalion's survivors and the smaller Ranger loss]. About 150 VC bodies were found, against 84 historically, and MACV's estimate of up to 826 VC casualties is no more reliable than it was [X-004]. Ba Gia was still a defeat. The VC had overrun the outpost, broken a battalion and held the field, and by 3 June they controlled much of northern Quang Ngai, because the ARVN pulled its battered task force back to the province capital [H11]. On 5 July they overran Ba Gia again, and the ARVN abandoned the post [H11]. In Washington it was read as evidence that the ARVN was being worn down faster than it could be rebuilt, not, as historically, as proof that a regimental task force could be wiped out; the distinction mattered to the 51st Regiment and not to the July decision. The 230 carbines went to the 2nd Division's local battalions, who were firing 5.56mm taken from the dead within the month (F004-7) [X-004].

The officer reforms changed Ba Gia at its edges and left its centre alone. The 1st Battalion, 51st Regiment was commanded by a major whom the corps had appointed in 1963, and its companies by lieutenants and captains of the classes of 1959–62; it was those company commanders, with the battalion's headquarters gone in the first half hour, who brought three companies onto the ridge north of the road, set the squad guns and called the battalion's two mortars onto the paddy below them [X-007]. Part of what memorandum 004 credited to the squad gun at Ba Gia belongs to the officers who placed it, and I do not count it twice: the losses above stand [X-007]. The 2nd Battalion, 51st had been given in March to Major Le Van Tri, one of the board's first selections, and on 30 May it moved to Phuoc Loc with a company on the high ground on each side of the road, which is why it was hit in the flank rather than in column [X-007]. The decisions that lost Ba Gia were taken above both battalions: the corps sent the task force forward piecemeal into ground the VC 1st Regiment had chosen, and pulled it back to Quang Ngai afterwards, and none of the officers who made those decisions had been through a course or before a board (F007-7).

## EV000-18 · The battle of Dong Xoai · 9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation [H6]. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers [H6]. The CIDG companies and the militia carried the carbines and Garands they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach them; the 111th RF Company and the Americans carried M4A1s (F004-1) [X-004]. The camp was largely overrun during the night, as it was historically, from the CIDG sectors inward, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters, where the 111th Company had fallen back with its carbines, until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out [H6] [X-004]. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overrun, losing about half its men against nearly all of them historically [X-004]; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it in the rubber [H6].

The Airborne fight on 12 June is the clearest measure in this war of what the squad weapons could and could not do. Historically about 150 of the 470 came back. On this course the battalion's lead company was lost in the opening fire as it was historically, but the three companies behind it each turned their squad guns into the trees and held, and about 270 men fought their way to the landing zone at Thuan Loi over the afternoon, bringing about 60 wounded [X-004]. The 271st had chosen the place, the hour and the range, and it won; the Airborne battalion was broken, though not wiped out [X-004]. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had [H6].

I put the allied losses at about 300 South Vietnamese killed, 210 wounded and 150 missing, and 18 Americans killed as historically, against 416 killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing on the historical course; about 300 civilians were killed and 2,500 made refugees, as historically [X-004] [H6]. More than 300 VC bodies were found historically; I put the figure here at about 400, with the same scepticism about the allied estimate of 625 more [X-004]. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured [H6]. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact [H6]. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had about two reserve battalions left, against one historically [X-004]. That was not enough to change Westmoreland's request of 7 June, which rested on the PAVN regiments entering the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and it did not change the answer.

At Dong Xoai the board had not yet reached the 5th Division. The 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment was landed at Thuan Loi on 10 June on a division plan that put it on an open landing zone beside the rubber where the 271st was known to be waiting, under a battalion commander appointed by the division in 1962 [X-007]. The board reviewed the landing in July on its own motion, under the thirty-day rule, and in August relieved the battalion commander and the division operations officer who had written the plan, the first reliefs for cause in the ARVN's history that were not punishments for political disloyalty (EV007-2) [X-007]. The 5th Division's commander was untouched, and remained so, because he had been appointed before the ranked list existed. The night at the camp and the 7th Airborne's fight on 12 June went as told above [H6].

## P1 · How the phase ends

By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though none of the general reserve has been wiped out. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and outside the held belt the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security it needs. Inside it the summer is not the historical one: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's [X-001]. Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

About 1,100 village posts are settled, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure has begun to leave the villages around the posts (EV002-2) [X-002]. The sea route to the coast is closed and the Cambodian route open, and the enemy's rear is on the U-2S's maps while his regiments under the canopy and his cadres in the hamlets are as hidden as before. The general reserve enters Phase II weakened but whole, and the Front enters it with between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines (EV004-1) [X-004]. On Guam the B-52Js hold about 1,400 measured targets in the cell's folders and authority to strike none of them in the North (EV005-1, EV000-15).

Phung Hoang ends the phase with about 140 district centres open, every one behind a settled post, and a method that has reached above the hamlet in one district on the edge of Saigon and in a dozen in the Delta (EV006-1, EV006-2) [X-006]. About 1,100 selected advisers are in the subsectors and battalions, speaking to their counterparts without interpreters; the rest of the field posts are still held on the old tour. The Front has begun to move its district committees out of the held belt, which costs it the villages' daily obedience and gains it the safety of the contested edge (F006-4).

By the end of July 1965 the board has filled about 70 of the army's roughly 190 manoeuvre battalion commands and relieved its first nine commanders; the rest are still held by men the generals put there, and about a third of all battalions are still commanded by captains, because the army is short of majors and the career-course gate makes none faster (EV007-1, F007-8) [X-007]. The companies are led almost everywhere by the classes of 1957–63. The generals are the generals of 1963, and every one of them has learned that the board can be bargained with and cannot be ignored.

## P2 · Overview

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967 [H40]. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed most. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 titled to about 640,000 families [X-001]. GOLDEN SHEAF, its first clearing operation in the western Delta, shows what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of what the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark (EV001-6, F001-4). The Commission survives the Buddhist crisis and the election behind the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 convicts its first general (EV001-7). With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case (EV002-4). The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its cadres into the towns (EV002-3, EV002-5), and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before (EV000-26). None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

The war of the enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch counts the Trail's trucks from December 1965 (EV003-3); the U-2S photographs the dispersed fuel drums before the POL strikes fly (EV000-27); and by March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree, from the radar's count of convoys out of Kompong Speu, that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4). The counts show the bombing raising the toll on the transit without lowering what arrives, and change no rule in Washington.

Every American and South Vietnamese infantryman carries a modern carbine, and every squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman. The Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle (EV000-23, EV000-34). The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by the corps, and the districts they leave pay for it (EV004-2). None of it changes who chooses the battle.

Above the Trail the B-52J is allowed against fixed crossings in the panhandle from April 1966 and cuts every bridge it can measure; the enemy answers with fords, causeways and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before (EV005-2). At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is measured every night and lifts three weeks early at a heavy price to the PAVN (EV000-36). It is the first siege the enemy loses badly, and Westmoreland draws a lesson from it that will cost the coastal hamlets.

Three changes of 1964 run through the phase without altering its battles. Phung Hoang's centres cover every district by the end of 1965 and break the Front's district administration in the held belt before Tet rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN takes the Can Giuoc committee itself, not only its security chief (EV002-4). The method spreads by its own success into the contested belt, where it becomes the historical Phoenix, and a PRU raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 makes "the American Phoenix" a press story three years early (EV006-4). The American battalions arrive and leave as units for twelve-month tours, trained together and commanded for the whole of them; they fight better in the contacts the enemy grants and get no more of them, and the enemy learns to aim at the week each one changes over, as it did at Dien Ban in August 1966 (EV006-3). And the field advisers, now chosen and trained and on eighteen-month tours, are the men who carry what the American battalions forget each year.

The officer reforms change the ARVN in this phase more than any provision before them. From the board's second season in 1965 the battalions are filled from its command list, their commanders serve eighteen months and are relieved when they fail, and by the end of 1966 about three in four of the army's battalion commanders are men a board chose, against about one in two competent commanders on the historical course (EV007-2) [X-007]. The enemy notices within a season and does what he does against every better unit: he declines to fight it, and lays his ambush for the battalion beside it, as the 272nd Regiment does on Route 13 in May 1966 (EV007-3, F007-18). The Buddhist uprising finds the board's commanders in I Corps on neither side (EV000-26), and in 1967 the ranked list gives the 25th Division its first commander chosen for competence, under the aid condition, while an agent in the board's secretariat gives COSVN every list it publishes (EV007-4). Above the battalions nothing moves: the corps are commanded by the same men, Westmoreland's strategy turns the ARVN to pacification as it did historically, and the generals still decide where the battalions go.

## EV007-2 · The command lists and the thirty-day reviews · 1 Sep 1965 – 30 Jun 1966 · The Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters; the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The board's second season was the first in which it had two years of evaluation reports to read, and it changed the army's battalions faster than anything since 1955. Between September 1965 and June 1966 the panels selected about 230 battalion and regimental commanders, most of the army's manoeuvre commands, and by the end of June 1966 about three-quarters of the army's battalions were commanded by an officer the board had chosen, about two-thirds of them majors and lieutenant colonels of the classes of 1957–59 who had passed the career course and the first Dalat staff year [X-007]. Every command was for eighteen months, against a median of under a year on the historical course [M007·S1]. The captain-commanded battalion, about a third of the army in 1965, fell to about one in ten by the end of 1966 [X-007].

The thirty-day reviews were the part the army felt. In the season the board relieved 27 battalion commanders and three regimental commanders for cause, each after a review that heard the officer, his raters and his American adviser's written statement, and each review's finding was sent to every battalion commander in the army in a monthly digest the Central Training Command called Lessons of Command [X-007]. Most of the reliefs were for what the courses taught against: a convoy moved without route security, a night defensive position without listening posts, a fire plan never made, a company sent forward without the battalion knowing where the others were [X-007]. Two were for the sale of rice rations, and the Commission Against Corruption prosecuted both (F007-4) [X-007]. The digest was read, as such things are read, mostly by officers who wished to know what the board punished; that was the point of it.

The board's own sale of commands ended and patronage moved to where the board did not reach. In the corps headquarters the price of a command fell to nothing, and the price of an assignment rose: the generals could no longer give a man a battalion, but they could still give him a division staff post, a province's sector headquarters, a seat on the career course ahead of his turn, or a battalion in the Delta rather than one on the Cambodian border (F007-4) [X-007]. Lindqvist estimated in June 1966 that about half of what had been sold in 1964 was no longer for sale, and that the other half had changed its name [X-007].

The field commissions began in the same season. About 600 sergeants with a combat record and a commander's recommendation were commissioned in 1966 after a twelve-week course at Thu Duc and the branch basic course, most of them sons of farmers from the Delta and the coastal Centre, men who would never have passed the diploma bar the decree had abolished (F007-22) [X-007]. They went to the platoons of their own regiments, and in the PF and RF training camps it was noticed within the year that the new lieutenants spoke to the recruits in the recruits' own accent. Major Bui Quang Trieu took command of the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, 5th Division from the September list.

## EV007-3 · Operation OPEN ROAD · 3–21 May 1966 · Route 13 from Chon Thanh to An Loc, the Xa Cam rubber and the Tau O crossing, Binh Long Province; Bau Bang, Binh Duong Province · NEW · 007
*Initiative: enemy*

The ambush that was never sprung on Route 13 is the clearest case in this war of what the officer reforms did and why no report showed it. In April 1966 the VC 9th Division ordered its 272nd Regiment, three battalions of about 1,500 men with 82mm mortars, 75mm recoilless rifles and RPD guns, to lay an ambush on Route 13 between Chon Thanh and An Loc for the next ARVN convoy to the Binh Long garrison, on the road where the regiment had broken relief columns in 1965 [X-007]. The regiment's commander, Lieutenant Colonel Dang Huu Thoi, chose the ground that every regiment on that road had chosen: the Xa Cam rubber south of An Loc, where the trees came to the edge of the laterite on both sides, the Tau O stream crossing to stop the head of the column, and an L of two battalions along the eastern side with the third astride the road behind [X-007]. The dry season had not yet broken, and the rubber's floor was open for 300 metres under the canopy.

The convoy left Chon Thanh at 0600 on 4 May: 62 trucks of rice, ammunition and fuel for the Binh Long sector, escorted by the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, with a troop of M113s from the 1st Armored Cavalry Squadron [X-007]. Its commander, Major Bui Quang Trieu, had been given the battalion from the board's list in September and had written the operation order himself on the pattern the career course taught for a route through ground the enemy could choose. Two rifle companies moved on foot through the rubber 300 to 400 metres on each side of the road, a kilometre ahead of the first truck, with PF guides from the hamlets at Tan Khai; the battalion's 81mm mortars and a battery of 105mm M101A1 howitzers at Chon Thanh were registered on the Tau O crossing and four points in the Xa Cam rubber; a US Army O-1 Bird Dog flew over the column with the battalion's adviser on its net; and the trucks did not move until the flanking companies reported each kilometre clear [X-007]. It was slow. The convoy covered 27 kilometres in eleven hours, about half the speed the sector had demanded, and Trieu's division commander told him so by radio twice.

The 272nd's reconnaissance saw the flanking companies at about 0830, coming through the rubber towards the eastern arm of the L at a walk, and Thoi had to decide. The Front's rule for its regiments was to fight only where victory was certain, and an ambush whose killing zone would be entered from the side by an infantry company with its fire already registered was not that [H]. His political officer argued that the regiment had been in position for nine days and that its men expected a fight. Thoi withdrew the regiment east into the rubber at 0910, without firing, and the convoy reached An Loc at 1700 [X-007]. The 5th Division's report of the day recorded Operation OPEN ROAD as completed without contact. MACV's weekly summary did not mention it.

On 21 May the 272nd's 1st and 3rd Battalions ambushed an RF convoy of the Binh Duong sector, two companies escorting fourteen trucks on Route 13 near Bau Bang, which moved in column with its escort on the trucks and no one in the trees [X-007]. The RF lost about 40 killed, 30 wounded and nine trucks burned; about 20 bodies were found and the sector's report of 85 enemy killed is no better than such reports usually were [X-007]. The regiment had not stopped fighting. It had moved its fight to the unit that did not scout its route (F007-5).

What happened on 4 May was learned only in January 1967, when a notebook of the 272nd's operations officer was taken near Lai Khe. It recorded the decision of 4 May in a sentence: that "the puppet battalion now walks beside the road and not on it", and that the regiment should "seek its targets among the units that still ride" [X-007]. That is the reform's largest effect in the field, and the one no indicator records: the battalions the board chose lost fewer men because they were attacked less, and an attack that is not made leaves nothing to count (F007-18, F007-24). I estimate that the ARVN lost about a third fewer battalions rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, a judgment that rests on cases like this one and cannot be measured [X-007].

## EV000-23 · Plei Me and the Ia Drang · 19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
*Initiative: enemy*

On 19 October 1965 the PAVN B3 Front under Major General Chu Huy Man attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, south of Pleiku, and the next day ambushed the ARVN relief column; the relief reached the camp at dusk on 25 October and the PAVN withdrew west towards the Chu Pong massif on the Cambodian border [H27]. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), newly arrived, was turned loose in pursuit. On 14 November the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray at the foot of the Chu Pong, into the assembly area of the 66th Regiment [H27]. For three days the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th, with elements of the 33rd, assaulted a perimeter held with the help of the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert Tully, massed tube artillery, tactical air and, from 15 November, B-52s striking about 7 km to the west, the first tactical use of the bombers, in five missions and 96 sorties over the campaign [H27]. The troopers carried M4A1s, with two M249s in each squad and an M110A2 marksman in most, where historically they carried the M16 and had the M60 at platoon; the perimeter held as it held historically, and I put American losses at X-Ray at about 70 killed and 115 wounded against 79 and 121 [X-004]. Moore's count of PAVN dead, 634 after his own reduction, and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record and the same caution applies to them [H27].

On 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade, marching overland to Landing Zone Albany, was ambushed in column in tall grass by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd Regiment [H27]. The PAVN, following Colonel Nguyen Huu An's order to "grab them by the belt", fought inside the column where American artillery and air could not reach them, for about sixteen hours [H27]. This is the kind of fight the squad gun was built for, and it helped less than it should have. Two belt-fed guns in every squad meant that the platoons which kept their cohesion, Alpha Company's and the battalion command group's at the head of the column, formed a perimeter in the clump of trees at Albany within minutes and held it all afternoon; but the companies strung out behind in the grass were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, and a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 125 killed, 130 wounded and 4 missing, against 155 killed, 124 wounded and 4 missing on the historical course, still the worst American loss of a single day so far [X-004]. Over the campaign the United States counted about 205 dead, against 237 historically, and claimed a kill ratio of about ten to one; a PAVN report of 1966 gives 559 killed and 669 wounded [X-004] [H27]. Both sides inflated the other's losses [H27].

Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success, and each drew the lesson that confirmed its strategy: Westmoreland that airmobile forces with heavy fire support could destroy PAVN regulars at a favourable rate; the PAVN that by closing to close quarters and choosing when to fight they could neutralize American firepower and withdraw across the Cambodian border to rebuild [H27]. The B3 Front's after-action study added one sentence that the historical one did not need: that the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and that the belt-grabbing assault must therefore be made from closer still, at night, or not at all (F004-10) [X-004]. McNamara, visiting after the battle, advised the President at the end of November that even a doubling of US forces in 1966 might produce only a military stalemate at a higher level [H].

The officer education changed Albany in the companies and not in the column's order. Moore and McDade had been commissioned long before 1957 and the decision to march overland in column through the grass was McDade's; but the company commanders of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry were captains of the classes of 1959–62, and they had been through the branch course and the career course, which taught the movement to contact in close country as a drill [X-007]. Two of them put flank security out into the grass on their own authority when the column halted at about midday, and their companies were the ones whose men were found in perimeters rather than in knots; two did not, because the column was moving and the battalion had given no order [X-007]. I put the battalion's loss at about 105–115 killed rather than about 125, a judgment on two companies' conduct that I hold loosely, and the campaign's American dead at about 190 [X-007]. It remains the worst American loss of a single day so far.

## EV000-26 · The Buddhist uprising · 10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · HIST-ALT · 000,002
*Initiative: neither*

At the Honolulu conference in February 1966 Johnson embraced Ky publicly, and Ky came away believing he had American backing to move against his principal rival, Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps [H30]. On 10 March the junta dismissed Thi [H30]. When Ky let him return to Da Nang and Hue on 16–17 March he was met by crowds, and the "Struggle Movement" of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers, led by Thich Tri Quang, took control of the two cities and called for civilian government [H30]. Ky's first attempt to reassert control, landing marines at Da Nang on 3–5 April, was blocked by I Corps troops and withdrawn [H30]; on 9 April US Marines interposed on a Da Nang bridge to stop a rebel convoy [H30].

On 15 May Ky airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang and took the city centre; the I Corps commander, General Ton That Dinh, fled to the US Marine headquarters, and III MAF found itself between two factions of its ally, preventing a bridge and an ammunition dump being blown and readying jets to intercept Vietnamese aircraft if Ky's air force struck the rebels [H30]. Thi agreed to go into exile at the end of May; on 26 May a funeral riot burned the US Information Service library [H30]. In June the Struggle supporters blocked Hue's streets with family altars until police removed them; Tri Quang was arrested and placed under house arrest in Saigon, and government control of Hue was restored by 19 June [H30]. About 150 people were killed and more than 700 wounded on both sides, including 23 Americans wounded [H30].

The uprising ended the Buddhist movement as an organised political force and confirmed Thieu and Ky in power [H30]. It also suspended operations in I Corps for three months at the height of the American buildup, and was answered with a political concession: elections for a Constituent Assembly, held on 11 September 1966 [H].

In this war the uprising runs on the historical dates and ends as it did, and the police that Ky used against it was the one the decree of 1964 had built (F002-16). On 12 March 1966, two days after Thi's dismissal, the Directory removed Director General Cuong by a decree "reorganising" the Directorate, which his statute did not allow, and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, already director of the Military Security Service [H] [X-002]. The US Mission protested and did not pull the lever; the Directory was the government the Americans had just embraced at Honolulu, and Washington would not quarrel with it over a policeman in the month the Struggle Movement began [X-002]. In May and June Loan took Field Force companies and Saigon police into Da Nang and Hue, and in Hue the Special Branch's provincial section gave him the family census of the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university, compiled in 1965 for another purpose [X-002]. About 1,300 people were detained in Thua Thien and Quang Nam by the end of July [X-002]. The magistrates' 72-hour review was the one part of the statute Loan could not suspend without a further decree, and the Mission made that decree the price of the Public Safety programme; by October about 1,000 of the detained had been released by the courts [X-002].

The repression was more efficient than the historical one and it cost more. The first American newspaper stories about "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, three years earlier than the Phoenix stories of the historical course (F002-14) [X-002]. Several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders in Hue, knowing that the census had found them, went to the Front's Hue city committee in the summer of 1966, as some of their number did historically [H] [X-002]. They took with them what they knew of how the census was kept and where its copies were held. The rural sections of the Special Branch were not used against the pagodas, because the Mission's line held in the provinces where it had failed in Saigon, and in the villages the police went on as before [X-002].

The officer reforms put a third party into the uprising, and the palace noticed it. Of the 1st Division's battalions in Thua Thien and Quang Tri, about two-thirds were commanded in March 1966 by officers from the board's list, most of whom owed nothing to Thi; when the division headquarters declared for the Struggle in April, four of those battalions stayed on operations in the hills west of Hue and refused orders from both Hue and Saigon to march on the cities, while the battalions whose commanders Thi had appointed went into the streets as they did historically [X-007]. The Struggle's armed support in Hue was therefore thinner than on the historical course, and Ky's operation of May took Hue no faster, because the Struggle's strength had always been in the crowds [X-007]. After the uprising the board's ranked list for the 1st Division was headed by Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong, and Ky, who needed a commander the Americans would trust in Hue, took the list's first name, as the historical junta took the same man [H]. The corps went to Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam, a loyalist the Directory chose for loyalty, because no list governed the corps [H]. In Saigon the Directory drew the lesson that the merit commanders had disobeyed its orders as well as Thi's, and the first report of the Military Security Service on "the board's officers as a political factor" is dated July 1966 (F007-14) [X-007].

## EV007-4 · The list and the 25th Division · 4 Mar – 31 Oct 1967 · Duc Hoa, Hau Nghia Province, and the 25th Division's districts in Hau Nghia and Long An; the Joint General Staff and the Officer Selection Board, Saigon · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The 25th Division was the ARVN's worst, and in 1967 it became the test of whether the ranked list could reach a division. Its battalions, in Hau Nghia and western Long An between Saigon and the Parrot's Beak, had the highest desertion rate and the lowest rate of contact in III Corps, its regiments had been commanded by the division commander's appointees until the board's lists of 1965–66, and its commander, appointed in 1965 before the list existed, had refused to move his headquarters out of Duc Hoa at night [H] [X-007]. When he was due for reassignment in March 1967 the board's ranked list put three colonels before the Directory. Ky wanted a fourth man, an Air Force patron's cousin who was not on it; Thieu, as Chief of State, took the second name, Colonel Vu Ngoc Hoan, a Dalat graduate of 1952 who had commanded a regiment of the 7th Division well under the board's list, after the US Mission told both men through the Aid Compliance Board that the division's counterpart funds depended on the list (Article 14) [X-007]. It was one of two division commands in ten filled from the list against a palace preference by the end of 1967 [X-007].

Hoan relieved three battalion commanders in his first ninety days, all through the board's thirty-day reviews and all for what the reviews called failure to close with the enemy, and put the division's battalions into the districts at night beside the Popular Forces, which the corps had never asked it to do [X-007]. By October the division's desertion rate had fallen by about a quarter, its battalions were in contact about twice as often as in 1966, and the Front's district companies in Duc Hoa and Duc Hue had moved their bases toward the Cambodian border [X-007]. None of this changed who held most of Hau Nghia's hamlets after dark, which the division could visit and could not hold.

In July the board extended its lists to the commanders of the Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, at the request of the III Corps commander after the losses of the RF convoys on Route 13 (EV007-3) [X-007]. About 280 RF company commanders were reviewed by the end of the year and about 60 replaced, most of them by field-commissioned lieutenants of their own provinces [X-007].

On 11 October the Military Security Service arrested Lieutenant Truong Cong Hy, a clerk of the board's secretariat since 1965, as an agent of the B2 Front's military intelligence [X-007]. He had copied every command list the board had published, with the panels' ratings of each selected officer, and passed them by courier to the Cu Chi district committee (F007-6) [X-007]. The COSVN target notes taken with his courier in November showed what the lists had been used for: the B2 Front's regiments had been told which battalions in III Corps were under new commanders and which were not, and had planned their ambushes of 1967 against the second [X-007]. The board's best evidence of its own effect came from the man who had betrayed it.

## P2 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam [H40] and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year [H40]. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December [H9], and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December [H39]. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000 [H39], and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view" [H39]. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi [H]. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general (EV001-6, EV001-7) [X-001]. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside [X-001].

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest (EV002-4, EV002-5) [X-002]. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

The enemy's external supply is measured: the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count, the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what the South's forces need from the North (EV003-3, EV003-4) [X-003]. That knowledge is honest from 1965 rather than from 1970, and it has not changed the rules, the bombing or the strategy. Its main effect on Washington has been to deliver bad news early.

The RVNAF enters 1968 with the rifles it has carried for four years and a Regional Force that the corps commanders want for their own operations, bound since 1967 by the rule that keeps it in its provinces (EV004-2). The American army has had no rifle crisis and no inquiry, and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights; it is the same army in every other respect, fighting the same battles on the enemy's terms.

The bombers have learned two things in two years: that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot (EV005-2, EV005-3), and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle (EV000-36). The second lesson is the one the headquarters remembers going into 1968.

Phung Hoang has a centre in every district and an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, about one in five of them members of district or village committees, and the Front's district administration in the held belt now governs from the contested edge by courier (EV006-2, EV002-4, F006-4) [X-006]. The word Phoenix has been a press story since March 1967 (EV006-4). About 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps; the American battalions have changed over once or twice each as units, have fought better in the contacts they were granted, and have learned at Dien Ban to keep their reliefs off the dark of the moon (EV006-3) [X-006]. None of it has changed who chooses the battle.

The ARVN ends 1967 with about four in five of its battalions commanded by officers the board chose, on eighteen-month commands, with the captain-led battalion nearly gone and the thirty-day review feared and read; about a third fewer of its battalions have been rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, which no report shows because the enemy declined the fights that would have shown it (EV007-2, EV007-3) [X-007]. The 25th Division has a commander from the list and the corps have the commanders they had (EV007-4). The Military Security Service has its first file on the board's officers as a political factor, and COSVN has had every list the board published.

## P3 · Overview

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war [H40], and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end [H40].

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp [H39]. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone [H39].

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way; Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington (EV000-45) [X-001]. The difference is in the Delta and in the hamlets. The Front enters the offensive with a smaller base in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre, recruits fewer men there in 1968 (EV000-52), and loses the towns of the western Delta faster because the hamlets around them warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers (EV000-42). When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled, and the Commission audits the new Phoenix programme against the land register (EV000-54) [X-001]. The American political calendar is not changed by any of it.

The police changes Tet in the same places and in the same way: in the held belt of the Delta the Front's urban cadres are known and its guides do not arrive (EV000-42), and in Hue its lists are better because the Republic's own census fed them (EV000-44). Phoenix, here, is only the name Washington learned for a programme whose district centres had run since 1964 without quotas and under an audit (EV000-54).

Above all this the radar watches the border, and at Khe Sanh it gives the headquarters and the White House the most complete picture of an enemy concentration either has ever held, while the forces attacking the cities move among the Tet crowds where no radar can tell them from anyone else (EV000-41). After the halt it measures exactly how the North uses it (EV003-5), and Commando Hunt opens with a second ledger beside the Air Force's claims (EV000-55).

The ARVN and the territorials go into Tet with the weapons the historical ARVN received only after it. In the district towns, which the first wave attacked with local forces, about a third of the attacks never get inside the compound walls; in the cities, where the PAVN and the main force fight block by block, the rifles lower the cost and do not shorten the battles (EV000-42, EV000-44). The mobilization of June arms its new territorials from stock within the month (EV000-51).

In the hills of Quang Tri the siege of Khe Sanh is measured every night and its trenches destroyed before they reach the wire; the PAVN pulls back about two weeks early, at a much heavier price, and the garrison loses about a third fewer men (EV000-41). It is the most complete American victory of the war on the ground and changes nothing in Washington; the brigade pulled from Thua Thien's hamlets to win it is missing when the Front returns to them. Abrams refuses to make the victory a doctrine of border bases and closes Khe Sanh on the historical date (EV000-51), and Commando Hunt opens against a panhandle net built since 1966 to be cut (EV000-55).

The district centres change Tet in the held belt in one more way: the leaders who came into the Delta towns to direct the uprising had been charted for three years, and a third of them were taken in February (EV000-42). In Hue the province centre's informant cards burn in the first hour and the district centres' do not (EV000-44). The American battalions that fight the offensive have been together for months, and the advisers beside the ARVN have been in their posts for a year; the battles are shorter at the edges and the same at their centres.

The officer reforms meet Tet at the battalions and the companies, which is where they had worked. The ARVN units that fought in the towns had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and in IV and III Corps they recalled their men from leave faster and fought the first day closer to their strength (EV000-42) [X-007]. The mobilization of June finds its platoon leaders without the historical crash course, because the field commissions of 1966–68 had already made about 3,000 lieutenants from the army's sergeants, and the new territorials pay for it in sergeants (EV000-51). COSVN, reading its failure in the towns, opens a proselytizing campaign aimed at the officers the board had passed over (EV000-52). At Hue and Khe Sanh the battles are the historical ones, and the decision of 31 March is unchanged.

## EV000-42 · The Tet Offensive: the first wave · 30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,004,006
*Initiative: enemy*
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps [H39]. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet [H]. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country [H39]. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital [H39].

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns [H]. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed [H39]. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing [H39]. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed [H39].

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces [X-001]. In IV Corps it goes differently in two of them. At Can Tho, the corps headquarters on the Bassac, the battalions that attacked the town and the Binh Thuy air base moved up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh that GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left in the hands of their own Popular Forces (EV001-6); PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, two of them fought, and the 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault [X-001]. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters, and Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, with far less of the town destroyed than on the historical course [X-001]. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days [X-001]. At Ben Tre, in Kien Hoa, the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta, where the government had titled little outside the town because it held little outside the town, the battle and the destruction were as they were historically [X-001]. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964 (F001-4, F001-17). Allied and enemy losses for the country as a whole are not materially changed by it, and I do not adjust them [X-001].

The police and the chiefs changed the first wave in the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon, and changed it less than they might have, because the infrastructure they had driven from the villages had gone to the towns (EV002-5). In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced on 31 January to lead the uprising, and in the first two weeks of February arrested about 1,700 of them on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs [X-002]. Where the Front's guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later: the battalions that attacked Can Tho waited on the night of 30–31 January for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal who never arrived, because both had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier [X-002]. The ARVN 21st Division, whose desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its strength on the muster roll, and the half of the ARVN that was on Tet leave elsewhere was a smaller half in IV Corps (F002-15) [X-002]. In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands, where the police counted for little against PAVN regiments and VC main-force battalions, the battle went as it did historically [X-002].

The rifles changed the first wave everywhere the ARVN and the territorials fought, and most where the fight was small. Historically most of the ARVN went into Tet with the M1 Garand and the carbine, the M16 having reached only the Airborne, the Marines and a few regiments in 1967, and the Regional and Popular Forces with weapons of the Korean War; American officers watched ARVN soldiers in Saigon and Hue fight with eight-round rifles against attackers with AK-47s and B40s, and the M16 airlift to the ARVN was one of the first decisions after the offensive [H]. In this war every ARVN soldier and every territorial had carried a modern carbine for four years. The difference is clearest in the district towns, where the first wave was made by VC local-force battalions and companies against garrisons of an RF company or two, a PF platoon and the district police. Of the 64–72 district towns attacked historically, I estimate that the attackers penetrated about two-thirds on this course against nearly all historically, and that the ones they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, as at Long Huu in 1964 (EV002-2) (F004-16) [X-004]. At Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st with one squad gun lent by the 7th Division and its own carbines, and the battalion, which had planned to be through the town by 0400, was still in the market when the 7th Division's 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived at 0730 [X-004]. In the provincial capitals and the cities, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments and the fighting was block by block, the rifles changed casualties and not outcomes. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,500, against 2,788 historically, and do not adjust the enemy's losses, which were never measured to a precision the change would show in [X-004].

Phung Hoang added a layer to that harvest. The province committees of Phong Dinh, Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long had sent their senior cadres into the towns to direct the uprising, the men who were to form the "revolutionary committees" of each city, and the PIOCCs at Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long had carried charts of those committees since 1965 with photographs from the biometric enrolment of their arrested relatives and ralliers (EC006-1) [X-006]. Of about 140 province- and district-committee cadres who came into the three towns, about 50 were taken by 15 February, most of them on the identifications of ward police who had their photographs, and the Can Tho "revolutionary committee" never held its first meeting [X-006]. The ARVN battalions that cleared the three towns had American advisers who had been with them for about a year, and in Can Tho the 21st Division's 1st Battalion, 31st Regiment had an adviser, Captain Jerome Pike, who took the call of a PF corporal on the Xa No canal in Vietnamese at 2340 on 30 January and moved the battalion's reaction company to the university before the attackers reached it (F006-2) [X-006]. The American units committed to Tet in IV Corps, the 9th Division's 2nd Brigade and the Mobile Riverine Force, had been together since their arrival in 1967 and fought the streets of My Tho and Vinh Long as the units they had trained as [X-006]. None of this touched Saigon, Hue or the Highlands, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments that no district centre had ever charted.

The officer reforms changed the first wave in the ARVN's battalions, and changed it most where the battalions were in the towns. By January 1968 about four in five ARVN battalion commanders had been chosen by the board and had held their battalions for more than a year; the order that sent half the army on Tet leave was the Joint General Staff's, and the board's commanders obeyed it, but in IV and III Corps they had recall plans written to the career course's pattern, and by the evening of 31 January the battalions of the 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions were at about 70 percent of their strength in the towns against about half historically [X-007]. The Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, whose commanders the board had reviewed since July 1967, fought the district compounds under lieutenants many of whom had been sergeants in the same companies two years before (EV007-4) [X-007]. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,300 rather than about 2,500, a judgment on faster recall and steadier companies that I hold loosely, and I do not adjust the enemy's losses [X-007]. At Hue the 1st Division under Truong fought as it fought historically, and its commander had been the list's first name since 1966 (EV000-26).

## EV000-51 · Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned · 10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff [H50] [H]. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces [H50]. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy [H50].

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended [H62]. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969 [H62]. Historically the expansion outran the rifles: the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better [H]. In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment, about 260,000 carbines already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June against the year's expected growth, and were on the ranges within a month of induction [X-004]. The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but sergeants: the training centres could zero a carbine in an afternoon and could not make a squad leader in less than a year, and Venn's successor reported in September that about a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before (F004-3) [X-004]. The old weapons the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law [X-004].

On Khe Sanh the decision was harder than it had been historically, because in this war the base had been a victory rather than an ordeal. Westmoreland, in his last weeks, argued for keeping it as the anchor of a line of border bases under the siege box, and the III Marine Amphibious Force's staff drew up a plan for three more such bases along Route 9 and the Laotian border, to be held by battalions drawn from the coastal districts (F005-14) [X-005]. Abrams refused it on 15 June. His reason, in a message to Wheeler that his staff kept, was that a base the enemy would not attack was a base that held nothing, and that after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one [X-005]. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July [H46]. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ. Here it was read in Hanoi correctly, as a decision by an American commander that the siege the PAVN had lost was not worth inviting again, and in the American press, less correctly, as a retreat from a battle won [X-005].

The officers for the mobilization came differently. Historically the Thu Duc candidate course was cut from 37 weeks to 24 in June 1968 to commission the platoon leaders the expansion needed [M007·S2]. In this war the cut was to 32 weeks, and the gap was filled by the field commissions: about 3,000 sergeants had been commissioned since 1966 and about 2,500 more were commissioned in the second half of 1968, after the branch basic course that the decree did not allow to be waived (F007-12, F007-22) [X-007]. The cost was in the ranks they came from. The sergeant shortage that Venn's successor reported in September, a third of the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank, was in part the field commission's price: the army had made lieutenants of the sergeants who would have led those platoons [X-007]. The board's lists governed the new RF companies from their first month, and the new PF platoons not at all.

## EV000-52 · The third offensive · 17 Aug – 23 Sep 1968 · Provincial towns and bases, especially in III Corps and the Central Highlands · HIST-ALT · 000,001
*Initiative: enemy*

The third phase of the general offensive began in mid-August with attacks by fire and ground assaults on towns and bases, including attacks around Tay Ninh in III Corps [H39] [H]. It was weaker than its predecessors: the local forces that had carried the attacks of February and May were largely gone, and the main forces would not commit themselves against American firepower in the same way again [H39]. It ended in late September without lasting gains [H39]. Taken together, the three offensives of 1968 cost the VC about 50,000 dead by one estimate against about 4,000 US and 6,000 ARVN [H39]. Their recruitment in the South collapsed: in Region 5, 1,700 recruits in 1969 against 8,000 in 1968, in the Mekong lowlands 100 against 16,000 [H39]. By the end of the year about 70 percent of the main-force troops in the South are reported to have been northerners [H39]. MACV estimated total PAVN and VC strength at 287,000 in January 1968 and 251,000 at its end [H39]. The losses forced a reassessment in Hanoi that would carry into 1969 [H].

In this war the third offensive runs as it did historically, in a part of the country the provisions barely touched [X-001]. What changes is the arithmetic behind it. The Front's recruitment in the Mekong lowlands in 1968, 16,000 on the historical course [H39], is about 11,000 here, because about a third of the tenant families who would have supplied the recruits had been titled by the government in districts it held at night, and the Front's own conscription in those districts had become coercion that the PF platoons could resist (F001-8, F001-13) [X-001]. The 1969 collapse comes from a lower base and is no less complete. The consequence is that COSVN begins filling its Delta main-force battalions with northern replacements in the summer of 1968, several months earlier than on the historical course, and the northerners who arrive in Chuong Thien and Kien Giang are strangers in hamlets where the Front's strength had always been that its soldiers were local [X-001]. The proportion of northerners in the main forces at the year's end, about 70 percent historically [H39], is a few points higher here [X-001].

COSVN read the year's failure in the towns, among other things, as a failure of its proselytizing among the ARVN's officers, and it chose a new target. A military proselytizing directive of August 1968, captured in Tay Ninh in December, ordered the province committees to work among "the officers whom the puppet promotion board has humiliated": the commanders relieved for cause, the majors passed over three times, the colonels kept from the list (F007-19) [X-007]. It attributed the board, wrongly, to American selection of the ARVN's officers, and its propaganda leaflets of the autumn called the board's commanders "officers chosen by the Americans" [X-007]. About 30 relieved or passed-over officers were identified by the Military Security Service as contacted in 1968–69, and two were arrested as agents; the directive's yield was small and its cost to the Front was smaller [X-007]. In the third offensive itself the attacks on Tay Ninh met the 25th Division's battalions in positions they had held since 1967, and the town was not entered [X-007].

## P3 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent [H40].

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that. About 900,000 hectares have been titled, the Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier [X-001]. The American decision of 1968 is unchanged, and so is its timing.

The National Police numbers about 95,000 and the Special Branch has buried its first constables of the Tet battles; the infrastructure in the held belt is weaker than anywhere since 1959, and every file it keeps is now held under a destruction plan [X-002].

The enemy's rear enters Phase IV counted: the surge after the halt is measured, the first dry season of Commando Hunt is scored against the radar as well as the claims (EV003-5, EV000-55) [X-003], and the American decision of 1968 is unchanged by any of it.

The RVNAF ends 1968 larger and armed throughout with weapons the historical one was still waiting for; its new territorials have rifles and too few sergeants (EV000-51) [X-004]. The gap the rearmament opened over history is now closing from the historical side, as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969.

The PAVN leaves 1968 having learned that it does not lay siege to a position the Americans can measure; that is the one way of choosing its battles it has lost [X-005]. It chooses all the others. The panhandle is harder to cut than history's, because the bombers taught its engineers how (EV000-55).

Phung Hoang enters 1969 with five years of files, an audited count the press has learned to quote, and no quotas; the Front's Delta committees have lost a third of the leaders they sent into the towns (EV000-42, EV000-54) [X-006]. The Hue district files have shown what the national file would be worth to the enemy (EV000-44). The selected advisers are in every field post, the first of them on their second tours, and the American battalions have fought the year as units.

The ARVN's battalions come out of 1968 with commanders who have held them through Tet and a board that has not stopped working; the mobilization's platoons have lieutenants who were sergeants and squads that have no sergeants (EV000-51) [X-007]. COSVN has opened a campaign among the officers the board passed over, and the Military Security Service has a second file on the board's officers, this time as targets (EV000-52).

## P4 · Overview

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971 [H40]. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 [D] to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971 [H40]. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest [H62]. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The revelation of My Lai, the Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the trial of Calley and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win [H]. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington [X-001]. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001]. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle (EV001-8) [X-001].

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses (EV002-6). The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective (EV000-66); Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season (EV000-68); and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically (EV000-71).

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open (EV000-58). Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy (EV000-68). In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home (EV000-71).

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless (EV000-60). The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election (EV002-6). And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute (EV000-72). The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

The officer reforms reach their limit in this phase, and it is the limit their memorandum named: the American lever. As the aid conditions are dropped with Vietnamization, the palace makes the board's general-officer list advisory in January 1970, keeps the board for everything below colonel, and in December 1970 answers a petition of colonels of the classes of 1957–59 by moving about forty of the army's best regimental and battalion commanders to staff and schools (EV007-5). Lam Son 719 goes into Laos weeks later with new commanders in two of the 1st Division's regiments and an Airborne brigade, and fails for the historical reasons, which were decided at corps and in Saigon (EV000-71). Thieu's election of 1971 is run against the background of the colonels' petition, read into the record of the Lower House (EV000-73). The battalions themselves are the best-led the Republic ever had.

## EV007-5 · The list in the palace and the colonels' petition · 12 Jan 1970 – 20 Jan 1971 · The Presidential Palace and the Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The ranked list for generals lost its lever in the same month as the Selection Board for the provinces, and in the same way. On 12 January 1970, with the aid conditions of 1964 being folded into Vietnamization's programmes and the Aid Compliance Board reduced to an office that reported to the Embassy rather than to this headquarters, Thieu decreed that the board's general-officer list would be "advisory to the Supreme Commander" [X-007]. He did not abolish the board, which went on choosing battalion and regimental commanders, promoting captains and majors and relieving for cause below the rank of colonel; it had become the army's machinery, and he had no other (EV002-6) [X-007]. Above it the divisions and the corps returned to the palace. In 1970 four division commands were filled, and none of them from the list's first name (F007-7) [X-007].

The colonels answered. The officers of the classes of 1957–59, who had been promised a career on merit when they were captains, were by 1970 lieutenant colonels and colonels commanding regiments, serving as division chiefs of staff and teaching at Dalat, and about 30 of them signed a petition to the Chief of the Joint General Staff in September asking that the list be restored, drafted by Colonel Nguyen Dac Khiet, commanding a regiment of the 9th Division [X-007]. It was a petition, not a plot. The Military Security Service and the Central Intelligence Organization reported it to the palace as a "colonels' group" with contacts among Ky's supporters and Minh's, which was true of four of its signers, and with the intention of a coup, which no evidence ever showed (F007-14) [X-007].

Thieu acted in December, before the dry season. Between 5 December 1970 and 20 January 1971 about 40 regimental and battalion commanders, the petition's signers and the officers the CIO's report placed near them, were moved to staff posts, the schools and the provinces' sector headquarters, nine of them in I Corps; two of the 1st Division's regimental commanders and the commander of an Airborne brigade were among them [X-007]. Khiet went to Dalat as an instructor. Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had commanded a regiment of the 5th Division since 1968 and had refused to sign, was kept in command and transferred to the new 3rd Division forming in Quang Tri in 1971, where the palace wanted a commander whose loyalty its report had cleared [X-007]. The petition's effect in Saigon was the one the decree's critics had feared from its first day: an educated officer corps whose promotion was blocked had become, in the palace's eyes, a faction, and the palace had treated it as one.

## EV000-71 · Lam Son 719 · 8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam [H56]. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak [H56]. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons [H56]; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units [H56]. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery [H56]. MACV J-2's estimate on 4 February, built from the U-2S's signals and the panhandle watch, put the enemy's strength within reach of Tchepone at about 35,000–40,000 men and rising, about twice the historical estimate given to the planners; the operation went ahead on its date [H] [X-003].

In this war the operation carried a siege box with it. Sorrell's detachment at Quang Tri, a dozen Americans who could not cross the border, received the Global Hawk's imagery of every firebase north and south of Route 9 each night and passed measured positions to the Js on Guam and at U-Tapao; the ARVN battalions on the hills passed nothing, because they had no means to give a coordinate the bombers could use, and no American advisers were there to give it for them (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. The box worked where the enemy dug and failed where he closed. At Fire Support Base Lolo and at Sophia, south of the road, the PAVN 2nd Division's regiments dug in around the hills from late February, their trenches were imaged and struck each night at 400–700 metres, and the bases held until they were evacuated in the withdrawal [X-005]. North of the road, Ranger North and Ranger South fell on 20–21 February and Fire Support Base 31 on 25 February much as they fell historically, to infantry and tanks of the 308th and 320th Divisions that came straight at the wire in daylight and were inside 400 metres before they could be measured; on 25 February the radar tracked about twenty tanks forming up north of the hill at about 0900, strike aircraft destroyed six before they reached it, and the base fell that afternoon all the same (F005-6) [H] [X-003] [X-005].

On 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began [H56]. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world [H56] [H]. Along Route 9 the radar showed the PAVN columns closing on the retreating units, and the Js with GBU-38s were put on the anti-aircraft guns the PAVN had dug in around the landing zones, which the cell measured each night; about 110 gun positions were destroyed in the last two weeks, and I estimate that the US Army lost about a sixth fewer helicopters than the historical 108 destroyed [H56] [X-005].

The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April [H56]. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing [H56]. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded [H56]. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the landing zones and the command, not by what anyone could see or strike [X-005]. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded" [H56]. Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after [H56]. The ARVN's I Corps read the operation in a way history's did not: the bases it had held were the bases where the American bombs had come every two hours, and it began to build its plans for the defence of the North around them (F005-20) [X-005].

The officer reforms went into Laos with the battalions and stopped at the corps. Two of the 1st Division's three regiments and one Airborne brigade had changed commanders in the six weeks before the operation, in the palace's answer to the colonels' petition, and their new commanders met their staffs in January (EV007-5) [X-007]. The battalions under them had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and it showed in the withdrawal: the battalions that came down Route 9 as units, carrying their wounded and their radios, outnumbered those that came out on the helicopters' skids by more than on the historical course, though the pictures that reached the world were the same pictures [X-007]. It did not show in the outcome, which was decided by the plan's limits, Thieu's instruction to stop the operation if losses reached about 3,000, Lam's command of divisions whose commanders reported to Saigon over his head, and the enemy's five divisions, none of which any board had touched (F007-7) [H] [X-007]. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses here either. The reform had made the battalions better than the operation they were sent on.

## EV000-73 · Thieu's unopposed re-election · Oct 1971 · Republic of Vietnam · HIST · 000,001
*Initiative: neither*

In the presidential election of October 1971 Thieu ran alone. Ky was at first disqualified under an election law requiring endorsements from deputies or provincial councillors, then reinstated by the Supreme Court, and withdrew; Duong Van Minh withdrew, charging that the election was rigged [H60] [H]. Thieu received all the valid votes, 5,971,114, and the reported turnout was 87.97 percent of 7,192,660 registered voters; valid ballots were 94.37 percent of those cast [H60]. A government that had won with a third of the vote in a contested election in 1967 was now returned in a referendum on itself. The army and administration remained Thieu's, and the regime was more stable than at any time since 1963; but its claim to stand for the whole non-communist South had narrowed, at the moment American forces were leaving it to stand alone.

In this war the election runs as it did historically, with the same withdrawals, the same single candidate and the same returns [X-001]. Its context is different in one respect and the same in another. Thieu's government could claim in the countryside what it could not claim on the historical course, that it had given about 800,000 families their land seven years before the election, and its province chiefs campaigned on it [X-001]. But the subordination of the Commission in July (EV001-8) had removed the one body with the standing to hear the opposition's complaints about the endorsement law, and Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard in Saigon as a description of a government that had shut down its own inspector in the year it chose to run alone [X-001].

The colonels' petition entered the election in August, when a deputy of the Lower House read its text into the record and asked why its signers had been removed from their commands before an operation in which the army had lost so much (EV007-5) [X-007]. Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard by the officer corps alongside it. The board went on promoting captains and majors through the campaign as if nothing had happened, which was in its way the strongest thing it ever did; the officers it promoted that autumn knew that the list above colonel was now the President's [X-007].

## P4 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving [H40]. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before [H62]. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed (EV000-65) [X-001]. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it (EV001-8) [X-001]. These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal [H56] [H]. The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent (EV002-6) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

The radar's ledger has made the interdiction debate honest and changed nothing it was about: the Trail is larger at the end of 1971 than ever, the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia is mapped and open, and the aircraft that count both will leave with the Americans (EV000-68) [X-003].

The ARVN's I Corps ends the phase believing, on the evidence of Lolo and Sophia, that a besieged base under American bombers holds (EV000-71). It is true, and it is the belief that will cost it most when the bombers go [X-005].

Phung Hoang ends 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon, serving the President as well as the war (EV000-72, EV002-6) [X-006]. About 3,500 selected advisers remain, with the territorials and the province teams more than with the divisions; the American battalions have gone home whole and their discipline has decayed on its historical course (EV000-60) [X-006].

The board ends 1971 as the machinery of the army below colonel and nothing above it: battalions and regiments are commanded by officers it chose, for terms it sets, and the divisions and corps by the President's choices (EV007-5) [X-007]. The colonels who asked for the list back are in the schools and the staffs. The battalions that went into Laos came out of it better than the operation did (EV000-71).

## P5 · Overview

1972 is the test of Vietnamization, and the answer it gives is exact: the Republic of Vietnam can stop a full PAVN conventional offensive with American air power and American advisers, and cannot do it without them. On 30 March the PAVN opens the largest offensive of the war, called the Nguyen Hue Offensive in Hanoi and the Easter Offensive in the West, with about fourteen divisions and hundreds of tanks [H] on three main fronts: across the DMZ into Quang Tri, from Laos and Cambodia into the Central Highlands at Kontum, and from Cambodia down Route 13 at An Loc towards Saigon [H64]. US ground strength has fallen to about 69,000 men, few of them combat troops, and is on its way to 27,000 [H64]. US air power returns in strength: B-52s and tactical aircraft over the battlefields of the South, and, for the first time since 1968, a bombing campaign against the North without most of Rolling Thunder's restrictions, with the mining of Haiphong.

The shape of the phase is a near-collapse in the spring and a recovery in the summer. Quang Tri Province falls on 1–2 May; Kontum and An Loc are besieged and held by narrow margins and B-52s; and by the autumn the ARVN, under better commanders, has retaken Quang Tri City. The PAVN loses most of the armour it committed and, by most estimates, about 100,000 men, killed and wounded, while keeping about a tenth of the South's territory, mostly thinly populated border regions in the west [H64]. The offensive creates almost a million refugees and kills more than 25,000 civilians [H64]. Its political result is the one Hanoi needed: a settlement on terms that leave PAVN troops in the South. The year ends with the October draft agreement, Thieu's refusal of it, and the eleven days of Linebacker II.

In this war the armour of the Easter Offensive is seen before it crosses the DMZ and is struck by radar-bombing B-52s through the cloud that grounded the fighter-bombers (EV000-74); Linebacker chooses from target folders seven years in the making and counts the effect of the mining on the North's own roads (EV000-75); and at An Loc the B-52 boxes of 10–12 May fall on assembly areas the radar shows (EV000-76). Quang Tri still falls, An Loc and Kontum still hold, and the lessons both sides draw are the historical ones.

The B-52J's year is this one. Freedom Train drops the Thanh Hoa bridge on its first night (EV000-75); the siege box breaks the penetration of An Loc on 12 May and the assault on Kontum short of the wire, and Kontum is secure five days early (EV000-76, EV000-77); and Linebacker II loses about six bombers rather than fifteen and does not strike Bach Mai or Kham Thien Street (EV000-82). Quang Tri still falls, and the terms of January are the terms of October.

The officer reforms meet the offensive at regiment and battalion, and the offensive is decided above them. On the DMZ front the 3rd Division's battalions, under commanders the board chose, give ground in better order than history's, and the 56th Regiment's commander, a board-chosen officer with a hero's record from Hue, surrenders Camp Carroll all the same (EV000-74). At Quang Tri the contradictory orders of the corps and the division are the historical orders, and the division still breaks, but one of its regiments holds the road south open long enough to bring out its column (EV000-78). At Tan Canh more of the 22nd Division gets out, and Kontum and An Loc are held as before (EV000-77). The army fights the year as it was taught to fight, with American air at the centre of every plan, and depends on it more for having learned to use it well (F007-23).

## EV000-74 · The Easter Offensive opens: the DMZ front · 30 Mar – 2 Apr 1972 · Quang Tri Province, from the DMZ to the Cua Viet and Camp Carroll · HIST-ALT · 000,003
*Initiative: enemy*

The armour was seen before it crossed. From 12 March the Gulf watch, standing off the Quang Tri coast outside the SA-2s that the North had moved into and just north of the DMZ, tracked tracked-vehicle columns moving south at night on Route 1A and Route 101 from Vinh Linh toward the Ben Hai, and the panhandle watch tracked others moving east along Route 9 in Laos toward Khe Sanh [X-003]. By 25 March the J-2 count of armoured vehicles in assembly areas within 20 kilometres of the DMZ was about 90–120, and MACV warned I Corps on 27 March of an armoured attack across the demarcation line within days [X-003]. Lieutenant General Lam, the I Corps commander, expected the main blow from the west, as most of the American and Vietnamese staffs did; the warning reached the 3rd Division, and the rotation of its regiments between firebases, which history records as under way when the attack fell, went ahead in part [H] [X-003].

At midday on 30 March 1972 PAVN artillery, including 130mm guns that outranged anything the ARVN had, opened on the firebases strung along the DMZ, and the 304th and 308th Divisions with armoured regiments attacked across the demarcation line, while the 324B came in from the A Shau and the west [H64] [H67]. In all, PAVN forces on the northern front were supported by more than 100 tanks, T-54s, T-34s and PT-76s [H64]. The front was held by the ARVN 3rd Division under Brigadier General Vu Van Giai, a new division formed in late 1971 from mixed and unsteady units, with the 147th and 258th Vietnamese Marine Brigades under its control [H67] [H]. Low cloud kept most air support away for the first days [H64]. It did not keep the radar away. The B-52s, which bombed by radar in any weather, were put on the crossing points of the Ben Hai and the roads south of Gio Linh where the Gulf watch showed the armour moving, and on 31 March and 1 April they struck three columns in the open; by our own estimate, about 20–30 armoured vehicles were destroyed or disabled before they reached the firebases [X-003].

The firebases fell back or were overrun all the same. On 2 April the 56th Regiment at Camp Carroll, under Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, surrendered with about 1,500 men [H67]. That afternoon at Dong Ha, Captain John Ripley, US adviser to the 3rd Vietnamese Marine Battalion, spent about three hours under fire placing charges on the highway bridge over the Cua Viet, which was blown at 16:30 as PAVN tanks approached; the Marines held the river line [H67]. The PAVN halted to regroup and bring up supplies, and the 3rd Division, under fire from guns it could not reach, settled into a defence of Quang Tri City along the Thach Han and Cua Viet rivers. The radar had seen the tanks; it had not made the 3rd Division's regiments steady or the 56th's commander stay.

The officer reforms did not make the 3rd Division steady; nothing done in six months could have. Its regiments had been formed in late 1971 from units of the 1st Division, Regional Forces and men returned from desertion, and its battalion commanders, though chosen by the board, had held their battalions for less than six months when the guns opened [H] [X-007]. What they changed was the manner of the withdrawal from the DMZ outposts on 1–2 April. The 57th Regiment, given to Colonel Bui Quang Trieu in 1971 (EV007-5), came back from the firebases along Route 1 toward Dong Ha by bounds, a battalion holding while the next passed through it, with its 105s towed out and its wounded on its own trucks, and reached the Cua Viet line as a regiment; on the historical course it arrived as a crowd among the refugees [X-007]. Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, who had commanded the 56th since 1971 from the board's list on the strength of his battalion's fight at the Hue Citadel in 1968, surrendered Camp Carroll on 2 April as he did historically [H67] [X-007]. The board had chosen him for what he had done, and could not choose for what he would do.

## EV000-77 · Tan Canh, Dak To and the battle of Kontum · 24 Apr – 9 Jun 1972 · Tan Canh, Dak To, Ben Het and Kontum city, Kontum Province · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

In the Central Highlands the PAVN 320th Division began pressing the firebases on Rocket Ridge in early April, and on 24 April the 2nd Division's 1st and 66th Regiments, with the D10 Sapper Battalion and tanks, overran the ARVN bases at Tan Canh and Dak To II, held by the 42nd and 47th Regiments of the 22nd Division; the division "ceased to exist as a fighting unit", and its commander, Colonel Le Duc Dat, disappeared [H66]. Tan Canh fell in a day, to tanks and infantry already inside the wire before dawn, and nothing in the J reached a fight of that kind (F005-6) [X-005]. The road to Kontum lay open. The PAVN paused for three weeks [H66]. John Paul Vann, the civilian director of the II Corps advisory effort, effectively directed the defence; he had Colonel Ly Tong Ba of the 23rd Division put in command of Kontum, and on 10 May Major General Nguyen Van Toan replaced Ngo Du at II Corps [H64] [H66]. On 9 May at Ben Het, ARVN Rangers destroyed three PT-76s with BGM-71 TOW missiles, among the first combat uses of the weapon, and TOW-armed helicopters followed [H66].

The three weeks were the B3 Front's to lose, and they were the time the siege box needed. From 26 April a Global Hawk was given to Kontum every night, and Vann had a detachment of the coordinate cell flown to the city's airfield on 28 April [X-005]. By 13 May it had measured about 600 positions in the arc north and west of the city: the 320th Division's assembly areas along the Krong Poko, gun pits on the ridges, the trench lines dug toward the 23rd Division's perimeter, and the tank harbours of the 203rd Armored Regiment [X-005]. Cells of Js struck the measured positions from 2 May, and by the eve of the assault about a third of the guns the PAVN had brought to Kontum had been destroyed or abandoned in their pits [X-005].

The main assault on Kontum came on 14 May, by the 320th Division's 48th, 52nd and 64th Regiments and the 2nd Division [H66] [H64]. B-52 strikes on 14 May and on the night of 16–17 May fell close to the ARVN lines and broke the momentum of the attack [H66]. Here the cells put GBU-38s on the PAVN regiments' measured start lines through the night of 13–14 May, and on 14 May the attacking battalions came forward through ground that had been struck one position at a time; the 48th Regiment's assault on the northern perimeter was broken short of the wire, and the penetration on the city's northern edge, which historically took two weeks to clear, was held to a few blocks [X-005]. From 26 May to 3 June the ARVN cleared the city bunker by bunker, with the cell putting GBU-38s on bunkers 300 metres from Ba's lines, and on 4 June Ba, now a brigadier general, declared Kontum secure, five days earlier than historically [X-005]. On 9 June Vann's helicopter flew into a hillside in low cloud and he was killed; a Vietnamese account says it was shot down [H66]. US estimates put PAVN casualties in the Kontum battle at about 4,000 and in the Highlands campaign at 20,000–40,000, figures with no better basis than the body counts of earlier years [H66]. I estimate that the 23rd Division lost perhaps a quarter fewer killed than historically, and I do not adjust the PAVN's figures [X-005].

At Tan Canh the board's battalion commanders could not hold a position whose wire the tanks were inside before dawn, and did not; what changed was how many of their men got out. The 42nd Regiment's battalions broke out of Tan Canh on 24 April in their companies, not as individuals, and about a third more of the regiment's men reached Kontum over the following week than on the historical course [X-007]. The 22nd Division's command did not change; its commander had been the palace's choice after the list became advisory, and his disappearance is the historical record's [H66] [X-007]. Kontum was held by the 23rd Division under Ba, whose regiments were commanded by board officers, and it was held as told above, by the siege box and the infantry together.

## EV000-78 · The fall of Quang Tri · 27 Apr – 2 May 1972 · Quang Tri City and Highway 1 south to the My Chanh River · HIST-ALT · 000
*Initiative: enemy*

On 27 April the PAVN renewed its attack on the 3rd Division's defence of Quang Tri City [H]. Contradictory orders from the I Corps commander, Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam, and from Giai left units moving in opposite directions, and on 1 May Giai ordered the city abandoned [H67] [H]. The division broke. Soldiers and refugees fled south on Highway 1, and PAVN artillery fired on the column, killing roughly 2,000 soldiers and civilians on the road [H67]. Quang Tri Province was lost by 2 May; the Marines who held at the My Chanh River were the one coherent force left north of Hue [H64] [H]. On 2 May Thieu relieved Lam and gave I Corps to Lieutenant General Ngo Quang Truong, who stopped the rout, restored order in Hue and held the My Chanh line with the Marine and Airborne Divisions and the 1st Division [H64]. Giai was later court-martialled and sentenced to five years' imprisonment [H64]. The fall of Quang Tri was the first loss of a province to a PAVN conventional offensive, and Truong's recovery the clearest demonstration of what leadership was worth in the ARVN.

In this war the orders were the same and one regiment's conduct was not. When Giai ordered the city abandoned on 1 May, the 57th Regiment under Colonel Bui Quang Trieu was holding the bridges over the Thach Han south of the city, and Trieu held them until about 1500 on 1 May, three hours after his orders allowed, while the division's rear echelons and about 20,000 refugees crossed; he then brought the regiment south on Highway 1 behind the column with its two battalions astride the road and the third on the dunes to the east, where the PAVN artillery's observers had been placed historically [X-007]. The column was still shelled. I put the dead on the road at about 1,500 rather than about 2,000, and the 3rd Division's losses in men who simply disappeared at about a quarter fewer, judgments I hold loosely [X-007]. The fall of Quang Tri was not prevented and could not have been at that level; it was lost by the corps and the division, by orders that sent units in opposite directions, and Truong's recovery of the line in May was the recovery of a commander the list had named in 1966 and the palace had chosen in 1972 when it had no other (F007-7) [X-007].

## P5 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1972 the war is on the verge of a settlement that both governments in Vietnam regard as provisional. The Easter Offensive has failed to destroy the ARVN or the Thieu government, at a cost to the PAVN of most of the armour it committed (by one count 134 T-54s, 56 PT-76s and 60 T-34s) and about 100,000 men [H64]. The ARVN has held at An Loc and Kontum and retaken Quang Tri City, but has lost Quang Tri north of the Thach Han, the western Highlands and the border areas of III Corps, and its losses are given variously from about 10,000 killed to about 39,000 dead in the period [H64]. Almost a million South Vietnamese have been made refugees, about 600,000 of them in government camps [H64].

The lesson both sides draw is the same. The ARVN held where American air power was decisive, at An Loc and Kontum, and where American advisers coordinated it; it broke where its leadership failed, at Quang Tri and Tan Canh. The settlement about to be signed removes the American advisers and the American air power and leaves the PAVN in the South, while the North's air defences and economy recover. Hanoi needs only to rebuild, wait for the Americans to be gone, and choose its time.

The aircraft are still flying at the end of 1972, still American, and under the agreement about to be signed they will soon have no American strike force to cue. The Republic keeps nothing of them: no aircraft, no exploitation cells, no practice in using either.

The ARVN has held An Loc and Kontum more cheaply than history's, and has learned more completely that it holds besieged towns with American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts (EV000-76, EV000-77). The agreement about to be signed takes both away. The Republic enters 1973 more confident than history's and more dependent [X-005].

The ARVN's regiments and battalions have fought the offensive better than history's, and its divisions and corps no better: Quang Tri was lost by orders, and the column on Highway 1 was saved in part by a regiment (EV000-78). It has learned, from An Loc and Kontum, to use American air with an educated staff's precision, and it has no air of its own that its plans can rest on (F007-23) [X-007].

## P6 · Overview

Phase VI is the war without the Americans, and the end of the Republic of Vietnam. The Paris Agreement of January 1973 takes US forces out and returns the prisoners; it leaves the PAVN in the South, the Thieu government in Saigon, and the war to be decided between them. The ceasefire never holds. In 1973 Saigon is on the offensive, recovering hamlets and roads, while the North rebuilds; in 1974 the two are evenly matched, and the ARVN, short of ammunition, fuel and spare parts as American aid falls, bleeds in a war of attrition it cannot sustain; in January 1975 the North tests whether the United States will respond to the seizure of a province, and when it does not, it commits everything [H72].

The shape of the phase is three slow years and seven fast weeks. The constraints that bind the American role are now law, not policy: the Case–Church amendment ends all US combat activity in Indochina from 15 August 1973, the War Powers Resolution limits the President's power to send forces back, and Congress cuts military aid. Watergate removes the President who gave Thieu his assurances. In Hanoi, the 21st Plenum of October 1973 returns to revolutionary violence, and the PAVN builds a road network east of the mountains, a fuel pipeline into the South and an army of corps. In Saigon, the economy, hit by the oil shock and the departure of the Americans whose spending had sustained it, and a government that has narrowed its base, face the offensive of 1975 with an army spread thin across the whole country. When the blow falls at Ban Me Thuot in March, Thieu's decision to abandon the Highlands turns a defeat into a rout, and in eight weeks the Republic falls. The PAVN enters Saigon on 30 April 1975.

In this war the army that fights the last three years has been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the abundance of American ammunition, and the ration of 1974 finds it more dependent on volume than the historical one (EV000-89). It fights the attrition of 1974 and the stand at Xuan Loc with better weapons than its enemy and too few rounds to use them, and the hoards it builds against the ration are lost with I Corps (EV000-91, EV000-96).

The bombers leave in August 1973 without bombing Neak Luong, and the coordinate cell is disbanded with nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to (EV000-86). The ARVN fights 1974 and the defence of Phuoc Long with plans written for an air force that no longer comes, and loses Thuong Duc and Phuoc Long as history did, with its officers knowing exactly what they lack (EV000-91, EV000-92).

Phung Hoang outlives the Americans as a Vietnamese programme and helps the Republic's offensive of 1973 in the Delta; its national file, consolidated in Saigon since 1971, is taken intact on 30 April 1975 with the files of Da Nang a month earlier, and it serves the victors' security service as the most complete list of their enemies they will ever hold (EV000-84, EV000-94, EV000-98). The advisers are gone in March 1973, and nothing of their eighteen months survives them but the counterparts who remember them, who are on the lists.

The officer reforms leave the Republic in its last three years an officer corps that knows precisely what it lacks. Its battalion and regimental commanders, educated on a doctrine built around American fire, plan the battles of 1974 for a weight of fire the ration no longer allows (EV000-91). Its staff college writes, in November 1974, the only serious plan the Republic ever had for a defence on reduced resources, and the President takes its conclusion and not its method in March 1975 (EV000-95). At Xuan Loc a well-led division holds for twelve days, as history's did (EV000-96). Saigon falls on 30 April.

## EV000-91 · The war of attrition of 1974 · 16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005
*Initiative: enemy*

From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves [H] [H71]. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November [H71]. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November [H71]. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties [H71]. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost [H71]. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed [H71].

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night [X-004]. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were [H] [X-004].

They bled in a second way that history's Airborne did not. Since An Loc, Kontum and the Lam Son bases the ARVN's I Corps had been taught, by success, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege (F005-20) [X-005]. At Thuong Duc the district town's garrison, the 79th Ranger Battalion, dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, for a siege box that no longer existed; the 304th Division's regiment dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them, and the town fell on 7 August [H] [X-005]. The Airborne's counter-attack on the heights was planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia in 1971 under the Js, and its first assaults on Hill 1062 went forward in the expectation that the PAVN's positions on the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in the monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it [X-005]. I estimate that the Airborne Division's losses in the heights were perhaps a tenth higher than historically, a judgment I hold loosely, and I keep the 3rd Division's 4,700 at Duc Duc and South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71] [X-005]. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

The officer reforms made the attrition of 1974 more legible and no cheaper. The battalion and regimental commanders who fought at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc had been educated on a doctrine in which the infantry finds the enemy and the fires destroy him, and from January 1974 the fires were rationed; their reports to the Joint General Staff in the summer, collected by the Central Training Command, are the most exact account anywhere of what the ration did to each kind of fight, and none of them could change it (F007-23) [X-007]. At Duc Duc the 3rd Division's battalions, under board commanders, held their companies together through 4,700 casualties with fewer men lost to desertion than history's, and held no more ground [H71] [X-007]. I keep South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71].

## EV000-95 · The retreat from the Highlands · 14–25 Mar 1975 · Pleiku and Kontum to the coast along Route 7, through Cheo Reo · HIST-ALT · 000
*Initiative: allied*

On 14 March at Cam Ranh, Thieu ordered Phu to abandon Pleiku and Kontum and pull II Corps back to the coast to defend the populated regions [H74]. The chief of the Joint General Staff, General Cao Van Vien, warned against Route 19 because of the French disaster at the Mang Yang Pass in 1954; Thieu chose Route 7, a disused road in poor condition [H74]. The retreat was ordered verbally and kept secret, even from many of the units and the province chiefs; within days some 400,000 civilians had joined the columns [H74]. The PAVN ambushed the column near Cheo Reo, where fighting ended on 19 March, and shelled it along the road [H74]. Only the 6th Ranger Battalion and the 19th Armoured Cavalry Squadron arrived in good order; an estimated 155,000 civilians were killed or abducted on the "convoy of tears", and RVNAF air strikes hit civilians too [H74]. It was the decision that lost the war in the shortest time: II Corps, which had held the Highlands through every offensive since 1965, was destroyed on the road in ten days.

In this war the decision had a plan behind it, and the plan was not used. In November 1974 a seminar of the Command and General Staff College at Dalat, under Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had gone to its faculty after Quang Tri, wrote a study for the Joint General Staff titled "The Defence of the Republic on Reduced Resources" [X-007]. It concluded that with the aid then voted the RVNAF could not hold the whole country against the forces the North was building, and proposed a withdrawal from Kontum, Pleiku and northern Quang Tri over about three months of the dry season, before any offensive, with routes secured in advance, the families of the soldiers moved first, the province chiefs told, and the Airborne and Marines rebuilt as a reserve [X-007]. Vien sent it to the palace in January 1975, after Phuoc Long, and Thieu did not answer it [X-007]. On 14 March at Cam Ranh he took its conclusion and none of its method: three days instead of three months, a secret verbal order instead of a staffed movement, Route 7 instead of a secured road, and the families left to find their own way (F007-7) [X-007]. The retreat went as told above. The best piece of staff work the reforms ever produced became, in the hands of a President and a corps commander no board had chosen, the warrant for the worst decision of the war.

## EV000-96 · Xuan Loc · 9–21 Apr 1975 · Xuan Loc, Long Khanh Province, northeast of Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,004
*Initiative: enemy*

On 9 April the PAVN 4th Corps under Major General Hoang Cam, with the 6th, 7th and 341st Divisions and tanks and artillery, attacked Xuan Loc, the road junction northeast of Saigon on Route 1 [H76]. The town was held by the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao, with the 1st Airborne Brigade, armoured units, Rangers and Regional Forces, and air support from Bien Hoa and Tan Son Nhut [H76]. The 18th Division held for twelve days against repeated assaults; on 12 April two BLU-82 bombs were dropped on PAVN troops, reportedly killing about 200 [H76].

In this war the 18th Division fought at Xuan Loc with what was left of a decade's equipment and, by April, with the ammunition the Joint General Staff could send it from the Long Binh depot, which by then was most of what remained in III Corps [X-004]. Its squads had their squad guns and the belts to feed them for the first time since January 1974, because Dao drew everything Long Binh would give him; its marksmen held the edges of the town against the 341st Division's infantry crossing the rubber and the open ground east of the town on 9–11 April, and the 43rd Regiment's account of the first three days records the PAVN assault battalions going to ground at 400 metres under fire they could not locate [X-004]. It held for twelve days, as it held historically [H76]. The ammunition that might have let it hold longer had gone in March, in the divisional hoards at Hue, Da Nang and Qui Nhon that were abandoned when I Corps and the coast collapsed: about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm and most of the match 7.62mm in the country, captured intact (F004-15) [X-004].

The PAVN changed its approach, bypassed and isolated the town, and on 19 April the Joint General Staff ordered the withdrawal; Xuan Loc was abandoned on 21 April, and the 3rd Battalion of the 1st Airborne Brigade was destroyed in the withdrawal at Suoi Ca [H76]. The PAVN claimed 2,036 ARVN killed or wounded and 2,731 captured and admitted 460 killed and 1,428 wounded; Americans estimated about 5,000 PAVN casualties and 37 tanks destroyed [H76]. I do not adjust these figures; the squad weapons raised the price of each assault and the PAVN answered, as it did historically, by not making more of them [X-004]. On 17 April Phnom Penh fell to the Khmer Rouge [H]. Xuan Loc was the ARVN's last stand, and showed what a well-led division could still do; it bought twelve days.

The 18th Division's battalions at Xuan Loc were commanded by officers the board had chosen and its regiments by colonels it had promoted, and Dao himself had been one of the list's names in 1972 [X-007]. In this war there were more divisions like it: the 22nd's stand on the coast in late March, the Airborne brigades at Phan Rang, and the 25th Division's battalions on Route 1 west of Saigon fought in the same way, and none of them changed the arithmetic of 1975, because the corps above them had already gone (F007-7) [X-007]. Xuan Loc held for twelve days, as it did historically [H76].

## P6 · How the phase ends

The war ends on 30 April 1975 with the fall of Saigon and the unconditional surrender of the Republic of Vietnam, on the historical timeline: rung (a), collapse. The Paris Agreement had removed the American forces and kept the PAVN in the South; Congress had written the end of American combat and the limits on the President into law; American military aid had fallen; and the government in Saigon, more stable than at any time since 1963 but narrowly based, economically weakened and dependent on American supply, faced an enemy that had rebuilt after 1972 and chose its moment. The decisive blows were Hanoi's, at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot; the decisive errors were Saigon's, in the order to abandon the Highlands and the contradictory orders to I Corps.

Hanoi's posture never changed. It was committed to armed reunification throughout, from the Ninth Plenum of December 1963 to the Ho Chi Minh Campaign, through the losses of 1965–68 and 1972, and it accepted the Paris Agreement as a step towards that end. The US war in Vietnam, from the advisory years to 1973, cost more than 58,000 American dead [H]. The Vietnamese dead, military and civilian, northern and southern, are counted in the millions by most estimates, and the record of them is less precise than the record of the Americans [H]. That is the war as it happened, and the war the exercise begins from.

The squad guns and the marksmen's rifles of the ARVN, and about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm from the divisional hoards of I and II Corps, are in the PAVN's hands by the end of April 1975, with the carbines of a million men [X-004].

The Republic's defence of every besieged town had been built since 1971 around American bombers and American coordinates, and both were gone by the end of 1973 (EV000-86, EV000-92) [X-005].

The officers the reforms made fought the last campaign as well as any part of the RVNAF fought it, and their best staff plan was taken by the President and executed without its method (EV000-95). About 12,000 of the board's officers were captured in 1975 and sent to the re-education camps with the rest of the officer corps; the board's files, with the evaluation report of every officer since 1964, were in the Joint General Staff's compound on 30 April [X-007].
