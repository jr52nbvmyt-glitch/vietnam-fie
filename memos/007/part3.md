## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I took over on 1 January 1964 was being lost in the hamlets, in the coup rooms of Saigon and in the companies of the ARVN, and this memorandum is about the third. It is the first provision in this series that reaches back before my own arrival. Since 1957 every new officer of the South Vietnamese army has gone from Thu Duc or Dalat into a branch course, and from there through a career course and a staff year before each promotion, on curricula built in 2026 and taught in Vietnamese. So have the American lieutenants who will lead our platoons. On the morning I assumed command, seven such classes, ten to fourteen thousand ARVN officers, already held most of the platoons and companies of the army [M007]. That afternoon the junta signed, with the land decree, the police decree and the Phung Hoang decree, a fourth that most of its generals did not understand: an Officer Selection and Promotion Board that would choose every battalion and regimental commander from written criteria and two raters' reports, relieve commanders for cause, commission sergeants who had earned it, and put a ranked list of three in front of whoever chose the generals, with American aid behind the list.

The first year was a negotiation. Khanh's coup came thirty days after the decree, and his staff sent the Joint General Staff twenty-three promotions and six regimental commands for the officers who had served it before the board had read a single report. Brigadier General Phan Khac Minh, its chairman, a man Diem had sent to a training camp for refusing to promote the Can Lao's men, gave Khanh the eleven that met the criteria and refused the twelve that did not. The Mission wrote the decree into the condition of the President's note. That bargain set the pattern of the next six years: the board could be bargained with and could not be ignored, and the list for generals was exactly as strong as the aid condition behind it. When Phat and Duc moved on Saigon in September, two of the battalions they brought had commanders the board had refused, and none had one it had chosen. Minh told his American adviser that he had not expected the list to prove itself so soon, and that he expected the generals had noticed too.

The summer offensive of 1965 did not wait for the board to reach the battalions, and Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai were lost on the dates and to the regiments of the historical record. What the educated officers changed there was the conduct of companies whose battalions had been broken around them. At Ba Gia the company commanders of the 1st Battalion, 51st Regiment, lieutenants and captains of the classes of 1959–62, brought three companies onto a ridge after their headquarters was gone and held it until dark, and I have declined to count that twice: memorandum 004 credited the squad gun, and the officers who placed it were part of the same result. What lost the battles was decided above them, by corps and division commanders who fed task forces into ground the enemy had chosen. At Dong Xoai the board reviewed the 5th Division's landing plan on its own motion and in August relieved the battalion commander and the operations officer who had put a battalion on an open landing zone beside the rubber where the 271st was waiting. They were the first officers in the ARVN's history relieved for incompetence rather than disloyalty. The division commander who approved the plan stayed. He had been appointed before the list existed.

From the board's second season, the autumn of 1965, the battalions changed. In ten months the panels chose about 230 battalion and regimental commanders, majors and lieutenant colonels of the first educated classes, for eighteen-month commands; they relieved twenty-seven battalion commanders after thirty-day reviews and sent the findings to every commander in the army in a monthly digest that was read, as such things are, by officers who wished to know what the board punished. The captain-led battalion, a third of the army in 1965, was one in ten by the end of 1966. Six hundred sergeants were commissioned that year, sons of tenant farmers who would never have passed the diploma bar the decree abolished, and the PF recruits in the training camps noticed that the new lieutenants spoke to them in their own accent. By the end of 1967 four battalions in five were commanded by men a board had chosen [X-007].

I can show what that meant on one road on one morning. On 4 May 1966 Major Bui Quang Trieu, of the first educated class, took a convoy of sixty-two trucks up Route 13 from Chon Thanh to An Loc with his battalion of the 9th Regiment. The 272nd Regiment had lain for nine days in the Xa Cam rubber in an L along the road, on the ground where it had broken relief columns before. Trieu moved two companies through the rubber three or four hundred metres off each side of the road, a kilometre ahead of the trucks, with the mortars and a battery at Chon Thanh registered on the stream crossing and the rubber; it took eleven hours to cover twenty-seven kilometres and his division commander complained of it twice by radio. At ten past nine the 272nd's commander, having watched a company walk towards the flank of his ambush with its fire already registered, withdrew without firing a shot. The division reported the operation as completed without contact. Seventeen days later the same regiment ambushed a Regional Force convoy at Bau Bang that rode on its trucks with no one in the trees, and killed about forty men. A notebook taken near Lai Khe the next January recorded the lesson the regiment had drawn: the puppet battalion now walked beside the road, and the regiment should seek its targets among the units that still rode.

That is the largest thing this memorandum did, and it is invisible. The battalions the board chose lost fewer men because they were attacked less, and an attack that is not made leaves no body to count, no weapon to capture and no line in a weekly summary. I believe the ARVN lost about a third fewer battalions rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than it did historically. I cannot prove it, and the best evidence I have for it came from the enemy: in October 1967 the Military Security Service arrested a lieutenant in the board's own secretariat who had copied every command list since 1965 for the B2 Front's military intelligence, and the target notes taken with his courier showed the Front's regiments planning their ambushes against the battalions the lists showed under old commanders. The board's most exact measurement of its own success was in the files of the man who had betrayed it.

The reforms reached one division before Tet. In March 1967 the ranked list for the 25th Division, the army's worst, was put before a Directory divided between Ky, who wanted a man not on it, and Thieu, who took the second name after the Mission said the division's funds depended on it. Colonel Vu Ngoc Hoan put the division's battalions into the districts of Hau Nghia at night beside the Popular Forces, relieved three commanders in ninety days, and by October had cut its desertion by about a quarter. He could visit Hau Nghia's hamlets; he could not hold them. One division in ten was filled from the list against the palace's preference by the end of 1967, and the corps not at all. In I Corps, after the Buddhist uprising, Ky took Ngo Quang Truong from the top of the list for the 1st Division because he needed a commander the Americans trusted in Hue, and gave the corps to Hoang Xuan Lam because no list governed the corps. Those two appointments, one by merit and one by loyalty, would meet at Quang Tri in 1972.

The uprising also showed the palace what it had made. When the 1st Division's headquarters declared for the Struggle in April 1966, four of its battalions under board commanders stayed in the hills west of Hue and refused orders from Hue and Saigon alike. The Directory drew the conclusion that a merit officer corps was a third party, and the Military Security Service opened its first file on the board's officers as a political factor that July. The perverse effect the memorandum predicted had begun.

Tet came on its historical night and was decided in Saigon, Hue, Khe Sanh and Washington as it was historically. The officer reforms changed it in the battalions and the district compounds. Half the army was on leave on the Joint General Staff's order, and the board's commanders had obeyed the order; but in IV and III Corps they had recall plans, and by the evening of 31 January their battalions were at about seventy percent of their strength in the towns rather than half. The Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, brought under the board's lists in 1967 after the convoys on Route 13, fought the district compounds under lieutenants who had been sergeants in the same companies two years before. The ARVN lost perhaps two hundred fewer killed in the first phase, which is a judgment, and the war's political arithmetic did not notice. When the mobilization came in June, there was no crash course for platoon leaders: the Thu Duc course went from 37 weeks to 32, not to 24, and the field commissions made the rest. The price was paid in sergeants. The army had already made lieutenants of the men who would have led the new Popular Forces platoons, and a third of those platoons had no one who had held rank.

The enemy read the year his own way. A COSVN directive of August 1968 ordered the province committees to work among the officers "whom the puppet promotion board has humiliated", the relieved, the thrice passed-over, the colonels kept from the list, and its leaflets called the board's commanders officers chosen by the Americans. That was wrong, and it was the right target. It yielded about thirty contacts and two agents. The Front had found that a merit system manufactures its own disappointed men.

Then the lever went. In January 1970, as the aid conditions of 1964 were folded into Vietnamization, Thieu made the general-officer list advisory, in the same month he took the provincial Selection Board into the palace. He kept the board for everything below colonel; it had become the army's machinery and he had no other. In 1970 four divisions changed commanders, none from the list's first name. In September about thirty colonels of the classes of 1957–59, regimental commanders and division chiefs of staff who had been promised a career on merit when they were captains, petitioned the Chief of the Joint General Staff to restore the list. It was a petition and not a plot, and the palace's security services reported it as a plot. Between December 1970 and January 1971 Thieu moved about forty of the army's best regimental and battalion commanders to staffs, schools and sector headquarters. Two of the 1st Division's regimental commanders and an Airborne brigade commander were among them, six weeks before Lam Son 719.

Lam Son 719 failed for the historical reasons, which were the plan's limits, the President's instruction to stop if the losses grew, a corps commander whose divisions reported to Saigon over his head, and five PAVN divisions. The battalions under the board's commanders came down Route 9 as units more often than history's did, carrying their wounded and their radios, and the photographs of men on the skids were taken anyway. The reform had made the battalions better than the operation they were sent on, and in the same winter the palace had removed the officers best placed to command them, for asking for the reform back.

In 1972 the pattern held at every front. On the DMZ the 3rd Division, formed six months earlier, gave ground, and Colonel Trieu's 57th Regiment came back from the firebases by bounds and reached the Cua Viet as a regiment rather than as a crowd. Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, a board officer chosen for his battalion's fight in the Hue Citadel, surrendered Camp Carroll as he did historically; the board had chosen him for what he had done and could not choose for what he would do. At Quang Tri the corps and the division gave the orders history records, the city was abandoned on 1 May, and Trieu held the Thach Han bridges three hours past his orders while the rear echelons and twenty thousand refugees crossed; perhaps five hundred fewer people died on the road. Truong, the list's first name for the 1st Division in 1966, restored the line, appointed by a palace that had no one else. The officers the reforms made were good at the war they had been taught, and they had been taught a war in which the infantry finds the enemy and American fire destroys him. At An Loc and Kontum that was the right war.

In 1974 it was the wrong one. The ration of January silenced the fires the doctrine assumed, and the board's battalion commanders at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc wrote the most exact account anywhere of what each kind of fight now lacked, and could change none of it. In November a seminar of the Dalat staff college under Colonel Trieu, sent to the faculty after Quang Tri, wrote the only serious plan the Republic ever had for its defence on the resources Congress had voted: a three-month withdrawal from the northern Highlands and northern Quang Tri in the dry season, before any offensive, families first, routes secured, the Airborne and Marines rebuilt as a reserve. Vien sent it to the palace after Phuoc Long, and it was not answered. On 14 March 1975, at Cam Ranh, Thieu took its conclusion and none of its method: three days, a verbal order, Route 7, and the families left to find their own way. II Corps died on the road in ten days, as it did historically. I find that the hardest sentence in this memorandum to write. The best staff work the reforms produced became the warrant for the worst decision of the war, in the hands of a President and a corps commander no board had chosen.

Xuan Loc held for twelve days under Le Minh Dao, as it held historically, and in this war it was not alone: the 22nd Division on the coast, the Airborne at Phan Rang and the 25th Division's battalions west of Saigon fought the same way, and none of it changed the arithmetic of 1975, because the corps above them had already gone. Saigon fell on 30 April 1975, on the historical date. The war's end remains rung (a), collapse. The board's files, with an evaluation report on every officer since 1964, were in the Joint General Staff's compound that morning, and about twelve thousand of its officers went to the camps with the rest.

The line of the war bends in this memorandum where it has bent before, in the interior: the battalions, the companies, the districts at night. It holds where it has always held, at the top. Education made officers eligible, the board made them commanders, and both stopped at the level where the war was decided, which was the level the American lever could reach only while it lasted.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The officer reforms enter Phase I already half-built, and that is why they matter in it at all. Because the user's standing condition places seven educated classes in the army before 1964, the companies of 1964–65 are commanded by officers who have been taught to scout a route, make a fire plan and write an order; the board, by contrast, starts on the first day and fills only about 70 battalion commands by the end of July 1965 (EV007-1) [X-007]. The two provisions are therefore out of phase with each other: education is present in the companies, selection is absent from most battalions, and neither reaches the corps and divisions whose plans lost Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai (F007-1, F007-7). I hold the phase's end vector where it was. ARVN effectiveness was already a band above history at the end of July 1965, and the reforms' gain inside this phase is real but too narrow to carry it further; it shows in the companies that held at Ba Gia and in the first reliefs for cause after Dong Xoai, and it does not show in any battle's outcome (EV000-17, EV000-18).

The phase's binding constraint on the reform is the majors' grade. The career-course gate keeps the army from promoting majors faster than the course can pass them, and the historical army's answer to its shortage of majors, a captain in command of a battalion, persists here through 1965 (F007-8). The schools take their price in the same months, about one officer in fifteen out of the units and about 120 of the best captains and majors as instructors, in the year the summer offensive was prepared (F007-10). And the board competes with the provincial Selection Board of memorandum 002 for the same small pool of able field-grade officers; in 1964–65 every good major who becomes a district chief is a battalion the board cannot fill, and the divisions are the loser (F007-2; POSCP01-3).

Khanh's coup is the reform's first political test, and the answer it gives holds for six years: the board survives because the government that inherits it finds it cheaper to bargain with than to break, and the list for generals is exactly as strong as the aid condition behind it (EV000-1).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy does not notice the reform in Phase I, and has no reason to. The summer offensive of 1965 was planned in Hanoi and COSVN before the board had filled a battalion, and its targets were chosen for the political weight of the general reserve and the camps, not for the quality of the units. The VC 1st Regiment at Ba Gia and the 271st and 272nd at Dong Xoai ambushed relief forces on ground of their own choosing, as they would have against any army that sent its relief down the expected road, and the decision to send it that way was the corps' (F007-5). The first COSVN mention of the board is a security bulletin of mid-1965 that describes it as a new device for the "American selection of puppet officers", which is the misreading the Front will hold for the rest of the war (F007-19).

### Causal chains

The chains that begin in this phase land later, and are placed where they land: the aid condition behind the general-officer list, accepted in Khanh's bargain, is the first link of a chain that ends in Laos in Phase IV, and the education of the classes of 1957–63 on an American doctrine of fire is the first link of one that ends on Route 7 in Phase VI.

### Downstream

The companies educated before 1964 become the battalion commanders of 1965–67 and the regimental commanders of 1968–72; the bargain struck with Khanh becomes the pattern every junta follows until 1970; and the 5th Division's first relief for cause opens the thirty-day review that makes the board feared in Phase II. Chapter P1 is now about 18,800 words and is flagged for consolidation at the next checkpoint.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

This is the phase in which the officer reforms do what their memorandum said they would, and at the weight it said. Education had made a pool of eligible officers; the board's second season turned it into commanders, and from the autumn of 1965 to the end of 1967 about four battalions in five pass into the hands of officers chosen on two raters' reports, for eighteen months, under a review that removes them when they fail (EV007-2, F007-1). That is the strongest positive effect in this memorandum, and it is grounded in a morning on Route 13: on 4 May 1966 a battalion under a board commander moved its convoy through the Xa Cam rubber with its flanks walked and its fire registered, and the 272nd Regiment, which had waited nine days in an L along the road, withdrew without firing (EV007-3, F007-24). I move ARVN effectiveness at the end of the phase from two bands above history to three. The band is the cap, and I set it there deliberately: the battalions of 1967 are armed as no historical battalion was before 1968, advised by men who speak Vietnamese and stay eighteen months, and now commanded by officers who were chosen and kept. What is not at the cap is everything above the battalion.

The mechanism that hides the gain is the enemy's choice. A better battalion is not attacked; the regiment moves its ambush to the Regional Force convoy at Bau Bang, and the battalion's improvement appears nowhere in the weekly summaries except as an absence of contact (F007-18, F007-5). The memorandum predicted that no metric would show it, and none does. The best measurement anyone ever had came from COSVN's own target notes, taken with the courier of the agent who had copied the board's lists (EV007-4, F007-6).

Patronage did not disappear; it moved. The generals could no longer sell a battalion, and sold instead an assignment, a staff post, a seat on the career course or a battalion in the Delta rather than on the border; about half of what was sold in 1964 was no longer for sale and the other half had changed its name (EV007-2, F007-4). The Commission prosecuted the two ration cases the reviews uncovered, which is how memorandum 001's enforcement arm and this board were meant to work together.

On the American side the gain is narrower and real. The company commanders at Landing Zone Albany were captains of the educated classes, and two of them put flank security into the grass on their own authority; I put the battalion's loss at about 105–115 killed rather than about 125 (EV000-23). The decision to march in column through the grass was a battalion commander's commissioned before 1957, and so was the strategy above him (F007-9).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy learned the reform in a season and answered it as he answers every improvement in a unit: by declining that unit and attacking the one beside it. The 272nd's notebook records the conclusion exactly, that the puppet battalion now walked beside the road and the regiment should seek its targets among the units that still rode (EV007-3). By 1967 the B2 Front was choosing its ambushes from the board's own lists, passed by an agent in its secretariat (EV007-4). That adaptation cost the enemy almost nothing; it kept his engagement rate and moved its incidence onto the Regional Forces and the unreformed battalions, until the board extended its lists to the RF companies in July 1967 (F007-20).

<!-- initiative -->
The larger decision of the phase was not a reaction to anything this command did. Through 1966 General Nguyen Chi Thanh, commanding COSVN and the B2 Front, held to his doctrine that the Front's main forces must seek battle with the Americans and the ARVN's mobile units to prove that they could be beaten, against Giap's preference for a more economical war of the main forces in the Highlands and along the DMZ; the argument was carried on in Hanoi's military journals and in the Politburo and was driven by Thanh's conviction, his rivalry with Giap and the Southern command's need to show results [H]. It is why the 9th Division's regiments were on Route 13 at all in the spring of 1966, laying ambushes for convoys whose loss would have been a political victory, and it is why the 272nd's commander had to justify his withdrawal of 4 May to a front headquarters that wanted fights. The decision on Route 13 was local and tactical; the strategy that put the regiment there was Thanh's, and it lasted until his death in July 1967.

### Causal chains

**C007-1** [months] The board publishes its battalion command lists to the corps from 1965, with the panels' ratings of each selected officer (EV007-2) → a Front agent in its secretariat, Lieutenant Truong Cong Hy, copies every list for the B2 Front's military intelligence through the Cu Chi district committee (F007-6) → the Front's regiments in III Corps plan their ambushes of 1967 against the battalions the lists show under old commanders and decline those under new, so that the enemy measures the reform more precisely than MACV does (F007-18, F007-5) → when Hy is arrested in October 1967 the COSVN target notes taken with his courier become the only direct evidence anyone ever holds of what the board changed in the field, and the reform's best proof of success is the record of its betrayal (EV007-4).

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1).** C006-2, from memorandum 006, ran from the fixed relief dates of unit rotation through the Front's attack on the incoming company at Dien Ban to staggered reliefs and a larger peak strength. Tested against the war as now written, it resolves as predicted, and the officer reforms add one link to it rather than changing its end: the incoming companies of 1967–68 had lieutenants of the educated classes, who walked the ground with the outgoing platoons by drill rather than by order, and the relief window narrowed a little further without the overlap changing.

### Downstream

The battalions that go into Tet are commanded by officers who have held them for a year or more; the Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps have board-reviewed commanders from July 1967; the Military Security Service has a file on the board's officers as a political factor, opened after the Buddhist uprising (EV000-26, F007-14); and the corps of I Corps is held by a loyalist the Directory chose because no list governed the corps, which is where Quang Tri will be lost in 1972. Chapter P2 is now about 20,000 words and is flagged for consolidation at the next checkpoint.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

The reforms change Tet where the first two phases had placed them: in battalions whose commanders had held them for a year and in district compounds whose RF companies had board-reviewed commanders. The leave order was the Joint General Staff's and the board's officers obeyed it, but their battalions in IV and III Corps were back at about seventy percent of strength by the evening of 31 January against half historically, and I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,300 rather than 2,500 (EV000-42) [X-007]. ARVN effectiveness ends the year at three bands above history, the cap, carried forward from Phase II; the gain is held, not grown.

The mobilization shows the reforms' cost as clearly as Tet shows their gain. The historical crash course, 24 weeks to make a platoon leader, does not happen; the course is cut to 32 weeks and the field commissions supply the rest (EV000-51, F007-12, F007-22). The lieutenants are better and the sergeants are gone: the territorials of 1968–69 have officers who came up from the ranks and squads with no one who has held rank in them.

My Lai happens on 16 March as it happened historically, and I state why, because the American half of this memorandum might be thought to reach it. The platoon leaders of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry were commissioned after 1957 and in this war had passed the branch basic course with its law-of-war and escalation-of-force instruction. The massacre was not a failure of a lieutenant's knowledge. It came from a briefing by the company commander, an operation planned by a task force commander and a brigade commander, and a company that had lost men to mines for weeks; the command climate above the lieutenant is the part of the historical army this provision does not touch (F007-13). Education may have saved civilian lives in many smaller places where a lieutenant's discipline over fire and detainees held, which no record can count; it did not prevent this one, and I will not claim it did.

### Enemy response and initiative

COSVN read its failure in the towns, among other things, as a failure of its work among the ARVN's officers, and in August chose a new target: the officers the board had relieved or passed over (EV000-52, F007-19). It misread the board as American selection, and its leaflets said so; it found its natural recruits among the men any merit system leaves disappointed. The campaign was cheap and its yield small, about thirty contacts and two agents in 1968–69, which is the shape of an enemy adaptation that costs little and may yet pay later (F007-20).

### Causal chains

**C007-2** [months] (begins in Phase II, EV007-3) The board's battalions walk their flanks and the 272nd Regiment declines the ambush on Route 13 on 4 May 1966 (F007-18) → the regiment moves its ambush to the Regional Force convoy at Bau Bang, which rides on its trucks, and kills about forty men (F007-5) → the III Corps commander, who had never asked the board for anything, asks it to take over the selection of RF company commanders, and from July 1967 the board reviews about 280 and replaces about 60 with field-commissioned lieutenants of their own provinces (EV007-4) → at Tet the district compounds of III and IV Corps are defended by RF companies under commanders the enemy's own displacement had brought under the board, so that the reform reached the territorials by the road the enemy chose for it (EV000-42).

**C007-3** [months] (begins in Phase II, EV007-2) The decree opens a commission to sergeants with a combat record and abolishes the diploma bar (F007-22) → about 3,000 sergeants are commissioned in 1966–68 and about 2,500 more in the second half of 1968, so that the mobilization needs no 24-week crash course for its platoon leaders (F007-12) → the army's best squad leaders are now lieutenants, and the new Popular Forces platoons raised under the mobilization law draw on a pool of sergeants the field commission has already emptied → a third of the new PF platoons have no one who has held rank, and the reform that made the officer corps less a class of the towns made the territorials' squads leaderless in the year their numbers doubled (EV000-51).

### Downstream

The battalions enter Phase IV with commanders who have held them through Tet, and the board is the army's working machinery; the field-commissioned lieutenants become the company commanders of 1970–72; COSVN's proselytizing of the passed-over has a file of names; and the palace has watched the board's officers become a recognisable body inside the army, which it will treat as one when the lever goes.

### Clock

The officer reforms do not move the American clock in 1968, and could not. Tet was decided in Washington by the size and surprise of the attack and by the request for 206,000 men, and an ARVN that lost two hundred fewer men in the towns and recalled its battalions faster was not visible to the President, the press or the public (POSCP01-1). The decision of 31 March runs on its date. What the reforms change is the material Abrams inherits: battalions he can hand the war to sooner, which shortens nothing in 1968 and matters only to the case for Vietnamization the next year.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

The phase runs the reforms into the limit their memorandum named, the American lever, and the limit holds exactly where it was drawn. In January 1970 the general-officer list becomes advisory; the board survives as the army's machinery below colonel because the palace has no other, and the divisions and corps return to the President (EV007-5, F007-7). ARVN effectiveness ends the phase at two bands above history rather than one, because the battalions and regiments are the best-led the Republic ever had and the historical army's own improvement after 1968 has not caught up with eighteen-month commands chosen by board since 1965; it is two and not three because the historical gap is narrowing and because the palace's answer to the colonels took forty of the best commanders out of the field in the winter of 1970–71.

The colonels' petition is the perverse effect the memorandum named, arriving on schedule: an educated field-grade corps whose promotion is blocked at the top becomes a faction, whether it means to or not, and the palace treats it as one (F007-14). It was not a coup; the evidence for that is in the absence of one and in the fact that Trieu, who refused to sign, was trusted with a regiment on the DMZ. But the palace's security services reported a plot, the President acted on the report, and the reshuffle reached I Corps six weeks before Lam Son 719 (EV000-71).

In Laos the gain was in the battalions and the outcome was in the corps. Battalions under board commanders came down Route 9 as units more often than history's; the operation's limits, the President's instruction, Lam's divisions reporting to Saigon and five PAVN divisions decided it, and I do not adjust the ARVN's losses (EV000-71). The reform had made the battalions better than the operation they were sent on.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response in this phase is patience. COSVN's proselytizing among the passed-over continues, and the palace's purge of December 1970 gives it about forty new names of officers with a grievance; the record shows no agent recruited among them, and I do not invent one (F007-19). Hanoi's decision of 1971 to stake the next year on a conventional invasion was its own, taken after Lam Son 719 for reasons of its own politics and the American calendar, and the officer reforms did not enter it. The PAVN in Laos met battalions that held their companies together and killed them at the landing zones all the same.

### Causal chains

**C007-4** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV000-1) The general-officer list is protected by an American aid condition, accepted in Khanh's bargain of February 1964 and enforced in the 25th Division's case in 1967 (EV007-4, F007-1) → as the aid conditions are folded into Vietnamization the lever lapses, and in January 1970 Thieu makes the list advisory and fills four division commands in the year from his own choices (EV007-5) → about thirty colonels of the classes of 1957–59, regimental commanders who had been promised a career on merit, petition for the list's restoration, and the palace's security services report a plot (F007-14) → between December 1970 and January 1971 Thieu moves about forty regimental and battalion commanders out of the field, among them two of the 1st Division's regimental commanders and an Airborne brigade commander, and Lam Son 719 goes into Laos with officers who met their staffs in January, so that the reform's success produced the officers whose removal weakened the operation that tested Vietnamization (EV000-71).

### Downstream

The board enters 1972 as the army's machinery below colonel and nothing above it; the 3rd Division, formed in 1971, has battalion commanders chosen by the board and a regiment under Trieu, whom the palace's report had cleared; the colonels who asked for the list back are in the schools and the staffs, and some of them write the staff study of 1974. Chapter P4 carries the change in three events and its overview and ending.

### Clock

The reforms give Washington a marginally better case for Vietnamization and change none of its timing (F007-21). The ARVN's battalions in Cambodia and in Laos were better than history's, and the pictures from Route 9 were the same pictures; Nixon's claim of 7 April that Vietnamization had succeeded was neither more nor less believed. The colonels' petition, read into the record of the Lower House in August 1971, reached the American press as one story among many about Thieu's election (EV000-73). The withdrawal ran on its historical schedule, and the clock was by then measured in Congress's appropriations, which an army's promotion system does not reach.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- chapter P5 -->

### Mechanism

The offensive meets the reforms at regiment and battalion and is decided above them, which is the memorandum's own finding that the binding constraint on ARVN leadership had moved up to division and corps (F007-7). On the DMZ the 57th Regiment came back from the firebases by bounds and reached the Cua Viet line as a regiment, and the 56th's board-chosen commander surrendered Camp Carroll as he did historically (EV000-74). At Quang Tri the corps and the division gave the historical orders, the city was abandoned on 1 May, and one regiment held the Thach Han bridges three hours past its orders; perhaps 1,500 rather than 2,000 died on the road (EV000-78) [X-007]. At Tan Canh about a third more of the 42nd Regiment reached Kontum (EV000-77). I move ARVN effectiveness at the end of the phase from the historical state to one band above it, for regiments and battalions that fought better through a defeat and a recovery that were both decided by generals.

Truong's recovery of the line in May is the reforms' most visible success of the war and the clearest picture of their limit. He was the list's first name for the 1st Division in 1966, chosen by a Directory that needed the Americans' trust; he was given I Corps in 1972 by a palace that had no one else. The list found him. Only defeat put him where he was needed.

The counter-directional finding is at work in every battle of the year (F007-23). The ARVN's officers were educated on curricula that put American fire at the centre of the infantry's plan, and at An Loc and Kontum they used the B-52 and the siege box with an educated staff's precision; that is why the cities held as they did. The same education made the army's plans more dependent on that fire than history's, in the year the agreement that would remove it was being negotiated.

### Enemy response and initiative

The PAVN's plan for 1972 took no account of the board and needed none: it attacked with fourteen divisions and tanks where the ARVN's corps commands were weakest, and the corps commands were the part of the army the reforms had not reached. Hanoi's choice of the DMZ front against a division formed six months earlier was a judgment about divisions, not battalions, and it was right. Where the PAVN met board-led battalions in the open, as on Route 1 south of Dong Ha, it found them harder to break and broke the division around them.

### Causal chains

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1).** C006-4, from memorandum 006, ran from the eighteen-month selected advisers through their honest reports and the cutting of the division advisory teams first, to a war of 1972 and 1975 decided by divisions that were the least advised part of the RVNAF. Tested against the war as now written, it resolves as predicted at its end and differently on its way there. The divisions were the least advised and also, now, the least changed by the officer reforms; the regiments and battalions inside them, which the advisers had left first, were the part of the division the board had improved most. The chain's terminal holds, because 1972 was decided by the division and corps commands, and the reason it holds is now doubled: the advisers left the divisions, and the lists never reached them.

### Downstream

The army enters 1973 with regiments and battalions that fought the offensive better than history's, a corps in I Corps under the list's best general, and a doctrine built around American fire that the agreement about to be signed takes away; Trieu goes to the Dalat faculty after Quang Tri.

### Clock

The offensive's political effect is the historical one: Nixon's position in the election was strengthened by the ARVN's survival and the mining, not by anything a promotion board did. A smaller rout on Highway 1 made fewer photographs, and the October terms were the terms of history. The clock in 1972 was already a matter of the agreement's date, and the reforms did not move it.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

The reforms change the last three years by making the Republic's officers more exact about what they lacked, and in one decision that precision is used against them. In 1974 the battalion and regimental commanders at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc fought under a ration that silenced the fires their doctrine assumed, and wrote the best account anywhere of what it cost; the losses stand at history's (EV000-91, F007-23). In November 1974 a seminar of the Dalat staff college wrote a plan for the defence of the Republic on reduced resources that a President could have used. On 14 March 1975 Thieu took its conclusion and none of its method, and the retreat from the Highlands went as it went historically (EV000-95, F007-7). At Xuan Loc and in three other places, well-led divisions and brigades fought as the 18th Division did historically, and none of it changed the end (EV000-96).

I leave Phase VI's end vector at the historical state on every headline variable. The phase ends after the Republic's fall, and the gain carried in from Phase V, a band in ARVN effectiveness in the regiments and battalions, is answered here by the ration, which made a better-led battalion of 1974 bleed as history's did, and by the decisions of March 1975, which were taken by the President and the corps commanders and executed in days.

### Enemy response and initiative

The PAVN's plan for 1975 was Hanoi's own, taken in the 21st Plenum's line and tested at Phuoc Long, and the officer reforms played no part in it. Its divisions met better-led ARVN units at Xuan Loc and on the coast and did what the PAVN had always done against a better unit: bypassed it, isolated it and let the collapse around it decide the matter. At Ban Me Thuot it struck where the corps commander did not expect it, which is a corps commander's failure and not a battalion's.

### Causal chains

**C007-5** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV007-1) The classes of 1957 onward are educated on a 2026 American doctrine in which the infantry finds the enemy and the fires destroy him, and from 1965 they command the battalions (F007-23) → at An Loc and Kontum in 1972 their staffs use American air with a precision history's did not, and the army's plans for every besieged town rest on it (EV000-77) → when the ration of 1974 silences the fires, the same officers are the only ones in the Republic who can state exactly what the army can no longer do, and a seminar under Colonel Trieu at Dalat writes in November 1974 a plan for a three-month withdrawal to a defensible Republic (EV000-91) → Thieu takes its conclusion and none of its method on 14 March 1975 and orders the Highlands abandoned in three days by Route 7, so that the most rigorous staff work the reforms produced became the warrant for the decision that lost the war fastest (EV000-95).

### Downstream

There is no later phase. About 12,000 of the board's officers went to the camps in 1975, and the board's files, with an evaluation report on every officer since 1964, were in the Joint General Staff's compound on 30 April; I do not claim their capture as a consequence the record shows, beyond noting that the files existed and were not destroyed.

### Clock

The clock had run out before this phase began. Case–Church, the War Powers Resolution and the aid cuts of 1974 ran on their historical dates, and no improvement in an ally's officer corps reached a Congress that had decided to stop paying for the war. The one American contact with the reforms in these years was the Defense Attaché Office's reports on the ARVN's officers, which were more favourable than history's and changed no vote.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the cumulative force now has an ARVN whose battalions were led as the historical army's never were. On the historical course 80 of 133 battalion commanders had held command a year or less in May 1971, and 46 of 133 battalions were led by captains [M007·S1]; in this war the captain-led battalion was about one in ten by the end of 1966, battalion commands ran eighteen months from 1965, about four battalions in five were commanded by an officer a board had chosen by the end of 1967, and about 30 commanders were relieved for cause in the first year of reviews [X-007]. I judge that the ARVN lost about a third fewer battalions rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than historically, a judgment on a rate shown so far on Route 13 and in the target notes taken in 1967, and not a measurement (EV007-3, EV007-4).

The officer corps changed its character from below. About 5,500 sergeants were commissioned by the end of 1968, the diploma bar to major was gone from 1964, and the mobilization's platoon leaders came from a 32-week course and the ranks rather than a 24-week crash course [X-007]. The price was a sergeant shortage in the new territorials of 1968–69.

In the battles, the margins are small and real: the 57th Regiment reaching the Cua Viet as a regiment, perhaps 500 fewer dead on Highway 1 in May 1972, a third more of the 42nd Regiment out of Tan Canh, perhaps 200 fewer ARVN killed in the first phase of Tet [X-007]. On the American side, the educated company commanders at Albany saved perhaps ten to twenty lives, and across 1965–69 I would put the saving at a rate of one to three percent of American deaths in small-unit fighting, a judgment shown so far in one event that does not move the casualty band [X-007].

At the top nothing changed that the lever did not change. One or two division commands in ten were filled from the list against the palace's preference by 1967; Truong reached the 1st Division by the list and I Corps by default; the list became advisory in 1970; and the corps commanders who lost Quang Tri in 1972 and the Highlands in 1975 were chosen as they were chosen historically. The end is unchanged: Saigon fell on 30 April 1975.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state stays at rung (a), collapse on the historical timeline. This is the most substantial provision the ARVN has received in this series, and it does not move the rung, for a reason the memorandum stated before the war was run: education and selection stop at the level where the war was decided. Rung (b) would require the Republic to fail only for the withdrawal of American combat forces and not on the historical schedule; it still falls on that schedule because the decisive failures of 1972 and 1975 were at corps and in the palace, because the American lever that protected the general-officer list lapsed in 1970, and because the officer corps the reforms built was educated on a doctrine of American fire that the agreement of 1973 and the aid cuts of 1974 took away (F007-7, F007-23; POSCP01-2). Rung (c), the RVN defeating a conventional offensive with American air, was already shown in 1972 and is not new here.

What the reforms have changed is the distance between the Republic's battalions and its government. By 1967 the ARVN's battalions are at the cap of this exercise's bands and its corps are where they were; the war's binding deficits are political and at the top, as the Theater Needs Assessment said they would be (POSTNA-1). A provision that chose corps commanders as the board chose battalion commanders, and kept the choice after the Americans left, would be the next test of that judgment.

The war's shape has not outgrown its phases, and I propose no redraw. Chapters P1 and P2 are over the length at which the checker flags a chapter, about 18,800 and 20,000 words, and I flag both for consolidation at the next checkpoint.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- The ARVN's battalions were commanded from 1965–67 by officers chosen on two raters' reports for eighteen-month commands and relieved when they failed; the captain-led battalion and the six-month command, both features of the historical army as late as 1971, were gone by 1967 (EV007-2). This is the largest single gain to ARVN fighting power in this series and it sits at the level where most of the war's contacts were fought.
- Better battalions were attacked less: the 272nd's ambush on Route 13 was declined on 4 May 1966, and I judge about a third fewer battalions were rendered ineffective in 1966–67, a gain that shows in no metric because the enemy chose not to make the fights that would have shown it (EV007-3).
- The companies of 1964–65, and the American companies of 1965–69, were led by officers educated to scout, plan fires and write orders; at Ba Gia, Albany and Tan Canh more men came out than history's (EV000-17, EV000-23, EV000-77).
- The officer corps began to change its class from below: about 5,500 sergeants were commissioned by the end of 1968, and the mobilization needed no crash course for its platoon leaders (EV000-51).
- One division in the worst state in the army, the 25th, was given a commander from the list in 1967 and improved its desertion and its contact rate within the year; Truong reached the 1st Division by the list in 1966 (EV007-4, EV000-26).
- In 1972 regiments held where the historical ones dissolved, and the road south of Quang Tri cost perhaps 500 fewer lives (EV000-74, EV000-78).

### What has not changed

- The corps and the palace: the general-officer list was only as strong as the aid condition, became advisory in 1970, and never governed the corps; Quang Tri in 1972 and the Highlands in 1975 were lost by orders from generals no board had chosen (EV007-5, EV000-78, EV000-95).
- The enemy still chose every battle; he declined the better battalion and attacked the one beside it, and he read the board's lists through an agent in its own secretariat (EV007-3, EV007-4).
- An educated officer corps under a palace-chosen top became a faction in the palace's eyes, and the President's answer in 1970–71 removed forty of its best commanders before Lam Son 719 (EV007-5).
- The doctrine the officers learned deepened the army's dependence on American fire; in 1974 they could state exactly what the ration had taken and could not replace it (EV000-91).
- My Lai happened, and the late-war decay of American discipline ran on its historical course; the lieutenant's education did not reach the command climate above him.
- The American clock, the withdrawal and the aid cuts ran on their historical dates, and Saigon fell on 30 April 1975.
