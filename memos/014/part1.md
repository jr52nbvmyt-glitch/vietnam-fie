The provision commits the whole of the 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta to Vietnam, in its 2026 configuration and at full strength [U]. It comes as the unit alone: its assault squadrons, its reconnaissance and advanced-force squadron, its own small aviation squadron and its support squadrons, with no 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment and no dedicated JSOC surveillance wing [U]. It is assigned to a joint special operations command inside MACV, formed after the Gulf of Tonkin incident [U]. That headquarters must work with the military, intelligence and civil structures already in Vietnam for logistics and deconfliction [U], and the user will assign further units to it later [U]. Delta has five missions [U]:
- hunting PAVN and VC leadership and the infrastructure;
- COSVN and the base areas;
- recovering prisoners of war;
- raids into the North;
- reconnaissance.

The borders bind as they did in history [U]. I read that as follows, and Part 2 will hold to it:
- **Laos.** No American ground entry before history's first cross-border mission on 18 October 1965 [S3]. After that, only on SOG's terms, under the ambassador's limits on depth, target and duration [S3].
- **Cambodia.** No entry before September 1967 [S3]. From then on, reconnaissance on foot without tactical air and with no exploitation force [S3]. Then the incursion of May–June 1970 on history's terms [H].
- **North Vietnam.** No American ground raid until the one history made, at Son Tay on 21 November 1970 [S5].

CONFIG's `laos_ground`, `cambodia_ground` and `north_ground` therefore stay MODELLED, held at history's practice. Nothing in this memorandum suspends them.

I date the headquarters to 15 August 1964, eleven days after the second Tonkin report [E: a week to decide and a week to stand up a cell], and Delta's arrival to 1 September 1964 [E: two weeks to move a unit that history deploys in days]. That puts the unit in Vietnam three years before American teams could legally enter Cambodia, and fourteen months before they could enter Laos. For its first year, every mission Delta has lies inside the South. I think that is the right place to start reading the provision, because it is where most of the unit's value will lie.

The provision makes two dossiers:
- **P014a** is the unit. It is read as a ground force.
- **P014b** is the headquarters. It is read as an institutional provision.

## §1.1 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta, full commitment, 2026 configuration — P014a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The unit is the 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta of the United States Army (SFOD-D, Delta Force). Charles Beckwith and Colonel Thomas Henry raised it on 19 November 1977 [S1]. It is organised under Army Special Operations Command and controlled by the Joint Special Operations Command [S1]. The 2026 unit is the reference configuration [U]. Its strength is classified [S1]. The best published figure is "nearly 1,000 soldiers" in 2001, of whom "about 250 to 300" were trained for direct action and hostage rescue [S1]. Since then a fourth assault squadron has been added (D, 2006) and a combat support squadron (2005) [S1]. I take the 2026 unit at about 1,000–1,500 people, with about 250–350 operators [E: the 2001 figure grown by one assault squadron and one support squadron].

The organisation is the unit's own [S1]:
- **Four assault squadrons, A to D.** Each is commanded by a lieutenant colonel and has two assault troops and one reconnaissance troop. An assault troop has four teams of five or six operators.
- **G Squadron, advanced force operations.** It goes ahead of a raid to look at the target and the ground.
- **E Squadron, aviation.** It is company-sized and flies unmarked light helicopters and a handful of fixed-wing aircraft [S2].
- **A signal squadron and a combat support squadron.**
- **A selection and training element.**

"Full commitment" sends all of it [U]. I base the unit at Tan Son Nhut, inside the MACV compound's perimeter, with the training element and the support squadron [E: closeness to MACV J-2 and to the fixed-wing runway]. In each corps area one forward operating base holds one assault squadron, and from 1965 the base areas each forward base faces set its position [E: SOG's pattern of forward bases, S3]:
- I Corps at Da Nang;
- II Corps at Kontum or Pleiku;
- III Corps at Bien Hoa;
- IV Corps at Can Tho.

The squadrons rotate between the forward bases and Tan Son Nhut. In any month three assault squadrons are on operations and one is resting and retraining inside the country [E: the unit's own rotation applied inside one theater].

The provision has no manufacturer. Its equipment is the 2026 unit's, which is described in D3 and D4.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The working element is the team of five or six operators [S1]. An assault troop is four teams, about 20–24 operators. A sabre squadron is two assault troops and a reconnaissance troop, about 60–80 operators with a squadron headquarters [E: S1's troop structure]. The four sabre squadrons hold about 250–320 operators. With G Squadron's advanced-force teams, the unit fields about 280–350 people who go out on operations [E: S1].

The rest of the unit is about 750–1,150 people: aviators and maintainers in E Squadron, signallers, intelligence analysts, medics, armourers, parachute riggers, and the training and selection cadre [E: the 2001 ratio of about three supporters to each operator, S1]. The unit spends 1,000–1,500 personnel spaces [E: D1]. CONFIG has no troop ceiling, so they come out of no one else's [U: CONFIG troop_ceiling = NONE].

The figure that matters is not the 1,500. It is the 280–350 who go into the bush and through the doors. They are irreplaceable at any speed the war can use. Selection passes about one man in eight to ten, and the Operator Training Course takes about six months after that [S1]. A killed or crippled operator is replaced from Fort Bragg's pipeline, not from Vietnam [E: S1's pipeline]. I count every loss against a stock of about 300 that refills at perhaps 30–50 a year [E: two selection courses a year at S1's pass rates, less the unit's ordinary wastage].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

Each operator carries a 5.56mm carbine of the HK416 or M4A1 family, with a suppressor, an infrared laser and illuminator, and a magnified optic [E: published accounts of the 2026 unit's individual kit]. He also carries a pistol, fragmentation and flash-bang grenades, and breaching charges [E: published accounts of the 2026 unit's individual kit]. Each team has:
- one or two 7.62mm or 6.5mm marksman rifles [E: published SOF inventories];
- a belt-fed machine gun (an M240 or Mk 48);
- a Carl Gustaf M3E1 84mm recoilless rifle, or AT4s;
- a 60mm commando mortar for a defended target [E: published SOF inventories].

[E: the 2026 unit's team weapons as publicly reported]

Each squadron holds a few long-range rifles in .300 Norma or .338 calibre, and Javelin missiles for a target that needs one [E: published SOF inventories].

What the unit does not carry matters more than what it does. It has no artillery, no armour, and no vehicle that can move a wounded man through paddy. Its fire beyond its own weapons comes from someone else: Army and Air Force aircraft, the Js' boxes [M005], or division and corps guns. Against a defended base area the team fights for minutes with what it carries and then calls. E Squadron's helicopters can mount door guns or light rockets [S2]. Those are self-defence weapons for an insertion, not fire support.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The decisive sensor is the night-vision goggle. Every operator wears a 2026 tube goggle of the AN/PVS-31A type or a fused-thermal ENVG-B, with an AN/PEQ-15 weapon aimer [E: the unit's standard kit]. At night that gives a team an advantage no 1964 enemy can match. The PAVN and VC of the period moved and fought at night because the Americans and the ARVN could not see. A Delta team sees a man at 150–300 metres under starlight in the open [E: third-generation tube performance against a standing man], and sees his heat behind light foliage with a thermal sight.

Under triple canopy the advantage shrinks to the length of a path. Starlight does not reach the floor of a primary forest, and a thermal sight sees what is in front of it: 20–50 metres through undergrowth, often less [E: tropical forest sight lines]. The team sees no further than any patrol. It sees in the dark what its enemy sees in the day. That is still a large edge for an ambush or a raid. It is no edge for finding a base camp from the air.

The monsoon degrades the thermal picture but hardly touches the goggle. Heavy rain at night washes out the thermal contrast between a body and the ground, and the overcast cuts starlight, so the tubes need infrared illumination, which an enemy with a converter can see [E: rain effects on long-wave infrared]. The PAVN of the 1960s had very few such converters [E: Soviet equipment of the period]. I count an IR illuminator as close to invisible until about 1970.

The unit's own surveillance is small. G Squadron and the reconnaissance troops carry hand-launched small drones of the RQ-20B Puma and RQ-11B Raven class [E: the 2026 unit's organic small UAS], with an endurance of one to two hours and a range of 10–20 km. They also carry unattended ground sensors and laser rangefinders [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. Anything bigger comes from the record: the U-2S and RQ-4B imagery and signals take [M003], and the combined task force's files [M012]. The provision's "unit alone" means the unit brings no MQ-9 or Gray Eagle orbit of its own [U].

### D5 · Performance

A raid on a known target in the South, by helicopter, at night, against a target within about 80 km of a forward base [E: the light helicopters' radius with reserve, S2], takes about 2–6 hours from launch to recovery. It needs 6–24 hours of planning if the target is new, or about 1–2 hours if it is a pattern the squadron has worked [E: the cycle the combined task force achieved in the record, M012, applied to a smaller, faster unit]. At maturity in 1966–67, three assault squadrons on operations can mount about 40–120 direct-action raids a month in the South [E: about one raid every second or third night for each of the six assault troops on operations, limited by targets and lift rather than by men]. That is a fraction of the combined task force's 300–450 raids a month at maturity [M012].

What each raid returns is the question. I estimate that 15–35% of Delta raids reach a cadre at district level or above who is on a prior target list [E: the combined task force's 20–35% for its own raids, M012, adjusted for Delta taking harder, higher targets with a lower success rate]. That gives about 80–400 listed senior cadre taken a year at maturity [E: the raid rate multiplied by the yield]. I push FIND.vci by 50–250 a year in 1967, because many of these raids take a target that the task force's raid forces would otherwise have taken later.

Reconnaissance runs at a different tempo. The unit can keep about 15–25 reconnaissance teams of four to six in the field at once [E: four reconnaissance troops and G Squadron]. A team is out for 3–6 days and stands down for about a week [E: SOG's pattern, S3]. That gives about 40–80 team-missions a month across the theater [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. SOG ran 454 missions into Cambodia alone in 1969 [S3], so Delta adds to SOG's reconnaissance and does not replace it.

E Squadron moves a few teams at a time. A company of light helicopters lifts about 2–4 operators each [E: the MD 500 and Little Bird cabin, S2]. One night's lift is about one or two assault troops, and an operation larger than that needs UH-1s and CH-47s from the theater [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

Protection is a plate carrier and a helmet. Against the AK-47 and the SKS a rifle plate stops the round. Against a 12.7mm or an RPG it does nothing [E: protection standards].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The team carries a multiband radio with satellite channels, and every operator carries an intra-team radio [E: the AN/PRC-117G and PRC-163 class]. Under the standing condition the GPS and military satellite constellations are present from the campaign's opening [E01]. A team in the Mimot rubber or the A Shau can therefore speak to Tan Son Nhut directly, by voice or text, with its position to a few metres. That is the second decisive advantage over SOG, whose teams depended on a FAC overhead to relay and to find them [S4].

The unit talks to the ARVN badly, and only through people. It has no Vietnamese linguists of its own beyond a handful [E: the unit's selection is not language-based]. Its interpreters come from the combined task force, from the Vietnamese services, or from hired Vietnamese. Each of these is a channel the enemy can enter (INTEL.penetration). Its radios are encrypted American sets; the ARVN of 1964 has PRC-10s and PRC-25s and no crypto [E: ARVN equipment of the period]. A raid with an ARVN partner force works through an American liaison officer with an ARVN set, or through the task force's combined desks [M012]. I would keep the targets themselves inside American channels until the strike, and tell the ARVN corps commander only that a restricted area exists for a night. That is how SOG worked, and SOG's Vietnamese partners still lost teams to agents [S3].

### D7 · Domain conditions

The unit fights on foot and lands by helicopter. In paddy it moves at walking pace or slower, as everyone does; at night with goggles it moves along dikes that the enemy thinks are dark. In the Delta's canals and the U Minh it needs boats it does not own: Navy river forces or Vietnamese sampans [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. On laterite in the dry season it moves well. In the monsoon the Highlands become mud and the helicopters fly less, but the teams on the ground keep working [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

In the forest the canopy closes the sky. A helicopter needs a landing zone or a rope. A wounded operator three kilometres from a clearing is a stretcher carried by four men for half a day [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. The base areas of War Zone C and the Fishhook, the A Shau, and the tri-border were chosen by the enemy for exactly that reason.

Over the South the threat to E Squadron's light helicopters is small arms and the 12.7mm [E: the air-war study, studies/air-war], and every insertion within range of a defended base area meets them. Over the Trail, from 1966, the 37mm and 57mm guns at the passes [E: the air-war study's account, studies/air-war] close daylight insertions and make night ones a choice of route. Over the North the SA-2 and the MiG are against any raid. Son Tay needed 116 aircraft in all and 28 in the target area to put 56 soldiers on the ground for 27 minutes [S5]. Delta's own aviation cannot do that alone.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The unit is small and expensive. It needs:
- its own armourers, parachute riggers and medics;
- an airfield for E Squadron's helicopters and King Airs, with a hangar the press cannot photograph [S2];
- a secure compound for planning;
- fuel of about 5,000–15,000 gallons of JP-4 or JP-8 a day for its own aircraft [E: a company of light helicopters and a few turboprops].

The 2026 equipment is the problem. Batteries, night-vision tubes, radios and drones are all built in 2026. No American depot of 1964 can repair a fused goggle or a satellite radio, and no South Vietnamese plant can either, even the electronics plant of memorandum 008, which works at one generation above 1964 [M008]. Every spare comes from the unit's own stock. I size the stock at two to three years of normal wastage at the unit's own scale [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. After that the 2026 kit thins, and the unit falls back on what the 1960s can supply: starlight scopes, PRC-25s, M16s. The decline is gradual and I describe it in D12.

The RVNAF can supply none of this except rice, guards and petrol.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The unit cannot be transferred. Its people are American soldiers chosen by a selection the ARVN does not have. Its equipment does not exist in 1964 outside the unit.

What can be transferred is a standard. A Vietnamese counterpart unit could be built at Delta's standard by a training cadre, beginning in 1965 with the ARVN's Rangers, the airborne and the LLDB as the pool [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. It would take about four weeks of selection and six months of operator training, as Delta's own course does [S1]. The first squadron of about 60–80 Vietnamese operators could be ready about 18–30 months after the cadre starts [E: two selection cycles, OTC, and a year of partnered operations]. It would have 1960s equipment, because the 2026 kit cannot be handed over in quantity: starlight scopes for goggles, PRC-25s for satellite radios. By 1972 a Vietnamese special unit of two to three squadrons could exist [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. Whether it survives the coup politics of Saigon is a question for Part 2. A unit that can raid a headquarters at night can raid a palace, and every Vietnamese general would see that first.

If such a unit is built, the American unit's withdrawal in the drawdown leaves the Vietnamese unit with its training and without its night, its satellite radio and its helicopters.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The unit performs to its standard under these conditions:
- **At night.** This is its whole advantage.
- **Inside the South.** The target is within the range of its helicopters or of theater lift.
- **On a target that someone has found.** The combined task force's files, a captured document, a defector, or signals [M012] [M003].
- **Fast after the find.** It must arrive before the target moves. The VCI cadre who sleeps in a hamlet moves every two or three nights.

Performance collapses under these conditions:
- **The target is a main-force unit, not a person.** A battalion of 400 is not a raid target for a troop of 24.
- **Under triple canopy far from a clearing.** Insertion and recovery decide the operation.
- **Monsoon weather.** The helicopters cannot fly.
- **The target is warned.** COSVN's leaders learned of B-52 strikes from Soviet trawlers and moved [S7]. An American raid planned through Vietnamese channels is warned through those channels.
- **Weeks of sustained tempo.** The squadrons must rotate. The unit can surge for a few weeks to perhaps twice its sustained rate and then must rest.
- **Across a border before history's date.** The constraint, not the unit, is the limit there.

### D11 · Failure modes

The commonest failure is the dry hole. The team arrives, and the target left the night before, or was never there. This is not a loss. It is the ordinary cost of the business, and the task force of the 2000s ran many raids for each capture [M012]. It becomes a failure when the dry holes teach the enemy the pattern: which hamlets draw raids, which informants were followed by helicopters. When that happens, the sources dry up.

The second failure is compromise. The enemy learns a raid is coming and waits for it. SOG's agent teams in the North were captured at or soon after insertion in more than three cases out of four, and their radios were turned [S3]. A Delta team that walks into a prepared ambush in a hamlet loses half its men in a minute. Its value as the force that goes in at night is lost for as long as the leak goes undiscovered.

The third failure is the helicopter. A light helicopter shot down on insertion with four operators aboard, or a recovery that cannot reach a team in contact under canopy, kills more men in an hour than a year of raids. At Oscar Eight in June 1967 SOG lost 7 Americans dead and missing and more than 40 Nung soldiers in one operation [S3].

The fourth is political. A raid that kills the wrong people in a hamlet, or that is discovered across a border, shuts down more than the unit. Washington halted all northern operations in 1968 [S3].

While the unit is unavailable (after a compromise, a heavy loss, or a political halt), the combined task force goes on with its raids and arrests [M012], and the conventional war goes on as it would have. Delta is not load-bearing for anything except its own targets.

### D12 · Degradation curve

In month one the unit is at its best in equipment and at its worst in knowledge. It has every goggle and every radio. It does not know Vietnam. It does not know what a hamlet chief's house looks like, how the VCI cell structure runs, which Vietnamese officer can be trusted, or where the PAVN put its guns at the passes. Its first months are learning. I expect a raid yield in late 1964 at perhaps a third to a half of the 1966–67 figure [E: the curve of the 2000s task forces, M012].

At month twelve (September 1965) the unit knows the South, has a working relationship with the combined task force, and has learned which Vietnamese channels leak. Its yield rises toward the 1966–67 rate.

What declines first is the operators, not the kit. Over 1966–68 the unit loses men steadily: on raids, in helicopter losses, and on reconnaissance across the border from October 1965. SOG lost 79 Special Forces soldiers killed or missing in 1968 alone [S3]. Delta's teams are smaller and its losses lower, but a loss rate of 20–50 operators a year in the cross-border years is plausible [E: SOG's rate scaled to Delta's teams]. That is a sixth of the operators, refilled at about the same rate (D2). The unit holds its strength and loses its experience.

The 2026 equipment declines second. From about 1966–67 goggles, batteries and radios are rationed (D8). From about 1968 a team may go out with one goggle in two. By the drawdown the unit fights much as a 1970 SOG team did: well trained, with starlight scopes and PRC-25s and the GPS still overhead [E: the stock sized in D8]. The GPS lasts as long as the standing condition holds [E01].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

There are two benchmarks.

**Against SOG's reconnaissance teams.** A SOG team of 1965–69 was two or three Americans and three to twelve indigenous soldiers [S3] [S4]. It moved by day, navigated by map and compass, called a FAC overhead to relay, and was found by its enemy often. Its strengths were the indigenous soldiers, who looked and sounded like the enemy, and its volume: 454 Cambodian missions in 1969 alone [S3]. A Delta team has five or six better-trained Americans. It sees at night, knows its position to a few metres, and talks to Saigon by satellite. I judge a Delta reconnaissance team about two to three times as likely as a SOG team to complete a mission undetected and to bring back a target located well enough for a strike [E: night vision and GPS against a 1960s team]. But it looks like an American wherever it goes, and the unit has a fifth as many teams as SOG fielded [E: D5 against S3].

**Against the raid forces of the combined task force and the PRUs [M012].** Those forces raid by the hundred a month and arrest on warrants. They take the district and village cadre. Delta takes the top of the list. It goes where the task force's forces would be shot up or betrayed, and comes back with documents and a prisoner who can describe a province committee. The margin is not numbers. Delta's 40–120 raids a month [E: D5] are a third of the task force's at most. The margin is the reach of each raid: a province or region committee member, not a hamlet tax collector.

**Against POW recovery.** History's JPRC attempted 39 recoveries in 1967 and 32 in 1968 [S6]. It succeeded 8 times in 1968 and 9 in 1969, recovering 155 and 117 South Vietnamese prisoners. It never recovered an American POW [S6]. The failures were mostly lateness: by the time a report of a camp reached a team, the camp had moved [E: the JPRC's pattern]. Delta's speed and night capability make it a better instrument for recovery than anything JPRC had. Its success depends on how fresh the intelligence is, as JPRC's did.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P014a -->
Delta cannot find what no one has found. It finishes targets that the combined task force, the U-2S and Global Hawks, a defector or a captured document have located [M012] [M003]; it locates very little itself beyond the reach of its own patrols, and nothing under triple canopy from the air. It cannot destroy a main-force unit: a battalion of 400 is not a raid target for a troop of 20–24 operators [E: D2]. It cannot reach COSVN by raid while COSVN is in Cambodia, because no American ground force crosses that border until September 1967, and then only on reconnaissance on foot without air support, and with no exploitation force [S3]; and COSVN, about 2,400 staff dispersed and mobile, evaded Junction City, Menu and the 1970 incursion [S7]. It cannot raid the North before history's one raid at Son Tay in November 1970 [S5]. It cannot enter Laos before October 1965 [S3]. It brings no 160th SOAR and no drone orbit of its own [U]: its lift is a company of light helicopters [S2], and any operation larger than a troop or deeper than their range needs the theater's lift. It cannot speak Vietnamese, so every target that comes through Vietnamese hands comes with the risk of a leak. It cannot be handed to the ARVN, and its 2026 equipment cannot be repaired in 1964 (D8). And it is not numerous: about 280–350 people who go on operations [E: D2], refilled at perhaps 30–50 a year.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P014a -->
It denies the enemy's senior cadre in the South the night. From 1964 a province committee member, a regional military commander or a COSVN courier who sleeps in a hamlet that the task force's files have named can be taken from his bed by men he cannot see, in 2–6 hours from the decision [E: D5]. It denies the infrastructure the comfort that only the hamlet cadre are hunted. It denies the base areas inside the South (the Iron Triangle, the Ho Bo woods, the Do Xa, the U Minh) their freedom from observation at night. It denies the Trail and the Cambodian base areas their freedom from observation from October 1965 and September 1967, within history's limits [S3]. It denies Hanoi certainty that a prisoner camp in the South is beyond reach. It does not deny the enemy its main force, its sanctuaries or its strategy.

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P014a -->
Delta is the best night raid force in the world, put into a war fought at night by an enemy who believed the night was his. That is worth something real and specific: from late 1964 the senior infrastructure in the South (province committees, regional commanders, couriers and their documents) can be taken when it is found, at night, by men who see. The unit's whole value runs through the combined task force's files [M012]. Delta adds the hands for the hardest 10–20% of the task force's targets [E: D13] and the speed to reach them before they move. That will be 50–250 more senior cadre a year at maturity, against an infrastructure counted in the tens of thousands [E: D5]. It is reconnaissance that finds the Trail and the Cambodian base areas a little better than SOG did, from history's dates. It is a better POW recovery force than any history had. It does not touch the main force, the sanctuaries or Hanoi's decision. COSVN, which the provision names, is beyond its reach until 1970 and probably after, because it was a moving headquarters of 2,400 people in a country no American could enter [S7]. The raid into the North that history made, the unit could make better. It is one raid. Delta is a scalpel. The war has places to use one, and it is not won with one.

## §1.2 MACV Joint Special Operations Command, formed August 1964 — P014b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The provision is a joint headquarters under COMUSMACV, MACV-JSOC, formed after the Gulf of Tonkin incident [U]. It commands Delta (P014a) and the special operations units the user will assign to it later [U]. It must interact with the military, intelligence and civil structures in Vietnam for logistics and deconfliction [U]. Its real-world model is the Joint Special Operations Command, established on 15 December 1980 on Beckwith's recommendation after Eagle Claw [S8]. Its nearest period precedent is MACV-SOG, formed on 24 January 1964 [S3], which already sits beside MACV with its own lines to the CIA and to Washington. The quantity is one headquarters [U]. I date it to 15 August 1964 [E: see the introduction].

The organisation is small. It has:
- a command group, a brigadier general with a joint deputy;
- J-2 targeting and intelligence, with a joint targeting cell;
- J-3 operations, with a joint operations centre that runs night operations;
- J-4 logistics;
- J-6 communications;
- liaison cells in each of the bodies it must deconflict with (D6).

[E: the shape of a joint task force headquarters]

It sits at Tan Son Nhut beside Delta's compound, and is sized to grow when the user assigns more units [U].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

About 250–450 people:
- **Command group and staff:** about 150–250 [E: a joint task force headquarters of the early JSOC's scale].
- **Joint operations centre and signals:** about 50–100 [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].
- **Liaison officers:** about 40–80 in fifteen to twenty cells (D6) [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

It consumes 250–450 personnel spaces [E: D2], under no ceiling [U: CONFIG troop_ceiling = NONE]. Its scarce people are its targeters and its liaison officers. A liaison officer who can sit in the CIA station, in the combined task force's National Fusion Centre [M012], or in a corps commander's office and be trusted is a man the Army has a few dozen of in 1964 [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

Read as *instruments and authorities*. The headquarters holds four things:
- **Operational command of Delta** and of later special operations units [U].
- **Authority to declare restricted operating areas** for a night or a few days, inside which no other American or allied unit fires or flies without clearance. This is the core of deconfliction [E: standard joint practice].
- **A seat on MACV's target boards** and a share of the combined task force's target list [M012], with the right to claim a target for Delta.
- **A call on MACV's theater lift and fire support** through the MACV J-3 [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

It holds no cross-border authority. Laos targets stay with the ambassador in Vientiane, as SOG's did [S3]. Cambodian entry is Washington's from September 1967 [S3]. A raid into the North is the President's [S5]. It holds no authority over the CIA, the GVN, or the ARVN; with them it can only ask. It can compel nothing outside MACV.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

- the U-2S and RQ-4B take [M003];

### D7 · Domain conditions

Read as *reach and security preconditions*. The headquarters reaches everywhere in the South that a helicopter and a cleared raid can reach. It needs no hamlet security. It needs the opposite: a closed compound and a clean channel. Its precondition is counter-intelligence. ### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The headquarters cannot be transferred as it stands; its purpose is to keep targets out of Vietnamese channels. A Vietnamese joint special operations headquarters could be built beside it from about 1968, to command the Vietnamese special unit described in D9 of P014a [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. It would inherit the deconfliction problem in a worse form: the coup politics of Saigon would make every Vietnamese special unit a political weapon.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The headquarters works when:
- Delta's targets come from the combined task force's files with the task force's consent;
- the corps commanders accept restricted areas without asking why;
- the CIA station shares its agents' locations;
- the ambassador in Vientiane accepts Delta's teams on SOG's terms from October 1965.

It fails when:
- the agencies hoard their sources. Each agency has a reason to: its sources are its value;
- a corps commander's fire plan kills a Delta team in a restricted area that was never passed down;
- the headquarters is captured by its own operations and stops sharing results with the task force.

### D11 · Failure modes

The first failure is fratricide: a Delta team shot by an American or ARVN unit that was not told of the restricted area. The second is the blown source: Delta raids a target the CIA was watching, and the agent who was reporting dies. The third is rivalry: the headquarters, SOG and the combined task force fight over the same targets and the same helicopters. The fourth is capture by success. A headquarters that raids well becomes the instrument Washington reaches for, and its targets drift from what the war needs to what the briefing wants.

During the window the headquarters is not working, Delta works through MACV J-3 directly, more slowly, and with more leaks [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice].

### D12 · Degradation curve

The headquarters improves for about a year as its liaison cells earn trust, and then holds [E: judgment from the period's establishments and practice]. What declines first is the liaison: liaison officers rotate on 12-month tours, and each replacement must earn trust again [E: MACV's individual rotation, as memorandum 006 changed it for units but not for staff officers]. The second decline is the joint watch, which tires through the cross-border years.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is MACV-SOG's own arrangement. SOG was a subsidiary command of MACV whose authority ran to Washington, with its cross-border operations cleared by the ambassador and by Washington mission by mission [S3]. It had no standing link to a national targeting file like the combined task force's. The headquarters improves on SOG in one way: it shares a target list with the task force and a watch with the corps. I judge that worth a few hundred cadre over the war (P014b's FIND.vci push), not more.

### D14 · Negative specification

The headquarters cannot authorise any cross-border operation: Laos stays with the ambassador, Cambodia with Washington from September 1967, the North with the President [S3] [S5]. ### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P014b -->
It denies the enemy the seams between the American services, SOG, the CIA station and the combined task force, which a single raid force working through MACV J-3 would leave open: fewer raids planned through channels that leak, fewer sources burned, fewer teams shot by friendly fire.

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P014b -->
The headquarters is the price of using Delta well. Without it, Delta would work through MACV J-3 and the corps, its targets would pass through hands that leak, and its raids would collide with the task force's and SOG's. With it, a small joint staff keeps the targets in American hands, clears the ground for each raid in hours, and shares what the raids bring back. It adds nothing new to the war's knowledge. It adds speed and order to a scalpel. It is also the frame for the units the user will add later, and its value will grow or shrink with them.
