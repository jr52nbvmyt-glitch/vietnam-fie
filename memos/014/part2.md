## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

Delta joins two systems and only two. It is a **SUBVERT** instrument and a **FIND** instrument, in that order of weight.

In SUBVERT it is the finishing arm for the top of the Front's organisation. The combined interagency task force of memorandum 012 built a national file and raids on it 300–450 times a month at maturity, taking 300–700 infrastructure members a month of whom 20–35% are district level or above [M012]. Delta does not add to that volume: at a sustained rate it mounts 25–50 raids a month (EC014-11), a tenth of the task force's. What it adds is reach into the targets the task force's own forces cannot take: the province and region committee member sleeping in a hamlet the Police Field Force would be shot up entering, the courier whose house is inside a base area's outer belt, the COSVN section chief on a visit south of the border. I judge 40–150 more listed cadre at district level or above a year by 1967, which is why the manifest's FIND.vci push came down from Part 1's figure.

In FIND it adds patrol-level eyes where the record has had none. The U-2S and the RQ-4B see movement in the open and nothing under canopy (POS003-1). The settled position on finding the main force has stood through eleven memoranda: the enemy is exactly as hard to find under canopy as he was in history (G-7). Delta does not change that. Four reconnaissance troops and G Squadron put 8–12 teams in the field at once, each with night vision, satellite voice and its position to a few metres, which is a better instrument than SOG's teams of 1965 by a factor of two or three in missions completed undetected (D13). Twelve teams against a theater is twelve teams.

It joins **STRIKE** only as a customer. A team that finds a truck park or a gun position passes a coordinate, and from March 1964 the cell at Tan Son Nhut turns coordinates into aimpoints true to a few metres [M005]. The J's precision has always been limited by what can be located (POS005-1), and a Delta team is the first thing in this record that can locate a point target under canopy at all. That matters, in the small number of places a team is standing.

It joins **INTERDICT** from October 1965 in Laos and September 1967 in Cambodia, on history's terms and within the ambassador's limits on depth, target and duration [S3]. It joins **SECURE** not at all; it holds nothing. It joins **ADVISE** not at all, which is the most important sentence in this section and is treated below. It consumes **SUSTAIN** without contributing to it.

**F014-1** (SUBVERT, FIND) Delta's entire value runs through other men's files. Its raids come from the task force's National Fusion Centre and the district centres of memorandum 006, its reconnaissance tasking from the U-2S and RQ-4B take, and its border work from SOG's target folders [M012] [M006] [M003]. The unit adds hands and speed to the hardest tenth of a target list it does not build. A provision that depends this completely on another provision's product cannot be assessed on its own, and in a world without memorandum 012's file Delta would be a superb raid force with a half-empty target deck.

**F014-2** (FIND) The find-to-fix problem is unchanged and the record should not be allowed to drift on this point. Twelve reconnaissance teams see what twelve patrols see. They see it at night, which SOG's teams did not, and they report it instantly and accurately, which SOG's teams could not. In the base areas inside the South (the Iron Triangle, the Ho Bo woods, the Do Xa, the U Minh) that converts a handful of regimental assembly areas a year into measured coordinates. It does not convert the main force into a findable enemy (POS003-1, G-7).

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

The chain is: locate a formation, hold it in place, kill it. Delta touches the first link in a few places a year and the second not at all. A reconnaissance troop of 20–24 men cannot fix a PAVN battalion of 400; it can watch one, and the watching is the contribution. The enemy's choice of whether to accept battle is untouched (POSTNA-3), and the record's position on that choice is the most durable thing in it: the enemy still chooses every fight except the siege (G-7).

There is one real change, and it is narrow. A team sitting on a trail junction in a base area at night reports a column moving before the column reaches the hamlets, with its position and strength, in time for an airmobile reaction or a J's box. Historically that warning came from a captured document a week later or from the first mortar round. I judge FIND.main_force 0–2 points of main-force contacts begun on located units, at the bottom of the range, because twelve teams cannot be where the columns are.

**F014-3** (FIX) Delta subtracts nothing from the enemy's control of engagement and the memorandum should not pretend otherwise. The enemy initiated about two engagements of platoon size in three in 1966–68 [M-ST01, studies/air-war's source S5], and a unit that raids houses at night does not change the arithmetic of who accepts battle. Where a Delta team is detected in a base area, the enemy's choice is to hunt it with a company, which is a fight the enemy chooses on ground he knows. That is the inversion of the find-to-fix chain and it is the cause of most of the unit's losses (F014-22).

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

This is Delta's chain and it is worth setting out link by link, because the unit occupies exactly one link of five.

1. **Identify.** The Special Branch constable in the village, the family census, the district centre's file, the national fusion centre's network map [M002] [M006] [M012]. Delta contributes nothing.
2. **Locate tonight.** A source, a rallier, a captured courier, a telephone the Front does not have. Delta contributes nothing except what its own raids return.
3. **Reach the man.** The link Delta owns. Six assault troops forward, 2–6 hours from decision to recovery, at night, against a target in a hamlet, a base-area fringe or a canal line that the task force's police arm cannot enter.
4. **Exploit.** The same-night follow-on that the task force built: documents read, the next target raided within 6–24 hours [M012]. Delta hands its take to the task force's exploitation cell; it has no analytical capacity of its own in Vietnamese.
5. **Hold the man.** The 1964 statute's magistrate review within 72 hours and the two-year detention cap [M002]. The task force already found this the binding constraint by 1968 (F012-12), and Delta pushes more men, and higher-value men, into the same pipe.

**F014-4** (SUBVERT) The constraint on the attack on the infrastructure does not move to Delta's link. Before the provision the binding constraint on taking the Front's senior cadre was reach into the places the police could not go; Delta relieves that constraint for 25–50 targets a month. The constraint then moves straight back to identification and to detention: the national file names where the apparatus is, not where a given man sleeps tonight, and a province committee member held for two years and released in 1967 goes back to work (F012-12). The hands were never the scarce thing. The addresses were.

### Dependence on SECURE

Everything in the countryside depends on night security and this provision depends on it twice. First for its sources: the file that names a house is built by a resident constable and a family census, and both exist only where the government holds the hamlet after dark (POS002-1). Second for its surprise: a raid into a hamlet the Front holds at night is a raid into an area where the Front's children watch the roads and its guerrillas hold the paths.

The record's measurement of night control after CP03 is what sets Delta's working area. III and IV Corps hold about 8–14 and 6–12 points more of their rural people after dark than history's by 1967; I and II Corps never more than a few points (POSCP03-3, G-2). Delta's raiding therefore concentrates in III and IV Corps, where the files are thick, and thins in I and II Corps, where they are not — which is precisely where the main-force war of 1965–68 is fought.

**F014-5** (SECURE, SUBVERT) Delta inherits the geography of the held belt and sharpens the record's existing asymmetry rather than correcting it. Its raid density in the Delta and the Saigon belt will be perhaps three to five times its density in I and II Corps, because that is where the file is [E: the record's own distribution of the task force's raids, M012]. Under DIRMT01-8 the four countrysides are not alike here: in the Delta the unit works a mature file and takes province committee members at Cai Lay and Cho Gao; in the coastal lowlands of Binh Dinh and Quang Ngai it is raiding on thin files into hamlets the Front holds at night; in the Highlands it has no file at all and works instead as reconnaissance in the tri-border, where FULRO and the Montagnard population are a source no American unit without language can use; in the Saigon belt it is at its best, with the task force at its elbow and helicopters twenty minutes away.

### The ADVISE cluster

Delta feeds nothing into the advisory effort. It is the purest American-owned provision in this record: an American unit, with American equipment that cannot be repaired in Vietnam, holding American targets in American channels for the deliberate reason that Vietnamese channels leak (D6 of P014b). Its nearest Vietnamese contribution is the counterpart unit that could be raised from the Rangers, the airborne and the LLDB from 1965 and would field two or three squadrons by 1972 with 1960s equipment (D9).

The record has a settled position on exactly this pattern and it has not been contested in eleven memoranda: every provision so far is American-owned and withdrawn by 1973, or works below the level at which 1972 and 1975 were decided, or was captured by the palace when the American lever lapsed (POSMT01-1). The American lever was never used to make the Republic own the instruments of the conventional war (POSCP01-2). Delta is the clearest case yet. On 29 March 1973 it flies home and the Republic has nothing of it except whatever counterpart unit was built beside it.

**F014-6** (ADVISE) MACV-JSOC's deconfliction duty is in direct tension with the provision's own compartmentation. The headquarters is required to interact with the GVN's military, intelligence and civil structures [U], and the only way it can protect a target is to tell those structures that a restricted area exists without telling them why. That buys safety for the raid and costs the thing the record calls advisory leverage: a Vietnamese corps commander who is handed a no-fire box over his own province eleven times a year, and never told what happened in it, learns that the Americans have a private war. Under POSTNA-6 that cost is named rather than assumed: the provision increases American effectiveness and reduces, by a small amount, the Republic's claim to be running its own war.

### Employment concepts

#### EC014-1 · The held-belt night lift

The standard operation and about two-thirds of everything the unit does in the South. A name and a house come from the task force's fusion centre in the afternoon. The squadron's troop commander plans for four to eight hours. Two MH-6-class aircraft of E Squadron, or four UH-1s where the theater provides them, lift one assault team and a Special Branch officer from the forward operating base after dark. Transit 20–40 minutes. The team lands 300–800 metres out and walks in on goggles. On the objective: 2–6 minutes. Recovery immediately, with the man, his papers and whoever was sleeping in the room. At Tan Son Nhut or the corps centre the papers are read the same night and the task force decides whether there is a follow-on by 04:00 [M012]. Cycle: one per troop per two to three nights sustained. Minimum viable package: one assault team, one Special Branch officer, two aircraft, and a file good enough to name the room. Without the Special Branch officer it is a kidnapping, and without the file it is a dry hole.

#### EC014-2 · The province committee take-down

The concept the unit exists for. G Squadron puts two men in to watch a meeting place for three to five nights — a pagoda, a copse, a particular sampan mooring — and confirms who comes. Then a troop takes the meeting whole. It is slow (two to three weeks from file to raid), rare (perhaps one or two a month across the theater), and returns more than everything else the unit does, because a committee taken together cannot warn one another. The take is the committee's courier net, which is the task force's next twenty targets.

#### EC014-3 · The base-area stake-out

A reconnaissance team of four to six sits on a trail junction or a cache approach inside a base area in the South for three to five days, reports movement, and calls a J's box or an airmobile company onto a column. Launch by helicopter at last light at a distance, then one or two nights' walk in. Minimum package: one team, one radio, and a strike asset within the hour (POS003-1's standard). Without the strike asset it is observation that produces a report.

#### EC014-4 · The cross-border road watch

From October 1965 only, in Laos, on SOG's terms and under the ambassador's limits on depth, target and duration [S3]. Two to four teams at a time, watching a stretch of Route 9, Route 92 or a pass approach, cueing Tiger Hound and the J. Delta's teams complete perhaps two to three times as often undetected as SOG's (D13), which is the whole of the improvement. The concept degrades to SOG's own if the ambassador declines the depth.

#### EC014-5 · The Cambodian foot reconnaissance

From September 1967 only, crossing on foot, without tactical air, without a FAC, and with no exploitation force behind it [S3]. Teams smaller than in Laos. The product is the count of what is in the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak — which the record already has from the Global Hawk's radar looking 150–200 km into Cambodia [M003] — plus what the radar cannot see under the rubber. In May 1970 those folders are what the incursion's forces work from.

#### EC014-6 · The camp snatch

Prisoner recovery in the South. A report of a camp holding American or Vietnamese prisoners arrives from a rallier or a source. G Squadron confirms in one or two nights; a troop takes the camp before dawn. History's recovery centre attempted 39 operations in 1967 and 32 in 1968, succeeding 8 times in 1968 and 9 in 1969, and recovered 155 and 117 Vietnamese prisoners and not one American [S6]. Delta's speed and night capability raise the success rate — I estimate a half to two-thirds of attempts reaching the camp while prisoners are still in it, against history's quarter — and do not change the fundamental problem, which is that the report is usually days old.

#### EC014-7 · The Son Tay package

One operation, 21 November 1970, and the only ground raid into the North that the borders permit [S5] [U]. Delta would fly it with G Squadron on the ground beforehand instead of relying on imagery alone. That is the concept's whole significance, and it is treated under counterfactual erasure (F014-15), because a team that looks at the camp in October finds it empty and the raid is never flown.

#### EC014-8 · The partnered ARVN cordon (abandoned)

Tried in the first months: a Delta troop as the entry force with an ARVN Ranger company as the cordon. Abandoned by about mid-1965 on two grounds. The cordon told the hamlet that something was coming, which cost the surprise the unit's whole advantage rests on; and the plan had to be passed to the Ranger battalion the day before, which put the target in a Vietnamese channel (INTEL.penetration). What replaced it was the small raid with no cordon at all, accepting that some of the people in the house get away into the dark. The abandoned concept names the constraint: Delta cannot work inside a combined plan at the tempo the combined command of memorandum 012 was built to run.

#### EC014-9 · The COSVN decapitation raid (abandoned)

Planned repeatedly from 1965 and never executed. COSVN was about 2,400 people, dispersed, moving by bicycle between bunkers, sitting in the Mimot plantation inside Cambodia from 1965 to 1970, warned of B-52 raids by Soviet trawlers from 1968, and evacuated to Kratie on 19 March 1970 before the incursion could reach it [S7]. There was no building to raid, no border to cross before September 1967, and after that no exploitation force permitted. The provision names COSVN as a mission and the constraint set refuses it.

#### EC014-10 · The Hanoi raid (abandoned)

Considered after the opening strike of March 1965 broke the North's radar net and fighter force [M013], when the North was briefly more open to a helicopter penetration than it would be again. Abandoned because the authority does not exist: a ground operation into the North is the President's, and history granted it once, for Son Tay, in 1970 [S5] [U].

#### EC014-11 · The sustained hundred-raid month (abandoned)

The rate Part 1 put at the top of its range, and the honest answer is that it is a surge and not a rate. Six assault troops forward, 120 raids a month, is a raid every second night per troop with 6–24 hours of planning before each. The unit can hold that for two or three weeks. Sustained across years the rate is 25–50 raids a month in the South, with surges to 100–120 for a fortnight when a file breaks open and a corresponding trough afterwards. The same correction applies to reconnaissance: 8–12 teams in the field at once, not the 15–25 of Part 1, because the top of that range needs the recon troops of three squadrons and all of G Squadron at once and leaves nothing resting. And the two concepts compete: a squadron raiding in the Delta is not watching a trail junction in the Do Xa. This is the finding of F014-13 and the reason the manifest's FIND.vci push fell to 40–150 cadre a year.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**F014-7** (SUBVERT, SECURE) The senior infrastructure stops sleeping where it can be reached, and the displacement is upward and outward rather than downward. The province and region committee member of 1966 stops spending nights in hamlets within helicopter range of a forward operating base and moves to three places: the base areas inside the South, where he is guarded by a guerrilla platoon; across the border into Cambodia, where no American ground force may follow until September 1967 and then only on foot; and into the towns, where a raid is a police matter and a night entry is a political event. This adds to the displacement the task force already caused (F012-8) and is the same mechanism one layer up: Delta pushes the men the task force pushed out of the hamlet still further from it. What the allies lose by pushing him there is the thing that made him a target in the first place. A committee member who sleeps in Memot visits his districts every second month instead of every second week, so the file that named his house stops being refreshed, and the courier line that replaces his presence is longer, slower and — this is the compensation — more interceptable.

**F014-8** (FIND, INTERDICT) In the base areas the enemy displaces by tightening rather than moving. From about 1966 the trail junctions in War Zone C, the Do Xa and the U Minh acquire standing security: a squad on the junction itself, listening posts out, and a practice of moving in darkness without lights that Delta's goggles can see and the enemy does not know can be seen. The teams' first eighteen months are therefore their most productive, and their yield falls as the security thickens. This is the ordinary life cycle of a reconnaissance advantage and the record should expect it rather than discover it in 1968.

### Bottleneck migration

**F014-9** (SUBVERT) On the attack on the infrastructure the binding constraint before the provision was reach into places the police arm could not enter; after it, for 25–50 targets a month, it is identification again — the file names the apparatus, not the bed — and behind that, detention (F012-12, F014-4). The new constraint is not the one the provision addressed, which is the ordinary result, and here it is the record's own long-standing one.

**F014-10** (SUBVERT, FIND) On Delta itself the constraint migrates twice. In 1964–65 it is targets: the task force's file is young and the unit is learning the country, so the squadrons are underemployed and the yield is a third to a half of the 1966–67 figure (D12). From about 1966 it is the operator pool and its rotation, which is the provision's real ceiling: about 280–350 people who go on operations, losing perhaps 20–50 a year in the cross-border years against a pipeline that returns 30–50, with no home station to rotate to because the commitment is full (F014-13). From about 1967–68 a third constraint arrives, the 2026 equipment stock (D8, D12). None of the three is the constraint the provision was granted to relieve.

**F014-11** (STRIKE) One bottleneck genuinely opens. The record's limit on precision has been location, not accuracy, in every memorandum since 005 (POS005-1), and under canopy there was no instrument at all. A reconnaissance team standing 400 metres from a bunker complex with a laser rangefinder and a satellite radio is that instrument. The number of targets it produces is small — perhaps 20–60 measured coordinates under canopy a year at maturity [E: 8–12 teams, each finding something worth a bomb on a minority of its missions] — and they are the first of their kind in this war.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F014-12** (SUSTAIN, FIND) The scarce things Delta consumes are helicopter hours and intelligence attention, not spaces. E Squadron is company-sized [S2] and covers the small lifts; everything larger competes for theater lift in the years the record has already identified as short (the 1964–65 hours the Global Hawk's tracks could not be reacted to, F003's dependence). I estimate Delta's demand at 1–3% of in-country helicopter hours in 1965–67, rising where a camp snatch or a committee take-down needs a company lift [E: a troop-sized operation every second night against the theater's monthly hours]. The larger cost is analytical: the task force's best Vietnamese-reading analysts are pulled toward Delta's take because it is the richest, which is the same upward pull the task force itself exerted on the district centres (F012-1). What is not being done is the patient district-level file work that produced the record's largest blow to the infrastructure (F012-27).

**F014-13** (SUSTAIN) The unit has nowhere to rotate to, and this is the provision's structural cost. Delta sustains its standard by cycling squadrons home to rest, retrain and run selection and the six-month operator course [S1]. Committed whole to one theater for eleven years, it has no home station in any meaningful sense, and the training and selection cadre that came with it is in Vietnam rather than at Fort Bragg running the course that replaces its losses. The arithmetic: 20–50 operators lost a year against a pipeline of 30–50 means the unit roughly holds its headcount and steadily loses its experience, and by about 1968 the median operator is a man selected and trained during the war rather than before it. The honest sustained rates follow (EC014-11), and they are about half what Part 1's upper figures implied. There is one mitigation and it should be stated: in 1964 there is no national hostage-rescue mission and no other theater, so the unit gives up no commitment elsewhere by being here.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F014-14** (SUBVERT) What stops happening is the slow route to the senior cadre. Historically a province committee member was taken, if at all, as a by-product: killed in a conventional operation (about 87% of Phoenix's killed were [M006]), or arrested months after his name entered a file, or never. In this war a proportion of those men are taken deliberately, at a time of the allies' choosing, alive, with their papers. The forces and attention that the slow route consumed — the sweep operations mounted on a week-old report, the cordons that found the house empty — are freed, and in this record they were already being freed by the task force. Delta extends the erasure one layer up.

**F014-15** (SUBVERT, FIND) The sharpest single erasure in this memorandum is Son Tay. In October 1970 G Squadron can put two men on the hill above the camp for three nights [E: EC014-2 applied to a fixed target]. They would report what the camp was: empty since 14 July 1970, the prisoners moved to Camp Faith [S5]. The raid of 21 November 1970 is then not flown. What disappears with it is a 116-aircraft operation, 56 soldiers on the ground, 42 PAVN guards killed, two Americans wounded and one F-105 lost [S5] — and also the only American rescue attempt into the North, which the prisoners themselves took as proof that they had not been forgotten, and which the American public took the same way. The raid was a political and moral event whose military result was nil. A better reconnaissance capability cancels the event and keeps the nil. The record should expect more of this: Delta's main effect on the spectacular operations of this war may be to prevent the ones that would have been flown on bad intelligence.

### Perverse effect

**F014-16** (SECURE, SUBVERT) The wrong-man raid at night, conducted by men whose faces nobody sees, is the provision's liability and it is worse than the task force's version of the same error. The task force's raids inside the held belt are Special Branch arrests on warrant with magistrate review in 72 hours, and the record's audit catches some of the errors within a week [M012] [M002] [M001]. A Delta entry into a house in a contested hamlet at 02:00 has no warrant, no uniform the family recognises and no explanation. The record already holds that where the wrong man is taken the household record that identified him makes the family a name the Front can use (F012-16, F012-17). Here the Front gets something better: a story of foreigners who come in the dark, which is true. I expect this to be a real cost in the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps, where the files are thin and the raids are therefore least accurate, and small in the Delta and the Saigon belt where they are thick.

**F014-17** (SUBVERT, GVN) A unit that can take a guarded house at night is a unit that can take a palace, and every Vietnamese general in Saigon will see that before he sees anything else. The record's governments through 1964–67 are coup-prone and coup-haunted (the Khanh period runs through memorandum 012's first year). Delta's presence is therefore read in Saigon as an American instrument of regime security or regime change, depending on who is speaking, and that reading is not wrong: a force at Tan Son Nhut that can be at the Gia Long palace in six minutes changes the calculus of the officers who might otherwise move. I judge this a mild stabiliser in 1964–66 and a standing irritant throughout, and it is a real reason a Vietnamese counterpart unit (D9) would be built slowly and watched closely.

**F014-18** (ADVISE) Success creates a reason to compartment further. Each raid that succeeds because the target stayed inside American channels is evidence for keeping the next one out of Vietnamese hands, and the headquarters' liaison officers will make that argument honestly. Over years it hardens into a private American war inside an allied one, which is the cost named in F014-6 compounding.

### Anticipation

**F014-19** (SUBVERT) The most important second-order effect in this memorandum is that the capability prevents the engagements that would demonstrate it, and the metrics improve while the organisation survives. By about 1966–67 the senior cadre in the raided provinces have stopped sleeping where Delta can reach them (F014-7). Delta's raids then find fewer senior men, and the honest reading of a falling yield is that the capability is working — exactly the inversion the record already recorded for the police and the task force, where arrests fall where they work best (POS002-1, F012-20). The measure is not Delta's raid yield. It is the Front's tax take, its recruitment, and how far its committees have to live from the districts they run (A13). The command will be tempted by the raid count because it is the number the unit generates, and the record's own position is that a provision which improves a metric without improving the war shortens the American clock (POSTNA-5).

### Enemy detection and learning

**F014-20** (SUBVERT, FIND) COSVN detects the new thing within six to twelve months of September 1964, from the pattern rather than from a document: committee members taken from houses at night, no cordon, no sweep, nobody seen, helicopters heard afterwards rather than before. Nguyen Chi Thanh's headquarters, and after July 1967 Pham Hung's [S7], derive three rules and propagate them to the regions within a season and to the district cells within two: senior cadre do not sleep twice in the same hamlet; meetings are held in base areas or across the border, never in villages; and couriers carry nothing written. That doctrine is cheap, correct, and it travels fast because it is three sentences.

The enemy also gets two things right that hurt. He works out, correctly, that the night-raiders cannot cross the border, because in three years they never do — so Cambodia becomes not merely a sanctuary but the specific answer to this specific threat, which is a conclusion the constraint set hands him. And he works out that the unit is small, because its raids are concentrated and intermittent rather than general, so the right response is dispersal rather than fortification.

What he gets wrong is its nature. The Front will describe Delta as an American assassination unit, as it described Phoenix historically and as COSVN described the task force in this record (F012-21). Here the description is closer to true than it was there, because these are soldiers entering houses at night without warrants in the contested belt, and the propaganda is correspondingly more effective. It is believed in the coastal lowlands where the raids are least accurate (F014-16).

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F014-21** (SUBVERT) The exchange rate is bad and it is the central judgment of this memorandum. The provision costs 1,250–1,950 personnel spaces, the best 300 special operators in the American army, a company of aircraft, and an equipment stock that cannot be replaced in theater. The counter-move costs the Front a courier's walking time. A province committee member who sleeps in a different hamlet each night, or across the border, loses perhaps a third of his contact with his districts and gains near-immunity; the cadre he governs through lose some tempo; nothing is paid in people. Set against the adaptations the record has made the enemy buy — the braided road net of 1966 that cost Group 559 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers [M005], the compartmentation and dawn moves the task force forced on 12–18 provinces' district administration (F012-27) — Delta's adaptation is the cheapest the enemy has been asked to make.

Two qualifications, both real. The adaptation is cheap per man and not free in aggregate: an organisation whose leadership lives two border provinces away from the districts it administers runs slower in every respect, and the record holds that this kind of friction is what broke the Front's district government of the held belt (F012-27). And the adaptation does not work against reconnaissance: a team watching a trail junction is not countered by sleeping elsewhere.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F014-22** (US, SUBVERT) Follow the loop from Delta's own losses. The unit takes 20–50 operator casualties a year in the cross-border years, of whom perhaps 8–25 killed [E: SOG's 1968 and 1969 rates scaled to Delta's teams, S3]. Those are 8–25 names a year added to the American dead, against a casualty variable that stood at 14,450–14,560 in 1968 — too small to move. But they are not ordinary names. A Delta operator lost on a mission in Laos in 1966 is a death that cannot be explained, because the operation cannot be acknowledged, and the record already holds that the American clock is spent fastest by the discovery that reported progress was false (POSTNA-5). Historically SOG's dead carried cover stories for thirty years. A larger, more visible unit doing the same work raises the chance of the story breaking during the war rather than after it, which is a route from a theater operation to American political tolerance that no provision in the record has yet opened. The loop returns into the theater as a restriction: the halt Washington imposed on all northern operations in 1968 [S3] is the precedent, and a halt on Delta's cross-border work in 1967–68 is a plausible consequence of one exposure.

**F014-23** (SUBVERT, NEGOT) The prisoner loop does not close, and saying so is the honest finding. The camp snatch (EC014-6) recovers Vietnamese prisoners at a better rate than history's 155 and 117 [S6] — I estimate perhaps 250–500 a year at maturity. American prisoners are almost all held in the North, where Delta cannot go. So the number that mattered to the Paris negotiation and to American domestic politics — Americans held in Hanoi — is unchanged, and the negotiating position is unchanged with it. The loop runs out rather than returning.

### Open channel

**F014-24** (FIND, SUBVERT) Two mechanisms the nine channels do not name. The first is that Delta's take becomes a measurement of the Front's senior organisation that the Front itself generates: a province committee taken whole with its papers is a census of a level of the enemy's administration that no other instrument in the record reaches, and it is a figure the allies did not have to estimate. The second is that the record holds no account of American prisoners of war, no event for the prisoner issue, and no variable for recovery, so one of the five missions this provision was granted cannot be scored at all. That is a gap of the record and not of the provision, and it should go to the register.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F014-25 · Counter-directional** (SUBVERT, ADVISE) The provision pulls the war's special operations effort toward the spectacular and away from the work that was actually breaking the enemy, and it does so through the command's own good judgment rather than against it. The record's largest blow to the infrastructure was the task force's grinding destruction of the Front's district government in 12–18 provinces, built on files, warrants, audits and the same-night follow-on (F012-27, POS012-1). Delta is more interesting than that in every way: it is faster, it takes bigger names, and its operations brief beautifully. Its 25–50 raids a month will absorb the best analysts' attention, the scarcest lift at night, and — once Washington knows the unit is there — the command's own appetite, because a headquarters that can mount a Son Tay will be asked to. Son Tay itself is the illustration: 116 aircraft and six months of planning for a camp that had been empty since July [S5]. In this war the reconnaissance would have found it empty (F014-15), and the next proposal would have come anyway.

Two smaller counter-directional effects compound it. The provision makes the Front's atrocity story true enough to work in the provinces where the files are thin (F014-16), and it hands the enemy a specific reason to value Cambodia that the constraint set itself supplies (F014-20). And the unit's own degradation under a full, unrotated commitment (F014-13) means the instrument the command comes to rely on in 1968 is not the instrument it received in 1964.

**F014-26 · Strongest positive effect** (SUBVERT, FIND)
<!-- spe:modest -->
The strongest effect is real, specific and modest, and it should be stated that way. From late 1964 the Front's senior cadre in the South lose the night in the provinces where the government holds the file: about 40–150 more listed cadre at district level or above a year by 1967, taken alive where possible, with their papers, at a time of the allies' choosing, and taken from the layer the record's other instruments could not reach — province and region committees rather than hamlet tax collectors. Compounded with the task force (F014-1), it accelerates the destruction of the Front's upper administration in 12–18 provinces by perhaps three to six months and raises the proportion of senior cadre taken alive, which is the difference between a dead man and a courier net. That is the honest ceiling. The organisation it attacks is counted in tens of thousands, it replaces a province committee member within weeks, and it answers the whole provision by moving where its men sleep (F014-21). Delta does not touch the main force, the sanctuaries, Hanoi's will, the Republic's self-sufficiency, or the American clock. It is the best instrument in the record for one narrow task that the record was already doing adequately by other means.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

MACV gains a joint special operations command of 250–450 people at Tan Son Nhut and loses about 40–80 of its own and the services' best staff officers into its liaison cells. MACV J-2 acquires a customer that wants names and addresses rather than orders of battle, which shifts a share of its analytical effort toward the political organisation — the shift memorandum 012's task force had already begun (F012-1). The selected advisory corps of memorandum 006 acquires an unadvertised function: the eighteen-month adviser is how the headquarters learns which Vietnamese staffs leak (F014-18).

### US conventional forces

Unchanged in employment. Delta neither relieves nor burdens an American infantry battalion, and it does not change the search-and-destroy pattern of 1965–68 in any way. The one contact point is reaction: a Delta team in trouble in a base area inside the South is extracted by whatever is available, and twice or three times a year that is an American airmobile company's day.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

This is where the provision bites hardest on what already exists. The 5th Special Forces Group's camps, the CIDG programme and the Mike Forces continue unchanged in mission, but they lose their claim to be the theater's special operations effort and some of their best people to Delta's selection, which draws from the same pool. MACV-SOG continues: it is not subordinated to MACV-JSOC in this memorandum, and the record should be explicit that two special operations headquarters now sit beside MACV, which is a deconfliction problem of its own (F014-6). SOG keeps the cross-border mission and the agent programme; Delta adds teams to the same areas under the same ambassadorial limits [S3]. The Project Delta of the 5th Group (Detachment B-52), which carried the same name historically, does what it did.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

Unchanged in capability. Changed in experience: an ARVN corps commander receives restricted operating areas over his own provinces without explanation (F014-6), and an ARVN Ranger company's brief period as Delta's cordon force in 1964–65 ends when the concept is abandoned (EC014-8). The JGS learns that the Americans have a unit it is not told about, which it will have worked out within months.

### RF/PF and territorial security

Unchanged. The territorial forces hold the hamlets whose files make Delta's raids possible, and they are told nothing. One real friction: a Popular Forces platoon that hears helicopters and shooting in its own hamlet at 02:00 and was not warned will respond, and the restricted operating area is what prevents that from becoming the provision's first disaster (F014-6).

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. Delta's teams do not enter the North before 1970 and have no part in the campaign the record now runs from Saigon (POS013-1).

### Trail interdiction

Changed at the margin from October 1965. Two to four road-watch teams at a time add to SOG's cueing, and their reports are better and more timely than SOG's (EC014-4). Against a braided net with no single point of failure (POS005-4), the effect is 0 to 4% of tonnage arriving at most, inside the width the chapters already hold.

### Arc Light

Changed slightly in quality of target. A reconnaissance team's measured coordinate under canopy is a better aimpoint than a map reference from a captured document, and 20–60 such coordinates a year reach the cell at Tan Son Nhut (F014-11). The tonnage and the pattern are unchanged.

### Airmobile formations

Unchanged in mission; they supply the lift Delta's own company of light helicopters cannot (F014-12), at 1–3% of in-country hours.

### Riverine and coastal forces

Changed only as Delta's transport in the Delta and the U Minh, where the unit has no boats of its own. Game Warden's PBRs and the Vietnamese river groups insert and recover teams in IV Corps perhaps twice a month at maturity [E: the share of raids in the Delta reachable only by water].

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

The district centres of memorandum 006 and the task force of memorandum 012 are Delta's supplier and its partner, and the relationship is the one in F014-1 and F014-4. The centres' files are the base layer; the task force's fusion centre is the deck; the exploitation of Delta's take goes back through both the same night. The programme's audited count, which the record has used as its measure since 1965 (POS006-5), now includes a few hundred senior cadre a year taken by soldiers rather than police, and the audit should mark them, because a neutralisation without a warrant is a different legal object from one with (F014-16).

### Third-country forces

Unchanged. The Korean and Australian forces of memorandum 012's combined command have no part in Delta's operations, and 1 ATF's own SAS squadron in Phuoc Tuy continues as historically. The record should note that Australian SAS and Delta working the same province would be a natural pairing and that nothing in this provision creates it.
