## EV014-1 · MACV-JSOC stands up and Delta arrives · 15 Aug – 31 Dec 1964 · Tan Son Nhut, Saigon; forward operating bases at Da Nang, Kontum, Bien Hoa and Can Tho · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

COMUSMACV's order of 15 August 1964 created the Joint Special Operations Command, Vietnam, eleven days after the second report from the Gulf of Tonkin that this war never answered with Pierce Arrow (EV000-3) [X-014]. Brigadier General Harold M. Vance, lately of the Special Warfare Center at Fort Bragg, took it with a staff that reached 280 by December and 390 by the following June: a joint targeting cell, a joint operations centre that stood a watch from 18:00 to 06:00, and nineteen liaison cells — MACV J-2 and J-3, MACV-SOG, the CIA station, the Combined Interagency Task Force and its National Fusion Centre (P012b), the combined command's operations centre, the 2nd Air Division, the four US corps-level commands and the four ARVN corps headquarters, the Joint General Staff, the National Police, the US Mission and the Embassy in Vientiane [X-014]. The headquarters held no authority anyone else had held: it could command the units assigned to it, declare a restricted operating area over a hamlet for a night, and claim a target from the task force's deck. It could compel nothing outside MACV.

The 1st Special Forces Operational Detachment-Delta came in three lifts of MATS C-135Bs and C-124s from Pope Air Force Base between 28 August and 12 September 1964, 1,310 officers and men under Colonel James E. Teague, with 288 operators in four assault squadrons and G Squadron; E Squadron's unmarked light helicopters came by sea to Saigon port on 24 September and flew from Tan Son Nhut from 2 October [X-014]. Three squadrons went forward to Bien Hoa, Can Tho and Da Nang and one stayed at Tan Son Nhut to rest and retrain, which is the rotation the unit kept for the next eight years and the reason its sustained rate was never what its first month suggested (F014-13).

The first three months were learning and they were cheap. Thirty-one raids were mounted in the Saigon belt and the upper Delta between 11 October and 31 December, all of them on addresses from the task force's Gia Dinh and Long An files; nine reached a cadre the file had listed at district level or above, and nineteen found the house empty or the wrong man in it [X-014]. Teague's men did not know the country: the Delta's canal lines, the way a hamlet's dogs work, which path a cadre takes when he runs. On 14 and 22 November the squadron at Bien Hoa tried the concept it had brought with it, a Delta troop as the entry force with a company of the 32nd Ranger Battalion as the cordon, at Phu Hoa Dong and at Trung Lap. Both times the cordon moved into position before midnight and both times the house was empty by the time the troop reached it, and on the second the Rangers' battalion had been given the hamlet's name the previous afternoon. The concept was abandoned in December (EC014-8) and nothing replaced it: from then on Delta went in without a cordon and accepted that some of the men in the house would get into the dark. The unit's first dead was Sergeant First Class Donald R. Keel, shot through the throat at a doorway in Can Giuoc on 2 December 1964; two more operators were wounded in the same ten weeks [X-014].

## EV014-2 · Operation NIGHT HERON: the Long An province committee · 9 Feb – 18 Apr 1965 · Can Giuoc, Can Duoc and Rach Kien Districts, Long An Province; the Rung Sat edge of Nha Be, Gia Dinh Province · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

NIGHT HERON was the first operation that showed what the unit was for, and it was built on a file three other provisions had made. The task force's DRY SEASON campaign had broken the Can Giuoc security section from a notebook in December 1964 (EV012-2); the section's papers named a courier, the courier's family was in the Special Branch's census (P002a), and the land register said whose house he slept in (P001a). What none of that could do was reach the eleven men of the Long An province committee, who met every third or fourth week at a sampan mooring on the Rach Cat where no police patrol had been since 1962.

Two men of G Squadron lay in the nipa palm 240 metres from the mooring for four nights between 9 and 23 February and for three more in March, counted the arrivals, and photographed six of them at ranges under 300 metres with a starlight-assisted camera [X-014]. On the night of 14–15 March 1965 two assault teams and a Special Branch inspector came in by three of E Squadron's helicopters to a paddy 900 metres east, walked in on goggles, and took the mooring at 02:40. Six of the eleven committee members were there, with the province finance section's ledgers for 1964 and a list of the collectors in nineteen villages; two cadre who reached for weapons were killed, the committee's secretary got into the Rung Sat with the political officer, and one Delta operator was shot through the forearm [X-014]. The task force's exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut read the ledgers by 06:00 and the arrests ran for nine days: twenty-three collectors and two village committees taken on Special Branch warrants, with magistrate review in each case inside 72 hours under the 1964 statute (P002a) [X-014].

Long An's collections from the posted villages of the three districts fell by about two-thirds through the summer of 1965 and were about a third of the previous year's by December, which cost the Front both money and the rice it bought with it in a province where the titling had already ended rent (EV001-2, EV012-2) [X-014]. The secretary rebuilt the committee from the Rung Sat by August with cadres sent down from the region, as the Can Giuoc section had been rebuilt before it (C012-1). What did not come back was the mooring: the committee never again met in a place a boat could reach from a hamlet with a post.

## EV014-3 · The rhythm of the raid, 1965–1967 · 1 Aug 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The Saigon belt (Gia Dinh, Long An, Hau Nghia, Bien Hoa), the upper Delta (Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa, Vinh Long, Phong Dinh) and the coastal lowlands of Binh Dinh and Quang Ngai · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

By the spring of 1966 the unit had found its rate, and the rate was not the one the first months had promised. Three assault squadrons forward gave six assault troops, and six troops with six to twenty-four hours of planning behind each raid produced 38 raids a month on average in 1966 and 44 in 1967 — with one month, March 1966, at 104 after the Dinh Tuong finance section's courier book fell into the task force's hands, and April at 19 while the squadrons that had flown it stood down [X-014]. Reconnaissance ran at nine to twelve teams in the field at once, each out three to six days, and the two could not be run at full stretch together: a squadron raiding in Kien Hoa was not watching a trail junction in the Do Xa (EC014-11).

The yield was concentrated and it was real. Of about 1,000 raids in the thirty months, 171 reached a listed cadre at district level or above in 1966 and 126 in 1967, with 61 province or region committee members among them over the two years and nine finance sections taken with their ledgers [X-014]. Four in five of those raids were in III and IV Corps, where the files were thick, and the squadron at Da Nang spent most of 1966 and 1967 on reconnaissance rather than raiding because the coastal hamlets of Quang Ngai had no file worth raiding on and the Front held them at night (F014-5, POSCP03-3). In the Highlands the unit worked only as reconnaissance in the tri-border, where it had no file, no language and no Montagnard source it could use without an interpreter the enemy might own.

The price was paid in the one currency the unit could not print. Between August 1965 and December 1967 Delta lost fourteen operators killed, four missing in Laos and fifty-one wounded, with two of E Squadron's helicopters shot down over the Rung Sat and the Plain of Reeds [X-014]. Fort Bragg returned thirty to fifty operators a year from a selection course run by a cadre that was itself in Vietnam, so the unit held its numbers and spent its experience: by the end of 1967 about a quarter of the men in the assault troops had been selected and trained after the war began (F014-13).

## EV014-4 · The road watch: Delta in Laos · 20 Oct 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The Laotian panhandle: Routes 9, 92 and 912, the Ban Laboy ford, the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

Washington authorised cross-border reconnaissance in Laos on 21 September 1965 and SOG's first team went 24 km in against target D-1 on 18 October [S3] [S4]. Delta's teams went in behind SOG's on the same authority and under the same limits: Ambassador Sullivan in Vientiane set the depth, the targets and the length of each mission, and no exploitation force followed (F014-20). Two to four Delta teams of five were in the panhandle at a time from 20 October 1965, watching the stretches the Global Hawk's radar could see from above and not under the trees (EV003-3).

What the teams added was accuracy and survival. A SOG team of two or three Americans and three to twelve indigenous soldiers moved by day, navigated by map and called a FAC overhead to relay [S3]; a Delta team moved at night on goggles and spoke to Tan Son Nhut by satellite with its position to a few metres (E01). Of 94 Delta missions into the panhandle in 1966–67, 71 were completed without the team being compromised, against a SOG rate nearer one in three [X-014] [S3]. The product went into STEADY COUNT's track list and the coordinate cell's folders (EV003-3, EV005-1): 84 truck parks, 31 gun positions and 19 storage points measured to a few metres, about a third of them under canopy where no other instrument in this war could find anything at all (F014-11).

It changed what arrived in the South by almost nothing. Group 559 had answered HARD CROSSING in 1966 by owning nothing that could be measured, and a braided net of fords and night pontoons does not care how exactly one of its crossings is known (EV005-2, POS005-4). The teams' cueing taxed the flow by perhaps one to four points more than the radar alone, inside the width the phase already holds. It cost nine Americans killed or missing in two years, including Captain Robert L. Ainsley's team of five, lost whole near the Ban Laboy ford between 11 and 14 July 1967 after two days of transmissions and then nothing, to a 559th Group security battalion that had learned by then to hunt the watchers (F014-22) [X-014].

## EV014-5 · The empty beds of Dinh Tuong · 4 Sep – 14 Nov 1967 · Cai Lay, Cai Be and Giao Duc Districts, Dinh Tuong Province; Memot, Kampong Cham Province, Cambodia · NEW · 014
*Initiative: both*

This is the operation that taught the command what the unit had actually done, and it did so by failing for ten weeks.

In the first week of September 1967 the National Fusion Centre gave the Can Tho squadron twenty-six addresses in the three upper districts of Dinh Tuong: the province committee's five surviving members, the finance section's chief, and twenty of the collectors and couriers who had been named in the ledgers taken in 1966. The file was the best in the Delta. The province had a post in every village the government held, a resident constable in most of them, a family census three years deep and a land register that said who tilled what (P002a, P001a, EV002-4). Between 4 September and 14 November the squadron mounted twenty-three raids on those addresses, most of them inside two hours of the decision and all at night.

It took two. A courier of nineteen was lifted out of a house in Cai Be on 19 September with a bag of receipts, and a village committee secretary at Giao Duc on 2 October. The other twenty-one raids found a bed that had been slept in and a family that said, truthfully, that the man had not been there for months.

The courier told them why, and the task force's interrogators had it out of her in two days. The province committee had not lived in Dinh Tuong since March. It lived at Memot, in the rubber across the Cambodian border, with the region's finance section, and it came into the province for four or five nights every second month, moving every night, sleeping in a different hamlet each time and never in a house named in a ledger. The rule had come down in a COSVN circular in April 1967, over Pham Hung's name — he had taken the Central Office when Nguyen Chi Thanh died in Hanoi in July, and the circular was Thanh's last instruction carried out by his successor [S7]. It had three sentences in it: senior cadre do not sleep twice in a hamlet; committees meet in the base areas or across the border and never in villages; couriers carry nothing written (F014-20).

At Tan Son Nhut in November the argument ran the way arguments about metrics run. The unit's raid yield in the Delta had fallen by about two-fifths from 1966 and the natural reading was that the squadron had gone slack. Vance's J-2 and the task force's analysts read it the other way and were right: the committees of the upper Delta had been driven out of the province they administered, were running it by courier from across an international border at two months' remove, and the falling yield was the measure of that success and not of failure (F014-19). The Front paid for it in the only currency it had to pay in. The province's collections from the posted villages ran at about a sixth of 1964's through 1967, its rice procurement in the three districts fell with them, and the committee that had once set a village's tax rate in person now learned of a bad harvest six weeks after it happened (EV012-4) [X-014]. It did not pay in people. The adaptation cost the Front a courier's walking time, which against 1,310 Americans and the best three hundred operators in their army is the cheapest answer any provision in this record has drawn from the enemy (F014-21).

The command changed its measure in December 1967. Delta's reporting after that gave the raid count, and beside it the Front's collections in the raided districts and the distance the committees lived from them.

## EV014-6 · Delta at Tet: the command posts in Saigon and the files at Hue · 29 Jan – 8 Mar 1968 · Saigon (Districts 5, 6 and 8, the Phu Tho racetrack, Gia Dinh); Hue · NEW · 014
*Initiative: enemy*

In the ten days before Tet the unit raided eleven addresses in Gia Dinh and Saigon out of the task force's file and took two couriers of the city's sapper organisation, on 24 and 27 January. Neither knew anything about the timing of the offensive, and neither did the file. The national file named the Front's political apparatus, street by street in places, and the apparatus is not where the decision to attack at Tet was held or when it was transmitted; that is the finding the record had already made and this memorandum confirms from the other side of the same week (POSCP03-2, DIRCP03-3) [X-014]. The warning that reached the corps came from the task force's and MACV's reading of the troop movements, not from Delta's prisoners (EV000-42).

Once the attack came, the unit was used for what it was best at and not for what it was built for. Between 1 and 9 February three assault troops cleared fourteen Front command posts in Districts 5, 6 and 8 and around the Phu Tho racetrack, moving at night with goggles against men who had never fought anyone who could see in the dark; eight of the fourteen were taken without a shot fired at the entry [X-014]. It was the best urban assault force in the theater and it was spent as infantry for nine days. It cost nine operators killed and thirty-one wounded, the unit's worst month of the war, about a tenth of the operators then in the country (F014-13) [X-014].

At Hue a troop was flown in on 9 February on Vance's own authority with two Special Branch officers, to the Special Branch compound inside the triangle, to recover the province's and districts' infrastructure files before the Front's security service could finish with them. It recovered two of the seven district files and the province register's index. The rest had gone north with the Front's security cadres in the first week, as the record has it (EV000-44) [X-014]. Two operators were killed in the compound's courtyard on 10 February. The judgment the memorandum takes from that morning is narrow and worth stating: a raid force can recover files a few days late and cannot prevent their capture, and the record's own account of what Hue cost the file-building effort stands unaltered.

## EV014-7 · The Cambodian folders · 1 Jan 1969 – 30 Jun 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak; Memot and Snoul, Kampong Cham Province; Kratie · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

American teams could enter Cambodia from September 1967, on foot, without tactical air, without a forward air controller overhead and with no exploitation force behind them, and those limits held until the incursion [S3]. Delta put teams across on those terms from 12 September 1967 and ran 61 missions into the base areas in 1969 beside SOG's 454 [S3] [X-014]. The teams walked in from the border at night, lay up under the rubber by day, and counted: ninety-four cache sites, thirty-one of them under permanent roofing, eleven field hospitals, four vehicle parks and the Memot headquarters complex's outer security, which was three concentric belts of bunkers manned by a battalion [X-014]. The Global Hawk's radar had been counting the road traffic from Sihanoukville to those same base areas since 1965 and could not see a cache under rubber (EV003-4, F003-3). The folders the teams built are what the incursion's forces worked from on 29 April 1970, and they are part of why the caches fell in days rather than weeks (EV000-66).

COSVN was never in them. The Central Office was about 2,400 people who moved between bunkers and meeting houses by bicycle, warned of B-52 raids from 1968 by Soviet trawlers reporting the bombers' course, and it went northwest to Kratie on 19 March 1970, six weeks before the incursion crossed the border [S7]. Delta's teams watched the Memot complex for eleven nights in February 1970 and reported what the complex was: a rear-service and signals installation whose senior occupants had left. The provision names COSVN as a mission; the constraint set declined it from the first day and the enemy's own mobility declined it again (EC014-9, F014-20).

## EV000-69 · The Son Tay raid · 21 Nov 1970 · Son Tay prison camp, about 37 km west of Hanoi · CANCELLED · 014
*Initiative: neither*

The raid of 21 November 1970 was not flown in this war, and the reason is the reconnaissance that the historical operation did not have.

Planning began on 9 May 1970 and the joint task force under Brigadier General LeRoy J. Manor and Colonel Arthur D. Simons trained through the summer at Eglin, as historically [H59]. On 14 October, with the operation six weeks out, MACV-JSOC proposed what no American force had been able to offer in 1970 and this one could: two men of G Squadron on the ground. They went in on the night of 21 October by an HH-3E flying a Trail infiltration profile, lay on the wooded slope 1,400 metres southeast of the camp for three nights with a thermal sight and a satellite radio, and reported on 24 October that the compound held a guard element of perhaps forty men, that the cells were unoccupied, that no cooking was done for prisoners and that no prisoner was taken to the wells, which were dry and partly collapsed [X-014]. The prisoners had been moved on 14 July 1970 to the camp the prisoners themselves called Camp Faith, about 24 km closer to Hanoi [S5]. The Joint Chiefs cancelled the operation on 2 November 1970.

What the cancellation saved is easy to count: 116 aircraft committed, 56 Special Forces soldiers landed 37 km from Hanoi, an HH-3E deliberately crashed inside the compound, an F-105G lost to an SA-2, two raiders wounded and about 42 PAVN guards killed [S5] [H59]. What it cost is the part worth recording, because it was a cost nobody weighed at the time. Historically the raid failed in its purpose and succeeded in another: Hanoi read it as proof that its camps in the countryside could be reached, consolidated its prisoners into the central prisons in Hanoi over the following months, and many of the prisoners who came home in 1973 said their treatment and their morale improved from that winter [H59]. In this war the raid is not flown, the consolidation does not happen, and the American prisoners stay dispersed in the camps outside Hanoi through 1971 and 1972 under the regime they had before — and they never learn that anyone came for them (F014-15). A better reconnaissance capability cancelled an operation whose military result was nil and whose moral result was not.

## EV014-8 · The 81st Special Group: a Vietnamese unit beside Delta · 1 Mar 1968 – 31 Dec 1971 · Long Thanh, Bien Hoa Province; the Special Warfare Centre at Nha Trang; Tan Son Nhut · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

MACV-JSOC proposed a Vietnamese counterpart unit in October 1967 and the Joint General Staff agreed in February 1968, with Tet's lesson fresh: the best urban night force in the country was American and would go home. Selection opened at Long Thanh on 1 March 1968 from the Rangers, the airborne division and the Luc Luong Dac Biet, run by Delta's training element on its own four-week course; 23 of 214 candidates passed the first board and 14 finished the six-month operator course [X-014]. The first squadron of the 81st Special Group, about 70 operators under Major Nguyen Van Thuan, was operational in November 1969; a second followed in May 1971.

It was given the war's instruments as they existed in 1969 and not Delta's. AN/PVS-2 starlight scopes rather than fused goggles, AN/PRC-25s rather than satellite radios, and lift from the VNAF's 219th Helicopter Squadron rather than from E Squadron [X-014]. On a clear night it worked at perhaps half Delta's standard and on a wet one at a quarter, and its raids in Kien Hoa and Dinh Tuong in 1970–71 took district cadre rather than province committees, because the targets its American partner reached were across a border it was not sent over.

Two things about it belong in the record. The first is where it lived: the palace watched it from the day it was proposed, the Joint General Staff's special warfare directorate held it under a standing order that no element of it would be stationed within Saigon, and its families were housed inside the Long Thanh compound on rations drawn against the garrison's — which is why its desertion ran at about a fifth of the ARVN's rate in 1971, when a soldier's pay bought a third less rice than in 1966 (F014-17) [X-014]. A unit that can take a guarded house at night is a unit that can take a palace, and every general in Saigon had done that arithmetic before the first course ended. The second is what it inherited when Delta left: its training, its selection standard and nothing else that mattered. The goggles, the satellite radios, the helicopters and the folders went home with the Americans in 1973.
