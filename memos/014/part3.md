## §4.0 The course of the war

I have had the best three hundred soldiers in the United States Army in this theater since September of 1964, and the honest account of what they bought is a narrow one. I will give it narrow rather than wide, because the alternative is the kind of claim this command has made before and had to retract.

The war begins as it has begun in every version of this record. On 1 January 1964 the Republic gives itself a land law and an anti-corruption commission, a police force with a constable in the villages, district chiefs chosen from a list, and a national programme against the Front's political apparatus; a combined command sits at Tan Son Nhut with operational control of the ARVN corps, and a combined task force begins to build one file on the enemy's organisation. Over that first year the file is the thing that matters. By the summer of 1965 there are about 1,100 settled village posts, about 140 district intelligence centres, and a task force of 1,100 men that has learned in Can Giuoc that a notebook taken in an ambush is worth two months of patrolling.

Delta arrives into that, in three transport lifts between 28 August and 12 September 1964, eleven days after a joint special operations headquarters was stood up at Tan Son Nhut under Harold Vance. I want to be exact about what arrived, because the record will be read by men who know what this unit is in 2026 and will expect more of it than 1964 can use. Thirteen hundred men came. Two hundred and eighty-eight of them were operators. The rest were aviators, signallers, riggers, analysts, medics and the cadre that trains the next ones. They brought night-vision goggles that see a standing man at two hundred metres under starlight, radios that talk to this headquarters by satellite from under the canopy, and a company of light unmarked helicopters. They brought no Vietnamese, no artillery, no armour, and nothing that any depot in Asia can repair.

Their first season was learning and it was dear in the way that learning is. Thirty-one raids in the Saigon belt in the last ten weeks of 1964; nine of them reached a man the file had named; one sergeant first class dead at a doorway in Can Giuoc on 2 December. They tried the thing they had been trained to do, which is to take an objective with a cordon around it, twice in November with a Ranger company holding the ring, and both times the house was empty before the troop reached the door. They gave the cordon up in December. From then on they went in small, with no ring, and let some of the men in the house go into the dark. That decision is the whole unit in one line: it will trade completeness for surprise every time, because surprise is the only advantage it has that the enemy cannot buy.

In March 1965 they showed what the trade is worth. The Long An province committee had met for three years at a sampan mooring on the Rach Cat where no patrol had been since 1962. Two men lay in the nipa palm for seven nights across two months and counted the arrivals. On the night of 14 March two teams took the mooring at twenty to three in the morning with a Special Branch inspector walking in behind them: six of the eleven committee members, the province finance section's ledgers for the year, and the names of the collectors in nineteen villages. The task force's people had the ledgers read by six o'clock and ran the arrests for nine days on warrants, with a magistrate seeing every prisoner inside seventy-two hours. Long An's collections in those three districts ran at about a third of the previous year's by December. The committee's secretary got away into the Rung Sat and had a new committee by August. That is also the whole unit in one line.

Phase II is the American war at full stride and Delta is a footnote in its battles and a chapter in its other war. Three squadrons forward, six assault troops, thirty-eight raids a month in 1966 and forty-four in 1967 — with one month at a hundred and four, after a courier's book fell open in Dinh Tuong, and the month after it at nineteen while the men who had flown it slept. Across those thirty months they took two hundred and ninety-seven cadre the file had listed at district level or above, sixty-one of them province or region committee members, and nine finance sections with their ledgers. Four of every five of those raids were in III and IV Corps, because that is where the file is, and the squadron at Da Nang spent two years on reconnaissance because the coastal hamlets of Quang Ngai had nothing worth raiding on and the Front held them after dark. In the panhandle, from October 1965, their teams went into Laos under the ambassador's limits and brought back the first coordinates this war has ever had from under the canopy: eighty-four truck parks, thirty-one gun positions, nineteen storage points, measured to a few metres. It taxed what reaches the South by one to four points, which is inside the noise of a number this command has learned not to trust (POSTNA-4).

Then, in the autumn of 1967, in the best-filed province in the Delta, twenty-three raids in ten weeks found two men and twenty-one slept-in beds.

I regard the ten weeks in Dinh Tuong as the most useful thing this unit has done for me, and it did it by failing. The courier we took on 19 September told the interrogators what the empty beds meant. The province committee had not lived in Dinh Tuong since March. It lived at Memot, in the rubber over the Cambodian line, and came into its own province for four or five nights every second month, never twice in the same hamlet and never in a house a ledger named. The instruction had come down in a COSVN circular in April over Pham Hung's name, three sentences long, and it had travelled from the Central Office to a district cell in a season. My J-2 and the task force read the falling raid count correctly: the committees of the upper Delta had been driven out of the districts they administered, were running them by courier at two months' remove from another country, and their collections were at about a sixth of 1964's. The raid count was going down because we were winning the only part of this that we were winning.

And the enemy had bought that with a courier's walking time. That is the exchange rate, and I want it in the record in plain words: thirteen hundred Americans, the best three hundred operators in my army, a company of aircraft and an equipment stock that cannot be replaced in this hemisphere, against an adaptation that cost the Front nothing it counts in people. I have made Group 559 spend twenty-five thousand porters on a road net (M005). I have made the Front's district administration in twelve provinces govern from the base areas (M012). This provision bought me the cheapest answer I have yet been given.

Tet spent them as infantry, and they were worth it that week. In the ten days before the holiday they took two couriers of the city's sapper organisation out of the file and neither man knew a date; the file names the Front's political apparatus and the apparatus did not hold the timing, which is the finding this record had already made and which Tet made again from the other side (POSCP03-2). Once the attack came I had the best urban night force in Asia sitting at Tan Son Nhut, and between 1 and 9 February three troops cleared fourteen command posts in Districts 5, 6 and 8 and around the racetrack, eight of them without a shot at the entry, against men who had never in their lives fought anyone who could see in the dark. It cost nine dead and thirty-one wounded in nine days — a tenth of the operators I had in the country — and a troop went into Hue on the 9th for the Special Branch's files and came out with two district files of seven and the province index, and two more dead in the compound courtyard.

The drawdown years are the quiet ones, and they hold the single event I would most want a reader to notice. From September 1967 my teams could cross into Cambodia on foot, with no air support, no controller overhead and no force behind them, and they counted what the radar could not see under the rubber: ninety-four cache sites, eleven hospitals, the Memot complex's three belts of bunkers. Those folders are part of why the incursion found the caches in days in May 1970. COSVN was not in them; it went to Kratie on 19 March, six weeks before we crossed.

Then in October 1970 I put two men on a wooded slope fourteen hundred metres from the prison camp at Son Tay for three nights, and they reported that the cells were empty, that nobody cooked for prisoners and that the wells were dry. The prisoners had gone in July. The Joint Chiefs cancelled the raid on 2 November. One hundred and sixteen aircraft were not committed, fifty-six men did not land thirty-seven kilometres from Hanoi, forty-two guards did not die and an F-105 was not lost — and the consolidation that followed the historical raid did not happen either. Hanoi never learned that we could reach its country camps, our prisoners stayed scattered through 1971 and 1972 under the regime they already had, and no man in those camps ever heard that somebody had come for him. I cancelled a raid that would have failed. I am not certain I came out ahead, and I will not pretend to be.

What is left at the end is what is left of every American provision in this record. In March 1968 the Joint General Staff agreed to raise a Vietnamese unit beside mine, and the 81st Special Group's first squadron was operational in November 1969 with starlight scopes instead of goggles and PRC-25s instead of satellite radios, kept out of Saigon by standing order because every general in the city had worked out what a unit that can take a guarded house at night can also take. It is a good unit. It works at half my standard on a clear night and a quarter on a wet one. In 1973 Delta flew home with its goggles, its radios, its helicopters and its folders, and the 81st kept its training and its selection standard and nothing else.

The line of this war does not bend here. The Republic still stands at the end of 1975 in III and IV Corps and on the coast south of Nha Trang, because of what the land law, the police, the officer boards and the task force did to the countryside between 1964 and 1968, and it still falls, because none of that reached the corps commands or gave it an air force it owned. What Delta changed is a layer: the Front's senior apparatus in the provinces where we held the file lost the night from the autumn of 1964, and lost it hard enough that by 1967 it was administering its districts from another country. That is a real thing and it is not the war.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The phase gains a headquarters and a unit and almost nothing else, and the reason is arithmetic. MACV-JSOC stood up on 15 August 1964 and Delta's main body was complete on 12 September (EV014-1); the unit had three and a half months of operations before the year ended and ten before the phase did. In that time it raided only on the task force's and the district centres' files, which is the dependence that governs everything this provision does (F014-1). Its single finished operation, NIGHT HERON, is a clean demonstration of the identify-to-neutralise chain with Delta occupying one link of five (EV014-2, F014-4): the land register said whose house, the Special Branch census said who, the task force's notebook from DRY SEASON said where the money went, G Squadron said which night, and Delta reached the mooring. Take any one of the other four away and the operation does not exist.

The abandoned partnered cordon (EC014-8) is the phase's second useful finding, because it names a constraint the dossier had not surfaced: the unit cannot work inside the combined command's planning cycle at the tempo that cycle runs. A Ranger company has to be told the hamlet's name the afternoon before, and an afternoon is long enough (F014-18, INTEL.penetration). The near-miss at Trung Lap on 7 March 1965, where a Popular Forces squad walked up a hamlet path toward a raid nobody had told it about, is why the headquarters' procedure changed in the same week to pass a restricted area down to the platoon holding the ground or not declare it at all, at a cost of eleven cancelled raids in the first year (EV014-9, F014-6).

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front does not detect the new thing in this phase. Three months and thirty-one raids in two provinces look, from the receiving end, like the task force's own raiding with a different cordon doctrine, and the province committee of Long An read the loss of its mooring in March 1965 as a security failure of its own courier net rather than as a new American capability (EV014-2). The region's answer was the answer it had already given in Can Giuoc: rebuild the committee from above, with cadres who had not been named in the captured ledgers (C012-1).

<!-- initiative --> The enemy's decision that shapes this phase was taken for his own reasons and against this provision not at all. In September 1964 the Politburo, with Nguyen Chi Thanh at the Central Office, committed the PAVN's regular regiments to the South and ordered the Trail's expansion, on a judgment about the American election and the Republic's coups and on the Chinese and Soviet aid then arriving (EV000-6). Nothing in the record's provisions bore on it, and it set the main-force war that Phase II is about. Thanh's circular of 1967, which is this memorandum's central enemy adaptation, came from the same office three years later and was a tactical rule; this was a strategic commitment, and the asymmetry between the two is the honest shape of what a raid force can and cannot touch.

### Causal chains

**C014-1** [months] (begins in Phase I) The task force's DRY SEASON campaign breaks the Can Giuoc security section from a notebook in December 1964 and the section's papers name a courier (EV012-2) → the courier's family is in the Special Branch census and the land register names the house he sleeps in, so G Squadron can watch the Rach Cat mooring the courier serves and Delta takes six of the Long An province committee with the finance ledgers on 14 March 1965 (EV014-2) → the ledgers name nineteen villages' collectors and the province's collections fall to about a third of the previous year's by December, which costs the Front the rice its piastres bought in a province where the titling had already ended rent (EV001-2) → the committee is rebuilt from the Rung Sat by August 1965 with region cadres, and it never again meets anywhere a boat can reach from a hamlet with a post, which is the first turn of the displacement that ends with the committees of the Delta living in Cambodia (EV014-5).

The terminal link is not predictable from the first: a notebook taken in an ambush in one district ends in a change of habit across a region, and the loss to the Front is not the six men but the mooring.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a unit that has learned the country, a rate that will prove to be about half what the first months implied (F014-13), and a headquarters whose nineteen liaison cells are the reason its raids will not collide with the task force's or SOG's. It also inherits the displacement that NIGHT HERON began.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

This is the provision's phase and it produces three things, in descending order of weight.

The first is the senior cadre taken: 297 at district level or above in thirty months, 61 of them province or region committee members, nine finance sections with their ledgers, concentrated four to one in III and IV Corps because that is where the file is (EV014-3, F014-5, F014-26). Compounded with the task force this accelerates the destruction of the Front's upper administration in the held belt (F012-27), and the end vector carries it as 40–150 a year inside the existing width of FIND.vci rather than as a new band, because that is its true size against a file that was already taking 300–700 infrastructure members a month (M012).

The second is the first measured coordinates under canopy in this war (EV014-4, F014-11). The record's limit on precision has been location and not accuracy since memorandum 005 (POS005-1), and under the trees there was no instrument at all; ninety-four missions into the panhandle produced 134 measured points, a third of them under canopy. Its effect on what arrives in the South is one to four points, inside the width the phase holds, because Group 559's answer to precision was to stop owning anything measurable and that answer was given in 1966 (POS005-4). I judge FIND.main_force in units for the first time on this evidence, at 0–3 points, and the low end is the honest reading.

The third is the ten weeks in Dinh Tuong (EV014-5), which is the event this memorandum narrates at length because it embodies the second-order analysis rather than the provision's primary use. Twenty-three raids, two men, twenty-one slept-in beds, and the reason in a captured courier's statement: the committees had gone to Memot (F014-19, F014-7, F014-21). The command changed its measure in December 1967 from the raid count to the Front's collections and the distance the committees lived from their districts, which is the record's own standing rule about metrics applied in time rather than after the fact (A13, POS002-1).

Under DIRMT01-11 the rice and the piastre: in the three upper districts of Dinh Tuong the Front's collections ran at about a sixth of 1964's through 1967 and its rice procurement fell with them, because a committee that sets a village's assessment in person and a committee that learns of the harvest six weeks late are not the same instrument (EV014-5, EV012-4) [X-014]. Nothing in this provision touches the soldier's pay or the Saigon price index, and the devaluation of June 1966 cut the private's rice by a third regardless (G-17).

### Enemy response and initiative

Detection took about eighteen months and the conclusion was correct. By early 1967 COSVN had the pattern: men taken from houses at night, no cordon, no sweep, nobody seen, helicopters heard afterwards. Pham Hung's circular of April 1967, carrying out the last instruction of Nguyen Chi Thanh, who died in Hanoi in July, contained three sentences and travelled from the Central Office to the district cells in a season (EV014-5, F014-20) — and the enemy got two further things right. He concluded that the night-raiders could not cross the border, because in three years they never did, which made Cambodia the specific answer to this specific threat; and he concluded from the concentration of the raids that the unit was small, which made dispersal rather than fortification the right response.

What he got wrong was its nature, and the error helped him. The Front described Delta as an American assassination unit, as it had described Phoenix historically and the task force in this record (F012-21), and here the description was close enough to true — soldiers entering houses at night without warrants outside the held belt — that it worked in the coastal lowlands where the raids were least accurate (F014-16).

The adaptation's cost is the judgment of the phase. In the base areas the enemy did pay something: standing security on the trail junctions from 1966, listening posts, movement without lights, and the eighteen-month decay of the teams' advantage that follows (F014-8). In the hamlets he paid a courier's walking time (F014-21).

### Causal chains

**C014-2** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase II) Delta's raids take 61 province and region committee members out of the Saigon belt and the upper Delta in 1966–67, on the task force's files (EV014-3) → COSVN issues the three-sentence rule in April 1967 and the committees of the upper Delta move to Memot, inside Cambodia, visiting their districts four or five nights every second month (EV014-5) → the Front's district administration in those provinces is run by courier at two months' remove, its collections fall to about a sixth of 1964's, and its senior cadre are therefore outside the reach of every allied instrument including this one, so that in 1969–71 the national file names men that nobody can touch (EV014-7, P4 end vector) → when Lon Nol's coup and the incursion of May 1970 push the Cambodian sanctuary north to Kratie, the Front's southern administration moves with it and is further from the Delta in 1971 than it has ever been, which is why the countryside of 1969–71 is the best the government has held and why none of it is a measure of the enemy's organisation being beaten.

The terminal link is not predictable from the first: a raid force applied to the senior apparatus ends by making the government's pacification figures better and the enemy's apparatus safer in the same movement.

**C014-3** [months] (begins in Phase II) Delta's teams go into the Laotian panhandle under the ambassador's limits from October 1965 and bring back 134 measured points, a third under canopy (EV014-4) → the coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut can put a GBU-31 on a truck park under trees for the first time, and does, through 1966–67 → Group 559's answer is the answer it gave to HARD CROSSING, which is to disperse what can be measured, so the parks fragment into a dozen dispersal sites each and the measured point loses its value within a season (POS005-4, F014-8) → the lasting product is not tonnage but the ledger: a team's count of what passed a junction on a given night is the only interdiction figure in this war that was never contested in Washington, and it is what makes the Commando Hunt claims of 1969–71 indefensible from the first week (EV000-55, EV000-68).

### Chain review (DIRCP01-1, DIRCP02-2)

**C012-2**, written by memorandum 012 and terminating in an event this memorandum did not write, resolves differently now and in the enemy's favour. Its course was: STILL WATER drives the Hoc Mon committee out of its district → FOLLOW THROUGH reaches Cu Chi and takes the region finance section's ledger, and the committee and the section go to the Ho Bo woods → nobody quarters or guides the assault companies in the Ho Bo woods at Tet. With Delta raiding the base-area fringes from 1966, the Ho Bo woods is no longer far enough: the Hoc Mon committee and the region's finance section leave it for the Cambodian side of the Fishhook during 1967, as the Dinh Tuong committees left for Memot (EV014-5). The terminal link holds — nobody quarters the assault companies in January 1968 — and it holds harder, because the committee is now a border away rather than a day's walk. The cost is that the committee survives Tet intact where in memorandum 012's version it was within reach of a cross-province warrant. The chain's author predicted the outcome; he did not predict that a further provision would buy the same outcome at the price of putting the organisation permanently beyond arrest.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits a unit at its peak of experience and a measure the command has already corrected. Phase IV inherits an enemy apparatus whose senior layer is in Cambodia, which is the single most consequential downstream effect in this memorandum, and the folders the teams will build there from September 1967. The 81st Special Group, agreed in February 1968, is the only thing in the provision that outlives the American withdrawal.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet shows the unit at its best and least relevant at the same time, and both halves belong in the record (EV014-6). It gave no warning of the timing, which is what the record already holds about files on a political apparatus (POSCP03-2) and which DIRCP03-3 requires this memorandum to state in its own terms: an improvement in FIND.vci tells the allies where the enemy's apparatus will act and nothing about when his main forces will. Delta's two couriers taken on 24 and 27 January had the city's sapper organisation in their heads and not the date.

Once the offensive broke, three troops cleared fourteen Front command posts in Districts 5, 6 and 8 and around the Phu Tho racetrack between 1 and 9 February, eight without a shot at the entry, because the Front's urban units had never fought men who could see at night. It cost nine dead and thirty-one wounded, a tenth of the operators in the country, and it is the only week in the war in which the unit's night advantage was decisive in a conventional fight.

The troop flown into Hue on 9 February recovered two of seven district infrastructure files and the province register's index, and lost two men in the Special Branch compound's courtyard. The record's account of what Hue cost the file-building effort stands (EV000-44); the amendment is marginal and it is stated at that weight.

The phase's third event is the one the dossier predicted. At Thanh Phu in Kien Hoa on 18 June 1968 a team raided on a single-source target that had come through a Vietnamese province intelligence section rather than the Special Branch, walked into a reinforced guerrilla platoon with two machine guns on the only dry approach, and lost two dead and three wounded in fifteen seconds (EV014-10). The task force's counter-intelligence staff could not say in August whether the target had been planted or betrayed, which is the condition the record has described since 1964 (POS002-3). From September 1968 the headquarters would not raid on a single-source target outside the Special Branch and the National Fusion Centre, which closed a channel worth perhaps a dozen raids a year — the cost of operating in a language the unit cannot read (F014-22).

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's urban organisation learned in nine days what its rural committees had learned in three years, and it learned it too late to matter in 1968: command posts in a city cannot move every night, because the whole point of them is that the assault companies must be able to find them. Afterwards, the surviving city committees of Saigon and Gia Dinh moved their command element out to the Rung Sat and the Ho Bo woods and ran the city by courier, which is the same displacement as Dinh Tuong's one scale up (F014-7).

The enemy's own decision in this phase is the one the record already holds: the Politburo's commitment to the General Offensive, taken in 1967 for its own reasons about the American election and the Republic's stability, and the three-phase continuation through May and August 1968 that cost the Front its local forces in the Delta. Nothing in this provision bore on it.

### Causal chains

**C014-4** [months] (begins in Phase III) Delta's troops are used as urban assault infantry for nine days in Saigon and take 14 command posts, at a cost of nine dead and thirty-one wounded (EV014-6) → the unit is at about nine-tenths strength in its assault troops through the spring of 1968 and its raid rate in the Delta falls to about 25 a month, at the moment the Front's infrastructure in the Delta is most exposed by its own Tet losses → the senior cadre who would have been taken in that window are not taken, and the Front's provincial committees reconstitute from the base areas through the summer with cadres sent down from the region → by 1969 the file names a senior layer that is both intact and in Cambodia, and the best opportunity the provision ever had was spent clearing streets.

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits a unit that has been spent as infantry once and will be again if the command lets it, a Vietnamese counterpart unit agreed in February 1968, and the displacement complete: by 1969 there is no province in III or IV Corps whose committee sleeps where Delta can reach it.

### Clock

The provision's effect on American tolerance in this phase is small, adverse and worth naming. Delta's own dead in 1968 — nine in Saigon, two in Hue, and the men lost in the panhandle — are between a dozen and twenty names, against 14,592 historically and 14,450–14,560 in this war (P3 end vector): inside the width and not a change. What is not inside the width is the character of some of those deaths. A man lost in Laos in 1966 or 1967 is a death this command cannot explain, and the record's standing position is that the American clock is spent fastest by the discovery that reported progress was false (POSTNA-5, F014-22). Historically SOG's dead carried cover stories for thirty years; a larger unit doing the same work raises the chance of the story breaking during the war. In this war it has not broken by the end of 1968. The halt Washington put on all northern operations in 1968 is the precedent for what one exposure would cost (M-S3).

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

Three things happen and the second is the one worth the reader's time.

The folders (EV014-7). Sixty-one missions into the Cambodian base areas in 1969, on foot, without air support or a controller overhead and with no force behind them, counting what the radar could not see under rubber: 94 cache sites, 11 field hospitals, 4 vehicle parks, the Memot complex's three bunker belts. They are part of why the incursion of 29 April 1970 found the caches in days rather than weeks (EV000-66). COSVN was not in them and went to Kratie on 19 March, six weeks early, warned by nothing more than Lon Nol's coup and its own standing practice (F014-20). The provision names COSVN as a mission and never once got near it.

Son Tay (EV000-69, now CANCELLED). Two men of G Squadron on a slope 1,400 metres from the camp for three nights in October 1970 reported empty cells, no cooking for prisoners and dry wells; the Joint Chiefs cancelled on 2 November. The raid's historical military result was nil and its historical second-order result was not: Hanoi consolidated its prisoners into the central prisons in Hanoi that winter and the prisoners' treatment and morale improved [H59]. In this war neither happens, the camps stay dispersed through 1971 and 1972, and no prisoner learns that anyone came (F014-15). This is the clearest case in the record of better intelligence cancelling an operation and cancelling its unintended good with it, and I record it as a loss as well as a saving.

The 81st Special Group (EV014-8). Selection from March 1968, 23 of 214 passing, 14 finishing the operator course; the first squadron operational in November 1969 with starlight scopes and PRC-25s, the second in May 1971. Under DIRMT01-9 the families: housed inside the Long Thanh compound on rations drawn against the garrison's, which is why the unit's desertion ran at about a fifth of the ARVN's rate in 1971, the year a soldier's pay bought a third less rice than in 1966 (G-17).

I judge FIND.vci in units for this phase on this evidence and the task force's: 1,000–2,500 a year, below Phase II's, not because the file decayed but because the men it names are in Cambodia and at Kratie and nobody can reach them. That is the record's own pattern — an instrument improving while its object moves out of range — and it is stated here rather than discovered at a checkpoint.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's conduct in this phase is governed by his own rebuilding and by Cambodia, not by this provision. <!-- initiative --> The decision that shapes it is the PAVN's turn on Cambodia itself in March and April 1970 after Sihanouk's fall: the Central Military Commission in Hanoi, not COSVN, chose to fight for the Cambodian countryside and to build the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia and southern Laos, a commitment of engineer and transport regiments that had nothing to do with American special operations and everything to do with the loss of Sihanoukville (EV000-64, EV000-68). COSVN's own move to Kratie on 19 March was taken in the same week and for the same reason.

Against Delta specifically the enemy does one new thing: from 1969 the security battalions in the base areas hunt the watchers as a standing task rather than a reaction, and the teams' compromise rate in Cambodia runs about twice the panhandle's of 1966 (F014-8).

### Causal chains

**C014-5** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase IV) Two men of G Squadron lie on a slope 1,400 metres from the camp at Son Tay for three nights in October 1970 and report empty cells, no cooking for prisoners and dry wells (EV000-69) → the Joint Chiefs cancel the raid on 2 November 1970, saving 116 aircraft, 56 raiders, an F-105G and about 42 PAVN guards → Hanoi never learns that its camps in the countryside can be reached, so the consolidation of the prisoners into the central prisons that followed the historical raid does not happen, and the men in the camps outside Hanoi stay dispersed through 1971 and 1972 under the regime they already had [H59] → the one American instrument that ever improved the prisoners' treatment was an operation that failed, and better intelligence removed it, so the prisoner issue reaches Paris in 1972–73 exactly as it did historically and the Republic's negotiating position gains nothing from the best reconnaissance capability in the war (F014-15, F014-23).

The terminal link is not predictable from the first: a successful reconnaissance makes American prisoners worse off, through a mechanism nobody weighed at the time.

### Downstream

Phase V inherits a provision whose cross-border half has been taken away by Cooper–Church (EV014-11): the unit's raids in the South are at about 30 a month and take district cadre, its reconnaissance is in Cambodia and Laos, and the conventional offensive of 1972 is fought by divisions. Phase VI inherits the 81st Special Group, two squadrons strong and equipped to the standard of 1969, and the American unit's departure in 1973 with everything that made it what it was.

### Clock

Adverse and small, and smaller than it looks. The unacknowledgeable deaths continue at a handful a year. The Cambodian reconnaissance from September 1967 is a programme that Congress did not know about, in a country the United States was not at war with, through the years in which the Cooper–Church amendment was written: an exposure in 1970 or 1971 would have landed in the middle of the Moratorium and Kent State, and would have cost more politically than everything the folders bought militarily (F014-22). It did not happen in this war. I record it as the live risk it was rather than as a hazard avoided.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, across all phases, this provision changes the following and nothing else.

There is a joint special operations headquarters in Vietnam from August 1964, sixteen years before JSOC existed [S8], and a unit of Delta's standard in theater from September 1964, thirteen years before Delta was raised [S1]. Historically the nearest thing was MACV-SOG, formed in January 1964, whose reconnaissance teams were two or three Americans with three to twelve indigenous soldiers, moving by day, relaying through a FAC [S3]; and Project Delta of the 5th Special Forces Group, which shared the name and nothing else.

Against SOG's own record: 94 panhandle missions in 1966–67 completed without compromise at about 71 in 94, against SOG's rate nearer one in three [X-014] [S3]; the first 134 coordinates measured under canopy in this war, where no instrument existed historically or in this record before 1964 (F014-11).

Against the historical attack on the infrastructure: 297 cadre at district level or above taken by raid in 1966–67, 61 of them province or region committee members, where historically about 87% of those killed in the Phoenix programme's count died as a by-product of conventional operations [M006]; and a senior apparatus driven across an international border by 1967 rather than, as historically, surviving in the base areas within the South until the incursion.

Against the historical prisoner effort: the recovery operations of the South run at a better rate — I estimate 250–500 Vietnamese prisoners a year at maturity against history's 155 in 1968 and 117 in 1969 [S6] — and not one American prisoner is recovered, as not one was historically, because they are held in the North. And the Son Tay raid is not flown: 116 aircraft, 56 raiders, one F-105G and about 42 PAVN guards are not spent, and the consolidation of the prisoners into Hanoi that followed the historical raid does not occur [S5] [H59].

Under DIRMT01-2 the running sums. American dead: the record's provisions have saved perhaps 2,500–5,000 American lives across the war, below any band (G-5); this provision adds between about 60 and 180 American dead across eleven years and subtracts none, and the sum is unchanged in the part of its range it supports. ARVN battalions not broken: unchanged, because this provision touches no ARVN battalion. Senior infrastructure taken: this is the first provision to add to a count of its own, 297 in 1966–67 and perhaps 700–1,400 across the war, of which the part that would not have been taken anyway is perhaps a half to two-thirds [E: the overlap with the task force's own reach, M012].

Under DIRMT01-1 the date of the Republic's fall: unchanged, a window of February to May 1976 as memorandum 011 and the checkpoints have it. The mechanisms in this provision that could pull it earlier (American deaths that cannot be explained, a Cambodian exposure before Cooper–Church) are political and did not fire; the mechanisms that could pull it later (a senior apparatus beyond the border, a Vietnamese special unit) operate below the level at which 1975 was decided. Neither dominates and the date does not move.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state does not change and the rung does not change. Rung (b), dependent survival: the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast south of Nha Trang on 31 December 1975 under Thieu, with no American air power, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976.

In its own terms, this provision bought the attack on the Front's senior apparatus a finishing arm it had not had, used it where the file was thick, and in doing so drove that apparatus out of the provinces it administered and across a border that the same constraint set had made a sanctuary. Both halves are true and they are of comparable size. The provision's best single effect — 40–150 senior listed cadre a year, taken alive with their papers, the upper administration of twelve to eighteen provinces broken three to six months sooner than memorandum 012 alone would have broken it — is real, modest, and bought at the cost of pushing the survivors permanently beyond arrest (F014-26, C014-2).

Why the next rung is not reachable: rung (c) requires that the insurgency be contained in every corps region and that Saigon stand with American air power available. Neither condition is touched here. Delta raided four to one in III and IV Corps and had no file to raid on in the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps, so the record's long asymmetry is sharpened rather than corrected (F014-5, POSCP03-3); and nothing in this provision bears on Case–Church, on the appropriation of 1974 or on the refusal of January 1975. Under DIRMT01-4, what absorbs the difference between a real gain in 1964–68 and an unchanged 1975 is the same thing that has absorbed it in eleven memoranda: the gain is American-owned and goes home in 1973, and the decisions of 1972 and 1975 were taken above the level this provision reached (POSMT01-1, POSCP01-2).

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The Front's senior apparatus in the held belt lost the night from the autumn of 1964.** Two hundred and ninety-seven cadre at district level or above taken by raid in 1966–67, 61 of them province or region committee members, nine finance sections with their ledgers, in operations that took hours rather than months and brought the men back alive (EV014-2, EV014-3). - **Son Tay was not flown.** A three-night reconnaissance in October 1970 found the camp empty and cancelled a 116-aircraft operation whose military result would have been nil (EV000-69).
- **The Republic has a special unit of its own from November 1969,** two squadrons by 1971, trained to Delta's standard on 1969's equipment, with its families inside the compound and its desertion at about a fifth of the ARVN's rate (EV014-8).

### What has not changed

- **The main force.** The enemy is exactly as hard to find under canopy as he was, he still chooses every fight except the siege, and a reconnaissance troop cannot fix a battalion (F014-2, F014-3, G-7, POSTNA-3).
- **The sanctuaries, and the reason for them.** Cambodia and Laos kept their ground bar, and this provision gave the enemy a new and specific reason to value them: the raiders could not follow (F014-20, G-8).
- **What arrives in the South.** One to four points at most, inside a width the record already holds, against a Trail that is larger at the end of 1971 than ever (POSTNA-4, POS005-4).
- **The exchange rate.** The enemy answered 1,310 Americans and the best 300 operators in the army with three sentences in a circular and a courier's walking time, which is the cheapest adaptation any provision in this record has bought (F014-21).
- **American prisoners.** None was recovered, as none was historically, because they were held in the North; and the dispersed camps of 1971–72 were never consolidated, so the men in them never learned that anyone had come (F014-15, F014-23).
- **The Republic's ownership of its own war.** The unit was American, its equipment could not be repaired in Asia, its targets were kept out of Vietnamese hands by design, and in March 1973 all of it went home (POSMT01-1, POSCP01-2).
