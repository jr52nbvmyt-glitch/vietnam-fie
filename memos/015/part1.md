# Memorandum 015 — Part 1 of 3 · §1 Provision dossier
<!-- audit -->
*Audit — Memo 015, Part 1/3 · v1.0.8 · provisions: 2 (P015a, P015b) · dossier fields: 16/16, 16/16 (floor 16) · tagged figures: 230 · untagged warnings: 19 · sources: 9 · designations: ~63 (floor 20) · variables pushed: 11 · structural deviation: none*

The provision takes something out before it puts anything in. Every fixed-wing gunship the United States and the Republic flew in this war is gone, and with it the programmes that made them: no AC-47 Spooky of the 4th Air Commando Squadron over the Delta in December 1964, no AC-119G Shadow or AC-119K Stinger, no AC-130A or AC-130E Spectre at Ubon, no Gunship II test aircraft at Wright-Patterson, and no VNAF 817th, 819th or 821st Squadron flying them after the Americans left. In history that force numbered 135 converted aircraft over the war and 53 deployed at its peak [S6]. In its place the Air Force has 64 AC-130J Ghostriders from the war's first day, and the Vietnamese Air Force 48 AC-130U Spooky IIs from the first day of 1967 [U].

I have read the provision with three assumptions, which I state here so that nothing later rests on them silently. First, the Ghostriders arrive as the 2026 aircraft arrive in every memorandum of this series: flown and maintained by crews qualified on them, with modern maintenance and munitions at their bases limited by realistic production (A2). Second, the 48 Spooky IIs are aircraft, not crews: the provision names the Army of the Republic, and in this force a gunship belongs to the air arm, so they go to the VNAF, and the Vietnamese who fly and maintain them have to be found and trained, with American help, from January 1967. Third, helicopter gunships are untouched. The UH-1B and UH-1C gunships of the 68th and 197th Aviation Companies and their successors are helicopters, not the fixed-wing gunships the provision removes, and they remain as history had them.

## §1.1 Lockheed Martin AC-130J Ghostrider, Block 30 — P015a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The aircraft is the AC-130J Ghostrider, Lockheed Martin's MC-130J Commando II airframe converted by the Air Force Special Operations Command's Precision Strike Package into a side-firing gunship, in its Block 30 configuration of 2026 [S1] [S4]. It reached initial operational capability in September 2017, and the first Block 30 aircraft flew its first combat mission over Afghanistan in June 2019 [S2]. The real force was 37 aircraft by fiscal 2024 at $165 million each [S1]; this provision gives the Air Force 64 [U], about 1.7 times the whole of today's fleet [D], and removes every fixed-wing gunship it would otherwise have flown.

Sixty-four aircraft is more gunships than the Air Force ever had in Southeast Asia at one time: history's peak was 53, of every type [S6]. I organise them as the 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship), activated at Nha Trang on 1 January 1964, with four squadrons of sixteen aircraft each [E: the historical AC-130 squadron of 12–18 aircraft, the 16th SOS]. The 4th Air Commando Squadron goes to Bien Hoa for III and IV Corps, the 14th to Nha Trang for II Corps and the coast, the 16th to Da Nang for I Corps, and the 17th is held at Nha Trang as the wing's reserve and training squadron until Thai basing is agreed, then moves to Ubon for the Trail [E: the historical gunship bases, Bien Hoa, Nha Trang, Da Nang, Phan Rang and Ubon, per S6]. Squadron numbers are the historical gunship squadrons' own, which this provision leaves vacant; the units are new.

In 1964 that is a large force for an advisory war, and I do not pretend otherwise. Under the Farm Gate rules of 1964 an American combat aircraft over the South flew with a Vietnamese aboard and in a training-and-support role [H], and the wing's sixty-four aircraft are not going to be hidden under that formula. I will ask for them to fly under Farm Gate's rules with a VNAF observer aboard each until the rules change in early 1965, and I expect a third to half of them to sit at Nha Trang and in the Philippines, flying training sorties, for much of 1964 [E: the political posture of the advisory war before the Gulf of Tonkin; see D10].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The Block 30 aircraft flies with nine: two pilots, a combat systems officer, a weapons systems officer and five special mission aviators, of whom one is the sensor operator, one the loadmaster and three the gunners [S4]. The Air Force Special Operations Command's own fact sheet gives eight, two pilots, two combat systems officers and four special mission aviators [S1]; the difference is a gunner, and I plan on nine.

At 1.5 crews per aircraft the wing needs about 96 crews and about 860 aircrew [E: the AFSOC crew ratio for sustained combat operations, 1.25–1.5; 64 × 1.5 × 9]. Maintenance for an aircraft of this complexity runs at about 15–20 people per aircraft, about 960–1,280 for the wing [E: C-130J-family maintenance manning plus the gun, sensor and weapons specialists of the Precision Strike Package]. Armament, intelligence, operations, life support and a contractor field team for the sensors and mission processors add about 100–250 [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. The wing is about 1,900–2,400 people [D].

CONFIG sets no troop ceiling. About 1,100–1,500 of those spaces are in the Republic, the rest at Ubon once the 17th Squadron moves [E: three squadrons and the wing headquarters in-country]. Against history the net cost is smaller than the gross, because the historical gunship force had its own people — the AC-47 squadrons, the 71st, 17th and 18th Special Operations Squadrons and the 16th at Ubon — and they are removed with their aircraft [S6]. I count the net at about 600–1,000 spaces more than history at the 1968–69 peak and about 1,100–1,500 more in 1964–65, when history had almost no gunship people at all [E: historical gunship units of about 400–800 men each at their peaks].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The Ghostrider carries two guns and two families of guided weapons [S1] [S4].

**GAU-23/A 30mm chain gun.** An ATK (now Northrop Grumman) Mk44 Bushmaster II, trainable, on the left side, firing 30×173mm ammunition [S2]. Its rate is about 200 rounds a minute, slow by the standard of a Gatling but fast enough for a gunship that fires in bursts from orbit [E: the Mk44's published rate]. A load of about 500–1,000 rounds is typical [E: open reporting of AC-130J loads; not in the sources consulted]. The high-explosive round is effective against men in the open, light vehicles and light cover; it will not kill a dug-in bunker with overhead cover.

**105mm cannon.** A modified M102 howitzer, trainable, firing the standard 105mm howitzer families [S1]. It is the gunship's heavy hand: a 33-pound high-explosive shell [E: M1 HE weight] that kills trucks, light armour and field fortifications, delivered from orbit to within a few metres of a laser or sensor point. A load of about 80–100 rounds [E: the historical AC-130E and AC-130U loads; not in the sources consulted].

**Wing-mounted guided weapons.** Up to eight on wing racks: GBU-39/B Small Diameter Bombs, the laser-guided GBU-39B/B, and AGM-114 Hellfire missiles [S4]. The GBU-39 is a 250-pound-class GPS-guided glide bomb with a range of tens of miles from altitude [E: published glide range of 60 nm or more from medium altitude]; the Hellfire is a laser-guided missile of about 8 km range [E: published figures for the AGM-114].

**Common launch tube weapons.** Ten tubes in the cargo door for the AGM-176 Griffin and the GBU-69/B Small Glide Munition [S4]. The Griffin is a small laser-guided missile with a warhead of about 13 pounds, built for a target the size of a truck or a squad position, launched from several miles out [E: published figures for the AGM-176].

The real figures matter more here than in any memorandum since the B-52J. A gunship's damage is in the guns, which fire for hours; the missiles and glide bombs are a dozen or so shots a sortie. On the Trail the guided weapons do the long-range work against a truck the crew can see but will not approach; over an outpost the guns do almost all of it.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The Block 30 aircraft carries two electro-optical and infrared turrets, the MX-20 and the MX-25, each with a laser designator and rangefinder, plus video, data and communications links [S4]. One trade source credits it with an all-weather synthetic aperture radar pod [S2]; the Director of Operational Test and Evaluation's report on Block 30 lists no radar [S4]. I do not count on one. The gunship that matters in this memorandum sees with light and heat.

**At night**, these turrets are the best in the theatre by a generation. A truck with a warm engine shows at 10–20 km slant range on the infrared channel; a man in the open at 5–8 km; a mortar's flash or a recoilless rifle's backblast at any range the crew can see the ground [E: performance of third-generation cooled mid-wave infrared turrets of the MX-20 and MX-25 class]. The crew can read the figure of a man and tell whether he carries a rifle. Nothing in the 1964 force sees half as well; the AC-130A's night observation devices of 1967 saw a truck at perhaps 2–4 km [E: starlight scope and early infrared on Gunship II].

**Under triple canopy** the turrets see the canopy. They pick up heat and light where it leaks through: a cooking fire, a vehicle on a road the trees do not close over, a column crossing a clearing, a muzzle flash. A PAVN battalion bivouacked under the forest of the Chu Pong massif or the Iron Triangle is invisible to them, as it is to every optical sensor. The gunship does not find the main force under the trees (A6).

**In monsoon** the turrets see nothing through cloud. The north-east monsoon over I Corps and the coast from October to March and the south-west monsoon over the Highlands and the Delta from May to October bring cloud bases of 1,000–3,000 feet for days at a time [E: the climatology of the theatre]. A Ghostrider without radar can fire only from below the cloud, inside the reach of every machine gun on the ground, or by GPS coordinates passed to it — the SDB can be dropped on coordinates through any weather, the guns cannot be aimed through cloud without a sensor that sees the ground. This is the Ghostrider's largest limitation in this theatre, and it is the reason the older Spooky II of §1.2, which carries a strike radar, outperforms it on a monsoon night.

The Ghostrider addresses the main-force half of find-and-fix only at the moment of contact, when the enemy reveals himself by attacking. It does not touch the infrastructure half at all (A6).

### D5 · Performance

The airframe is the MC-130J, with four Rolls-Royce AE 2100D3 turboprops of 4,700 shaft horsepower each and six-bladed Dowty propellers [S1]. Maximum speed is about 362 knots at 22,000 feet; the ceiling is 28,000 feet with a 42,000-pound payload [S2]. Maximum take-off weight is 164,000 pounds [S1]. Wingspan is 132 feet 7 inches, length 97 feet 9 inches [S1].

Range is 3,000 miles, limited by the crew's duty day and extendable by air refuelling [S1]. In practice a sortie of 6–8 hours gives 4–6 hours on station at 150–250 nautical miles from base [E: C-130J fuel consumption at loiter speeds, the crew duty day]. From Bien Hoa that covers the whole of III and IV Corps; from Nha Trang the Highlands to the Cambodian border; from Da Nang the whole of I Corps and the near Laotian panhandle; from Ubon the Trail from Mu Gia to the tri-border.

The attack orbit is a left-hand pylon turn around the target. The Ghostrider fires its guns from about 7,000–12,000 feet above ground and its 105mm from as high as 15,000 feet in clear air [E: modern gunship employment, higher than the AC-130A's 3,500–6,000 feet of 1967–69]. Its guided weapons go out from 5–15 miles. It carries composite armour over the crew and fuel-tank ullage protection against fire [S2].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The aircraft carries the Air Force's full set of 2026 radios: UHF and VHF voice, VHF-FM on the Army's band, satellite voice and data, and the video and data links of the Precision Strike Package [S4]. Under the standing condition of E01, the satellite constellations exist and serve equipment built for them, so its satellite links and GPS work from 1964 [CONFIG space_constellations].

It talks to the ground forces of 1964 on the one channel they have, VHF-FM. The AN/PRC-10 and, from 1965, the AN/PRC-25 sets of the US and ARVN infantry, and the domestic PRC-25 and PRC-77 sets of P008b, all speak to it in the 30–76 MHz band [M008]. What the ground forces lack is everything else the aircraft expects: no ground party in 1964 has a video downlink receiver to see what the sensor sees, a laser marker to show the crew a target, or a GPS receiver to give a coordinate a gunship can fire on [E: none of these exist in the historical force; A3a]. They mark with flares, tracers, a strobe in the bottom of a helmet and a description over the radio, as history's outposts marked for the AC-47.

To the ARVN and the territorial forces it speaks through a VNAF observer or interpreter on the aircraft, or through the American adviser on the ground [E: the historical arrangement for AC-47 support to RF/PF outposts]. The Ghostrider cannot hear a Popular Forces platoon leader directly unless he has a radio on its net and someone aboard speaks Vietnamese. In 1964 that limits it, in the hamlets, to outposts with an American adviser or a radio relay through the district.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Basing.** The C-130J flies from a 3,000–5,000-foot runway at combat weight, paved or hard-packed [E: C-130J short-field performance]. Bien Hoa, Tan Son Nhut, Nha Trang, Da Nang and Binh Thuy all serve; Phan Rang from 1966; Ubon in Thailand once the Thai government agrees, as it did for the gunships in history from 1966–67 [S6]. The aircraft need revetments against mortar and rocket attack. History's gunships were destroyed on the ground at Binh Thuy and Phan Rang in 1968 by sapper and mortar attack [S7]; a $165 million aircraft parked in the open at Bien Hoa in November 1964, when the VC mortared the field and destroyed or damaged about 27 aircraft [H], is a target worth a regiment's mortar ammunition.

**Over the South, against small arms and 12.7mm.** From 7,000–12,000 feet above ground the Ghostrider is above the reach of the rifle and the 7.62mm machine gun and at the edge of the 12.7mm DShK's effective slant range of about 1,500–2,000 metres [E: DShK effective anti-aircraft range]. In clear air over the South it is safe from almost everything the VC and PAVN carry before 1972. In monsoon, when it must come below a cloud base of 1,000–3,000 feet to see, it is inside the reach of every weapon on the ground and slow, and a 12.7mm can hit it [E: D4].

**Over the Trail, against 37mm and 57mm.** The Soviet M1939 37mm gun reaches about 3,000 metres effective, the 57mm S-60 about 6,000 metres with radar direction [E: published figures]. The Ghostrider's guns, fired from 3,000–4,000 metres above ground, are at the edge of the 37mm and inside the 57mm [D]. Its Griffins and SDBs, launched from 5–15 miles out, let it strike a truck park without entering the guns' reach at all. That is the decisive difference from history's AC-130A, which had to orbit within the 37mm's reach to see and shoot [S6].

**Against the SA-7 and the SA-2.** The 9K32 Strela-2 (SA-7), first fired in the South in the spring of 1972 [H], reaches about 2,300 metres [E: published ceiling of the Strela-2]; the Ghostrider orbits above it in clear air, and carries the AN/AAR-47 missile warning receiver and AN/ALE-47 flares against it, with provision for the LAIRCM laser jammer [S2]. A first-generation uncooled seeker of 1972 is the easiest infrared missile a modern countermeasure suite will ever face [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. The SA-2, which historically moved into the panhandle and destroyed an AC-130A over Laos in March 1972 [S7] [H], is a different matter: an AN/ALR-56M warning receiver tells the crew they are tracked [S2], and the real Block 30 aircraft's active radio-frequency jammer was still a future upgrade in 2020 [S4]. A gunship in orbit is a slow, predictable target for a radar missile. Over an SA-2 belt the Ghostrider does not go.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Fuel.** The AE 2100D3 burns JP-8 by preference and JP-4, the theatre's fuel of the 1960s, as an alternative [E: turbine fuel tolerance]. A sortie of 6–8 hours burns about 25,000–30,000 pounds [E: C-130J consumption]. At about 60–70 sorties a night across the wing at the peak, that is about 1.5–2 million pounds of fuel a day, about 750–1,000 tons [D], a small fraction of the Air Force's in-theatre fuel throughput but a real load on the pipelines and tank farms of Bien Hoa and Nha Trang.

**Ammunition.** The 105mm fires standard howitzer families already in theatre, including the shell bodies of P008a's arsenal [M008]. The 30×173mm Bushmaster round, the GBU-39, the Hellfire and the Griffin do not exist in 1964 and come from 2026 production under A2. Production is the limit on the guided weapons. A realistic allotment is about 300–600 Griffins and GBU-69s, about 1,500–3,000 GBU-39s and about 1,000–2,000 Hellfires a year for this wing [E: a share of the 2020s production lines for each weapon]. At ten tubes and eight wing stations a sortie, the wing could fire its year's Griffin allotment in a few weeks on the Trail; the guided weapons are rationed and the guns do the steady work.

**Spares and specialists.** The aircraft's mission processors, turrets and Bushmaster guns are 2026 equipment. A contractor field team of about 60–100 technicians in theatre [E: AFSOC's contractor logistics support], 2026 spares through the Air Force supply system, and engine changes for the AE 2100 at a depot in the Philippines or Japan [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. None of this exists in the RVNAF, or in the Air Force of 1964 outside the provision.

**What it needs that the RVNAF cannot supply.** Everything except the 105mm shells and the fuel.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The Ghostrider can be handed to the VNAF in principle and should not be in practice. The airframe is a C-130, which the VNAF historically flew from late 1972 [H]; the gunship is a C-130 with a 2026 sensor, mission-processor and weapons system written in English, sustained by contractors, and fed with munitions that only American production makes. A VNAF crew could be trained to fly it in two to three years and to fight it in three to four [E: AFSOC training timelines from pilot wings to qualified gunship aircraft commander, for an air force without a gunship tradition]; a VNAF maintenance organisation could not sustain its sensors and processors without American contractors in the 1970s at all.

The provision has already answered the question in its own way: it gives the VNAF a different gunship, the Spooky II of §1.2, from 1967. The sensible course under Vietnamization is to keep the Ghostriders American and to make the VNAF's own gunship force work. If any Ghostrider were left in 1973, it would be grounded within a year of the last contractor's departure.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The Ghostrider performs to specification at night, in clear or broken cloud, over a target that reveals itself — an attack on an outpost, a convoy on a road, a unit firing on a landing zone — within about 250 nautical miles of base, under a gunship net that can put an aircraft over the call within 15–30 minutes [E: three in-country squadrons holding standing orbits over each corps]. In those conditions it is the best night fire support anyone has ever had, and it is available from the first night of 1964.

Its performance collapses under three conditions. First, cloud: under a monsoon overcast below 3,000 feet [E: D4] it cannot see, and it must either drop GPS weapons on coordinates nobody on the ground can give it, or come down under the cloud into the guns. Second, canopy: under the forest it sees nothing that does not show itself. Third, the political envelope. In 1964 Farm Gate's rules apply, every attack on the South needs the province chief's clearance under the standing rules of engagement [CONFIG roe = MODELLED], and the fire of a 105mm and a 30mm into a hamlet is the most visible thing the United States can do in the countryside. Clearance took 20–60 minutes historically [E: the AC-47 experience of 1965–67], and the Ghostrider's speed of arrival is wasted if the clearance is slow.

**Tempo over weeks.** At a sortie rate of about one a day per mission-capable aircraft, with about 70–80% of aircraft mission-capable [E: AFSOC gunship rates with contractor support], the wing flies about 45–50 sorties a night — enough to keep two to three aircraft over each corps all night and a squadron on the Trail [D]. That is a sustained rate, not a demonstration.

**The Trail.** From 1965, when the ambassador in Vientiane clears armed reconnaissance areas in the panhandle (CONFIG laos_ground = MODELLED, but the air war in Laos ran under the ambassador's control from Barrel Roll in December 1964 and Steel Tiger in April 1965 [H]).

### D11 · Failure modes

**Weather is the first failure, and the commonest.** A monsoon night over the Highlands with a 1,500-foot ceiling grounds the gunship's eyes. The outpost that calls at 0200 gets an aircraft that can see the glow of fire through the cloud and nothing else. A partial failure is the commoner case: one turret sees through a gap, the other does not, and the crew fires slowly and from lower than it wants.

**Sensors and guns break.** The Director of Operational Test and Evaluation recorded gun and sensor malfunctions throughout the Block 30 evaluation that degraded precision strike, and usability of the crew stations still "marginal" [S4]. A Ghostrider with one turret down still fights; with the 30mm jammed it still has the 105mm and its missiles; with both turrets down it is a transport aircraft. I expect about one sortie in eight to ten to lose a major system during the sortie [E: S4's qualitative finding applied to sustained combat].

**Losses come in nine.** A gunship lost is a crew of nine. History lost 30 gunships and about 160 men in them [S7]. A Ghostrider lost on the Trail to a 57mm battery it flew too close to, or on the ground at Bien Hoa to a mortar, costs more men than a fighter-bomber and a larger share of the force.

**When the gunship is not there.** The outposts of the South go back to what history gave them: artillery if they are within range of a district gun, a flare ship, a helicopter gunship if one is close, and the fighter-bombers in the morning. The provision has removed the AC-47 that was history's answer; when the Ghostrider fails, nothing of history's gunship force is left under it.

### D12 · Degradation curve

Month one is the best month. The crews are 2026 crews, the aircraft new, the spares and contractors in place. By month twelve the rate of decline is set by three things. First, wear on the guns and turrets: the Bushmaster's barrels and feed system, and the turrets' gimbals, under nightly firing and the dust and humidity of the coast; mission-capable rate falls from about 80% to about 70% unless the contractor team grows with the tempo [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. Second, guided weapon stocks: if the Trail squadron fires its Griffins at the rate the trucks invite, the year's allotment is gone in the first dry season, and the Trail work falls back to the guns, closer in. Third, crew rotation. The wing's first crews rotate home after a year, and the replacements are trained by the Air Force's training base of 1965, which has to be built for this aircraft; crew proficiency dips for two to four months each spring as the first-tour crews arrive [E: the 12-month tour of the period].

What declines first is the Trail squadron's standoff. The in-country squadrons over the outposts degrade slowly, because their work is with the guns and the targets come to them.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against history's AC-47 Spooky, the gunship it displaces over the outposts.** The AC-47 carried three 7.62mm miniguns, attacked from about 1,500 feet at a top speed of 200 knots [S6], saw with the naked eye and flares, and flew from December 1964 [S6]. The Ghostrider carries a 30mm and a 105mm, fires from four to eight times as high [D], sees with infrared turrets at five to ten times the range [E: D4], and flies from January 1964. On an outpost under attack, the Ghostrider's 105mm breaks an assault forming in the treeline, which the AC-47's rifle-calibre fire could only suppress. It is a different class of weapon, not a better one of the same class.

**Against history's AC-130A and AC-130E Spectre on the Trail.** In Commando Hunt VII the AC-130s averaged 5.37 trucks a sortie and accounted for almost 70% of the trucks hit by gunships, against 0.29 a sortie for the F-4 [S6]. That is claims, and the CIA and DIA discounted about 75% of the claims [S6]. The Ghostrider's margin on the Trail is chiefly in survival and reach, not in kills a sortie: it shoots from farther out, it sees farther, and it arrives in 1965 rather than in late 1967 [S6]. I judge its truck kills a sortie, counted honestly, at about one and a half to two times the AC-130E's honestly counted figure [E: better sensors and standoff weapons; the same trucks hidden in the same parks].

**Against the whole of history's gunship force.** History's peak was 53 gunships of all types [S6]; the Ghostrider force is 64 aircraft of one type, each with roughly the firepower of the AC-130E and a better pair of eyes. Over the war, history converted 135 aircraft [S6]. In aircraft-nights of gunship fire, this wing gives the South about two to three times history's gunship availability in 1964–68 and about one to one and a half times in 1969–72 [E: history's gunship force grew from a few AC-47s in 1965 to 53 aircraft at the peak].

### D14 · Negative specification

The Ghostrider cannot see through cloud. The Block 30 aircraft's operational test reports list no radar [S4], and without one it cannot aim its guns through a monsoon overcast; under a 1,000–3,000-foot ceiling it either comes down into the guns or fires GPS weapons on coordinates the ground forces of this war cannot give it.

It cannot find a unit under triple canopy. It sees heat and light that leak through; a battalion in bivouac under the forest is invisible to it (A6).

It does nothing about the infrastructure. A gunship over a hamlet kills men who attack the hamlet; it cannot tell the tax collector from the farmer, and the VCI inside the hamlet are untouched by it (A6).

It does not stop the Trail. It kills trucks at night where it is allowed to fly and the guns are not; it does not reduce the tonnage arriving by more than the Trail's slack and repair allow, which is a few points (A7, POS005-4).

It is not survivable over SA-2 belts, and it is vulnerable on the ground. The wing's 64 aircraft are as exposed to a mortar at Bien Hoa as to a gun on the Trail.

It is not Vietnamese, and it cannot be made Vietnamese. It goes home with the Americans.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy

It denies the enemy the night over a defended position. A VC or PAVN attack on an outpost, a district town or a fire base at night has been, in history, a contest the attacker could win before help arrived; with a Ghostrider over the post within 15–30 minutes, the attacker has the first hour, and after that the assault forming in the treeline is under 105mm fire it cannot hide from. It also denies him the open road at night in the panhandle, at a price in trucks, and the clear night in general. It does not deny him the cloudy night, the forest or the hamlet.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card:verdict P015a -->
### D16 · Honest verdict

The Ghostrider is the best night close-support aircraft in the world, and it closes the gap the record has named for air power in this war: posts under attack at night (G-12). From the first night of 1964 the Republic's outposts, district towns and Special Forces camps can call down a gunship that arrives in minutes, sees the attackers in the dark at kilometres and kills them with a 105mm. That is a genuine transformation of one part of the war, and it comes three to four years earlier than history's gunships had any real numbers. It is not a transformation of the war. It does not see through monsoon cloud, it does not find the main force under the trees, it does not touch the infrastructure, and on the Trail it kills trucks the Trail can spare. Its value is that the enemy's best tactic of the village war, the night attack on an isolated post, becomes costly and often fails. Its limit is that the enemy chose that tactic because it was his best, and he has others.

## §1.2 Lockheed/Boeing AC-130U Spooky II — P015b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The AC-130U Spooky II is a Lockheed C-130H airframe converted by Boeing (originally Rockwell) into the Air Force Special Operations Command's gunship of the 1990s and 2000s; it was deployed in 1995 and the last aircraft retired to Davis-Monthan on 26 June 2020 [S3]. Seventeen were built [S3] [S5]; this provision gives the Vietnamese Air Force 48 [U], arriving on 1 January 1967 [U], and removes the VNAF's historical AC-47 and AC-119 gunships with their squadrons. I take its final service configuration: the GAU-12/U 25mm Gatling gun, the 40mm Bofors L/60 and the 105mm M102, the AN/APQ-180 strike radar, its television and infrared sensors, and its dual-target fire control [S5].

Forty-eight is three squadrons of sixteen. I organise them as the VNAF's 81st Gunship Wing at Nha Trang, with the 817th Squadron at Bien Hoa for III and IV Corps, the 819th at Nha Trang for II Corps, and the 821st at Da Nang for I Corps [E: the VNAF's historical gunship squadron numbers, vacated by this provision, and the bases of its gunship force of 1969–75]. The aircraft arrive in January 1967; the squadrons do not. The first Vietnamese crews are trained with American instructor crews from January, the 817th flies its first VNAF-crewed combat sortie about August 1967, the 819th about March 1968 and the 821st about the end of 1968 [E: D9 and D12, the training pipeline]. Until then the aircraft fly with mixed crews under an American training detachment, or wait.

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The AC-130U flies with thirteen: five officers — pilot, co-pilot, navigator, fire control officer and electronic warfare officer — and eight enlisted — flight engineer, television operator, infrared detection set operator, loadmaster and four aerial gunners [S3]. It is a larger crew than the Ghostrider's, built round an older, more manual system.

At 1.5 crews per aircraft the wing needs about 72 crews and about 940 aircrew, of whom about 360 officers [E: 48 × 1.5 × 13]. Maintenance runs about 20–25 people an aircraft for a C-130H gunship with its radar and fire control, about 960–1,200 [E: the historical AC-130U maintenance ratio for an older, more maintenance-intensive system]. With armament, operations and supply the wing is about 2,100–2,500 Vietnamese [D].

**Against the ceiling.** The crews and maintainers are VNAF and outside it. The American cost is a training and advisory detachment and contractor field teams: about 250–450 US personnel in 1967–69, of whom about 150–300 military [E: instructor crews for each squadron's first year, maintenance advisers and a contractor team for the radar and fire control], falling to about 100–150, chiefly contractors, by 1971 [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. CONFIG sets no ceiling.

**Against the VNAF.** That is the harder count. In 1967 the VNAF had about 16,000 people [E: the VNAF's strength of the period]; a gunship wing of 2,100–2,500 is about a seventh of it, and its 360 officers and its radar, fire-control and avionics technicians are the scarcest people the VNAF has. Where they come from is the subject of D9.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

Three trainable guns on the left side, aimed by the fire-control system and slewed to the sensor's line of sight [S5].

**GAU-12/U 25mm Gatling gun.** Five barrels, about 1,800 rounds a minute [S5], the gun of the AV-8B Harrier. It replaced the two M61A1 20mm guns of the AC-130H [S5]. A load of about 3,000 rounds [E: published AC-130U load]. It is the area weapon against men in the open and in light cover, and it fires at a rate that saturates a treeline.

**40mm Bofors L/60.** The Second World War's anti-aircraft gun, single-barrel, firing about 100–120 rounds a minute in clips [E: the L/60's rate]. A load of about 250 rounds [E: published AC-130U load]. Its ammunition is the M42 Duster's 40mm, already in theatre in quantity [H], which matters in 1974.

**105mm M102 howitzer, modified.** The same heavy hand as the Ghostrider's: about 100 rounds [E: published load], firing the standard 105mm families, including the domestic shell bodies of P008a's arsenal [M008].

**No guided weapons.** The U carries no missiles and no glide bombs. Its precision is the fire-control system's: the guns are aimed by a computer that solves the orbit, the wind and the ballistics to put the first round within a few metres of the sensor's point [E: the U's fire-control accuracy].

The dual-target fire-control system can engage two targets up to a kilometre apart at once, each with its own sensor and gun [S5]. Over an outpost attacked from two sides, that matters.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The AC-130U sees with three sensors. The all-light-level television and the infrared detection set (a forward-looking infrared) are its eyes on a clear night [S5]; the Hughes AN/APQ-180 synthetic-aperture radar, a derivative of the F-15E's AN/APG-70, is its eyes through cloud [S5]. It carries two laser designators [S5].

**At night** the television and infrared are a generation behind the Ghostrider's turrets: a 1990s infrared detection set that sees a truck at perhaps 5–10 km and a man at 2–4 km [E: the U's AAQ-117-class infrared set against the Ghostrider's MX-25]. That is still several times better than anything the VNAF or the ARVN had in 1967.

**In monsoon** the U is the better aircraft. The APQ-180 gives the U what the Ghostrider of §1.1 does not have: the ability to find and fire on a target through cloud and darkness [S3] [S5]. Its radar was first used in combat to identify and attack targets in Kosovo in 1999 [S3]. Over an outpost on a monsoon night, it can map the outpost and its surroundings, take a radar beacon or a known grid as its reference, and put 105mm and 40mm rounds on the ground around the wire with no sight of the ground at all [E: the U's adverse-weather attack mode]. It is less precise this way — offset fire on a radar reference rather than a point the crew can see — but it is fire, and on a night with a 1,500-foot overcast it is the only fire from the air anyone can deliver.

**Under triple canopy** its radar sees the canopy as surely as its optics do. A moving vehicle on a road the radar can see is a target; a battalion under the trees is not. The U, like the J, addresses the main force only when it shows itself, and the infrastructure not at all (A6).

### D5 · Performance

Four Allison T56-A-15 turboprops of 4,300 shaft horsepower each [S3]. Speed about 300 mph at sea level, ceiling 25,000 feet, range about 1,300 nautical miles, extendable by air refuelling [S3]. Maximum take-off weight 155,000 pounds [S3]. Loiter up to about 4.5 hours [S5].

It attacks from a five-nautical-mile pylon arc at about 5,000–10,000 feet above ground [S5]. That is lower than the Ghostrider's preferred orbit and inside the reach of the 12.7mm at its bottom; it is above the 7.62mm everywhere. An armour protection system covers the crew [S5].

From Bien Hoa the U covers III and IV Corps; from Nha Trang the Highlands and the central coast; from Da Nang I Corps. A 4.5-hour loiter at 100–150 nautical miles gives about three hours over a fight [E: D5 against S5].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The U carries UHF, VHF and VHF-FM voice, an inertial and GPS navigation system, and satellite voice in its 1990s fit [S5] [E: the U's radios]. It has no video downlink of the kind the 2026 Ghostrider has. Under E01, its GPS works from 1967 [CONFIG space_constellations].

It talks to the ARVN better than any American aircraft could, because its crews are Vietnamese. The pilot answering a Regional Forces company commander in Kien Hoa at 0300 speaks his language, uses his map, knows the district chief's name and the call signs of the province net, and needs no interpreter and no American adviser on the ground [E: the advantage of a national air arm]. On VHF-FM it speaks to the PRC-25 and the domestic PRC-77 sets of P008b [M008]. That is its largest single advantage over the Ghostrider over the hamlets, and it grows as the American advisers leave.

What it lacks is the 2026 integration. Its fire control was written in English for American crews: more than 609,000 lines of code in the mission computers and avionics [S5], with displays and manuals in English. The VNAF's crews must read them.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Basing.** The U operates from the same runways as the Ghostrider, 3,000–5,000 feet, paved or hard-packed [E: C-130H short-field performance], at Bien Hoa, Nha Trang and Da Nang, all of which the VNAF shared with the Americans until 1972–73 [H]. It needs revetments: a VNAF gunship on the ramp at Bien Hoa during a rocket attack is as exposed as an American one.

**Over the South, against small arms and 12.7mm.** From 5,000–10,000 feet above ground, it is above the 7.62mm and at the edge of the 12.7mm at the bottom of its orbit [E: D5 against DShK range]. Before 1972 that is safe enough for nightly work over the South.

**Against the SA-7.** The Strela-2 reaches about 2,300 metres [E: published ceiling]. The U's lower orbit is within reach at its bottom in the low and medium heights, and its 1990s electronic countermeasures and flares give partial protection [S5] [E: the U's AN/AAR-44-class missile warning and flares]. In history the SA-7 drove VNAF pilots above 10,000 feet, where their close support lost its accuracy [S9]. The U can work from 10,000 feet with its fire control; a U above the SA-7 still fires with precision, which an A-37 above 10,000 feet could not. That is a real advantage, and it is bounded by the radar-laid 37mm and 57mm that arrived with the PAVN's corps in 1972 and 1975 [S9].

**Over the Trail.** The VNAF does not fly in Laos. The U is a weapon of the South.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Fuel.** JP-4, the theatre's fuel, in the T56 [E: the C-130H's standard fuel]. About 18,000–22,000 pounds a sortie [E: C-130H loiter consumption]; at about 30–40 sorties a night across the wing, about 270–400 tons a day [D].

**Ammunition.** The 105mm is the standard howitzer round, and the 40mm the Duster's round — both in theatre and in the RVNAF's own system, and the 105mm in the domestic arsenal's production [M008]. The 25mm for the GAU-12 is 2026 production under A2, and its supply depends on the American aid line. Over a year, at about 150–250 rounds of 25mm, 50–80 of 40mm and 10–20 of 105mm a sortie [E: typical expenditure over an outpost], the wing fires about 2–3 million rounds of 25mm, 0.6–1 million of 40mm and 100,000–250,000 of 105mm [D].

**Spares and specialists.** The U is a C-130H, which the VNAF historically flew from late 1972 [H], with a radar and fire-control system of the 1990s. The T56 engines and airframe are within reach of a VNAF depot built with American help by 1970 [E: the VNAF's depot at Bien Hoa]. The APQ-180, the fire-control computers and the sensors are not; they need American contractors or American depots, in the Philippines or the United States, throughout.

**What it needs that the RVNAF cannot supply.** In 1967, almost everything except the fuel and the 105mm and 40mm rounds. By 1972, with the domestic electronics base of P008b and a trained VNAF depot, perhaps half the routine work on the radios and the airframe [M008] [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]; never the radar.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

This provision is the transfer. The question is whether the VNAF can absorb it, and on what timeline.

**The pilots.** The VNAF of 1967 had C-47, C-119 and C-123 pilots [H], and in this force its officers carry the 2026 officer education of memorandum 007 [CONFIG officer_cohorts_1957]. A C-47 or C-119 aircraft commander converts to the C-130H in about four to six months in the United States, and qualifies as a gunship aircraft commander in about a further six to nine months [E: USAF transition and gunship qualification times]. The 72 crews need about 144 pilots; the VNAF can find perhaps 30–50 transport pilots to convert in 1967 and must train the rest from new pilots over 1967–69 [E: the VNAF transport force of the period].

**The fire control officers, navigators and electronic warfare officers.** These are the rare men: about 216 officers who can run a radar, a fire-control computer and an electronic-warfare panel in English [E: 72 crews × 3]. The VNAF had navigators for its transports and almost no one else with this training [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. They come from the officer courses of P007b and from the technical schools, take about a year in the United States, and are the bottleneck [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above].

**The sensor operators and gunners.** The enlisted men of the basic training of P009b and the NCO education of P010b [CONFIG enlisted_bct_1957, nco_education_1957] train as gunners in three to four months and as sensor operators in six to nine [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above].

**The maintainers.** Technical training was in English historically, and that restricted it to the men who read English well [S9]. The radar and fire-control technicians are the hardest: about 150–250 men, each a year or more in training [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above].

**Timeline.** First VNAF crew operational about August 1967; first squadron at full strength about mid-1968; all three by the end of 1969 [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. The aircraft wait for the people, not the other way round. Before 1969 a third to half of the 48 sit on the ramp or fly training sorties with American instructor crews aboard.

**After transfer.** The VNAF keeps flying them as long as it gets fuel, 25mm ammunition, spares for the radar and fire control, and contractors to keep the sensors working. From 1973 that is a matter of the appropriation (D12).

### D10 · Employment envelope

The U performs to specification at night, in any weather the radar can see through, over an outpost or a unit that calls for it on the province or division net, within about 150 nautical miles of base, with a Vietnamese crew that knows the ground [E: D4–D6]. The radar makes the U the one aircraft in the theatre that delivers precision fire on a monsoon night.

Its performance collapses under four conditions. First, canopy, as with every sensor (A6). Second, the SA-7 at its lower orbits from 1972, which pushes it up to 10,000 feet and lengthens its time to put rounds on [S9]. Third, radar-laid 37mm and 57mm with the PAVN's corps in 1972 and 1975, against which it does not survive in orbit [E: history lost an AC-130A over the South in June 1972 and two over Laos that year, S7]. Fourth, the VNAF's maintenance: if its radar and fire control are out, the U is a 1960s gunship with better guns.

**Tempo over weeks.** At about 50–60% of aircraft mission-capable in VNAF hands in 1968–72 [E: VNAF rates for complex aircraft of the period, raised by the contractor teams], the wing flies about 25–30 sorties a night at full strength [D]: one or two aircraft over each corps all night. After 1973, at about 35–50% [E: the VNAF's historical decline of 1974, S9], about 15–25 [D].

**Rules of engagement.** The U fires under the RVNAF's rules: the province chief clears fire into his province. A Vietnamese crew, on a Vietnamese call, cleared by a Vietnamese province chief, is faster than an American one if the chain works and slower if it does not.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The fire control and radar fail first.** The U's system is complex, and the VNAF's technicians are new. A U with its radar down is a clear-night gunship; a U with its fire control down fires by the crew's eye and the gunners' skill, as the AC-47 did.

**Crews make mistakes.** A gunship firing a 105mm and a 25mm Gatling at night beside a hamlet kills civilians when the crew misreads the ground, the call or the friendly position. The historical record of gunship fratricide and civilian harm is not small [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above]. A Vietnamese crew over Vietnamese hamlets carries that risk in every sortie, and an incident in a contested province would be exploited at once by the Front.

**The partial failure.** A squadron that has lost half its radar technicians to the airlines, to transfer or to death flies half its sorties with a working radar. In the monsoon that is half its value.

**When the U is not there.** The outpost has the district's 105mm, the VNAF's A-1s and A-37s in daylight, and the Ghostrider while the Americans are there. After 1973 it has the district gun and nothing else at night.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The U degrades on a different curve from the J, because its strength is people, and the people take years. In month one of 1967, the U is flown by American instructors with Vietnamese trainees aboard, and its capability is the instructors'. In month twelve, the first VNAF squadron flies with about 60% of aircraft mission-capable, and its crews are new [E: VNAF rates for complex aircraft, D10]. By 1970–71, the wing is at its best: three squadrons at strength, crews with two to three years on the aircraft, and an American contractor team for the radar and fire control [E: this headquarters' judgment on the figures above].

After 1973, two things decline first. First, the radar and fire-control systems, as the contractor teams leave and spares slow; by the end of 1974, I expect half the wing's radars to be down at any time [E: the VNAF's historical maintenance decline of 1974, S9]. Second, the 25mm ammunition, if the aid line thins; the U then fights with the 40mm and 105mm alone, both of which the RVNAF can supply. The airframe and engines last longest.

The degradation is bounded by one fact in this record that history lacked: the fiscal 1975 appropriation of about $950 million kept a third more of the VNAF flying [M011] [X-011]. A larger share of that money goes to gunships if the JGS judges them worth it, and I expect it to.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the VNAF's historical AC-47 and AC-119.** History gave the VNAF AC-47s from 1969 and AC-119G and AC-119K gunships from 1971–72, as the Americans transferred them [S8] [S9]. The AC-47 had three 7.62mm miniguns and fired from 1,500 feet [S6]; the AC-119G four miniguns, the AC-119K two 20mm guns as well [S8]. None had a radar to fire through cloud, and none survived over the SA-7 at its attack altitude [S9]. The U has a 25mm Gatling, a 40mm and a 105mm, a radar, a fire-control system and a 10,000-foot attack height [S5] [S9]. Against the VNAF's historical gunship force, it is about five to ten times the night firepower a sortie and the only all-weather capability, and it arrives two to four years earlier [E: D3 and D4 against S6 and S8].

**Against the Ghostrider of §1.1.** On a clear night the J is better: better sensors, standoff missiles and bombs, a higher orbit, a more reliable system. On a monsoon night the U is better, because it has a radar and the J does not [S4] [S5]. The U is Vietnamese, and stays.

### D14 · Negative specification

The U cannot find the main force under canopy, any more than any sensor can (A6). It cannot tell the cadre from the farmer, and it does nothing about the infrastructure.

It cannot survive radar-laid 37mm and 57mm, or the SA-2, and it is pressed above 10,000 feet by the SA-7 from 1972; against the PAVN's corps air defences of 1972 and 1975 it does not orbit over the battle.

It does not arrive as a capability in 1967. The aircraft arrive then; the crews and maintainers take until 1969 to reach strength, and the radar and fire-control technicians are the last.

It carries no guided weapons; its precision is its fire control and its radar.

It cannot be sustained by the RVNAF alone. Its radar and fire control need American contractors or depots, and its 25mm needs the aid line, for as long as it flies.

It is not a substitute for American air power in a conventional offensive. Three squadrons of gunships do not break a siege of the kind An Loc and Kontum were.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy

It denies the enemy the night attack on a post after the Americans leave. History's VNAF had no answer to the PAVN's and the VC's night attacks on RF and PF outposts in 1973–74, and the land-grabbing war of those years ran on them; a Vietnamese gunship with a radar takes the monsoon night away from that war. It denies him nothing in the forest, nothing in the hamlet's politics, and little in a set-piece offensive with corps air defences.

### D16 · Honest verdict

The Spooky II is the most important aircraft the Vietnamese Air Force has ever been given, and it will take three years to be worth what it is. Once its crews and technicians are trained, it gives the Republic something history's never had: its own night and all-weather fire over its own outposts, from a crew that speaks the language of the man calling for it. That matters most after 1973, when every American aircraft has gone and the war in the countryside is a war of outposts attacked at night. It does not change the war's set-piece battles; it cannot survive the PAVN's corps air defences, it does not break a siege, and it lives on the appropriation for its radar, its 25mm and its contractors. Its value is that it makes the territorial war of 1973–74 one the Republic can fight on its own at night, and makes the outposts that fell in history to a night attack, many of them, hold. That is not a rung of the ladder. It is a part of the self-sufficiency the next rung needs.
