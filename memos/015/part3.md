## §4.0 The course of the war

The war this command inherited on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war being lost in the hamlets, and the first thing this record changed was the hamlet. On that day the junta decreed land to the tiller and an independent commission against corruption, put a resident constable and a Popular Forces platoon beside each other in the villages of the held belt, and began to choose its province and district chiefs from a ranked list (EV001-1, EV002-1). The men who would hold those posts already carried modern carbines and had been trained, privates and sergeants and lieutenants alike, on the American programmes of 2026 since 1957; the radios that would let them call for help came from a plant at Go Vap (EV004-1, EV008-1, EV009-1, EV010-1). Two U-2s and four Global Hawks watched the coast and the Trail from the first day, and on Guam the B-52Js waited with GPS bombs for a war that Washington would not yet let them fight (EV003-1, EV005-1).

And from the first night, over the posts, there was a gunship. Sixty-four AC-130J Ghostriders flew under Farm Gate's rules with a Vietnamese observer aboard, and on the night of 8–9 January one of them broke a company's assault on the post at Phu Tuc in Kien Hoa from 12,000 feet with eighteen rounds of 105mm (EV015-1). That night is the hinge of this memorandum. For fourteen memoranda the post had been built — land under it, a chief above it, a rifle, a radio, a trained private and a schooled sergeant inside it — and every memorandum had ended at the same sentence: the post can call, and the answer is the weak link. The gunship was the answer, on clear nights, within the net, in fifteen to thirty minutes. Its limits were found as fast as its value. Clearance from the province chief took half an hour in January, and in July this headquarters delegated fire within 300 metres of a post to the district chief, buying speed with the hamlet's risk. In the first north-east monsoon, over Tam Quan on the night of 14 December, a Ghostrider that went under the cloud to see was nearly shot down by two 12.7mm guns on the dikes, and the wing forbade attacks below 3,500 feet; the coast's monsoon nights belonged to the weather from then on (EV015-2).

The Front learned the gunship in a season. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee wrote its directive on "the aircraft that sees in the dark" in March 1964: no assault on a post with a radio on a clear night within reach of the airfields; attack in rain, attack far away, attack what cannot call, and be over the wall before the aircraft comes (EV015-1). That much it got right, and the night war moved where it said. It got the radar wrong, crediting the aircraft with a sight through cloud it did not have, and its coastal provinces lost a winter's advantage before they learned better. By the summer of 1965 assaults on covered posts on clear nights had nearly stopped in the held belt, and the war there was the mortar from 300 metres, the mine on the dike and the hamlet chief killed by day — quieter, and no less the Front's.

The escalation came on history's schedule and in this war's shape. The Tonkin night produced no second attack and Pierce Arrow was not flown; the opening strike of 2 March 1965 took the North's economy in a week and did not reach the South; the Marines and the Koreans came ashore in the spring, among them, now, the battalions that guarded the gunship bases after a mortar team burned a Ghostrider on the ramp at Bien Hoa on 1 November 1964 (EV000-3, EV013-1, EVCP01-3). At Nam Dong and Dong Xoai the gunships saved men at night and none by day, and the B-2 Front won both battles where it chose them (EVCP01-3, EV000-18).

The main-force war of 1965–67 was fought as history fought it and cost fewer Americans. At Plei Me and in the Ia Drang the Ghostriders held the nights, broke the 33rd Regiment's third night assault and the 7th Battalion's dawn attack at X-Ray before it formed, and lit the grass at Albany on the night of 17 November (EV000-23); the campaign's American dead fell to about 165–170, and the B3 Front wrote that the belt-grabbing attack must now be made under cloud or in the forest. The Ubon squadron went onto the panhandle on the night STEADY COUNT began, and for the first time a Global Hawk's track had a gun over it within twenty minutes (EV015-3). The video counted 1,100–1,500 trucks in the first dry season and 2,000–2,600 in the second, and Group 559 answered as it had answered the J: convoys at intervals, parks under the trees, bypasses under the canopy, and 37mm and then 57mm guns a year early, one of which killed six men of Ghostrider 22 over the Ban Laboy ford on 2 March 1967. Tonnage arriving fell by two to four points more than history's. The Trail's capacity was several times its requirement, and Moscow sent trucks.

In the countryside the gunship's success showed as quiet, and the quiet had two faces. At My Phuoc Tay on 9 March 1967 the Front fixed the province's gunship over a diversion at Long Dinh and ambushed the relief at the culvert with the Republic's own wire and batteries; three months later, with a Ghostrider flying the road ahead of the trucks under Major Liem's fourth rule, the 514th's ambush was found in the cane and destroyed (EV008-4). That was the clear night. The cloudy night belonged to the Front: through the monsoon of 1966 the posts of Hoai Nhon and Phu My were attacked on eighteen of the thirty nights the cloud was low and on two of the thirty-one it was not, and eleven fell while a Ghostrider orbited above them, blind (EV015-4). The Korean posts beside them held, because each had a battery in range. The PF platoons of the held belt stayed behind their wire under the gunship's protection, and the hamlets outside were walked at night by the tax collectors as before. Posts held is a real thing, and this headquarters reported it as that and not as pacification.

On 1 January 1967 forty-eight AC-130U Spooky IIs arrived for a Vietnamese Air Force that had no one to fly them (EV015-5). It took the whole of 1967 to find the crews and most of 1968 to find the radar technicians, and the first Vietnamese gunship sortie was flown on the night of 19–20 August 1967 over the post at Hiep Hoa, its fire-control officer talking to the post's commander in his own language through a cloud the radar saw through. II Corps' figures from the monsoon of 1966 sent the second squadron to Nha Trang for the coast, and from 1969 the coast's monsoon nights had, for the first time, an answer.

Tet came on history's night and broke on history's walls, against an army that held its district towns better and a Front whose district government in the held belt had already been broken; the Ghostriders flew over Saigon and the district towns as history's AC-47s had, with more guns, and the year was decided in Washington by the attack itself (null P3). Commando Hunt opened in November 1968 with the Ubon squadron three years on the panhandle, and its gunship video was the honest third of the radar's ledger: three to four thousand trucks a season, two or three points more of net loss, and a Trail that grew to 150–200 trucks a night regardless (EV000-68).

Vietnamization handed the night over the posts to the Vietnamese in stages, and it is the one handover in this record that worked at both ends. The Ghostrider squadrons left from October 1970; the 81st Gunship Wing, at about 55% of its aircraft operational, answered about two-thirds of the posts they had answered, through cloud on the coast where they never could, in Vietnamese (EV015-6). At Long My the posts had two Vietnamese answers by 1971, the district's own guns and a Spooky II from Binh Thuy, and at Xeo Mat on 23–24 February the district platoon fired first and the gunship found the withdrawing company in the canal (EV008-7). Over Cambodia in 1970 the 817th learned what the dossier had warned, losing a gunship to 37mm on Route 7 near Snuol, and came home to the Delta's posts, where it belonged (EV015-7). The posts the Vietnamese gunships did not reach — the far Highlands, the islands of the lower Delta — were attacked more often as the Americans left. Lam Son 719 and the Easter Offensive were decided by the B-52Js and the ARVN's divisions, and in 1972 the Spooky IIs went above 10,000 feet when the SA-7 came south, firing on radar from heights an A-37 could not use (null P5).

The Americans left in March 1973 and took their gunships, and the Republic kept its own. In the war of the flags the 81st Wing answered about two calls in three in the Delta and on the coast, and through 1973 the Front's districts attacked the posts beyond its reach, as they had learned from the Ghostriders nine years before (EV000-84). In the spring of 1974 the Military Region 9 Party Committee, short of rice and reading the VNAF as grounded, opened a campaign against the canal posts of Chuong Thien on its own reckoning. The gunships were not grounded: the Joint General Staff had put the Binh Thuy detachment first on the ration. About 46 of 64 attacked posts held, the spring harvest behind them was sold in Vi Thanh rather than taxed in the U Minh, and in July COSVN refused the region a division (EV015-8). It is the clearest night of the territorial war the record holds, and it was won by Vietnamese.

The gunships could not fly over the PAVN's divisions. At Thuong Duc in August 1974 the 304th Division brought its own 37mm guns and SA-7s, and the first Spooky II over the trenches went down with all thirteen of its crew; the town fell on 7 August as before (EV000-91). At Pleiku in April 1975 the 819th found the 320A's assault battalions forming in the dark on 2 and 5 April and made their cost a fifth higher, and lost two aircraft to the 968th's radar-laid guns in a night, and the siege ended when the 130mm guns closed Cu Hanh from 27 kilometres, beyond anything a gunship could reach (EV000-95). The Highlands fell on 19 April and I Corps north of Da Nang with them; the Politburo, its calendar broken by three to five weeks, put Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76 (EVMT01-4, EV011-3).

On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south under Thieu, with no American air power and, now, with a gunship wing of about twenty-five aircraft that holds the Delta's nights. Its fall is expected between February and May 1976. The gunships did not change that date. They changed what the Republic is at the end: a state whose posts in the Delta hold at night on their own, and which cannot stop a corps.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The phase changes at night and in the held belt, and it changes because the gunship completes the post's chain (F015-1). The record's earlier provisions gave the post its platoon, its rifle, its chief and its radio; the Ghostrider gave the radio's call an answer that arrived in the dark. Phu Tuc on 9 January 1964 is the mechanism in one night: a call on a PRC-10 at 0140, an aircraft overhead at 0209, a company broken by 0240 (EV015-1). The binding constraint moved at once from the answer to the clearance, and the July rule that put clearance in the district chief's hands moved it again, to the weather (F015-7).

The weather showed itself in December at Tam Quan, and it defined the limit for the rest of the war (EV015-2, D14). The Delta's south-west monsoon clears by midnight; the coast's north-east monsoon does not. The gunship therefore helped the held belt of III and IV Corps most and the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps least, which is the opposite of where the war was being lost.

Regionally (DIRMT01-8): in **the Delta** the posts of Kien Hoa and Dinh Tuong were covered and held (EV015-1); on **the coast** the posts of Binh Dinh were covered on clear nights and lost on cloudy ones (EV015-2); in **the Highlands** Nam Dong showed the canopy's limit, the attackers inside the wire before the aircraft came (EVCP01-3); in **the Saigon belt** Song Be's losses were taken inside the wire in the first hour and the gunship changed nothing that mattered (EVCP01-3).

The soldier's family (DIRMT01-9): the PF platoon of 1964 lived with its families inside or beside the post, and a post that held kept its women and children alive. That is the first thing the gunship protected, and the PF knew it.

Rice and the piaster (DIRMT01-11): the posts that held kept the held belt's rice moving to the government's buyers in 1964–65, a trace in the phase's figures, and nothing in the gunships touched the piaster.

### Enemy response and initiative

COSVN's Military Affairs Committee detected the gunship in the first week and wrote its answer by March (EV015-1, F015-15). The directive was right about the clear night and wrong about the cloud, and the coast's provincial units of Military Region 5 paid for the error through the winter of 1964–65 before learning, at Tam Quan and nights like it, that the aircraft did not see through rain (EV015-2). The adaptation cost the Front tempo, not men: attacks only on some nights and in some places (F015-16). The base attack at Bien Hoa on 1 November 1964 was the Front's own decision, made for the American election, and it burned a Ghostrider on the ramp because the gunships were there (EVCP01-3).

### Causal chains

**C015-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV015-1) The Ghostriders answer the held belt's posts from January 1964 and Farm Gate's clearance takes half an hour → this headquarters and the Joint General Staff delegate clearance within 300 metres of a post to the district chief in July (F015-7) → the Front moves its assaults to the hamlet's edge, where a district chief whose post is under attack decides in minutes whether to fire on the hedge line (F015-5, F015-13) → the district chiefs of the merit list, chosen for their judgment, become the war's arbiters of civilian harm at night, and the Inspectorate's first cases against list chiefs in 1965 are for fire beside hamlets rather than for corruption [X-015].

### Downstream

Every later phase inherits three things from this one: the Front's habit of attacking in cloud and beyond the net (Phase II's monsoon nights, Phase VI's Chuong Thien), the district chief's power to clear fire at night (Phase II's civilian harm), and the gunship bases as targets (every phase). The ripple into Phase II is the night-control facet of IV Corps, raised a point at the top.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Three findings carry the phase. First, the post's chain, completed in Phase I, is now defended against the Front's best answer to it: the ambush of the relief. At My Phuoc Tay the Front drew the gunship away with a diversion and killed 31 men at the culvert; under Liem's fourth rule a gunship flew the road ahead of the next relief and found the 514th in the cane on 14 June (EV008-4, EC015-2). Second, the night attack moved where the gunship could not follow, and the monsoon nights of Binh Dinh in 1966 are the place that shows it, the event this memorandum narrates at length for a downstream effect rather than the provision's purpose (EV015-4, F015-5). Third, the radar's tracks met a gun in the air over the panhandle, and the trucks died without the tonnage noticing (EV015-3, F015-2, F015-6).

The strongest positive effect shows itself here at its true weight (F015-20). The posts of the held belt were seldom assaulted on clear nights by 1966; Liem's district held every post it had; the Delta's quietest months of the war so far were the south-west monsoon of 1966 (EV015-4). I judge 4–10 points more of the South's attacked posts held across 1964–67 than without the gunships, on top of everything else the record had given them [X-015]. The perverse effect sits beside it: the PF stayed behind their wire under the aircraft, and IV Corps' night control rose by up to two points, not by the four or five the posts' survival suggested (F015-12).

At the Ia Drang and Plei Me the gunships held the nights of a main-force campaign and changed its days not at all (EV000-23). They saved perhaps 10–20 American lives, chiefly at Albany, and taught the B3 Front to fight in cloud or in the forest. That is the main-force effect in one battle: the enemy who chooses to fight at night pays more, and he chooses less often (F015-14).

The VNAF's gunships arrived in 1967 and found their constraint where the dossier placed it, in the radar technicians (EV015-5, F015-8). The 817th's first sortie over Hiep Hoa is the beginning of the only part of this provision that survives 1973.

Regionally (DIRMT01-8): **the Delta** — My Phuoc Tay and Hiep Hoa (EV008-4, EV015-5); **the coast** — the monsoon nights of Binh Dinh (EV015-4); **the Highlands** — Plei Me (EV000-23); **the Saigon belt** — NIGHT LANTERN's post on the Can Giuoc canal, now held under a Ghostrider rather than an AC-47 (EV002-4).

Rice (DIRMT01-11): in the coastal districts of Binh Dinh the eleven posts lost in November and December 1966 took the villages behind them out of the government's rice purchase for the 1967 spring harvest, as history's lost posts did; in the Delta the posts that held kept the Xa No canal's rice moving to Can Tho (EV001-6) [X-015].

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's answers in this phase were cheap and clever (F015-16). The diversion at Long Dinh was the Dinh Tuong provincial unit's own design, and it worked once (EV008-4). Military Region 5 waited for the monsoon (EV015-4). Group 559 moved the trucks under the trees and brought the guns a year early, and the guns killed a Ghostrider over the Ban Laboy ford (EV015-3). The adaptation that cost the enemy most was the guns: 12.7mm guns with the main-force battalions and 37mm and 57mm on the Trail, a few percent of the Trail's tonnage spent to keep the gunships off.

### Causal chains

**C015-2** [months] (begins in Phase II, EV008-4) The Dinh Tuong provincial unit draws the province's gunship to a diversion at Long Dinh and ambushes the relief at the culvert on 9 March 1967 → Liem's fourth rule puts a gunship over the road ahead of every night relief (EC015-2) → on 14 June the 514th's ambush is found in the cane and destroyed, and it never ambushes that road again on a clear night → the district's RF companies move by road at night again in the dry season of 1967–68, and the rule of fire first (POS008-4) relaxes in Cai Lay for the clear nights only, so that the district's posts have their reaction force back on the nights the gunship can see and only then [X-015].

**C015-3** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV015-2) The wing forbids attacks below 3,500 feet after Tam Quan in December 1964 → the coast's posts are attacked on monsoon nights through 1966, and eleven fall in Hoai Nhon and Phu My while a Ghostrider orbits above them (EV015-4) → Vinh Loc's figures send the VNAF's second gunship squadron to Nha Trang for the coast in 1967 rather than to Da Nang → from 1969 the coast of II Corps has the war's only all-weather night fire, and the 819th, not the 821st, is the squadron at Nha Trang that flies over Pleiku in 1975 (EV015-6, EV000-95) [X-015].

### Downstream

The phase hands to Tet a held belt whose posts hold on clear nights and a coast whose posts do not; to Commando Hunt a gunship force three years on the Trail and an enemy whose guns are a year ahead of history's; and to Vietnamization a Vietnamese gunship squadron in being. The ripple into Phase III is IV Corps' night control and the year's American dead.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

The phase is where the provision's second half begins to matter, and it is the strongest evidence in the record that a handover can work when both ends are Vietnamese (F015-4). The Ghostriders left from October 1970; the 81st Gunship Wing answered about two-thirds of the posts they had answered, through cloud on the coast where they never could (EV015-6). At Long My in February 1971 a post called and both answers were Vietnamese, the district's guns and the Binh Thuy gunship (EV008-7). Against history's Republic, which had AC-47s from 1969 and AC-119s from 1971 with no radar, this is about five to ten times the night firepower a sortie and the only all-weather capability [M015].

The counter-directional finding begins here (F015-19). The posts beyond the wing's reach — the islands of An Xuyen and Bac Lieu, the Highland posts of Kontum and Darlac — had been covered by Ghostriders since 1964 and were attacked more often as the Americans left, and their commanders, who had called the sky for six years, were slower to rebuild the ground reaction history's had never stopped relying on (EV015-6).

On the Trail the Ghostriders' video was the honest third of Commando Hunt's ledger, and the campaign failed as history's did, at two or three points more net loss (EV000-68, F015-18). Over Cambodia the 817th learned the dossier's lesson on Route 7 (EV015-7).

Regionally (DIRMT01-8): **the Delta** — Xeo Mat and the Binh Thuy detachment (EV008-7, EV015-6); **the coast** — the 819th's monsoon nights of 1969–70 (EV015-6); **the Highlands** — the uncovered posts of Kontum and Darlac, attacked more often (EV015-6); **the Saigon belt** — the 817th at Bien Hoa, and Route 7 beyond the border (EV015-7).

The soldier's family (DIRMT01-9): the PF families inside the canal posts of Chuong Thien were the reason the platoons held, and the gunship's answer was the reason the families stayed.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front answered the Vietnamese gunships as it had answered the American ones, by attacking beyond the net and in the gaps the drawdown opened; it did not have to learn anything new (F015-5). On the Trail Group 559 put an SA-2 across the border north of the DMZ in February 1971 and killed a Ghostrider with it, and the 57mm batteries took another (EV000-68). On Route 7 the PAVN 7th Division's rear services put 37mm guns on the road within a month of the gunships' arrival (EV015-7).

### Causal chains

**C015-4** [months] (begins in Phase IV, EV015-7) The 817th flies Route 7 in support of the incursion and loses Spooky 34 to a 37mm battery near Snuol on 14 August 1970 → Khiem asks for the squadron's withdrawal to the Delta, where the posts need it and the guns are few → the squadron forms a five-aircraft detachment at Binh Thuy for the lower Delta (EV015-6) → at Xeo Mat in February 1971 the post's call is answered by a district gun and a Vietnamese gunship together, and Sergeant Danh Son adjusts both on one set (EV008-7) [X-015].

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1, DIRCP02-2): C008-2.** Memorandum 008's chain ran from the PF radio (1964) through My Phuoc Tay's ambush and the rule of fire first to the territorial artillery platoons the Delta's districts received from February 1970. In this war it resolves as predicted, with one difference its author could not foresee. By 1967 the gunship covered the clear nights, and the Delta's case for district guns rested on the cloudy ones and on ownership; the Joint General Staff formed the platoons anyway, because they were Vietnamese and under the district chief, and at Long My they fired first and the gunship second (EV008-7). On the coast the case was stronger than 008 wrote, because the monsoon nights of 1966 had shown what a post without a gun in range lost (EV015-4). The chain's terminal link stands; its weight shifts from the Delta to the coast.

### Downstream

Phase V receives a Vietnamese gunship wing at its best and an enemy about to bring the SA-7 south; Phase VI receives the wing that will fight the territorial war alone. The ripple into Phase V is the RVNAF's own fire support, raised up to four points at the top.

### Clock

The gunships' effect on American political tolerance in this phase is nil, and I state it plainly (F015-17). They saved perhaps 500–1,500 American lives across 1965–71 on a rate of night fire over positions in contact [X-015], below any band; the Laos squadron was a secret the newspapers did not find; and the Vietnamese wing was not a story in the United States. The one effect that touches the clock is indirect and later: a Vietnamese instrument that works is an argument for the appropriation, which the Republic made in 1974 (Phase VI).

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

The phase shows the provision's whole value and its whole limit, and they are separate (F015-20, F015-19). In the territorial war the Spooky IIs were the Republic's own answer to the night attack on the post, and they won most of the nights they reached: two calls answered in three in the war of the flags, a third fewer posts lost to the flag-raisers, and in Chuong Thien in the spring of 1974 about 46 of 64 attacked canal posts holding (EV000-84, EV015-8). In the conventional war they could not fly over the PAVN's divisions: at Thuong Duc the 304th's own air defence killed the first gunship over the trenches, and at Pleiku the 968th's radar-laid guns killed two in a night (EV000-91, EV000-95). The gunships changed which posts the Republic held and nothing about which divisions it could stop.

The state of the Republic at the end of 1974, before the last offensive (DIRMT01-3): the Delta's posted villages held more firmly than history's and more firmly than in any earlier memorandum's account, about five to twelve points more of IV Corps' rural people held after dark than history's; a gunship wing of about 30–34 aircraft flying on the first line of the ration; the posts beyond its reach lost at nearly history's rate; the coast of I and II Corps no better held than history's, because the PAVN's divisions sat in it; and the Airborne and Marines tied to I Corps' static defence as before [X-015].

The PAVN's 1972 losses (DIRMT01-6): the record keeps history's count of about 100,000 killed and wounded in 1972 [H64], with the cadre lost in the measured assaults replaced more slowly than the men (MT01). The gunships do not change it, because they did not change the year's sieges (null P5), and they do not change the rebuilding of 1973–74, which went on beyond their reach on the strategic road.

The critical path of 1975 (DIRMT01-7): the gunships do not move it. The palace's orders of 13–14 March were Thieu's and the record's (EV000-95); the 1st Corps' march and the Politburo's calendar of 25 March turned on the collapse of I and II Corps, which came three to five weeks late on the record's existing mechanisms (EVMT01-4). The gunships raised the cost of the 320A's assaults at Pleiku by about a fifth and did not lengthen the siege by a day, because the 968th's 130mm guns closed the airfield from beyond their reach; that is an effect on a fight the PAVN could not bypass and still did not need to win quickly. No mechanism in this provision delays the collapse further.

Regionally (DIRMT01-8): **the Delta** — Chuong Thien's rice war (EV015-8); **the coast** — the 821st over Phu Loc's hills in the monsoon (EV000-91); **the Highlands** — the 819th over Pleiku (EV000-95); **the Saigon belt** — the war of the flags around Saigon, where the 817th answered from Bien Hoa (EV000-84).

The soldier's family (DIRMT01-9): in Chuong Thien the posts whose PF families lived inside them held, and the posts whose families had been moved to Vi Thanh in 1973 fell (EV015-8). The gunship protected the post; the family decided whether the platoon stayed in it.

Rice and the piaster (DIRMT01-11): in the spring of 1974 about 15,000–25,000 tons of Chuong Thien's harvest were sold to the government's buyers in Vi Thanh rather than taxed by Military Region 9, at a piaster worth a third less than in January (EV015-8) [X-015]. The gunships did not touch the inflation; they touched who got the rice.

### Enemy response and initiative

<!-- initiative --> The phase's enemy initiative has an author and a cause of its own. In February 1974 the Military Region 9 Party Committee, short of rice after the 1973 harvest it could not tax and with its main-force regiments on half rations in the U Minh, decided before Hanoi's directives for the year reached it to open a dry-season campaign against the canal posts of Chuong Thien, to collect the harvest behind them (EV015-8). The decision was about rice, not about anything this command or the VNAF did, and it rested on a misreading: the region's staff took the VNAF to be grounded for want of fuel, as most of its A-1s were. COSVN, asked in July for a division to finish what the region began, refused it for the dry season of 1974–75 in the east; the region's war and Hanoi's were not the same war (DIRMT01-10).

The PAVN's answer to the gunships in the conventional war needed no new decision: its divisions brought their air defence with them, as they had since 1972 (EV000-91, EV000-95). That is the adaptation that cost the enemy nothing new and cost the Republic five gunships in two years.

### Causal chains

**C015-5** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase II, EV015-5) The gunship wing takes the VNAF's scarcest radar and avionics technicians from 1967 (F015-8, F015-10) → the Joint General Staff bars their transfer in 1971 to keep the wing flying (EV015-6) → in the ration of 1974 the JGS puts the wing's Binh Thuy detachment first on the fuel list because it is the one VNAF unit still fully manned, while a third of the VNAF is grounded → Military Region 9's rice campaign of the spring, planned on the reading that the VNAF is grounded, meets the gunships every night, and the harvest behind the canal posts goes to the government's buyers in Vi Thanh (EV015-8) [X-015].

### Downstream

The record ends here. What the provision leaves at the end of 1975 is a Republic whose Delta nights are its own and whose divisions are no stronger; a gunship wing of about 25 aircraft that the 1975–76 ration must feed; and an enemy that knows exactly where the gunships cannot go.

### Clock

The gunships' effect on the clock in this phase runs through the appropriation and is small. An instrument the Republic owns and flies well is an argument for the money that buys its fuel and its 25mm, and the larger fiscal 1975 appropriation the record already holds kept it flying (EV000-89, P011a). It did not change the vote, and I do not judge that it would have changed the vote of 1975 had the Republic made the argument with its own results: Congress in January 1975 refused the bombers on grounds no gunship touched (EV011-2).

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the cumulative force with this provision differs in four concrete ways that are new.

**Posts held at night.** History's posts were defended at night from the air by AC-47s from December 1964, by AC-119s from 1969 and, in Vietnamese hands, by a few squadrons of both from 1969–72, none with radar [M015·S6] [M015·S9]. This war's posts had a gunship over them from January 1964 and a radar gunship from 1967. I judge 4–10 points more of the attacked posts held in 1964–72 and 3–8 points more in 1973–74, perhaps 1,000–2,500 posts that did not fall over the whole war [X-015]. In the Delta this is the largest margin over history the record holds in the territorial war; on the coast it is small, because the coast's monsoon nights went to the Front until 1969 and its divisions after 1972.

**American lives.** Perhaps 500–1,500 American lives across 1965–71, on a rate of night fire over positions in contact, less about 40–60 Ghostrider crewmen lost against history's roughly 160 gunship crewmen [X-015] [M015·S7]. Below any band.

**The Trail.** Two to four points more of net loss in the dry seasons of 1966–71, three to four times history's honest truck count from the gunships, and no change in what the South's forces received (EV015-3, EV000-68).

**The Republic's own night fire.** History's VNAF had no answer to the night attack on a post in 1973–75; this war's had about 36–40 radar gunships in 1973 and about 25 at the end of 1975 (EV000-84, EV015-8). It changed the Delta and nothing in the battles of 1974–75 that the PAVN fought with its divisions (EV000-91, EV000-95).

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

**The outcome in its own terms.** The Republic at the end of 1975 is what memorandum 011 made it, a state that survived the spring offensive by about a year and holds III and IV Corps under Thieu without American air power, and this provision makes one part of it stronger: the Delta's nights. That part was already the strongest, and the war is not being lost there.

**The rung.** Rung (b), dependent survival, stands. The gunships pass nothing new: they do not make the Republic defeat a conventional offensive with or without American air (c1 and d1 stand as they stood), they do not contain the insurgency in every corps region, because the coast's posts went to the Front on monsoon nights until 1969 and to the PAVN's divisions after 1972 (c2), and they do not give the Republic the means to stand without American combat forces against a corps (d3). The next rung up is not reached because the Republic's binding gap is fire against the 130mm and the division, not fire over the post at night, and a gunship that dies over a division's air defence does not close it.

**The date of the fall (DIRMT01-1).** The window stays February–May 1976. Mechanisms pulling it later: a Delta whose posts hold on their own, a harvest sold to the government, a gunship wing that the Front's local forces cannot beat at night. Mechanisms pulling it earlier: a ration that must now feed the gunships' fuel and 25mm, and posts beyond their reach whose commanders forgot how to fight without them. The later ones dominate in IV Corps and decide nothing, because the fall of 1976 will come from the north and the east, through the PAVN's divisions, where neither set reaches.

**Running sums (DIRMT01-2).** American dead saved across the record: perhaps 2,500–5,000 before this memorandum (POSMT01-5), less 60–180 added by memorandum 014, plus about 500–1,500 here on a rate of night fire over positions in contact; the sum supports the middle of its range and crosses no band [X-015]. Territorial posts not lost: this memorandum opens the count, at about 1,000–2,500 over the war on a rate of 4–10 points of the attacked posts in 1964–72 and 3–8 in 1973–74, overlapping with the radio of P008b (whose own effect was shown as attacks not made) by perhaps a third; its upper bound is marked [X-015]. The Republic's dead saved in 1974: memoranda 008 and 011 judged 1,000–2,500 and 1,500–4,000; this memorandum adds 200–400 in I Corps and perhaps as many again in the Delta, inside the uncertainty of the year's 30,000 [H71] [X-015].

No phase redraw is proposed.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The night over the post.** From January 1964 a post under attack on a clear night within the net has a gunship over it in 15–30 minutes, and the Front stops assaulting covered posts in clear weather; 4–10 points more of the attacked posts hold in 1964–72 than they would have, the largest gain the record has given the territorial war at night (EV015-1, F015-20).
- **The Republic owns its night fire.** From 1969 the VNAF's radar gunships answer about two-thirds of the posts the Ghostriders answered, through cloud and in Vietnamese, and they stay after 1973: in Chuong Thien in 1974 about 46 of 64 attacked canal posts hold and the harvest goes to the government (EV015-6, EV015-8). It is the first instrument of the air war in this record that the Republic keeps (POS015-2).
- **The relief ambush answered.** The Front's best answer to the post's radio, the ambush of the relief, is found from the air on clear nights from 1967 (EV008-4).
- **A few hundred to fifteen hundred American lives,** at Plei Me, the Ia Drang, the camps and the night positions, on a rate of night fire, below any band (EV000-23).
- **An honest count on the Trail.** The gunships' video makes their truck claims the war's most accurate, and Commando Hunt's net loss two or three points higher, without changing what arrives (EV015-3, EV000-68).

### What has not changed

- **The cloud and the forest.** The Ghostrider cannot see through monsoon cloud or canopy, and the coast's posts went to the Front on monsoon nights until the Vietnamese radar gunships arrived (EV015-2, EV015-4).
- **The hamlet outside the wire.** The post holds and the platoon stays behind its wire; night control rises by a point or two, not by what the posts' survival suggests, and the gunships add civilian harm beside the hamlets (F015-12, F015-13).
- **The infrastructure.** Nothing in this provision reaches the cadre; Military Region 9 lost its rice in 1974 and kept its regiments (EV015-8).
- **The divisions.** The gunships cannot fly over the PAVN's corps air defence; Thuong Duc fell, Pleiku's siege was no longer, and the critical path of 1975 is unchanged (EV000-91, EV000-95).
- **The Trail's tonnage and the clock.** Trucks died and the tonnage barely noticed; American tolerance, the appropriation and the date of the fall are as the record had them (F015-6, F015-17).
