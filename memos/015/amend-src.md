## EV015-1 · The gunship wing's first season · 8 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Bien Hoa, Nha Trang and Da Nang air bases; the posts of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and Vinh Long Provinces · NEW · 015
*Initiative: allied*

The 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship) flew its first combat sortie on the night of 8–9 January 1964, a week after its 64 AC-130J Ghostriders were placed under the 2nd Air Division at Tan Son Nhut (P015a) [X-015]. The rules were Farm Gate's: an American combat aircraft over the South flew with a Vietnamese aboard, in support of the RVNAF, and fired only with the province chief's clearance [H]. Each Ghostrider carried a VNAF observer from the 1st Air Division, who spoke to the province and sector nets, and the wing's crews learned in their first week that the observer was not a formality but the only man aboard who could tell a Popular Forces platoon from the men attacking it [X-015].

The first engagement was over Kien Hoa. At 0140 on 9 January the PF post at Phu Tuc, on the Ham Luong north of Truc Giang, called the district on its PRC-10 that a company was in the coconut groves on its eastern side and that its wire was being cut. The district relayed the call to the sector, the sector to the air support operations centre at Can Tho, and Can Tho to Ghostrider 41, on station over the Mekong 30 nautical miles north. The aircraft was over Phu Tuc at 0209. Its infrared turret showed about sixty men in two lines among the palms 150–250 metres from the post's eastern wall and a mortar team of four in a ditch beyond them [X-015]. The province chief's clearance came at 0221. The 105mm put eighteen rounds on the two lines and the mortar ditch by 0240, and the 30mm took the men who ran toward the river [X-015]. The company of the Ben Tre provincial battalion left 21 dead on the eastern side; the post lost two wounded [X-015]. The district reported 70 enemy killed. The video, reviewed at Tan Son Nhut the next day, showed between 18 and 25, and it was the video's figure that went to MACV (F015-18) [X-015].

Through the spring the wing flew about 400–600 sorties a month in III and IV Corps and fewer in I and II Corps, and answered about 120–180 calls a month from posts under attack [X-015]. Two lessons came fast. The first was that the gunship was worth as much as the radio that called it: posts with a set on the district net were answered, and posts without one were found burning (F015-1) [X-015]. The second was clearance. In January the median wait for a province chief's approval was about 35 minutes, and on bad nights over an hour; in the middle of a contested district the post was often overrun while the aircraft circled with its guns trained [X-015]. In July this headquarters, with the Joint General Staff, delegated clearance for fire within 300 metres of a post under attack to the district chief, and the median wait fell to about 12 minutes (F015-7) [X-015]. The price of that rule was paid later, in hamlets beside the posts (F015-13).

The Front's answer came in March, and it was the first of many. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee, from reports of its Delta regions, issued a directive to the regional commands on "the aircraft that sees in the dark": no assault on a post with a radio on clear nights within reach of the airfields; attacks on posts in rain, on posts far from the airfields, and on posts that could not call; and the assault made fast, to be over the wall before the aircraft came (F015-15) [X-015]. The directive credited the aircraft with radar it did not have, and through the north-east monsoon of 1964–65 the provincial units of the coast did not exploit the cloud as they could have. By the end of June 1964, in the held belt of Long An and Dinh Tuong, assaults on posts with radios on clear nights had nearly stopped, and the Front's local companies had turned to the mortar from 300 metres, the mine on the dike and the hamlet official killed by day (F015-5, F015-14) [X-015].

## EV015-2 · The night under the cloud at Hoai Nhon · 14–15 Dec 1964 · The PF post at Tam Quan and the district town of Hoai Nhon, Binh Dinh Province; Qui Nhon airfield · NEW · 015
*Initiative: enemy*

The night that ended the gunship's work below the cloud came in the first north-east monsoon of the war. At 2310 on 14 December the post at Tam Quan, on Route 1 north of Bong Son, called Hoai Nhon district that it was under attack by a company of the 2nd Regiment of the Front's Military Region 5 [X-015]. The cloud base over the coast was about 1,800 feet, with rain. Ghostrider 63 of the 14th Squadron, on station south of Qui Nhon, came north and found nothing on its turrets but the glow of the post's flares through the cloud. Its commander, Major Thomas R. Gaither, took the aircraft down through the overcast to 1,500 feet to see, as two crews of the squadron had done successfully in November (EC015-7) [X-015].

He found the attack at once and it found him. The company had two 12.7mm machine guns sited on the paddy dikes south of the post, and they opened on the aircraft's first turn. The Ghostrider fired for about six minutes and broke the assault on the southern wall; in those minutes it took about thirty rounds of 12.7mm through the left wing and the fuel tanks, lost an engine and a hydraulic system, and was flown back to Qui Nhon on three engines with a fire in the wing that the fuel-tank protection kept from spreading [X-015]. Two crewmen were wounded. The post at Tam Quan held until 0400, when the attack was renewed in the rain with no aircraft overhead, and fell; nineteen of its thirty men were killed or taken [X-015].

On 16 December the wing forbade attacks below 3,500 feet. From then on, on monsoon nights over the coast, the Ghostrider could give the posts of Binh Dinh, Quang Ngai and Quang Nam nothing but a GPS weapon on a coordinate the posts could not give it, and the Front's provincial units, which had believed since March that the aircraft saw through cloud, learned over the winter that it did not (F015-15) [X-015]. It was the provision's limit (D14), found in a night.

## EV015-3 · Operation NIGHT LEDGER: the gunships on the panhandle's tracks · 6 Dec 1965 – 30 Apr 1967 · The Laotian panhandle from the Mu Gia pass to Tchepone and the Ban Laboy ford; Ubon Royal Thai Air Force Base; the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut · NEW · 015
*Initiative: allied*

The Thai government agreed in October 1965 to base a gunship squadron at Ubon, at the price of an increase in American aid and on the condition that nothing be said of it, and the 17th Air Commando Squadron's sixteen Ghostriders moved there from Nha Trang in November [X-015]. They flew their first armed reconnaissance over the panhandle on the night of 6 December, the night STEADY COUNT began (EV003-3). In this war the operation had a name of its own: NIGHT LEDGER, because from its first week every engagement was recorded on the gunship's video and counted against the radar's tracks in the same cell at Tan Son Nhut (F015-18) [X-015].

The method was the hand-off the radar had needed (F015-2). A Global Hawk track of six or more vehicles moving south was passed by satellite link to the airborne command post and from it to the nearest Ghostrider, which flew to the track, found the convoy on its infrared turret at 10–20 kilometres, and engaged it from about 12,000–15,000 feet with Griffins on the lead and rear trucks and the 105mm on the rest [X-015]. In the first dry season the squadron flew about 8–10 sorties a night and engaged about one track in three within twenty minutes of its passing, against the one in six that the strike force had reached in forty minutes (F003-5) [X-015]. The video counted about 1,100–1,500 trucks destroyed or disabled from December 1965 to April 1966; the crews claimed about 2,600 [X-015].

Group 559 answered within the season. By February 1966 the convoys on Routes 912 and 23 moved at intervals of five to ten minutes rather than in columns, the truck parks went under the canopy, and two battalions of 37mm guns were moved from the North to the crossings at Ban Laboy and the Xe Bang Fai, about a year earlier than history placed them [X-015]. Over the wet season the PAVN built bypasses under the trees, and by the dry season of 1966–67 a third of the southbound movements the radar counted ran on roads the canopy closed over, where the gunships could not see them (F015-6) [X-015]. The 57mm batteries came in the autumn. On the night of 2 March 1967 Ghostrider 22, working a convoy at the Ban Laboy ford at 11,000 feet, was hit by a 57mm battery the electronic order of battle had not shown and went down in the hills west of the ford; three of its nine crewmen were picked up the next morning by an HH-3E from Nakhon Phanom, and six were not found [X-015].

NIGHT LEDGER's honest arithmetic was the radar's. In the dry season of 1966–67 the video counted about 2,000–2,600 trucks destroyed or disabled, perhaps three to four times what the historical gunships could have done at that date had they existed, and the radar's estimate of the net loss to the throughput rose by about two to four points over the season [X-015]. Tonnage arriving in the South fell by less: the Trail's capacity was several times its requirement, and the trucks the North lost were replaced by Soviet and Chinese deliveries within the year (POSTNA-4, EV013-2) [X-015].

## EV015-4 · The monsoon nights of Binh Dinh · 15 Oct – 31 Dec 1966 · Hoai Nhon, Phu My and Tam Quan Districts, Binh Dinh Province; the posts on Route 1 and the coastal railway from Bong Son to Phu Cat · NEW · 015
*Initiative: enemy*

This is the season that shows where the night war went when the gunship took the clear nights from it (F015-5). By October 1966 the posts of the Delta and the Saigon belt within the gunship net were seldom assaulted on clear nights, and in the coastal lowlands of II Corps the Front's Military Region 5 had drawn the conclusion its Delta regions had drawn two years earlier. The 2nd Regiment of the PAVN 3rd (Sao Vang) Division and the Binh Dinh provincial battalions, rebuilt after the Bong Son operations of the spring, prepared through September to attack the posts of the coast in the north-east monsoon, on the nights the cloud came down (EVCP02-3) [X-015].

The monsoon arrived in the third week of October. On the night of 26–27 October, under a cloud base of about 1,200 feet and in heavy rain, three companies attacked three posts on Route 1 between Bong Son and Tam Quan within an hour of each other [X-015]. All three called Hoai Nhon district on their sets. The Ghostrider on station south of Qui Nhon came north and orbited above the cloud at 5,000 feet, seeing on its infrared turret only the diffused glow of the flares and the fires; its crew, under the wing's rule of December 1964 (EV015-2), did not go down. It dropped two GBU-39s on the coordinates the district gave for a mortar position, which the district had read from a 1:50,000 map in the rain, and the bombs fell about 400 metres from where the mortar had been [X-015]. Two of the three posts fell before dawn. The 105mm battery at Bong Son could reach one of them, and fired.

Through November and December the pattern held. The Front attacked the posts of Hoai Nhon, Phu My and Tam Quan on about 18 of the 30 nights the cloud was below 3,000 feet, and on 2 of the 31 nights it was not [X-015]. Eleven posts and one district police post fell, about as many as the coast lost in history's same months, and the Front took about 300 weapons, most of them M4A1s, and the files of the Phu My police post (EV002-3) [X-015]. The Korean Capital Division's posts south of Qui Nhon were attacked on the same nights and held, because each had a 105mm battery within range; the Korean commanders had not trusted the gunships from the first. In the Delta, where the south-west monsoon's cloud is afternoon convection that clears by midnight, the same months saw the fewest attacks on posts of the war so far.

Major General Vinh Loc's II Corps headquarters sent the season's figures to Saigon in January 1967, and they made two cases. The first was for guns: a district battery within reach of every post on the coast, which the Korean posts had shown worked and which the domestic arsenal could feed (EV008-4, EV008-7). The second was for a gunship that could see through cloud. The Spooky IIs promised to the VNAF had arrived on 1 January (EV015-5); their AN/APQ-180 radar could find a post and fire on offsets from it in any weather (P015b), and the Joint General Staff, on Vinh Loc's figures, sent the second VNAF squadron to Nha Trang for the coast rather than to Da Nang [X-015]. The coastal posts' night belonged to the weather for two more monsoons.

## EV015-5 · The Spooky IIs and the 817th's first night · 1 Jan – 31 Dec 1967 · Bien Hoa air base and the Air Training Centre at Nha Trang; Hurlburt Field, Florida; Duc Hue District, Hau Nghia Province · NEW · 015

Forty-eight AC-130U Spooky IIs arrived at Bien Hoa and Nha Trang from 1 January 1967 and were handed to a Vietnamese Air Force that had no one to fly them (P015b) [X-015]. The VNAF formed the 81st Gunship Wing under Lieutenant Colonel Pham Quang Khiem, a C-47 squadron commander who had flown Farm Gate's observer seat in the Ghostriders in 1964 and was among the first VNAF officers through the Air Command and Staff course, and an American training detachment of about 300 under the 14th Air Commando Wing [X-015]. Thirty C-47 and C-119 pilots went to the United States in January to convert; the first navigators, fire-control officers and electronic-warfare officers, drawn from the Thu Duc and Dalat classes of 1962–65 and from the VNAF's transport navigators, went in February for about a year [X-015]. The radar and fire-control technicians were the hardest to find, as the dossier had warned: the technical courses were in English, and the VNAF had perhaps eighty men who could take them in 1967 (F015-8) [X-015].

Mixed crews flew from March, an American instructor pilot and fire-control officer with a Vietnamese crew, and did not work: the Vietnamese crews fought on the province nets in Vietnamese and the instructors deferred or overruled at the wrong moments, and by March 1968 operational sorties were all-Vietnamese (EC015-9) [X-015]. The first all-Vietnamese combat sortie of the 817th Squadron was flown from Bien Hoa on the night of 19–20 August 1967 [X-015]. At 2350 the RF post at Hiep Hoa, on the Vam Co Dong in Duc Hue district, called the district in Vietnamese that it was under attack from the north-east; the Spooky II was over it at 0012, its fire-control officer, Captain Le Minh Tuan, spoke to the post's commander directly, and the 40mm and 105mm broke the assault in the cane north of the post in about twenty minutes, under a cloud base the radar saw through [X-015]. The post lost one man. It was the first time in the war that a Vietnamese post had been defended at night from the air by Vietnamese, and Khiem had the post commander's call played on the wing's loudspeakers at Bien Hoa the next morning.

By December 1967 the 817th had eleven crews and flew about 120 sorties a month over III and IV Corps; the 819th was forming at Nha Trang for the coast, and the 821st had not begun [X-015]. The cost was in the rest of the VNAF: the A-1 squadrons at Bien Hoa lost about a fifth of their avionics technicians to the gunship wing in 1967, and their aircraft were about two to three points less often operational for it (F015-10) [X-015].

## EV015-6 · The night net changes hands · 1 Jul 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · Bien Hoa, Binh Thuy, Nha Trang and Da Nang air bases; the posts of the four corps · NEW · 015
*Initiative: allied*

The Ghostriders went home on Vietnamization's schedule, and the night over the posts passed to the 81st Gunship Wing in stages (F015-4, F015-19). The 4th Air Commando Squadron left Bien Hoa in October 1970, the 14th left Nha Trang in December 1971, and the 16th at Da Nang and the 17th at Ubon stayed into 1972 [X-015]. The 817th Squadron took the night over III and IV Corps from October 1970 with a detachment of five aircraft at Binh Thuy for the lower Delta; the 819th at Nha Trang held the coast of II Corps from the spring of 1969; and the 821st at Da Nang reached strength at the end of 1969 and shared I Corps with the 16th [X-015].

At its best, in 1970–71, the wing flew about 700–850 sorties a month at about 55% of aircraft operational, against the Ghostriders' 1,300–1,500 at the peak of 1967–68 [X-015]. It covered about two-thirds of the posts the Ghostriders had covered, the radar beacons on the district towns and the most-attacked posts gave it the coast's monsoon nights the Ghostriders never had, and its answers came in Vietnamese (EC015-8) [X-015]. In the north-east monsoon of 1969–70 the 819th answered about 60% of the posts' calls in Binh Dinh and Phu Yen on cloudy nights, against almost none in history's coast and few in this war's before 1968 [X-015]. The posts it did not cover — the islands of An Xuyen and Bac Lieu beyond Binh Thuy's reach, the Highland posts of Kontum and Darlac — were attacked more often as the Americans left, and held about as history's did [X-015].

The wing lost four aircraft in the three years: one to 12.7mm fire over Kien Phong in March 1970, one to a 37mm battery over Cambodia in August 1970 (EV015-7), one in a landing accident at Nha Trang in the monsoon of 1970, and one to a mortar on the ramp at Da Nang in 1971 [X-015]. Its crews were the best-educated men in the VNAF and the most often sought by the airlines; about one in six left the wing in 1971, and the radar technicians, the scarcest of them, were held by an order of the Joint General Staff that barred their transfer (F015-8) [X-015].

## EV015-7 · Route 7 and the 817th over Cambodia · 10 May – 30 Sep 1970 · Route 7 from Krek to Snuol and Route 13 north to Kratie, Kampong Cham and Kratie Provinces, Cambodia; Bien Hoa air base · NEW · 015
*Initiative: allied*

When the incursion opened the Cambodian roads to the air, the Joint General Staff sent the 817th's gunships over them, and the war taught the wing what the dossier had said (EC015-10) [X-015]. From 10 May six Spooky IIs a night flew Route 7 and Route 13 in support of the ARVN's task forces and then of Lon Nol's battalions at Snuol and Kratie. The convoys of the PAVN 7th Division and the rear services of the B-2 Front moved at night on these roads in the open, and in the first month the wing's video counted about 140 trucks destroyed [X-015]. The calls from the Cambodian army came in French and Khmer through ARVN liaison officers, and twice in June the gunships fired on Cambodian positions given as enemy.

The roads were defended. The PAVN moved 12.7mm and 37mm guns onto Route 7 in June, and on the night of 14 August Spooky 34, orbiting at 7,000 feet over a convoy near Snuol, was hit by a 37mm battery and went down in the rubber east of the town; four of its thirteen crewmen were recovered by an ARVN Ranger company the next day [X-015]. On 2 September Khiem asked the Joint General Staff to withdraw the squadron to the Delta, where the posts needed it and the guns were few, and on 30 September it came home [X-015]. The gunship was a weapon of the South's posts, and it died over defended roads.

Through the rest of 1973 the Front's districts in the Delta made their night attacks on posts beyond the wing's reach, as they had learned to do from the Ghostriders nine years earlier (F015-5), and the posts the Americans had covered and the Vietnamese did not, about a third of them, were lost at nearly history's rate (F015-19) [X-015].

=== NEW P6
## EV015-8 · The rice war of Chuong Thien · 15 Mar – 30 Jun 1974 · Long My, Kien Hung and Kien Thien Districts, Chuong Thien Province; the U Minh edge of An Xuyen; Binh Thuy air base · NEW · 015
*Initiative: enemy*

The campaign was the Front's own and it had an author. In February 1974 the Military Region 9 Party Committee, short of rice after a 1973 harvest it had not been able to tax and with its main-force regiments living on half rations in the U Minh, decided on its own reckoning, before Hanoi's directives for the year reached it, to open a campaign against the posts of Chuong Thien in the dry season: to take the posts that guarded the canals, collect the spring harvest in the villages behind them, and feed its regiments to the monsoon [X-015]. It was a decision about rice, not about the Americans, who were gone, or about the gunships, which the region's military staff had not reckoned as a factor; its estimate in January held that the VNAF's aircraft were grounded for want of fuel, as most of its A-1s were [X-015].

It was wrong about the gunships. The appropriation of 1974 had grounded about a third of the VNAF, but the Joint General Staff had kept the 817th's Binh Thuy detachment and its fuel on the first line of the ration, on the evidence of 1973 [X-015]. From the night of 15 March the D2 and 10th Battalions and the provincial battalions attacked the canal posts of Kien Hung and Long My, one or two a night. The Spooky IIs answered about two calls in three, through cloud when the cloud came in May, on the district towns' beacons; the 21st Division's guns at Vi Thanh and the district platoons answered the rest [X-015]. Of 64 posts attacked between March and June, about 46 held, against history's season in which Chuong Thien lost most of the posts the Front attacked [X-015]. The 817th lost one aircraft, to a 12.7mm gun on the Cai Lon on 11 May, with seven of its thirteen crewmen.

The region did not get its rice. The harvest behind the posts that held, about 15,000–25,000 tons in the three districts by the province's estimate, was sold to the government's buyers in Vi Thanh at the subsidised price, and the piaster the farmers were paid was worth about a third less than in January because of the year's inflation, which the province chief could do nothing about (DIRMT01-11) [X-015]. The PF platoons whose families lived inside the canal posts, as most did, stayed with them; the posts that fell were the ones whose families had been moved to Vi Thanh in 1973 [X-015]. In July the region asked COSVN for a main-force division to finish the campaign, and was told that the divisions were wanted for the dry season of 1974–75 in the east.

The 821st Squadron at Da Nang flew over these fights too, and it met the limit the dossier had named. At Thuong Duc the 304th Division's regiment brought its own anti-aircraft battalion, 37mm guns and SA-7 teams, to the hills around the town; the Spooky IIs flew four nights over the trenches in late July from 10,000 feet, firing 105mm on radar offsets with too little precision to stop the digging, and on 3 August one was hit by a 37mm battery and went down west of the town with all thirteen of its crew [X-015]. The Joint General Staff withdrew the squadron from Thuong Duc that day, and the town fell on 7 August as told above: what absorbed the gunship was the regiment's air defence, which the PAVN brought with its divisions and the Front's local forces never had (DIRMT01-4) [X-015]. On the heights of Hill 1062 in September and at Phu Loc in October and November, where the monsoon cloud closed the hills, the 821st flew above the SA-7s on radar offsets from beacons the Airborne and the 1st Division carried forward, and broke three night assaults on the Airborne's positions in the open [X-015]. I judge the gunships saved perhaps 200–400 of the Republic's dead in I Corps in 1974, resting on those nights and on the posts of the lowlands behind them, and that they changed no position's fate [X-015].

The 819th Squadron's Spooky IIs flew over the siege of Pleiku from Nha Trang every night from 21 March, and they were the reason the defence's best fight was fought at night as well as by day.
