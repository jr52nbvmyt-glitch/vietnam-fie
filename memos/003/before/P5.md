# Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

1972 is the test of Vietnamization, and the answer it gives is exact: the Republic of Vietnam can stop a full PAVN conventional offensive with American air power and American advisers, and cannot do it without them. On 30 March the PAVN opens the largest offensive of the war, called the Nguyen Hue Offensive in Hanoi and the Easter Offensive in the West, with about fourteen divisions and hundreds of tanks [H] on three main fronts: across the DMZ into Quang Tri, from Laos and Cambodia into the Central Highlands at Kontum, and from Cambodia down Route 13 at An Loc towards Saigon [H64]. US ground strength has fallen to about 69,000 men, few of them combat troops, and is on its way to 27,000 [H64]. US air power returns in strength: B-52s and tactical aircraft over the battlefields of the South, and, for the first time since 1968, a bombing campaign against the North without most of Rolling Thunder's restrictions, with the mining of Haiphong.

The shape of the phase is a near-collapse in the spring and a recovery in the summer. Quang Tri Province falls on 1–2 May; Kontum and An Loc are besieged and held by narrow margins and B-52s; and by the autumn the ARVN, under better commanders, has retaken Quang Tri City. The PAVN loses most of the armour it committed and, by most estimates, about 100,000 men, killed and wounded, while keeping about a tenth of the South's territory, mostly thinly populated border regions in the west [H64]. The offensive creates almost a million refugees and kills more than 25,000 civilians [H64]. Its political result is the one Hanoi needed: a settlement on terms that leave PAVN troops in the South. The year ends with the October draft agreement, Thieu's refusal of it, and the eleven days of Linebacker II.

## EV000-74 · The Easter Offensive opens: the DMZ front · 30 Mar – 2 Apr 1972 · Quang Tri Province, from the DMZ to the Cua Viet and Camp Carroll · HIST · 000
At midday on 30 March 1972 PAVN artillery, including 130mm guns that outranged anything the ARVN had, opened on the firebases strung along the DMZ, and the 304th and 308th Divisions with armoured regiments attacked across the demarcation line, while the 324B came in from the A Shau and the west [H64] [H67]. In all, PAVN forces on the northern front were supported by more than 100 tanks, T-54s, T-34s and PT-76s [H64]. The front was held by the ARVN 3rd Division under Brigadier General Vu Van Giai, a new division formed in late 1971 from mixed and unsteady units, with the 147th and 258th Vietnamese Marine Brigades under its control [H67] [H]. Low cloud kept most air support away for the first days [H64].

The firebases fell back or were overrun. On 2 April the 56th Regiment at Camp Carroll, under Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, surrendered with about 1,500 men [H67]. That afternoon at Dong Ha, Captain John Ripley, US adviser to the 3rd Vietnamese Marine Battalion, spent about three hours under fire placing charges on the highway bridge over the Cua Viet, which was blown at 16:30 as PAVN tanks approached; the Marines held the river line [H67]. The PAVN halted to regroup and bring up supplies, and the 3rd Division, under fire from guns it could not reach, settled into a defence of Quang Tri City along the Thach Han and Cua Viet rivers.

## EV000-75 · Freedom Train, Pocket Money and Linebacker · 5 Apr – 23 Oct 1972 · North Vietnam, Haiphong harbour and the approaches to the DMZ · HIST · 000
On 5 April Nixon authorized strikes north of the 20th parallel, Operation Freedom Train, and on 10 April B-52s struck petroleum storage near Vinh [H68]. On 27 April and 13 May F-4s with laser-guided bombs dropped the span of the Thanh Hoa bridge that Rolling Thunder had failed to bring down, and on 10–11 May the Paul Doumer bridge at Hanoi was dropped the same way [H16] [H]. On 8 May (9 May in Vietnam) Nixon announced Operation Pocket Money, the mining of Haiphong and the other ports: Navy aircraft laid the mines, which were activated after five days, and about 11,000 more mines were sown in the secondary harbours and waterways over the following days [H68]. The mining of the northern ports, prohibited since 1965, closed the North's main entry point for Soviet supplies by sea.

Linebacker, from 10 May to 23 October, opened the North to bombing with targets chosen by the commanders rather than in Washington [H68]. On its first day, in 414 sorties, the heaviest air-to-air fighting of the war took place, and Lieutenant Randy Cunningham and Lieutenant (jg) William Driscoll became the first American aces of the war [H68]. Linebacker flew 41,653 missions and dropped 155,548 tons, and lost 134 aircraft, 104 in combat [H68]. Its effect was felt at the front: by a PAVN history only 30 percent of planned supplies reached its front-line units in May and June, the PAVN shelling of the South fell by half between 9 May and 1 June, and by September imports were 35–50 percent below the May level [H68]. The Soviet Union and China denounced the mining and did nothing; the Nixon–Brezhnev summit in Moscow went ahead from 22 May [H68] [H].

## EV000-76 · Loc Ninh and the siege of An Loc · 7 Apr – 18 Jun 1972 · Loc Ninh and An Loc, Binh Long Province, on Route 13 · HIST · 000
On 5 April the 5th Division of the PAVN B-2 Front attacked Loc Ninh, on Route 13 near the Cambodian border, and overran it on the afternoon of 7 April; the 9th Division took the Quan Loi base camp that evening, and the 7th Division cut Route 13 south of An Loc at the Tau O bridge with its 141st, 165th and 209th Regiments [H65]. An Loc, the provincial capital 100 km north of Saigon, was cut off by 8 April [H65]. It was held by the ARVN 5th Division under Brigadier General Le Van Hung, with the 3rd and 81st Ranger Groups, the 1st Airborne Brigade lifted in after failing to break through at Tau O, and the Regional Forces, Popular Forces and People's Self-Defence Force of Binh Long Province, all under III Corps (Lieutenant General Nguyen Van Minh) and advised by the American regional assistance command under Major General James Hollingsworth [H65].

The 9th Division assaulted on 13 April with tanks; in that first attack the defenders destroyed tanks with M72 LAWs at close range, and tank-killing teams were formed in every battalion [H65]. Further assaults followed in April and May. The town survived on airdrops, taken over by USAF C-130s on 19 April once anti-aircraft fire made helicopter landings impossible, and on air support, B-52s above all: on 11–12 May B-52s struck one mission every 55 minutes for 30 hours against the massed PAVN assault, with AC-119 and AC-130E gunships, A-37s and AH-1 Cobras [H65]. The 21st Division fought up Route 13 from Lai Khe [H65]. The PAVN was driven from the town by 12 June and III Corps declared the siege over on 18 June [H65] [H64]. US estimates of PAVN losses are 10,000 killed and 15,000 wounded and 36 tanks; Vietnamese figures are about 2,000 killed and 5,000 wounded [H65]. Brigadier General Richard Tallman, the deputy American commander, was mortally wounded by artillery on 9 July [H65]. An Loc lay in ruins. It became the symbol of an ARVN that could hold, if the B-52s came.

## EV000-77 · Tan Canh, Dak To and the battle of Kontum · 24 Apr – 9 Jun 1972 · Tan Canh, Dak To, Ben Het and Kontum city, Kontum Province · HIST · 000
In the Central Highlands the PAVN 320th Division began pressing the firebases on Rocket Ridge in early April, and on 24 April the 2nd Division's 1st and 66th Regiments, with the D10 Sapper Battalion and tanks, overran the ARVN bases at Tan Canh and Dak To II, held by the 42nd and 47th Regiments of the 22nd Division; the division "ceased to exist as a fighting unit", and its commander, Colonel Le Duc Dat, disappeared [H66]. The road to Kontum lay open. The PAVN paused for three weeks [H66]. John Paul Vann, the civilian director of the II Corps advisory effort, effectively directed the defence; he had Colonel Ly Tong Ba of the 23rd Division put in command of Kontum, and on 10 May Major General Nguyen Van Toan replaced Ngo Du at II Corps [H64] [H66]. On 9 May at Ben Het, ARVN Rangers destroyed three PT-76s with BGM-71 TOW missiles, among the first combat uses of the weapon, and TOW-armed helicopters followed [H66].

The main assault on Kontum came on 14 May, by the 320th Division's 48th, 52nd and 64th Regiments and the 2nd Division [H66] [H64]. B-52 strikes, on 14 May and decisively on the night of 16–17 May, fell close to the ARVN lines and broke the momentum of the attack [H66]. From 29 May to 8 June the ARVN cleared the city bunker by bunker, and on 9 June Ba, now a brigadier general, declared Kontum secure [H66]. That night Vann's helicopter flew into a hillside in low cloud and he was killed; a Vietnamese account says it was shot down [H66]. US estimates put PAVN casualties in the Kontum battle at about 4,000 and in the Highlands campaign at 20,000–40,000, figures with no better basis than the body counts of earlier years [H66].

## EV000-78 · The fall of Quang Tri · 27 Apr – 2 May 1972 · Quang Tri City and Highway 1 south to the My Chanh River · HIST · 000
On 27 April the PAVN renewed its attack on the 3rd Division's defence of Quang Tri City [H]. Contradictory orders from the I Corps commander, Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam, and from Giai left units moving in opposite directions, and on 1 May Giai ordered the city abandoned [H67] [H]. The division broke. Soldiers and refugees fled south on Highway 1, and PAVN artillery fired on the column, killing roughly 2,000 soldiers and civilians on the road [H67]. Quang Tri Province was lost by 2 May; the Marines who held at the My Chanh River were the one coherent force left north of Hue [H64] [H]. On 2 May Thieu relieved Lam and gave I Corps to Lieutenant General Ngo Quang Truong, who stopped the rout, restored order in Hue and held the My Chanh line with the Marine and Airborne Divisions and the 1st Division [H64]. Giai was later court-martialled and sentenced to five years' imprisonment [H64]. The fall of Quang Tri was the first loss of a province to a PAVN conventional offensive, and Truong's recovery the clearest demonstration of what leadership was worth in the ARVN.

## EV000-79 · The recapture of Quang Tri City · 28 Jun – 16 Sep 1972 · Quang Tri Province south of the Thach Han River and the Quang Tri Citadel · HIST · 000
On 28 June Truong opened the counter-offensive, Lam Son 72, with the Airborne Division and the Marine Division under Brigadier General Bui The Lan, whose 147th, 258th and 369th Marine Brigades did most of the fighting for the city [H70]. Against them were the 304th, 308th, 320B and 325th Divisions with the 312th identified [H70]. US forces provided fire and movement but no ground troops: in July alone, 5,461 tactical sorties and 2,054 B-52 strikes; US Marine helicopters of HMM-164 and HMM-165 lifted the Vietnamese Marines into landing zones, losing several to SA-7 missiles; and the 9th Marine Amphibious Brigade and the Seventh Fleet staged amphibious feints [H70]. The PAVN held the Quang Tri Citadel, its walls about 15 feet high, with interlocking tunnels and positions; the Marines breached the southeast wall on 10–11 September, held the Citadel by the late afternoon of 15 September, and raised the flag over the west gate on 16 September after 138 days of occupation [H70]. The city was rubble [H70].

The Marine Division lost 977 killed of 3,658 casualties, and the Airborne Division's 2nd Brigade was badly depleted [H70]. South Vietnamese claims are 8,135 PAVN killed; Hanoi's are 4,000–10,000 [H70]. About 20 Americans were killed [H70]. The province north of the Thach Han remained in PAVN hands, and both sides were too exhausted to move further [H70]. The battle proved that the ARVN's best divisions, under a good commander and with unlimited American fire support, could defeat the PAVN in a set-piece battle. Every one of those conditions was American or rare.

## EV000-80 · The last American ground combat units leave · Aug 1972 · South Vietnam · HIST · 000
In August 1972 the last US ground combat battalion in South Vietnam, the 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry of the 196th Brigade, stood down at Da Nang and left the country [H]. American advisers, aviation units, the air and naval forces in and around Vietnam, and a shrinking logistics command remained, and US strength fell to about 24,000 by the end of the year [H40]. US deaths by hostile action fell to 300 in 1972 [H40]. The withdrawal begun at Midway in 1969 had been completed for the ground war during the largest PAVN offensive of the conflict, and the defence of the South on the ground had become entirely the RVNAF's. US air power, at its 1972 peak, was the part of the American commitment that remained.

## EV000-81 · The October agreement and Thieu's refusal · 8 Oct – 13 Dec 1972 · Paris, Saigon and Washington · HIST · 000
On 8 October Le Duc Tho presented Kissinger in Paris with a draft that dropped Hanoi's demand for Thieu's removal: a ceasefire in place, the withdrawal of all US forces within 60 days, the return of prisoners, the Thieu government to remain alongside the PRG pending a political settlement through a council of reconciliation, and no requirement for PAVN forces to leave the South [H51] [H]. Kissinger accepted it as the basis for an agreement. Thieu rejected it: it left the PAVN in his country and gave the PRG standing, and he had not been consulted in its drafting [H51] [H]. On 26 October Kissinger announced that "peace is at hand" [H]. On 7 November Nixon was re-elected, carrying 49 states [H]. Washington then sent Saigon a mass of equipment under Operation Enhance Plus, aircraft, armour and artillery, to be in the country before a ceasefire froze replacement at one-for-one, and Nixon gave Thieu private written assurances that the United States would respond with full force to a major violation [H] [H51].

In November and December Kissinger brought Saigon's amendments to Paris, Hanoi hardened its own terms in reply, and the main round of talks broke down on 13 December [H69]. Nixon, concerned that the new Congress in January would cut off the war's funding, gave Hanoi an ultimatum [H69].

## EV000-82 · Linebacker II · 18–29 Dec 1972 · Hanoi, Haiphong and their surroundings · HIST · 000
On 18 December B-52s attacked Hanoi and Haiphong for the first time in the war [H69]. In eleven days, with a 36-hour pause at Christmas, 741 B-52 sorties were dispatched and 729 completed against rail yards at Kinh No, Lang Dang and Yen Vien, power plants including Thai Nguyen, petroleum storage, airfields and SAM sites, dropping about 15,237 tons, and fighter-bombers added about 5,000 tons [H69]. The B-52s flew fixed routes and timings on the first nights and suffered for it [H]. North Vietnam fired 266 SA-2s over the eleven days [H69]. The United States acknowledged 15 B-52s shot down, and some authors argue for 22–27 counting damaged aircraft written off; 12 tactical aircraft were lost; 33 B-52 crewmen were killed or missing, 33 captured and 26 rescued [H69]. By the last nights US commanders judged the North's SA-2 stocks nearly exhausted and its air defences largely suppressed [H].

North Vietnam's official count of civilian dead is 1,624, 1,328 in Hanoi and 306 in Haiphong [H69]. The Bach Mai hospital was struck on 22 December, and on 26 December bombs on Kham Thien Street killed 278 by Hanoi's count [H69]. About 80 percent of the North's electrical generating capacity was put out of action [H69]. On 30 December Nixon halted the bombing north of the 20th parallel, and Hanoi agreed to resume talks, which reopened on 9 January 1973 [H69]. Whether the bombing forced Hanoi back to terms or merely covered the American acceptance of terms close to October's is disputed; Le Duan later said it had "completely obliterated our economic foundation" [H69].

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1972 the war is on the verge of a settlement that both governments in Vietnam regard as provisional. The Easter Offensive has failed to destroy the ARVN or the Thieu government, at a cost to the PAVN of most of the armour it committed (by one count 134 T-54s, 56 PT-76s and 60 T-34s) and about 100,000 men [H64]. The ARVN has held at An Loc and Kontum and retaken Quang Tri City, but has lost Quang Tri north of the Thach Han, the western Highlands and the border areas of III Corps, and its losses are given variously from about 10,000 killed to about 39,000 dead in the period [H64]. Almost a million South Vietnamese have been made refugees, about 600,000 of them in government camps [H64].

The lesson both sides draw is the same. The ARVN held where American air power was decisive, at An Loc and Kontum, and where American advisers coordinated it; it broke where its leadership failed, at Quang Tri and Tan Canh. The settlement about to be signed removes the American advisers and the American air power and leaves the PAVN in the South, while the North's air defences and economy recover. Hanoi needs only to rebuild, wait for the Americans to be gone, and choose its time.
