## EV003-1 · Operation TIDE TABLE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · The II Corps coast from Qui Nhon to Cam Ranh Bay (Binh Dinh, Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa Provinces) and the Ca Mau coast of An Xuyen Province · NEW · 003
The first Global Hawk orbit went up from Clark Air Base on 6 January 1964 and took station off the II Corps coast, about 60 nautical miles out between Qui Nhon and Cam Ranh Bay, with its radar's maritime mode sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep (EC003-3) [M003]. The operation was named TIDE TABLE. Its quarry was the steel-hulled trawlers of about 100 tons run south from Haiphong and the Do Son peninsula by the PAVN's maritime transport group, Group 759, which became Group 125 of the PAVN Navy in the course of 1964 [H]. Its hunters were few: the Vietnamese Navy's Sea Force, with a handful of PC-461-class submarine chasers and PCE escorts out of Qui Nhon and Nha Trang, the motorised and sail junks of the Junk Force's coastal divisions, and, when the Seventh Fleet lent them, a US destroyer or two off the coast [H]. Before January nobody had been looking for the trawlers at all; the coast is about 2,000 kilometres long and carried tens of thousands of fishing junks [M-TNA].

The first track came on the night of 18–19 February. A contact moving at a steady ten knots on a course that had begun in the Gulf of Tonkin two days earlier, running 80 nautical miles offshore past Quang Ngai, turned west at dusk toward the Phu Yen coast; it did not stop, did not fish and did not change speed [X-003]. The exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut flagged it at 1940, and the Vietnamese Navy's coastal command at Nha Trang sent a PC-461 from her patrol line off Tuy Hoa. The radar held the trawler for seven hours, so the patrol craft did not have to search; she had only to steam. She came up with the trawler at 0310 about four miles off Vung Ro Bay, under a low overcast and in a moderate northeast swell. The trawler's crew ran her toward the beach and fired her demolition charges, as Group 759's standing orders required; the forward hold blew, the after hold did not [H] [X-003]. A boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of cargo before the fire reached it: Chinese Type 56 carbines and assault rifles, B-40 (RPG-2) launchers and their rockets, 82mm mortar rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm ammunition [X-003]. Seven of the crew were taken from the water, two of them wounded; nine died in the explosion or drowned [X-003]. The Vietnamese lost one sailor killed and four wounded to small-arms fire from the beach, where a VC reception party had waited with porters [X-003].

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven contacts that the cell judged to be trawlers, on the II Corps coast and on the Ca Mau peninsula, where the landings went into the mangrove creeks of the Bo De and the Cua Lon [X-003]. Four were intercepted: two scuttled or blew up, one was taken nearly intact off Binh Dinh in April by the destroyer USS *Edsall*'s boarding party with a Vietnamese liaison officer, and one ran into the Bo De ahead of a junk division and landed most of its cargo [X-003]. Six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon, which the radar saw as a reversal of course at about the time the trawler's own radar would have detected the patrol [X-003]. One landed unobserved in May, on a night when the orbit's aircraft had diverted to Clark with a generator fault and the relief was four hours late [X-003]. The casualty figures above are counts of bodies and prisoners and are reliable; the cargo tonnages are estimates from what was recovered and from the prisoners' statements of their loads, and carry a margin of about a third [E: recovery and interrogation].

The prisoners told the rest. Interrogated at Nha Trang, the crew of the April capture described the landing points, the signal lamps, the reception parties and about two years of earlier voyages, and the Vietnamese Navy found two of the landing beaches in Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa from their directions [X-003]. TIDE TABLE did not starve the Front; the tonnage it stopped in six months was a small fraction of what moved by sea and through Laos. What it ended was the cheapest and most direct route by which heavy weapons reached the coastal provinces of II Corps and the lower Delta, and it ended it by being seen, which is the subject of the next event in this chapter (EV003-2) (F003-17).

## EV000-3 · The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution · 2–10 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 30–31 July 1964 South Vietnamese commando boats under the covert OPLAN 34A programme, run by MACV's Studies and Observations Group, raided the North Vietnamese islands of Hon Me and Hon Ngu [H7]. On 2 August the destroyer USS *Maddox*, on a DESOTO signals intelligence patrol under Captain John J. Herrick, was attacked in daylight by three P-4 torpedo boats of the 135th Torpedo Squadron [H7]. The *Maddox* fired, four F-8 Crusaders from USS *Ticonderoga* attacked the retiring boats, and the North Vietnamese lost four killed and six wounded with three boats damaged; the *Maddox* was struck by one machine-gun round and suffered no casualties [H7]. Johnson, as historically, chose a warning rather than retaliation, and ordered the patrol resumed with USS *Turner Joy* in company [H7].

At Admiral Sharp's request I moved the Global Hawk orbit from the II Corps coast to the Gulf on 3 August (EC003-7). There were no SA-2s in the North and nothing that could reach 60,000 feet, and the aircraft took station about 40 nautical miles east of Hon Me, with the destroyers, the coast from Hon Matt to the mouth of the Song Ma and the open Gulf to the east in its radar's maritime mode [M003]. On the night of 4 August the *Maddox* and *Turner Joy*, in rough weather with thunderstorms, fired for about two hours at radar contacts they took to be a second attack [H7]. The watch in the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut that night was Major Harold Kessling's. His radar log showed the two destroyers and their manoeuvres, a cluster of fishing junks about 30 nautical miles to the west, rain cells moving across the Gulf, and no fast small craft within 40 nautical miles of the ships at any time between 2000 and 2330 local [X-003]. P-4 boats making attack runs at 40 knots are good radar targets even in a sea state of three and four, and the radar had tracked the three boats of 2 August from their base on the same set [X-003]. Herrick cabled that the "entire action leaves many doubts" [H7]. Kessling's report reached MACV at about midnight Saigon time and CINCPAC an hour later, which in Washington was the late morning of 4 August, while McNamara and Sharp were still trying to establish what had happened [X-003].

What followed was Washington's decision, and it went the other way from history. Historically Johnson had decided on retaliation within thirty minutes of the first reports [H7]. Here McNamara told him in the early afternoon that the ships' reports were contradicted by a radar that had watched the whole engagement, that Herrick himself doubted them, and that the Navy could not confirm an attack [X-003]. Johnson held the strike order and, at about 1900, cancelled it [X-003]. Operation Pierce Arrow was not flown. The 64 sorties against the torpedo-boat bases and the Vinh oil storage did not happen; Lieutenant (jg) Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Lieutenant (jg) Everett Alvarez was not shot down and taken prisoner that day [H] [X-003].

The resolution was sent anyway. Johnson had carried a draft since May, an election was three months away, and the attack of 2 August was real [H]. On 5 August he asked Congress for authority to repel armed attack against US forces and to prevent further aggression, citing the 2 August attack on a US destroyer in international waters and Hanoi's support of the war in the South, and he said nothing of the night of the 4th [X-003]. The debate ran three days longer than history's: Senator Wayne Morse, tipped about the 34A raids, asked why the destroyer had been near Hon Me at all, and Senator Gaylord Nelson's amendment against a land war was again declined by Senator Fulbright on the administration's assurance that no such war was intended [H] [X-003]. Congress passed the Southeast Asia Resolution on 10 August, 414–0 in the House and 85–3 in the Senate, in the words of the historical text [X-003]. Johnson signed it on 11 August. It was again the only legislative authority for the war that followed, but it rested on one attack that had happened rather than on two of which one had not, and the radar log of 4 August sat in the files at Tan Son Nhut and Honolulu as a record of a false alarm, not as the concealed proof of a pretext. B-57B bombers were moved to Bien Hoa in the aftermath [H3], within mortar range of the countryside around it.

## EV000-7 · The attack on Bien Hoa air base · 1 Nov 1964 · Bien Hoa air base, Bien Hoa Province · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On the night of 31 October–1 November 1964, two days before the American presidential election, a Viet Cong mortar team fired on Bien Hoa air base, about 30 km northeast of Saigon, for about thirty minutes [H2]. The B-57B Canberras moved there after the Tonkin crisis were parked wingtip to wingtip on the ramp. Five B-57Bs were destroyed and 13 damaged; with helicopters and other aircraft, as many as 30 aircraft were destroyed or damaged [H2] [H3]. Four Americans were killed and 72 people wounded [H2]. The attackers escaped.

In this war one of the rounds fell on the most expensive aircraft in the theater. The two U-2S were kept in open-fronted earth-and-sandbag revetments on the north side of the field, beside the exploitation station; the second aircraft was in maintenance that night [M003]. An 81mm round burst on the revetment wall, and fragments holed its left wing, the fuel cells inside it and the forward equipment bay [X-003]. No one in the detachment was hurt. The contractor field engineers patched the structure, and a replacement wing panel and sensor components came from the United States by C-133 [X-003]. The aircraft was out of service for seven weeks, from 1 November to 20 December, and for those seven weeks the theater had one U-2S, one sortie on most days and none when it was in maintenance [X-003]. The daily deep look over the Laotian panhandle, where the first trucks of the dry season were moving, lapsed to twice a week [X-003].

It was the first of at least 475 attacks on the primary air bases in South Vietnam during the war [H2]. Ambassador Taylor and the Joint Chiefs pressed for reprisal bombing of the North; Johnson, on the eve of the election, declined [H]. I asked for and got a covered, steel-arched shelter for each U-2S, built by the Navy's Seabees by February 1965, and a Regional Forces company for the hamlets of the Bien Hoa and Di An approaches where the mortar team had set up [X-003]. The Global Hawks at Clark were untouched, which was why they were there (EC003-4). The attack exposed what American airpower in South Vietnam would cost: every base needed a ground perimeter that the ARVN could not or would not hold, and the question of who would guard the bases was the question that would bring the first US combat troops ashore four months later.

## EV003-2 · The trawlers turn back; the Sihanoukville route opens · Sep 1964 – Jun 1965 · Haiphong and the Gulf of Tonkin; the II Corps coast; Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Cambodia · NEW · 003
Hanoi did not give up the sea at once. After TIDE TABLE's first half-year Group 125 paused through the summer of 1964, and in September, while the Global Hawk orbit was still held in the Gulf of Tonkin after the August crisis, it sent two trawlers south [X-003]. One landed its cargo at Vung Ro on the night of 21–22 September in a gap the Gulf watch had opened on the II Corps coast; the other was tracked off Binh Dinh after the orbit returned, and was intercepted on 9 November by the Vietnamese Navy escort *Chi Lang* and a Junk Force division off the Tam Quan coast, where her crew beached and burned her [X-003]. Sixteen of her crew were killed or drowned and four captured; the junk sailors lost two killed [X-003]. Those are counts, and reliable. In December a third trawler reversed course off Quang Ngai at dusk when a Seventh Fleet destroyer closed her, and went home [X-003].

The decision to change routes was taken in Hanoi in December 1964 and January 1965. Party records captured later in this war describe the Central Military Commission's conclusion that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night" and that the route was no longer worth the ships and the men [X-003]. The alternative was already prepared. Sihanouk, who had renounced American aid in November 1963 and was moving toward China, would break diplomatic relations with Washington on 3 May 1965 [H]. Through the winter Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators in Phnom Penh arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville, the port the French had built at Kompong Som, with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and Cambodian trucking contractors to carry the cargo inland along Route 4 to Kompong Speu and thence to the border base areas [H] [X-003]. Historically this arrangement took shape in late 1966 [H]. Here the first arms cargo for the Front came ashore at Sihanoukville under a Chinese flag in late June 1965, about eighteen months early, and was trucked to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak by night [X-003].

Two things followed from it that did not happen historically. The trawler that an Army helicopter pilot, Lieutenant James Bowers, found by chance unloading at Vung Ro Bay on 16 February 1965, with about 100 tons of arms aboard, was not there to be found [H] [X-003]; there was no trawler on the II Corps coast that month. And Market Time, which the Navy opened on 11 March 1965 historically because of Vung Ro [H], opened on the same date here because of TIDE TABLE's record, with a picture of what it was looking for and, increasingly, nothing to find [X-003]. The weapons the sea route would have landed in 1965 still reached the Front, more slowly and by a longer road, and from mid-1965 the enemy's principal line of supply to the southern half of the country ran through a neutral port that no American could stop or seize (F003-3).

## EV000-15 · The Thanh Hoa Bridge and the northern air defences · 3 Apr – 27 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong), Thanh Hoa Province, and the Hanoi region · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 3 April 1965 a 79-aircraft package led by Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner, 46 F-105s with F-100 escorts, RF-101Cs and KC-135 tankers, attacked the Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa; 16 F-105s carried AGM-12 Bullpup missiles and the rest 750 lb bombs [H16]. The bridge was hit repeatedly and stood; traffic stopped for a few hours; an F-100 and an RF-101 were lost [H16]. On 4 April 48 F-105s returned with 750 lb bombs. Eight MiG-17s of the VPAF 921st Fighter Regiment, half acting as decoys, attacked the F-105s and shot down two, killing Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson; a third F-105, Captain Carlyle Harris's, was lost and its pilot captured [H16]. The VPAF lost three MiG-17 pilots that day; how each was lost is disputed [H16]. The bridge, struck by more than 300 bombs, still stood; it would stand until laser-guided bombs dropped its span in 1972 [H16]. In this war the U-2S's radar pass over Ham Rong at dawn on 5 April told CINCPAC what the RF-101s had been lost trying to see: both spans standing, the approaches cratered, and rail traffic moving again within the day [X-003].

The missiles were seen coming. On 22 March the U-2S's radar change detection showed a new clearing southeast of Hanoi with the six-pointed pattern of revetments and access roads that every Soviet SA-2 site follows, and the ASIP signals payload heard the first emissions of the SNR-75 Fan Song fire-control radar on 18 April [X-003]. By 24 July the U-2S had mapped eleven prepared sites around Hanoi and Haiphong and judged, from emissions and vehicle movements, that four of them were occupied on any given day [X-003]. The Joint Chiefs asked to strike the sites under construction; McNamara and his deputies declined, as they had historically, for fear of killing Soviet technicians [H] [X-003]. On 24 July an SA-2 Guideline missile shot down an F-4C of the 47th Tactical Fighter Squadron near the munitions depot at Dien Bien Phu, the first US aircraft lost to a surface-to-air missile; Captain Roscoe Fobair was killed and Captain Richard Keirn captured [H9] [H]. The flight had been warned that morning that a site west of Hanoi was occupied; the missile came from a battery that had moved overnight [X-003].

The retaliation, Operation Spring High on 27 July, went differently. Historically the F-105s were sent against two sites, at least one of them empty, through guns massed around them, and lost aircraft for nothing [H]. Here the U-2S's pass of 26 July showed the eastern site with dummy launchers of bamboo and its missiles gone, and the guns doubled around it; the strike was re-targeted on the one occupied site, went in low from the west, destroyed two launchers and the Fan Song van, and lost two F-105Ds, one pilot killed and one recovered [X-003]. Within five months the North's air defence had become an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles, and the restricted zone around Hanoi had become its sanctuary; what had changed was that every part of it was on a map in Saigon before it fired.

## EV003-3 · Operation STEADY COUNT · 6 Dec 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes; Routes 23, 911 and 912 south to Tchepone; the Laotian panhandle · NEW · 003
STEADY COUNT was the first dry season of the panhandle watch (EC003-5), and it was named for what it did best. On 6 December 1965, the day Tiger Hound opened its strikes in the southern panhandle [H], the Global Hawk orbit moved from the II Corps coast to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the Laotian panhandle west of Tchepone, outside the guns and beyond the reach of anything the North could fly [M003]. From 60,000 feet its radar watched the roads that Group 559 had cut through the Annamite Chain that year: Route 23 south from the Mu Gia pass, Routes 911 and 912 from Ban Karai, and the fords and truck parks along the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Banghiang down to Tchepone and Route 9 [H] [M003]. The northeast monsoon gave Laos its dry season, and low cloud lay on the passes most mornings in January; the radar saw through it.

The engagement ran through three headquarters, and its weakness was in the joints. A track of six or more vehicles moving south at night went from the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut to the 2nd Air Division's control centre, from 1 April the Seventh Air Force's, and to the airborne command post, which passed it to whatever was in the air: A-1E Skyraiders of the 602nd Air Commando Squadron, B-57Bs, F-100Ds and F-105Ds on armed reconnaissance, and, from February, AC-47 gunships [H] [X-003]. Strikes in Laos needed the rules approved by Ambassador William Sullivan in Vientiane, which allowed armed reconnaissance on the motorable roads [H]. Of about 1,900 tracks passed in the five months, aircraft reached about one in six within forty minutes, found and attacked about one in ten, and destroyed or damaged trucks in about one in twenty [X-003]. The pilots claimed about 560 trucks destroyed; the cell's own count, from tracks that stopped and did not move again and from the U-2S's radar imagery the next day, was about 210 [X-003]. Nine US aircraft were lost over the panhandle in STEADY COUNT's sectors, most of them to 37mm guns that Group 559 moved to the passes from January; seven crewmen were killed or missing [X-003]. The drivers, porters and road troops killed are unknown, and any figure for them would be invented.

What the counts showed was the war's arithmetic. Southbound truck movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 a night at the peak in March, and the radar measured what came out at the southern end, on the roads toward Route 9 and the A Shau [X-003]. On 12 April 1966 B-52s struck the Mu Gia pass for the first time [H]; the orbit watched the first trucks move through the craters about twenty hours later [X-003]. By February the drivers had learned the orbit's habits: traffic fell by half on the nights when the aircraft was late on station and rose in the hours of its changeover, and the road troops began to trellis the open stretches with bamboo and living trees (F003-4) [X-003]. MACV J-2 estimated, from the tracks, that about 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle at the dry season's peak, and that this was more than the forces in the South required from the North [X-003]. It was the first time any headquarters had counted the Trail rather than estimating it, and the count said that the bombing raised the toll on the transit without lowering what arrived (POSTNA-4).

## EV000-27 · The POL strikes · 29 Jun 1966 · Hanoi and Haiphong petroleum storage areas · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 28 June 1966, after weeks of postponement for weather and leaks, McNamara asked the President for authority to strike the two principal petroleum, oil and lubricant storage installations in the Hanoi–Haiphong area; Johnson, worried among other things about hitting a Soviet tanker in Haiphong harbour, assented, and the strikes went in on 29 June [H44]. McNamara's argument was that the North's ability to support the war in the South depended on its fuel [H44]. The strikes destroyed most of the bulk storage the North then had [H].

In this war the argument against them was on the table before they flew, and it was the U-2S's. Its imagery of May and June showed 55-gallon drums stacked in their thousands under trees along the roads south of Hanoi, small buried tanks at the river crossings, and tank barges moored in the creeks of the delta: the dispersal had begun before the decision was taken [X-003]. MACV J-2 sent the photographs to CINCPAC on 14 June with the judgment that the bulk farms were no longer the system [X-003]. The decision was the President's and it did not change; the target was in the Rolling Thunder programme, and a better photograph of it did not alter the theory that had chosen it (F003-10). The U-2S's radar and camera pass on 30 June showed the Duc Giang farm at Hanoi and the Thuong Ly farm at Haiphong burning [X-003]. Over the next four weeks the Gulf watch counted the trucks on Route 1A and the coastal road south of Thanh Hoa, and found no fall in their number [X-003]. On 1 August J-2 reported to CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs that the POL campaign had not measurably reduced movement to the South, about three months before the intelligence community reached the same judgment historically [H] [X-003].

Imports through Haiphong continued [H]. It was the most important target system Rolling Thunder was allowed to attack before 1967, and the clearest demonstration that an agrarian economy supplied by its allies could absorb strategic bombing directed at a modern economy's vulnerabilities. What changed was when Washington knew it. McNamara had the measured result before the Jason summer study of August 1966 reported to him [H], and his doubts about the bombing, which historically hardened through the autumn, hardened in August instead [X-003]. The bombing went on; it was not this headquarters' to stop, and the President did not stop it.

## EV003-4 · The Sihanoukville count · Jul 1966 – Nov 1967 · Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Route 4 and Kompong Speu, and the roads to the base areas of the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak, Cambodia; Tan Son Nhut and Washington · NEW · 003
By the summer of 1966 the sea route that TIDE TABLE had closed had been a year in Cambodia, and the argument over it was the oldest in the intelligence community. Historically MACV J-2 held that most of the arms for the war in III and IV Corps came through Sihanoukville, and the CIA held that the port's traffic was mostly rice and contraband, until the records of the Hak Ly Trucking Company, seized after Lon Nol's coup in 1970, showed about 21,600 tons of arms and ammunition moved through the port between December 1966 and April 1969 [H]. Here the argument was settled by counting, in the first half of 1967.

The count had three sources. The U-2S photographed the port obliquely from the Gulf of Siam, from outside Cambodian airspace, about twice a month: ships alongside, their flags, cargo on the quays and trucks in the marshalling area [X-003]. The Global Hawk, on the nights the orbit could be spared from the coast, stood off over Tay Ninh and Binh Long inside South Vietnamese airspace and watched the road traffic up to 150–200 kilometres into Cambodia: convoys of ten to thirty trucks moving at night from Kompong Speu and Kompong Cham toward Snoul, Mimot and the Fishhook, and toward Svay Rieng and the Parrot's Beak, stopping at depots that did not appear on any Cambodian map [M003] [X-003]. And from May 1967 the reconnaissance teams of MACV's Studies and Observations Group, crossing the border under Operation Daniel Boone, were sent to look at the points where the convoys stopped [H] [X-003]. The radar did not see what the trucks carried. The teams, twice, found ammunition crates with Chinese markings in the depots the tracks had led them to; one team of three Americans and nine Montagnards was lost near Mimot in September 1967 [X-003].

On 9 March 1967 MACV J-2 and the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence agreed a joint estimate: that between July 1965 and February 1967 about 9,000–13,000 tons of military cargo had moved from Sihanoukville to the border base areas, and that the route supplied most of the arms and ammunition of the PAVN and VC forces in III and IV Corps [X-003]. Lieutenant Colonel Harold Kessling's cell had built the count night by night for twenty months. The Joint Chiefs proposed in April a naval quarantine of Sihanoukville and air strikes on the depots; the President declined both, and Sihanouk's neutrality, and the bar on operations into Cambodia, stood [H] [X-003]. The estimate changed no rule. It changed the argument: from 1967 the war's central sanctuary was a measured fact, and the case for and against relief of the Cambodian constraint was made from evidence, three years before history made it from captured ledgers (F003-17).

## EV000-39 · The barrier and Muscle Shoals · 8 Sep – Dec 1967 · The DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand · HIST-ALT · 000,003
In 1966 the Jason Division of the Institute for Defense Analyses, at McNamara's request, studied an anti-infiltration barrier of mines and sensors, and on 15 September 1966 McNamara placed Lieutenant General Alfred D. Starbird at the head of a Defense Communications Planning Group to build it [H43]. In this war the Jason study began from a count: the track data of STEADY COUNT and its second dry season, which showed where the trucks moved in the open, where they vanished under canopy, and how fast traffic recovered after a strike (EV003-3) [X-003]. Its conclusion was historical, that bombing alone could not stop the flow and that sensors were needed to find what the radar could not see [H43] [X-003]. Johnson authorized construction on 13 January 1967 [H43]. Its eastern, conventional part, a cleared strip with strongpoints at Gio Linh, Con Thien and the bases west of them, was built under fire by the Marines in 1967; its western, electronic part across the Laotian panhandle was named Muscle Shoals on 8 September 1967 [H43] [H42].

Air-delivered seismic and acoustic sensors, the ADSID and the ACOUSID among them, developed in part from the Navy's sonobuoys, would be dropped by OP-2E Neptunes of the Navy's VO-67 squadron and relayed by EC-121R aircraft to an Infiltration Surveillance Center, Task Force Alpha, at Nakhon Phanom in Thailand, where IBM 360 computers analysed the signals [H43]. The sensor strings were laid where the radar's tracks ended: at the edges of the trellised stretches on Routes 911 and 912, where the trucks went under the trees, and at the fords where they came out again [X-003]. Task Force Alpha took the Global Hawk's tracks by teletype from the cell at Tan Son Nhut from its first day, so that its plotters saw a convoy in the open on the radar, lost it under canopy, and picked it up again on the sensor strings [X-003]. Field testing began in southeastern Laos on 25 November 1967 [H43]. Construction cost $1–1.7 billion and operation about $1 billion a year [H43]. The system, renamed Igloo White in 1968, would find trucks on the Trail at night as nothing before had, under the trees where the radar could not; whether it reduced what arrived in the South is a question for later phases.

## EV000-41 · The siege of Khe Sanh · 21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province; Route 9 in Laos to Ban Houei Sane · HIST-ALT · 000,003
In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region [H46]. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion [H46].

In this war the concentration was watched from the day it began, and the watching is the story of this event. From mid-December the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos and the trucks of Group 559 turning east from Tchepone toward Ban Houei Sane, about 20–25 a night rising to about 80 by mid-January [X-003]. The U-2S's signals payload fixed the headquarters of the 325C Division northwest of Hill 881 North on 2 January and that of the 304th near Co Roc, across the border, on 11 January, and from the volume and pattern of their traffic the J-2 analysts identified a front headquarters forming above them [X-003]. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration this command had ever held. Every element of it was true. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", read it as confirmation, and it was hard to read otherwise [H46] [X-003]. The radar plots went every morning to Saigon and every evening to the White House, where the President kept a terrain model of Khe Sanh in the Situation Room; the counts of trucks on Route 9 went into his daily briefing [H] [X-003]. The 1st Cavalry Division moved north to I Corps in January, as it did historically, and in this war the movement picture was one of the arguments for sending it [H] [X-003].

The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that would attack the cities on 30–31 January moved into them in small groups, on foot and by bus and sampan among the millions travelling home for Tet, under the eyes of their own people; the Global Hawk, on the nights the second orbit was over the Delta, recorded the holiday traffic on the canals of Dinh Tuong and Long An as about three times the usual, which is what the holiday traffic was every year (F003-16) [X-003]. Nothing in the radar picture warned of Saigon, Hue or the provincial towns. General Frederick Weyand's decision of 10 January to pull fifteen battalions back toward Saigon rested, as it did historically, on signals and ground intelligence about the enemy's units in III Corps, and the radar added nothing to it [H] [X-003]. I do not say the picture caused the American fixation on Khe Sanh, which history had without it. I say it deepened it, and it made the war on the border the war the headquarters and the White House could see.

The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump [H]. The answer was Operation Niagara: intelligence collection by sensors and reconnaissance, followed by a campaign in which about 60 B-52s attacked on an average day and more than 100,000 tons of bombs fell by mid-April, with more than 158,000 artillery rounds fired in the first three months [H46]. Here Niagara's target boxes came from the U-2S's fixes and the radar plots of the parks and supply roads in the valleys west of the base as well as from the sensor strings and the reconnaissance flights, and the B-52s struck truck parks on Route 9 in Laos that the radar had seen fill during the night [X-003].

On the night of 6–7 February the panhandle watch tracked a column of eleven or twelve slow vehicles leaving the Ban Houei Sane area eastward on Route 9 at about 2250, moving at walking pace with infantry around them [X-003]. The cell flagged it as tracked vehicles at 2315, and the warning reached the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei, through the 5th Special Forces Group's operations centre at Da Nang, at about 2350 [X-003]. Twelve PT-76 amphibious tanks of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, attacked the camp at about 0030 on 7 February, the first PAVN tank attack of the war in the South [H46]. The forty minutes' warning put the camp at stand-to and its 106mm recoilless rifles and M72 LAWs manned when the tanks came out of the dark, and the defenders knocked out seven tanks rather than the historical five or more [X-003]. The camp still fell. Of about 500 CIDG some 160 were killed or missing and of 24 Americans eight were killed and eleven wounded, against about 200 and ten historically [X-003]; the improvement is an estimate, built on the historical figures, and I state it as such [H46]. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, much of it by C-130s using the Low Altitude Parachute Extraction System, and from late February the "Super Gaggle" of A-4s suppressing flak let helicopters supply the hill outposts [H46]. The air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters [H46].

Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April; it cost 92 Americans and 33 ARVN killed [H46]. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July; US intelligence counted 1,602 bodies and estimated 10,000–15,000 dead, while a secret MACV report estimated 5,550 to the end of March [H46]. I make no adjustment to them for better targeting; the B-52 boxes fell on better places, and what they killed there no one counted. Whether Hanoi ever meant to take the base, or used it to fix American attention on the border while the cities were attacked, is disputed to this day; a captured PAVN study of 1974 said the PAVN would have taken it if it could, at a price it was not willing to pay [H46]. In this war, if fixing American attention was the purpose, the best reconnaissance in the world had helped it.

## EV003-5 · The halt measured · 1 Nov – 31 Dec 1968 · The coastal road and railway from Thanh Hoa to Vinh and Dong Hoi, Route 15 to the Mu Gia pass, and the ferries at Quang Khe, North Vietnam · NEW · 003
The bombing of the North stopped on 1 November 1968 [H]. The Gulf watch did not. Its orbit, 40–60 nautical miles off the coast and outside the SA-2 belt, was a reconnaissance flight over international waters, and it went on counting the traffic on Route 1A, the coastal railway and Route 15 below the 19th parallel through November and December (EC003-7) [M003] [X-003]. What it saw changed within a week. Trucks that had moved only at night and in rain, in convoys of five or six, moved by day in columns of forty and fifty; the ferries at Quang Khe ran in daylight; the railway, repaired south of Thanh Hoa within three weeks, carried trains to Vinh [X-003]. Southbound vehicle movements on Route 15 toward the Mu Gia pass in December were about three times the October level [X-003].

The count went to Washington twice a week, as the bombing halt's terms required this command to report the North's use of it [H] [X-003]. It did not change the halt, which the President had traded for the opening of the expanded talks in Paris and which the President-elect did not reverse [H]. It changed the next campaign. When Commando Hunt opened on 15 November, its planners knew from the radar how much more was coming toward the passes, and when and where, and the U-2S found the new truck parks and the new fuel dumps north of Mu Gia that the surge required [X-003]. The halt was Hanoi's to use, and it used it exactly as expected; the difference was that the use was measured as it happened, and the headquarters that would fight the dry season of 1968–69 knew the size of it on its first night.

## EV000-55 · Commando Hunt begins · 15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads [H53]. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night [H53]. In this war the Global Hawk's panhandle watch was the campaign's third sensor from its first night: it saw the trucks in the open where the sensors could not hear them, and the AC-130s of the 16th Special Operations Squadron were sent to radar tracks as well as to sensor activations [X-003].

Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons [H53]. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000 [H53]. Here they were not believed by this headquarters either. The radar counted the trucks entering the panhandle at Mu Gia and Ban Karai and leaving it toward the South on Routes 92 and 96, and the difference between what went in and what came out, less what the radar saw stop and not move again, was the measure of what the bombing destroyed [X-003]. By that measure Phase I destroyed or disabled about 2,600–3,200 trucks and reduced the tonnage reaching the South by about a sixth, not the third the claims implied [X-003]. MACV J-2 sent that figure to Washington in May 1969 beside the Air Force's, and from then on the two numbers travelled together [X-003]. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it; this time the growth was counted.

## EV000-66 · The Cambodian incursion · 29 Apr – 30 Jun 1970 · The Parrot's Beak and the Fishhook, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 29 April 1970 ARVN forces of III and IV Corps under Lieutenant General Do Cao Tri attacked into the Parrot's Beak west of Saigon, and on 1 May the US 1st Cavalry Division and 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment, with ARVN airborne units, attacked into the Fishhook north of it; the 25th and 4th Infantry Divisions followed [H55]. In this war the attack went in on a map. Five years of the Sihanoukville count and the U-2S's radar change detection had located the depots where the convoys stopped, the road net that joined them and the clearings they hid in, and the 1st Cavalry's brigades were landed on the largest of them on the first two days rather than finding them by chance [X-003]. The great complex that the 1st Cavalry found on 4 May historically, and that the troops called "the City", was its objective on 1 May here [H] [X-003].

Nixon announced the incursion on television on 30 April as an attack on COSVN [H] [H55]. MACV had told CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs on 24 April that the U-2S had heard COSVN's transmitters move west and north out of the Fishhook from about 19 April, and advised against naming it as an objective [X-003]. The speech was written in Washington, and it named it [X-003]. COSVN had moved before the attack [H55]. The PAVN and VC mostly avoided battle, leaving rearguards, and withdrew westward [H55].

In the base areas the allies found the logistics of the war in the South: about 22,900 individual and 2,500 crew-served weapons, 7,000–8,000 tons of rice, about 1,800 tons of ammunition, 431 vehicles and 55 tons of medical supplies [H55]. Here most of it was found in the first ten days rather than through May and June, and the total was larger by about a tenth, because the depots on the map were searched first and the time left over went to the ones that were not [X-003]. The allies claimed 11,369 PAVN and VC killed and 2,509 captured [H55]; I make no adjustment to those claims, which carry the usual inflation (A13). US forces were out by 30 June; the ARVN stayed on [H55].

On 4 May Ohio National Guardsmen shot dead four students at Kent State University, and protests closed hundreds of campuses [H55] [H]. On 30 June the Senate passed the Cooper–Church amendment barring US ground forces from Cambodia [H55]. The incursion bought time: the loss of the caches and of Sihanoukville delayed the PAVN's recovery in III and IV Corps, and the southern half of the country was quieter for more than a year. It did not destroy the sanctuary. The PAVN rebuilt its supply routes, seized Attapeu and Saravane in Laos, opened a "Liberation Route" through northeastern Cambodia to the Mekong, and in 1972 would attack An Loc from Cambodia [H55]. The radar watched the Liberation Route being built from the summer of 1970, as it had watched Sihanoukville; it could not stop it either.

## EV000-68 · Commando Hunt and the growth of the Trail · 10 Oct 1970 – Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle and northeastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003
The dry-season phases of Commando Hunt were the main US effort against the North's logistics after 1968 [H53]. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks destroyed, a figure Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; in Phase VII the AC-130E gunships were credited with 7,335 of 10,689 trucks claimed [H53]. The CIA estimated that the entire North Vietnamese truck inventory was about 6,000, and a Defense Intelligence Agency model reached zero trucks remaining fourteen times in 1969 [H53]. Over the campaign the Air Force claimed 51,000 trucks and 3,400 anti-aircraft guns destroyed [H53].

In this war a second ledger stood beside the claims from 1969, and it was kept by the radar (EV000-55). Its Phase V figure was about 4,500–5,500 trucks destroyed or disabled, and its estimate of net loss to the throughput was about 8–10 percent, against the phases' own historical estimate falling to about 2 percent [H53] [X-003]. The DIA model's zero did not reach the Secretary of Defense's desk in 1969 because the radar showed, every night, the trucks that the model said did not exist [X-003]. The claims went on being made; the AC-130E crews were not lying, and a burning truck at night is easy to count twice. What changed was that no one in Washington or Saigon could believe them for long, and the Seventh Air Force's own reductions came a season earlier [X-003].

The Trail grew regardless. After the PAVN's offensives in Laos in 1970 its corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles; by 1970 a petroleum pipeline from Vinh reached towards the A Shau; the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads and paths with bulldozers and graders, and floated fuel drums down the rivers [H53]. The U-2S's radar mapped each new road within days of its cutting, and the Global Hawk counted its traffic; the count rose through 1971 to about 150–200 southbound truck movements a night at the dry season's peak [X-003]. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving (A13): the trucks destroyed rose, and what arrived in the South rose with them. In this war that sentence was written in 1969 by this headquarters, from its own counts, and not by historians. Over the war more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos [H53].

## EV000-71 · Lam Son 719 · 8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · HIST-ALT · 000,003
In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam [H56]. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak [H56]. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons [H56]; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units [H56]. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery [H56].

In this war the enemy's response was watched as it came, and it made the planning honest without making the operation succeed. From mid-January the U-2S heard the B-70 Corps headquarters come up on the air north of Route 9, and the panhandle watch tracked the 308th Division's vehicles and an armoured column moving south from the Ban Karai roads toward Route 92 [X-003]. MACV J-2's estimate on 4 February put the enemy's strength within reach of Tchepone at about 35,000–40,000 men and rising, about twice the historical estimate given to the planners [H] [X-003]. Abrams passed it to the Joint General Staff and to Washington. The operation went ahead on its date; its purpose and its timing were set in Washington and the Palace, and the estimate changed neither [X-003].

The advance stalled within days. Firebases north of Route 9 held by Rangers and Airborne were overrun or abandoned. On 25 February the radar tracked about twenty PAVN tanks, T-54s and PT-76s, forming up north of Fire Support Base 31 at about 0900, and strike aircraft cued on the tracks destroyed six before they reached the hill [X-003]. The base fell that afternoon all the same, when the forward air controller directing its air support was diverted to a downed F-4 and the remaining tanks came in behind the infantry [H] [X-003]. On 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began [H56]. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world [H56] [H]. Along Route 9 the radar showed the PAVN columns closing on the retreating units, and the B-52s and tactical aircraft were put on the roads behind them rather than on the jungle beside them [X-003].

The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April [H56]. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing, and 108 helicopters destroyed and 618 damaged [H56]. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded [H56]. The radar's tracks killed tanks and trucks the PAVN would otherwise have kept, perhaps 40–60 armoured vehicles over the six weeks against a historical toll that is itself disputed, and I do not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the helicopter landing zones and the command, not by what anyone could see [X-003]. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded" [H56]. Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after [H56]; the radar counted the rise.

## EV000-74 · The Easter Offensive opens: the DMZ front · 30 Mar – 2 Apr 1972 · Quang Tri Province, from the DMZ to the Cua Viet and Camp Carroll · HIST-ALT · 000,003
The armour was seen before it crossed. From 12 March the Gulf watch, standing off the Quang Tri coast outside the SA-2s that the North had moved into and just north of the DMZ, tracked tracked-vehicle columns moving south at night on Route 1A and Route 101 from Vinh Linh toward the Ben Hai, and the panhandle watch tracked others moving east along Route 9 in Laos toward Khe Sanh [X-003]. By 25 March the J-2 count of armoured vehicles in assembly areas within 20 kilometres of the DMZ was about 90–120, and MACV warned I Corps on 27 March of an armoured attack across the demarcation line within days [X-003]. Lieutenant General Lam, the I Corps commander, expected the main blow from the west, as most of the American and Vietnamese staffs did; the warning reached the 3rd Division, and the rotation of its regiments between firebases, which history records as under way when the attack fell, went ahead in part [H] [X-003].

At midday on 30 March 1972 PAVN artillery, including 130mm guns that outranged anything the ARVN had, opened on the firebases strung along the DMZ, and the 304th and 308th Divisions with armoured regiments attacked across the demarcation line, while the 324B came in from the A Shau and the west [H64] [H67]. In all, PAVN forces on the northern front were supported by more than 100 tanks, T-54s, T-34s and PT-76s [H64]. The front was held by the ARVN 3rd Division under Brigadier General Vu Van Giai, a new division formed in late 1971 from mixed and unsteady units, with the 147th and 258th Vietnamese Marine Brigades under its control [H67] [H]. Low cloud kept most air support away for the first days [H64]. It did not keep the radar away. The B-52s, which bombed by radar in any weather, were put on the crossing points of the Ben Hai and the roads south of Gio Linh where the Gulf watch showed the armour moving, and on 31 March and 1 April they struck three columns in the open; by our own estimate, about 20–30 armoured vehicles were destroyed or disabled before they reached the firebases [X-003].

The firebases fell back or were overrun all the same. On 2 April the 56th Regiment at Camp Carroll, under Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, surrendered with about 1,500 men [H67]. That afternoon at Dong Ha, Captain John Ripley, US adviser to the 3rd Vietnamese Marine Battalion, spent about three hours under fire placing charges on the highway bridge over the Cua Viet, which was blown at 16:30 as PAVN tanks approached; the Marines held the river line [H67]. The PAVN halted to regroup and bring up supplies, and the 3rd Division, under fire from guns it could not reach, settled into a defence of Quang Tri City along the Thach Han and Cua Viet rivers. The radar had seen the tanks; it had not made the 3rd Division's regiments steady or the 56th's commander stay.

## EV000-75 · Freedom Train, Pocket Money and Linebacker · 5 Apr – 23 Oct 1972 · North Vietnam, Haiphong harbour and the approaches to the DMZ · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 5 April Nixon authorized strikes north of the 20th parallel, Operation Freedom Train, and on 10 April B-52s struck petroleum storage near Vinh [H68]. On 27 April and 13 May F-4s with laser-guided bombs dropped the span of the Thanh Hoa bridge that Rolling Thunder had failed to bring down, and on 10–11 May the Paul Doumer bridge at Hanoi was dropped the same way [H16] [H]. On 8 May (9 May in Vietnam) Nixon announced Operation Pocket Money, the mining of Haiphong and the other ports: Navy aircraft laid the mines, which were activated after five days, and about 11,000 more mines were sown in the secondary harbours and waterways over the following days [H68]. The mining of the northern ports, prohibited since 1965, closed the North's main entry point for Soviet supplies by sea. The Gulf watch saw it close: the ships at anchor in the Haiphong roads that did not move, the lighters that tried the channels at night, and from June the trucks massing at the Chinese border crossings at Dong Dang and Lao Cai to replace the sea [X-003].

Linebacker, from 10 May to 23 October, opened the North to bombing with targets chosen by the commanders rather than in Washington [H68]. On its first day, in 414 sorties, the heaviest air-to-air fighting of the war took place, and Lieutenant Randy Cunningham and Lieutenant (jg) William Driscoll became the first American aces of the war [H68]. In this war the commanders chose from a target system mapped for seven years: the U-2S's folders of every bridge, rail yard and fuel dump between Hanoi and the DMZ, each with its repair history, and the Gulf watch's nightly count of what was moving on the coastal road and railway, so that the armed reconnaissance south of Thanh Hoa was sent to columns that were moving rather than to roads where traffic had been [X-003]. Damage was assessed the next day by radar through the summer cloud [X-003]. Linebacker flew 41,653 missions and dropped 155,548 tons, and lost 134 aircraft, 104 in combat [H68]; I estimate the electronic order of battle saved perhaps 10–15 of those losses, and I hold the estimate loosely [X-003]. Its effect was felt at the front: by a PAVN history only 30 percent of planned supplies reached its front-line units in May and June, the PAVN shelling of the South fell by half between 9 May and 1 June, and by September imports were 35–50 percent below the May level [H68]. The radar's count of southbound traffic below Vinh in July was about 40 percent of the March level, which is the same story told by the enemy's own trucks [X-003]. The Soviet Union and China denounced the mining and did nothing; the Nixon–Brezhnev summit in Moscow went ahead from 22 May [H68] [H].

## EV000-76 · Loc Ninh and the siege of An Loc · 7 Apr – 18 Jun 1972 · Loc Ninh and An Loc, Binh Long Province, on Route 13 · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 5 April the 5th Division of the PAVN B-2 Front attacked Loc Ninh, on Route 13 near the Cambodian border, and overran it on the afternoon of 7 April; the 9th Division took the Quan Loi base camp that evening, and the 7th Division cut Route 13 south of An Loc at the Tau O bridge with its 141st, 165th and 209th Regiments [H65]. An Loc, the provincial capital 100 km north of Saigon, was cut off by 8 April [H65]. It was held by the ARVN 5th Division under Brigadier General Le Van Hung, with the 3rd and 81st Ranger Groups, the 1st Airborne Brigade lifted in after failing to break through at Tau O, and the Regional Forces, Popular Forces and People's Self-Defence Force of Binh Long Province, all under III Corps (Lieutenant General Nguyen Van Minh) and advised by the American regional assistance command under Major General James Hollingsworth [H65].

From mid-April, on the nights the single orbit could be spared from the DMZ front and whenever the second aircraft was up, a Global Hawk stood over Binh Long and Tay Ninh and watched the roads out of Cambodia: the PAVN's tanks and trucks coming down Route 13 and the forest tracks from the Fishhook, and the ammunition columns feeding the guns around the town [X-003]. The 9th Division assaulted on 13 April with tanks; in that first attack the defenders destroyed tanks with M72 LAWs at close range, and tank-killing teams were formed in every battalion [H65]. Further assaults followed in April and May. The town survived on airdrops, taken over by USAF C-130s on 19 April once anti-aircraft fire made helicopter landings impossible, and on air support, B-52s above all: on 11–12 May B-52s struck one mission every 55 minutes for 30 hours against the massed PAVN assault, with AC-119 and AC-130E gunships, A-37s and AH-1 Cobras [H65]. Here the assault's assembly was on the radar from the evening of 10 May, as tanks and trucks moved up to the rubber north and east of the town, and Hollingsworth's targeteers placed the first B-52 boxes of the night on the assembly areas the radar showed rather than on the ones the prisoners described [X-003]. The 21st Division fought up Route 13 from Lai Khe [H65]. The PAVN was driven from the town by 12 June and III Corps declared the siege over on 18 June [H65] [H64].

US estimates of PAVN losses are 10,000 killed and 15,000 wounded and 36 tanks; Vietnamese figures are about 2,000 killed and 5,000 wounded [H65]. I estimate that the radar-cued boxes of 10–12 May added to the PAVN's losses in men and tanks, perhaps by a tenth, and I do not alter the reported figures, which already disagree by a factor of five [X-003]. Brigadier General Richard Tallman, the deputy American commander, was mortally wounded by artillery on 9 July [H65]. An Loc lay in ruins. It became the symbol of an ARVN that could hold, if the B-52s came, and in this war of an ARVN that could hold if the B-52s came and the Americans could see where to send them.
