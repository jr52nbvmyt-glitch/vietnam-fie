*COMUSMACV to CINCPAC · Saigon*

The provision gives me two U-2S Dragon Lady and four RQ-4B Global Hawk, and I have filed them as two provisions because they are two different instruments. The U-2S is a manned aircraft that flies above 70,000 feet with a radar, a camera and a signals intelligence payload; it is a deep look, sortie by sortie. The RQ-4B Block 40 is an unmanned aircraft that flies at 60,000 feet for more than thirty hours with a moving-target radar; it is a stare. The message gives no date, so both arrive on the campaign's opening date, 1 January 1964 [U: arrival defaults to campaign_opens]. Both are 2026 configurations: the U-2S as Air Combat Command flies it today, and the RQ-4B in the only block the Air Force still operates [S1] [S2].

I have to state at the outset how I read the word "granted" for aircraft built to work inside a world of satellites, because the answer decides whether one of these provisions flies at all. The 2026 RQ-4 navigates by an inertial system aided by GPS, lands on differential GPS corrections from its own launch station, and is controlled beyond the horizon through a military X-band satellite link [S7]. In January 1964 there is no GPS, and there will be none for fourteen years; there are no military communications satellites in orbit [H]. Standing Rule A6 says that aerial ISR I am given works as specified, and I read that to mean the aircraft and sensors arrive able to do what their specifications say. I do not read it to mean that a satellite constellation arrives with them. My reading, therefore, is that each RQ-4 is delivered with what it minimally needs to take off, navigate and land without satellites — a ground-based local positioning array at its home runway to stand in for differential GPS, and an inertial navigation solution corrected in flight against radar imagery of surveyed ground — and that its control and its data travel only by line-of-sight link [E: pseudolite and terrain-referenced navigation technology exists in 2026 but is not fitted to the fielded RQ-4; this is a reading of "granted", not a specification]. If that reading is wrong, and the RQ-4s arrive exactly as they fly from Grand Forks, they cannot land and should not be launched. The U-2S has no such problem: it has a pilot, an inertial system and a runway he can see.

These are the first provisions in this war that are machines, and the first that address the main-force half of the find-and-fix problem. The land programme, the Commission Against Corruption, the police and the province chiefs work on the other half — the infrastructure inside the hamlet [M001] [M002]. Nothing that flies at 60,000 feet can tell me who the tax collector is in a hamlet in Long An. I say that now, once, so that nothing that follows is read as a claim on it.

## §1.1 Lockheed Martin U-2S Dragon Lady (Block 20), 2026 configuration — P003a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**Designation.** Lockheed Martin U-2S Dragon Lady, Block 20, in its 2026 configuration: an ASARS-2A synthetic aperture radar, a SYERS-2C multispectral electro-optical and infrared camera, and the ASIP signals intelligence payload, with the glass cockpit, digital autopilot and modernised electronic warfare system of Block 20 and the avionics refresh of 2023 [S1]. The aircraft is the large U-2R airframe, first flown in 1967 and reopened for production as the TR-1 in 1981, re-engined from 1994 with the GE F118-GE-101 turbofan and redesignated U-2S [S1].

**Manufacturer and contractors.** Lockheed Martin for the airframe; Raytheon for the ASARS-2A radar; Northrop Grumman for ASIP; UTC Aerospace, now Collins, for SYERS and the optical bar camera [S1].

**In-service date.** U-2S from the re-engining programme of 1994 onward [S1]. The 2026 fleet is 27 U-2S and 4 two-seat TU-2S, based at Beale AFB in California with forward detachments at Osan, Akrotiri and Fairford [S1]. Air Combat Command has proposed retiring the whole fleet more than once since 2024, and as of mid-2026 Congress was resisting it [S1]. Two aircraft is about one in fourteen of the world's remaining U-2S.

**Quantity.** Two aircraft [U]. No two-seat trainer is provided; pilot currency and any new pilots must come from outside the theater [D].

**What they join.** On the opening date the Strategic Air Command already has orders to put U-2s over South Vietnam. President Johnson directed U-2 reconnaissance in support of South Vietnam on 31 December 1963, and three U-2E aircraft of the 4028th Strategic Reconnaissance Squadron arrived at Bien Hoa as Operating Location 20 on 5 March 1964, after the Philippine government refused to let them stay at Clark [S4]. Those three U-2Es carried film cameras and a modest SIGINT fit; their film came home in the aircraft and was processed after landing [H]. The two U-2S arrive into that arrangement two months early, and I organise them as a detachment of the same Operating Location: a Strategic Air Command reconnaissance unit under the operational control of CINCPAC, tasked through MACV J-2 for South Vietnam and Laos and through CINCPAC for the North [E: the historical OL-20 arrangement].

**Unit organisation in theater.** One detachment, two aircraft, at Bien Hoa, with the ground exploitation station beside the runway and a second, smaller line-of-sight ground terminal at Da Nang to receive data from northern tracks [E: the line-of-sight geometry in D6]. The detachment commander is a lieutenant colonel; he has four to five pilots, a mobile-officer cell (the pilot in the chase car who talks every U-2 down to its landing), life-support technicians for the full pressure suits, maintainers and contractor field engineers [E: the structure of a 2026 U-2 expeditionary detachment, scaled for two aircraft].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Crew per platform.** One pilot in a full pressure suit [S1]. The U-2 is among the most demanding aircraft in the inventory to land: it has bicycle landing gear, outrigger "pogo" wheels that drop away on take-off, and a wing that wants to keep flying in ground effect, and every landing is talked down by a second U-2 pilot driving a chase car along the runway [H]. So every sortie consumes two pilots' days, and a pilot who flies a nine-hour sortie in a pressure suit does not fly the next day [E: crew-rest practice for U-2 operations].

**Detachment complement.** About 220 people in all [E: four to five pilots and a mobile-crew cell, about 90 maintainers and life-support technicians, about 20 contractor field engineers, and an exploitation and SIGINT cell of about 100 in shifts]. The exploitation cell is the largest single element and the one most often forgotten: imagery analysts to read the radar and electro-optical take, SIGINT analysts and Vietnamese- and Chinese-language linguists to read the intercepts, and the communications technicians who run the datalink and the ground station [E: a two-aircraft detachment flying about one sortie a day].

**Personnel spaces against the ceiling.** CONFIG sets no troop ceiling, so these 220 spaces foreclose nothing by number [D]. They are still costly, and in a particular way. U-2 pilots are a small population — the 2026 Air Force trains them a handful at a time — and this detachment needs about one in eight of them [E: about 35–40 qualified U-2 pilots in the 2026 force for 31 aircraft]. The linguists are scarcer still. In January 1964 the United States has very few Vietnamese-language SIGINT linguists at all [H], and a 2026 detachment's linguists, if they come with it, are among the most valuable people in the theater, worth more doing ground and airborne direction-finding work than listening to the U-2's take alone.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The U-2S carries no weapons. Its payload is three sensors, any two or all three of which can be flown on one sortie depending on bay configuration [S1] [E: the 2026 U-2S carries the radar in the nose, SYERS in the nose or Q-bay and ASIP in the wing pods and fuselage].

**ASARS-2A synthetic aperture radar** (Raytheon). A multimode, real-time, high-resolution radar with search and spotlight imaging modes that detects and locates stationary and moving ground targets in all weather, day and night, from long stand-off range [S9]. Hughes developed it in the early 1980s; the ASARS Improvement Program delivered the 2A version from 2001 with more onboard processing and a faster datalink [S10]. The ASARS-2B, with an active electronically scanned array and nearly double the range, is under contract to 2027 and is not part of this provision [S9].

**SYERS-2C** (Collins Aerospace). A long-focal-length electro-optical and infrared camera, multispectral in its later versions, that produces near-real-time imagery at long oblique ranges [S1] [S11]. It is a daylight, fair-weather sensor in its visible bands; its infrared and multispectral bands extend it somewhat into night, fog and haze [S11].

**ASIP, the Airborne Signals Intelligence Payload** (Northrop Grumman). A wideband communications and electronic intelligence receiver suite, with direction-finding [S1] [E: ASIP's published role is COMINT and ELINT collection and geolocation; its frequency coverage and accuracy are classified].

**What is not provided.** The optical bar camera can still be mounted, but U-2 optical bar operations from Beale ended in 2022 [S1], and I do not assume the film processing chain for it exists in this provision. No air-refuelling capability exists [S5].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

This is the field that decides the provision's worth, so I take each sensor against the three conditions that define this theater.

**ASARS-2A against canopy.** A microwave radar sees the top of the forest, not what is under it. Radar that penetrates a forest canopy has to work below 1 GHz, at VHF and UHF [S12], and ASARS-2A is an X-band imaging radar built for resolution, not foliage penetration [E: ASARS-2A operates in the X band; the band is not stated in the sources consulted, but every high-resolution airborne SAR of its class is X- or Ku-band]. Under the triple canopy of War Zone D, the Do Xa, the A Shau valley or the Laotian panhandle, it sees trees. It sees the clearings, the rice fields cut into the forest, the river crossings, the roads where the canopy is broken, the new tracks bulldozed by engineers, the bomb craters, the vehicle parks that are not under trees, and the changes in all of these between two passes [E: change detection between successive SAR images is a standard ASARS product]. That is a great deal of information about a base area, and almost none about the men in it.

**ASARS-2A at night and in monsoon.** Darkness and cloud make no difference to it [S5]. This matters more than anything else on this page, because between May and September over the South and Laos, and between October and March over the North and the coastal provinces of I Corps, the cameras that the 1964 force depends on are often blind for days [H].

**ASARS-2A moving targets.** In its moving-target mode it detects vehicles and boats moving faster than walking pace on roads, rivers and canals in the open [S9] [E: minimum detectable velocity of airborne X-band MTI is typically a few kilometres an hour; ASARS-2A's figure is not published]. Men on foot under canopy it does not see.

**Detection ranges.** No published figure for ASARS-2A's range exists. Open descriptions say "long stand-off" and that the 2B nearly doubles it [S9]. I estimate that from 70,000 feet ASARS-2A produces search-mode imagery out to roughly 100 nautical miles slant range and spotlight imagery at somewhat shorter range [E: radar horizon of about 325 nm from 70,000 ft, and the U-2's practice of imaging targets well inside hostile territory from outside it]. Against target types: a truck on an open road is detectable in search mode at that range; a 37mm gun position in the open is identifiable in spotlight; a bunker under canopy is not detectable at any range [E: resolution of about 1 ft in spotlight and a few metres in search, from open-source descriptions of 1980s-generation airborne SAR].

**SYERS-2C against canopy, night and monsoon.** A camera sees no further into the forest than the human eye, though its infrared bands show heat and its multispectral bands can distinguish cut vegetation used as camouflage from living foliage [S11] [E: the general property of multispectral imaging against camouflage]. At night its infrared band sees fires, engines and warm vehicles in the open. In monsoon cloud it sees nothing [S11].

**ASIP against canopy, night and monsoon.** Radio signals ignore canopy, darkness and cloud. From 70,000 feet the radio horizon is about 325 nautical miles, so the U-2 can hear a VHF transmitter anywhere in South Vietnam south of Hue from a track over the coast near Phan Thiet (radio horizon ≈ 1.23 × √70,000 ft) [D]. This is the one sensor on either aircraft that addresses the enemy under the canopy, because the PAVN and VC main force talk on radio at regimental level and above, and the B-2 Front, the regional commands and the infiltration stations on the Trail send traffic by HF [H]. How much the 1964 enemy transmits is the limit, and he transmits little by our standards; most of his traffic below regiment goes by courier [H].

**Which half of find-and-fix.** The main-force half, entirely. The U-2 tells me where base areas, trails, crossings, depots and headquarters are, and by direction-finding roughly where a regimental or front transmitter is. It tells me nothing about the infrastructure inside the population, whose members do not use radios and do not live under the canopy.

### D5 · Performance

**Altitude.** Above 70,000 feet [S1]. The U-2R generation normally operated above 65,000 feet [S5].

**Speed.** About 410 mph, or 355 knots [S1].

**Range and endurance.** Ferry range more than 7,000 miles [S1]. Endurance more than 12 hours, but missions normally limited to about 9 hours by the pilot in his pressure suit [S5]; a 14-hour endurance sortie was flown on 31 July 2025 as a record [S1]. The U-2 cannot be refuelled in the air [S5].

**Engine and airframe.** One GE F118-GE-101 turbofan of 17,000 lb thrust; wingspan 105 feet, length 63 feet, height 16 feet, maximum take-off weight 40,000 lb [S1].

**Sortie rate.** With two aircraft, five pilots and one ground station, the detachment can sustain about one nine-hour sortie a day, about 25 a month, and surge to two a day for a few days [E: the historical U-2 practice of one sortie per aircraft every one to two days, and mission-capable rates of about 80% for a mature fleet with contractor support].

**Coverage per sortie.** A nine-hour sortie flies about 3,000 nautical miles of track (355 knots × about 8.5 hours on track) [D]. With a search swath I estimate at 60–100 nautical miles wide for ASARS-2A, one sortie can image something on the order of 100,000 square nautical miles in search mode, while also taking a few hundred spotlight images of named targets [E: from the estimated swath and track length; the real figure depends on mode and target density]. South Vietnam is about 50,000 square nautical miles [H]. In principle the whole of the South, the Laotian panhandle and the North's southern provinces can be imaged in radar once a day or two; in practice tasking spends most of each sortie on spotlight targets.

**Protection.** None in armour. The U-2's protection is altitude. Above 70,000 feet it is out of reach of every gun in the theater: the 37mm and 57mm guns that line the Trail by 1965 are effective to about 3,000 and 6,000 metres, and the 85mm and 100mm guns of the North to perhaps 9,000–12,000 metres [H] [E: effective ceilings of Soviet anti-aircraft artillery]. The MiG-17, which arrives at Phuc Yen from China in August 1964, cannot reach it [H]. The MiG-21, in combat over the North from 1966, can zoom-climb toward it but not hold an intercept there [E: MiG-21 service ceiling about 18,000 m and the U-2's historical experience over the North]. The SA-2 can reach it, as it did over Sverdlovsk in 1960 and Cuba in 1962 [H]. The Block 20 aircraft carries a modern self-protection suite against SA-2-era radars [S1] [E: the defensive system's capability is classified].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**Architecture.** The U-2S sends radar, electro-optical and SIGINT data down a wideband line-of-sight datalink to a ground station while it is "on tether", within about 220 miles of the station; beyond that it stores the data and transmits it when it comes back within range [S5]. Its 2026 configuration also carries Link 16 and the new datalinks of its modernisation programme [S1]. A 2026 U-2 in the Middle East would also pass data beyond line of sight by satellite; in 1964 it cannot, because no satellite exists to carry it [H].

**What the geometry means in Vietnam.** A ground station at Bien Hoa holds the aircraft on tether out to roughly Pleiku and Qui Nhon in the north and over the Cambodian base areas from the Fishhook to the Parrot's Beak, and to Sihanoukville at the edge of range [E: map distances from Bien Hoa — Pleiku about 200 nm, Sihanoukville about 190 nm]. A second terminal at Da Nang covers I Corps, the DMZ, the Laotian panhandle from Tchepone to the Mu Gia pass and the North's southern provinces to about Vinh [E: map distances from Da Nang — Mu Gia about 180 nm, Vinh about 200 nm]. Hanoi and Haiphong, about 340 nautical miles from Da Nang, are out of tether: tracks over the Red River delta are stored and downloaded on the way home, so northern collection arrives hours after the event, not minutes [E: map distance; the U-2's store-and-forward mode].

**Inside MACV.** The ground station's products go by secure landline and teletype to MACV J-2 at Tan Son Nhut and to the Seventh Air Force (still the 2nd Air Division in January 1964) [H]. The 2026 station produces digital imagery and formatted reports; the 1964 headquarters that receives them has teletype, courier and photographic prints [D]. So the station must make paper as well as files. A target folder for a strike in the North can be in Saigon by evening. A report that a column of trucks crossed the Ban Karai pass at 0300 reaches a division G-2 in II Corps the next day unless someone builds the channel to carry it faster.

**Talking to ARVN.** The aircraft cannot and should not. Its products are classified at levels the Joint General Staff will not be cleared for, and the Joint General Staff is penetrated by the enemy [H]. What ARVN receives is a sanitised product: a grid reference, a description, a time, with no indication of how it was obtained, passed through the US advisory chain. The SIGINT especially must never be attributed, because the first sign that we read his radio is the last good day we have reading it.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Basing.** Bien Hoa, the historical home of OL-20 [S4]. The U-2 needs a long, smooth, well-maintained hard runway, generous taxiways, and above all a clear runway centreline for its bicycle-gear landings; Bien Hoa's concrete runway serves [E: the U-2's historical operations from Bien Hoa]. Da Nang serves as a recovery alternate. Takhli in Thailand, from which the CIA's U-2s flew over the North and China until April 1964, is the natural second base for northern tracks if the Thai government permits [S4].

**Revetment and hardening.** The U-2 must be kept in a hangar or a covered, revetted shelter. On 1 November 1964 the VC mortared Bien Hoa from outside the perimeter and destroyed five B-57 bombers on the ramp, killing four Americans [H]. A U-2 caught on that ramp is a loss I cannot replace. The detachment needs two hardened shelters, a separate perimeter, and patrolling of the mortar belt around the base before the aircraft are exposed [E: the B-57 losses of November 1964].

**Survivability over the South.** Total. Small arms and 12.7mm machine guns reach about 1,500 metres [E: effective range of the DShK]; the U-2 flies at more than 21,000 metres (70,000 ft ≈ 21,300 m) [D].

**Survivability over the Trail.** Total against the guns at every date the record is likely to reach [E: effective ceilings in D5]. The risk over Laos is an engine failure and a pilot ejecting into the panhandle, not a shootdown.

**Survivability over the North.** Safe until the SA-2 arrives. The first SA-2 sites near Hanoi are photographed on 5 April 1965 and the first American aircraft falls to one on 24 July 1965 [H]. From then on, the U-2 flies over the North only along routes planned around known sites, and accepts a real risk on every penetration of the Hanoi and Haiphong defences; its self-protection suite reduces that risk but does not remove it [E: the U-2's historical losses to SA-2s in 1960 and 1962].

**Survivability near China.** China shot down several Nationalist U-2s over its territory in the 1960s [H]. Tracks along the Chinese border in 1964 are safe in practice from Chinese fighters, but an SA-2 in southern China would be a different matter, and a U-2 down in China is a political event of the first order [E: Chinese SA-2 deployments of the period].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Fuel.** The U-2S burns a special low-volatility thermally stable jet fuel, JPTS, which does not freeze or boil off at 70,000 feet [H]. JPTS is not stocked anywhere in Southeast Asia in 1964. It must come by tanker ship or by drums through Saigon and be stored separately [E: the U-2's fuel requirement]. At about one sortie a day the detachment burns roughly 2,500–3,000 US gallons a day [E: fuel capacity of about 2,900 US gallons for the U-2S airframe, most of it burned on a long sortie].

**Spares.** Under A2, the detachment has modern maintenance and its spares at its base. Each part comes from a 2026 depot through a supply chain that exists only by stipulation; nothing for the U-2S is manufactured anywhere in 1964 [D]. The two most sensitive items are the engine and the sensor line-replaceable units, both of which need contractor engineers to change and test.

**Specialist personnel.** Pressure-suit technicians; Lockheed, Raytheon, Northrop and Collins field engineers; ground-station technicians; imagery and SIGINT analysts; linguists [E: D2's complement].

**Sustainment rate.** About 25 sorties a month, 200–250 flying hours a month across the two aircraft (D5's sortie rate) [D].

**Cost.** Hourly operating cost is not published in a source I consulted. I estimate it at US$30,000–40,000 per flying hour at 2026 prices, so about US$7–10 million a month at the rates above [E: comparable high-altitude ISR operating costs of the 2010s].

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** All of it. The Vietnamese Air Force cannot maintain, fly, fuel or exploit the U-2, and nothing in its future can be made to.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**None.** The aircraft, the sensors and the take are among the most sensitive things the United States owns. The skills to fly a U-2 take a qualified fighter or multi-engine pilot about a year of conversion [E: U-2 pilot selection and training]. The Vietnamese Air Force of 1964 has under 400 pilots, flying A-1H Skyraiders, T-28s and C-47s, and its best are needed in them [H]. Transfer would also hand the most sensitive American technology to the most penetrated institution in the Republic [H].

**What can be transferred.** The product, sanitised, through the advisory chain, as described in D6; and in time a Vietnamese imagery-interpretation cell trained to read declassified radar prints and maps derived from them [E: a training pipeline of 12–18 months for imagery analysts]. Under Vietnamization the aircraft leave with the Americans. The Republic inherits maps.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**Performs to specification:** Radar imaging and moving-target search over open terrain, roads, rivers, the coast and the North's transport system, in any weather, day or night, on tether to a ground station. SIGINT collection against an enemy who uses his radios, at ranges no ground station can match. Electro-optical imaging in clear daylight.

**Performance collapses:** Against anything under canopy, for radar and camera alike. Against a disciplined enemy who keeps radio silence; his main force goes off the air before every major operation [H]. For SYERS, in monsoon cloud. Over the North once SA-2s are emplaced along the route, where every sortie carries risk. Beyond line of sight, where real-time becomes after-the-fact. And at the tempo of daily operations sustained over months, which two aircraft can meet only if neither is lost or down for long.

**The EW environment.** The North's early-warning radars will track a U-2 from the moment it crosses the coast; they cannot reach it, but they will tell Hanoi where it is looking [E: Soviet-supplied early-warning radars in the North by 1965]. Its datalink is not jammed by anything the enemy owns in this period [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions].

**Radius from base.** On tether, about 220 miles from Bien Hoa or Da Nang [S5]. Off tether, anywhere within roughly 1,500 miles (a 9-hour sortie at 355 knots, with reserves) [D].

**Tempo over weeks.** One sortie a day is sustainable for months if both aircraft remain serviceable; with one aircraft down, one sortie every other day [E: D5].

### D11 · Failure modes

**What breaks first.** The aircraft, and the pilot's day. A U-2 with an engine or flight-control problem is down for days waiting for a part and an engineer [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. A crosswind or a wet runway at Bien Hoa cancels a landing, and a monsoon squall over the base cancels a launch; the U-2 is notoriously sensitive to crosswinds on landing [H].

**Partial failure.** One aircraft unserviceable halves the sortie rate and ends surge. A ground-station failure turns every sortie into store-and-forward, and products arrive the next day. A failed SIGINT payload leaves the radar working; a failed radar leaves the camera, which in the monsoon is useless.

**Total failure.** Loss of an aircraft over the North, an accident on landing, or a mortar round through a hangar. With two aircraft, one loss halves the provision for good; nothing replaces it.

**What the force does in the gap.** It returns to what it had: the U-2Es of OL-20 from March 1964, the Ryan 147 Lightning Bug drones from October 1964, the RF-101C detachment at Tan Son Nhut, the Army's OV-1 Mohawks with side-looking radar, and the Army's radio direction-finding aircraft [S4] [S8] [H].

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** The aircraft are new to the theater, the pilots new to its weather, the analysts new to the enemy. The first month produces superb imagery that nobody yet knows how to read: radar shadows in the canopy that might be bunkers or might be rock; a transmitter that might be a regimental headquarters or might be a relay station [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Mission-capable rate high, about 85–90%, on fresh spares [E: a newly deployed detachment].

**Month twelve.** Analysts have built a library of change: which crossings are used in the dry season, which clearings are new, which transmitters move with which units. The product is far better than at month one. Mission-capable rate about 75–80% as the tropical climate works on seals, connectors and electronics [E: humidity and heat effects on airborne electronics in Southeast Asia]. Pilot proficiency holds, because these are career U-2 pilots.

**What declines first.** The SIGINT take, because the enemy learns. The first time an American strike lands on a headquarters located by direction-finding, his signal discipline tightens, as it did historically after ARDF began to tell [H]. Second, the electro-optical take, because the monsoon comes. Third, spares depth, if the supply chain from 2026 is slower than the stipulation assumes.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the U-2E of OL-20.** The U-2E is the same high-altitude idea with 1950s sensors: film cameras whose take returns with the aircraft and must be processed before anyone reads it, and a SIGINT fit of the period [H]. The U-2S has a radar that sees through cloud and darkness, a camera that downlinks while the aircraft is still overhead, and a modern SIGINT suite with direction-finding [S1]. In monsoon weather the U-2E produces nothing; the U-2S produces radar imagery every sortie. The margin is, at a guess, five to ten times the useful product per sortie in the dry season, and the difference between something and nothing in the wet [E: weather-limited photographic availability over the theater].

**Against the RF-101C Voodoo and, from late 1965, the RF-4C Phantom.** Low- and medium-altitude photographic reconnaissance on film, fast, and with real risk over the Trail and the North [H]. The U-2S covers in one sortie what an RF-101C covers in perhaps fifteen to twenty, without exposure to guns, but at lower resolution than a low-level pass and with no ability to look under the edge of the canopy the way a low oblique photograph can [E: coverage arithmetic].

**Against the Ryan 147 Lightning Bug.** From 11 October 1964 the drones fly from Bien Hoa, launched from a DC-130 and recovered by parachute and a CH-3E helicopter; their high-altitude models reach about 62,500 feet with a U-2-type camera, and their losses over the war are 578 drones [S8]. They are expendable; the U-2S is not. Per sortie, a U-2S with radar and SIGINT produces several times what a camera drone produces, and does so in weather [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions].

**Against the Army's OV-1 Mohawk with the AN/APS-94 side-looking radar.** The Mohawk's radar has a moving-target mode and flies at low altitude, with a range of a few tens of kilometres [H] [E: AN/APS-94 performance]. The U-2S sees several times further and from safety, but the Mohawk is under the command of the corps that uses its picture.

**Against the Army's radio direction-finding aircraft.** The 3rd Radio Research Unit's U-6A Beavers have flown airborne direction-finding since 1962 [H]. They fix transmitters to a few hundred metres; a single high-altitude U-2 fixes them to a few kilometres unless it builds a long baseline over the course of a sortie [E: direction-finding geometry from a single high, distant platform]. The U-2 hears much more; the Beaver locates better. They are complements, and the best use of ASIP is to cue the Beavers.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P003a -->
The U-2S cannot see under the canopy, with radar or camera, and the enemy's base camps, hospitals, depots and regiments are under the canopy. It cannot see the VC infrastructure in the hamlets at all; it addresses the main-force half of find-and-fix only. It does not provide real-time coverage beyond about 220 miles of a ground station; northern collection arrives after landing. Two aircraft give about one sortie a day, not persistent surveillance: it looks at a place, then goes away. Its SIGINT locates transmitters to kilometres, not metres, and only while the enemy transmits. Its camera is useless in monsoon cloud. It has no weapons, and its product is worth only what a strike, raid or airmobile force can do with it inside the hours the location stays true. Over the North it is not invulnerable once SA-2s arrive in 1965, and a loss cannot be replaced. Its products cannot safely be shared with ARVN except in sanitised form, because the Joint General Staff is penetrated.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P003a -->
The cover of weather and darkness over open ground: roads, rivers, fords, truck parks, depots and construction in the North, Laos and the base areas can no longer be hidden by cloud or night. Radio security at regiment and above: every transmission becomes a possible location. The surprise of a newly built road, airfield, SAM site or base area, which radar change detection reveals within days.

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P003a -->
A large improvement in what MACV and the air campaign know about the enemy's rear — the Trail, the North's transport system, the base areas and the radio nets of his higher headquarters — at a modest cost in people and none in lives. It is the best strategic reconnaissance in the world in 1964 by a wide margin, and over the North it will make target selection and damage assessment honest and air losses lower. It does not find a regiment in the jungle tonight, it does not find the VCI, and two aircraft cannot watch anything continuously. Its value to the war in the South is as a cue — for the direction-finding aircraft, the long-range patrols, the Special Forces and the bombers — and its value depends on there being something fast enough to act on what it finds. Real, durable, and not decisive alone.

## §1.2 Northrop Grumman RQ-4B Global Hawk Block 40, 2026 configuration — P003b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

**Designation.** Northrop Grumman RQ-4B Global Hawk Block 40: a high-altitude, long-endurance, remotely piloted aircraft carrying the Multi-Platform Radar Technology Insertion Program radar, MP-RTIP, an active electronically scanned array that provides ground moving-target indication and synthetic aperture imagery [S2] [S7]. The system is the aircraft, the Launch and Recovery Element that takes it off and lands it, and the Mission Control Element from which a pilot and a sensor operator fly the mission [S2].

**Manufacturer.** Northrop Grumman for the aircraft and radar; Raytheon for the ground segment; L3Harris for communications [S2] [S7].

**In-service date.** Global Hawk first flew on 28 February 1998; the Block 40 reached initial operational capability in August 2016 [S2]. The Block 10 was retired in 2011, the Block 20 converted to communications relays, and the Block 30, which carried the imaging and SIGINT suite, retired in 2022 [S2] [S3]. The 2026 Air Force fleet is nine Block 40s at Grand Forks AFB, with forward locations at Andersen, Sigonella, Fairford and Yokota; Congress has extended its retirement to at least 2030 [S2]. Four aircraft is almost half of it.

**Quantity.** Four aircraft [U], with one Launch and Recovery Element and one Mission Control Element [E: the minimum ground segment for a single operating location].

**Configuration.** As stated at the head of this memorandum: without GPS or satellite links, each aircraft is read as arriving with a local positioning array at its home runway and an in-flight navigation update against radar imagery of surveyed ground, and all control and data go by line-of-sight link [E: a reading of "granted", not a specification]. I mark every place below where that reading matters.

**Unit organisation in theater.** One expeditionary reconnaissance squadron, four aircraft, under CINCPAC's operational control through the 2nd Air Division, tasked by MACV J-2 [E: by analogy with the U-2 detachment]. The Launch and Recovery Element and the Mission Control Element sit together at the home base, because the MCE can control the aircraft only by line of sight. A relay terminal at Da Nang extends the line-of-sight control and data link to the north [E: D6].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

**Crew per platform.** None on board. Three people fly each mission: a Launch and Recovery Element pilot who takes the aircraft off and lands it, and a Mission Control Element pilot and sensor operator who fly the mission [S2]. A 30-hour sortie needs four to five shifts of the MCE crew (6–8-hour shifts) [D].

**Squadron complement.** About 380 people in all [E: six to seven three-person mission crews for one continuous orbit, an LRE detachment, about 140 maintainers and contractor technicians, and a GMTI and radar-imagery exploitation cell of about 150 in shifts]. As with the U-2, the exploitation cell is the largest element. A moving-target radar watching a 24-hour orbit over the Laotian panhandle generates thousands of tracks a day, and someone has to decide which of them are trucks of the 559th Transportation Group and which are a Lao logging lorry [E: the volume of GMTI data from a persistent orbit].

**Personnel spaces against the ceiling.** No ceiling is set, so the 380 spaces foreclose nothing by number [D]. They are 2026 specialists — remotely piloted aircraft pilots and sensor operators, radar analysts trained in GMTI exploitation — who do not exist anywhere in the 1964 force and cannot be trained by it [D]. Replacements come only from the stipulated supply that delivered them.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The RQ-4B carries no weapons. Its maximum payload is 3,000 lb [S2], and in Block 40 that payload is one sensor.

**MP-RTIP radar** (Northrop Grumman and Raytheon). An active electronically scanned array that collects synthetic aperture imagery of stationary targets and tracks ground moving targets at the same time [S6] [S7]. It provides wide-area surveillance of stationary and moving targets and has maritime modes for surface vessels [S7]. It can also track low, slow aircraft and cruise missiles, which matters little in this war [S2].

**What Block 40 does not carry.** No camera of any kind, and no SIGINT payload [S2] [S6]. The imaging and SIGINT suite flew on the Block 30, which was retired in 2022 [S2]. This is the line in the dossier most likely to be forgotten: the RQ-4 in this provision sees with radar and only with radar.

**Known deficiencies.** In its operational testing the Block 40's radar showed problems of SAR image quality and sensor stability that the Air Force addressed with software, and whose fixes were still not fully evaluated in 2014 [S6]. By 2026 the system has a decade of operational use [S2], and I assume the problems are largely resolved, but the SAR imagery of the Block 40 is a by-product of a GMTI radar, not the equal of the U-2's ASARS-2A [E: the Block 40's design priority].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

**Against canopy.** Like the ASARS-2A, the MP-RTIP is a microwave radar, and canopy defeats it [S12] [E: MP-RTIP operates in the X band, from its derivation from the radar programme for the E-10 and its published use]. It does not see men or vehicles under trees. It sees them where the trees stop: at a ford, on a road through a clearing, crossing a rice field, moving along a canal or a river, or on the open road from Vinh to the Mu Gia pass [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. The Ho Chi Minh Trail was built to avoid exactly this exposure, under trellised canopy where the forest allowed [H]. But even the Trail crosses rivers, climbs passes where the forest is thin, and leaves the trees where bombing has removed them.

**At night.** No difference [S3]. This is the sensor's great advantage in a war fought at night. A sampan convoy crossing the Plain of Reeds at 0200, a truck column on Route 15 north of the Mu Gia pass, a trawler standing in toward the coast of Phu Yen: all of them are as visible at night as at noon [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions].

**In monsoon.** No difference [S3]. Heavy rain attenuates an X-band radar somewhat and reduces its range in a cell of intense rainfall, but the sensor keeps working through the monsoon in a way no camera does [E: X-band rain attenuation].

**Detection ranges and target types.** Published figures do not exist. I estimate that from 60,000 feet the MP-RTIP tracks moving vehicles and boats out to about 100–150 nautical miles, can observe an area of tens of thousands of square kilometres from one orbit, and can detect a man walking in open terrain at shorter range [E: radar horizon of about 300 nm from 60,000 ft and the wide-area GMTI design of the programme]. Against target types: trucks on open roads, yes; boats on rivers and canals, yes; steel-hulled trawlers at sea, yes, at long range; a file of porters on a trail under canopy, no; a column of infantry crossing an open paddy at night, perhaps, if it moves together and fast enough [E: the slow-mover problem of GMTI against dismounts].

**The discrimination problem.** GMTI sees movement, not identity. In the Mekong Delta at night, tens of thousands of sampans move on the canals, and most of them belong to farmers and traders [E: the Delta's waterborne economy]. A radar track cannot tell a VC tax collector's sampan from a rice merchant's. This is the sensor's most dangerous property, and I return to it in D14.

**Which half of find-and-fix.** The main-force half — and, within it, only the part that moves in the open. It does not touch the infrastructure.

### D5 · Performance

**Altitude.** Ceiling 60,000 feet [S2].

**Speed.** Maximum about 357 mph; cruise about 357 mph [S2] [S7].

**Endurance and range.** More than 32 hours, with 24 hours on station at 1,200 miles from base [S2]; range about 14,150 miles [S2].

**Engine and airframe.** One Rolls-Royce F137-RR-100 turbofan of 7,600 lb thrust; wingspan 130.9 feet, length 47.6 feet, height 15.3 feet, maximum take-off weight 32,250 lb, empty weight about 14,950 lb [S2] [S7].

**Orbits.** From Bien Hoa, every station in the South is less than an hour's flying away (no point in RVN is more than about 350 nm from Bien Hoa) [D]. With a 30-hour sortie and an hour's transit, each sortie gives about 28 hours on station [D]. Four aircraft, at a mission-capable rate of about 75%, can sustain one continuous 24-hour orbit indefinitely and a second orbit for about a third of the time, or two orbits for a week's surge [E: RQ-4 availability in sustained operations, with three aircraft flyable, each flying one 30-hour sortie every three to four days].

**Coverage per orbit.** One orbit at 60,000 feet watches, at a time, an area of perhaps 20,000–40,000 square kilometres for moving targets [E: the wide-area GMTI footprint]. That is roughly the Laotian panhandle from the Mu Gia pass to Tchepone, or the whole of the Mekong Delta's southern provinces, or the coast of the II Corps provinces from Qui Nhon to Phan Rang out to 100 miles at sea [E: map areas].

**Protection.** None but altitude. At 60,000 feet it is beyond every gun and every MiG of the period [E: effective ceilings in §1.1 D5]. It is well inside the SA-2's envelope, and it has no pilot to see a missile coming or to manoeuvre against it [E: SA-2 ceiling of about 25,000 m].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

**Architecture.** The 2026 RQ-4 is controlled and sends its data both by line of sight and by satellite [S6], the latter through a military X-band satellite link to the Mission Control Element [S7]. A common data link delivers imagery directly to ground stations in line of sight [S7]. In 1964, only the line-of-sight links exist.

**What the geometry means.** The radio horizon from 60,000 feet is about 300 nautical miles (1.23 × √60,000 ft) [D]. A practical control and data link of about 200–250 nautical miles from a ground terminal is a fair estimate [E: by analogy with the U-2's on-tether figure of about 220 miles in S5]. A Mission Control Element at Bien Hoa controls the aircraft anywhere in the South from about Qui Nhon southward, over the whole Cambodian border, over Sihanoukville and the Gulf of Thailand coast [E: map distances]. A relay terminal at Da Nang, linked to Bien Hoa by landline or microwave, extends control and data over I Corps, the DMZ, the Laotian panhandle and the North to about Vinh [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. The Red River delta is out of reach.

**What happens if the link is lost.** The Global Hawk is designed to fly a pre-programmed route on its own if the link fails, and to return home [E: the lost-link procedure of HALE remotely piloted aircraft]. Without GPS it navigates on its inertial system, drifting perhaps a nautical mile or more per hour without updates [E: navigation-grade inertial drift], and the radar map-matching of my reading corrects it. A link outage over Laos is an inconvenience. Over the North it is a gift to the enemy if the aircraft drifts into a defended area.

**Inside MACV.** Moving-target tracks are a live product: a dot moving on a map display, with history. Their value is in minutes. The ground station can display them, but the 1964 force has no network to carry them to anyone but people standing in the station [D]. A forward air controller, a strike aircraft or an airborne command post can be talked onto a track by radio; a ground unit can be given a grid by voice [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Every hop costs time.

**Talking to ARVN.** As with the U-2, never directly. An ARVN corps commander can be given a reported location, unattributed, through his adviser. The tracks themselves stay in American hands.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Basing.** Bien Hoa as the main operating base, with the Mission Control Element and Launch and Recovery Element together; Da Nang for the relay terminal and as a recovery alternate [E: D6's geometry]. The aircraft needs a long hard runway and, on my reading, a surveyed local positioning array around it for automatic take-off and landing; the array is site-specific, so each runway the aircraft uses must have one [E: a reading of "granted"]. Bien Hoa is chosen over Tan Son Nhut because Tan Son Nhut is saturated with traffic [H], and over Da Nang because Bien Hoa sits under the middle of the coverage the aircraft is most needed for: the Delta, the Cambodian border and the coast.

**Revetment and hardening.** As for the U-2, the RQ-4 is a large, irreplaceable aircraft that must not stand on an open ramp. Its 131-foot wingspan needs a large shelter [S2]. The Bien Hoa mortar attack of 1 November 1964 is the warning [H]. I need four shelters and a protected perimeter for an aircraft that has none of a fighter's protection [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions].

**Survivability over the South.** Total against the enemy's weapons (60,000 ft ≈ 18,300 m against about 1,500 m for the 12.7mm) [D].

**Survivability over the Trail.** Total against guns; the 100mm gun's ceiling is about 12,000–15,000 metres in the most generous estimate [E: Soviet KS-19 performance]. SA-2s did not deploy into Laos until the last years of the war [H].

**Survivability over the North.** Low once SA-2s are emplaced in 1965. A slow, large, unmanoeuvrable aircraft at 60,000 feet is the SA-2's ideal target [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Over the North after mid-1965, the RQ-4 can look only from outside the SA-2 rings, which with a radar of 100-mile reach still covers much of the North's southern provinces from offshore or from over Laos [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions].

**Survivability near China and Cambodia.** Over Cambodia, a large unmanned aircraft is a violation of a neutral's airspace and a propaganda gift if lost [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Standing off inside South Vietnamese airspace, the radar sees perhaps 150–200 km into Cambodia without overflight [E: D4's estimated range], so the base areas along the border can be watched without crossing it. Near China, I do not fly it.

**Accident risk.** The Global Hawk programme lost four of its early prototypes and has had crashes throughout its service, including in 2018, 2021 and 2022 [S2] [S7]. In a tropical theater, without GPS, from runways the aircraft was not designed for, the accident rate will be higher than in peacetime operations from Grand Forks [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Over a war of several years, the loss of one or two of the four aircraft to accident is more likely than not [E: the programme's record and the harder conditions].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Fuel.** Standard JP-8 jet fuel, the kerosene that every jet in theater burns in a near equivalent [E: the RQ-4's fuel; the 1964 force uses JP-4, and the provision's stipulated supply provides compatible fuel]. A 30-hour sortie burns roughly 2,000–2,500 US gallons [E: about 15,000 lb of fuel in a 32,250-lb aircraft whose empty weight is 14,950 lb and payload 3,000 lb, from the weights in S2 and S7]. At one continuous orbit, about 2,000 US gallons a day [D].

**Spares and maintenance.** Contractor logistics support is about half the RQ-4's hourly cost [S7], which tells me who keeps it flying: Northrop Grumman and Raytheon field service engineers. They come with the provision [E: A2's stipulated maintenance].

**Cost.** About US$18,900 per flight hour in 2013 dollars [S7]. At one continuous orbit, about 730 flight hours a month, that is about US$14 million a month at 2013 prices [D]. Each aircraft is a flyaway cost of about US$130 million at 2013 prices [S7].

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Everything. The Republic has no fuel standard, no runway equipment, no maintenance capacity and no exploitation capacity for this aircraft, and never will.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

**None.** The aircraft cannot be operated without 2026 training and support that has no equivalent in the 1960s or 1970s [D]. A Vietnamese crew could be trained, in principle, in about a year, but the aircraft cannot be maintained without contractor engineers from 2026, and the product is so sensitive and the Republic's services so penetrated that handing them a live picture of what the United States can see would cost more than it gains [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions] [H].

**What can be transferred.** Sanitised reports. Under Vietnamization, the aircraft leave with the Americans, and what the Republic keeps is whatever its own forces learned to do with American reports in the meantime.

### D10 · Employment envelope

**Performs to specification:** Persistent moving-target surveillance of open terrain, roads, rivers, canals and the sea, day or night, in any weather, from a station held for 24 hours at a time, inside about 250 nautical miles of a ground terminal [E: D6]. Radar imagery of fixed targets in the same area, of lower quality than ASARS-2A.

**Performance collapses:** Under canopy. Against dismounts moving slowly or in small groups. Where the enemy is mixed into a civilian population whose movement looks the same. Beyond line of sight. Over the North after the SA-2 arrives. When the link drops. In typhoons that close the base, and in the occasional extreme rainfall cell [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions].

**Tempo over weeks.** One continuous orbit is sustainable for months, if the four aircraft survive. A second continuous orbit is not [E: D5].

**EW environment.** The enemy has no jammer capable of affecting the datalink or the radar in this period [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. He has radar to see the aircraft, and once he understands what it is, he will learn its pattern.

### D11 · Failure modes

**What breaks first.** The aircraft on the ground, from tropical heat and humidity in electronics designed for Grand Forks [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Then the link, when a terminal goes down or the aircraft flies beyond range. Then navigation, if the local positioning array at Bien Hoa is damaged by a mortar round or a power cut; with no fallback, take-off and landing at that runway stop until it is repaired [E: a reading of "granted"].

**Partial failure.** Three aircraft give one orbit with no margin for maintenance; two give one orbit with gaps of a day or more. A radar that drops its SAR mode still tracks moving targets. A broken exploitation chain leaves the station with a picture no one can act on.

**Total failure.** Loss of the ground segment at Bien Hoa, to an attack or a fire, grounds all four aircraft until it is replaced; there is only one [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. Loss of an aircraft in an accident or to an SA-2 reduces the force by a quarter for good.

**What the force does in the gap.** What it did historically: the OV-1 Mohawk's side-looking radar by corps, U-2Es and drones, and, from 1967, the seismic and acoustic sensors of Igloo White on the Trail [H].

### D12 · Degradation curve

**Month one.** The radar works; the analysts do not yet know the theater. They see thousands of tracks a night and cannot tell which matter. Mission-capable rate about 80% [E: a newly deployed RQ-4 detachment].

**Month twelve.** The analysts have built pattern-of-life for the roads, rivers and coast in their orbits: when the Delta's sampans normally move, what a convoy looks like on Route 15 in the dry season, what a trawler looks like among the junks off Phu Yen. The product is much more useful than at month one. Mission-capable rate about 70–75% [E: tropical climate and sustained operations]. One aircraft may already have been lost [E: D7's accident risk].

**What declines first.** The enemy's exposure, as he learns. A truck column that is struck in the open within the hour learns to move in rain under canopy, in small groups, in the gaps of the orbit [H: the enemy's historical adaptation to Igloo White and the gunships]. The second thing is the navigation reading: an array that is down for a week grounds the aircraft for a week.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

**Against the OV-1 Mohawk with AN/APS-94 side-looking radar.** The Mohawk is the only airborne moving-target radar in theater in 1964. It flies at low altitude for a few hours and sees a few tens of kilometres [H] [E: AN/APS-94 performance]. The RQ-4 sees perhaps five times further, from beyond the reach of any gun, for thirty hours at a time [E: judgment from the system's design and the theater's conditions]. The margin in area watched continuously is on the order of fifty times per aircraft [E: area ratio of a 150-km to a 30-km reach, times a sortie-length ratio of about 8–10].

**Against Igloo White** (from 1967 historically). The seismic and acoustic sensors on the Trail detected trucks and men under canopy, where they were placed, and needed an EC-121 relay and a large infiltration surveillance centre at Nakhon Phanom [H]. The RQ-4 sees nothing under canopy but sees everything in the open over a whole sector. They are complements, not rivals: one hears the Trail where it hides, the other sees it where it shows.

**Against the U-2S of this memorandum.** The U-2S sees farther and better in imagery and has SIGINT; it looks and goes away. The RQ-4 sees moving things continuously. The U-2 builds the map; the RQ-4 watches the traffic on it.

**Against the JSTARS of later wars.** The E-8C JSTARS of Desert Storm watched large armoured forces moving on open desert [H]. Here the enemy is an infantry force that moves on foot under canopy, and the comparison flatters the RQ-4: in 1991 a moving-target radar found an enemy army on the move; here it will find mostly trucks, boats and civilians.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P003b -->
The RQ-4B Block 40 carries a radar and nothing else: no camera, no infrared, no SIGINT. It cannot see under canopy, so it does not see the main force in its base areas or most of the Trail. It cannot reliably see men on foot moving slowly or in small groups. It cannot tell a VC sampan from a farmer's, or a supply truck from a civilian lorry; its tracks are movement, not identity, and firing on them under the historical rules will kill civilians. It has no satellite links in 1964: control and data are limited to about 250 nm of a ground terminal [E: line-of-sight geometry], and Hanoi and Haiphong are out of reach. It depends on a reading of "granted" for navigation and landing without GPS, and on a single ground segment at Bien Hoa. It is vulnerable to the SA-2 and, unlike the U-2, has no pilot to avoid one. Four aircraft sustain one continuous orbit, not two. It does not touch the VCI. It does not reduce tonnage arriving in the South; it shows where tonnage moves in the open, and only a strike reduces it.

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P003b -->
The cover of night and weather for anything that moves in the open: trucks on roads, sampans on canals and rivers, trawlers at sea, and columns crossing open ground. It forces the enemy to move under canopy or in small groups by day and in rain, which slows him and costs him. It denies the seaborne infiltration route its anonymity among coastal traffic.

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P003b -->
A persistent, all-weather, day-and-night watch on everything that moves in the open within about 250 nm of Bien Hoa or Da Nang [E: line-of-sight geometry] — the sea approaches, the Delta's waterways, the roads of the Laotian panhandle and the North's southern provinces. Its strongest use is at sea, where a steel-hulled trawler is a large, slow, isolated target among junks, and the coast is otherwise unwatched; its second is the Trail's open stretches once the enemy has trucks on it from 1965. Under canopy it is blind, against the infrastructure useless, and in the Delta and the populated coastal plain it is dangerous, because it shows movement without identity. It does not find the main force where the main force lives. It shows where it moves, which is less, and it is worth what can be put on a track inside the hour.
