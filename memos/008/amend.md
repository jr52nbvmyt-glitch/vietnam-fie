## EV008-1 · The Directorate of Defence Industry and the domestic share · 1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Bien Hoa industrial zone, Bien Hoa Province; the Go Vap and Thu Duc works, Gia Dinh Province; the Joint General Staff and the Aid Compliance Board, Saigon · NEW · 008
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Republic owned what no Southeast Asian state of its size owned: an electric-arc steelworks at Bien Hoa with two 30-tonne furnaces, a billet caster and a mill rolling bar, rod, light sections and light plate; a forge and heat-treatment shop beside it; an arsenal with four case-and-bullet lines and two forging lines for mortar and 105mm projectile bodies; engine, vehicle and weapons rebuild works; a steel boatyard at Thu Duc; fabrication shops for bridge spans and fortification steel; and, at Go Vap and in the Bien Hoa zone, a communications plant licensed for the AN/PRC-10, a dry-cell and storage-battery works, a wire and cable mill, a transistor assembly plant owned by an American firm, and a signal rebuild depot [M008]. About 35,000–50,000 people worked in the metal trades and 20,000–35,000 in electronics, with about 1,800–2,800 engineers between them [M008]. The same session of the junta that decreed land to the tiller and the rural police placed the state's share of all of it under a Directorate of Defence Industry in the Ministry of Defence, and named as Director Vuong Dinh Toan, a metallurgist trained at the École Centrale who had built the Bien Hoa melt shop [X-008].

For its first fourteen months the base had more capacity than customers, and that was the whole of its problem. The RVNAF's Ordnance and Signal services drew their ammunition, radios, batteries, wire and spares from American stocks under the Military Assistance Program, free and delivered, and no Vietnamese quartermaster would pay piasters for a cartridge case the Americans gave him [X-008]. The arsenal ran its case lines at about a third of capacity in 1964, selling to the National Police and the Combat Youth what the Army would not buy, and the steelworks ran at about half for want of scrap, rolling 100,000–150,000 tonnes a year, most of it reinforcing bar and the pickets and wire of the outpost programme (EV002-2) [M008]. The agricultural machinery shops did better than the arsenal, because a farmer who owned his land had a reason to buy a pump: about 3,000 diesel pumps and small engines went to owner-cultivators in An Giang, Vinh Long and Phong Dinh in 1964–65, bought on credit from the Agricultural Credit Office against the new titles (EV001-2) [X-008].

The fix was a rule, and this headquarters wrote it. Colonel Howard B. Teague, the Ordnance Corps officer who headed the advisory element to the Directorate, proposed in November 1964 that a fixed share of the RVNAF's requirement in eight categories be bought from the Directorate with counterpart piasters before any American stock was drawn: small-arms cartridge cases and bullets, 60mm and 81mm mortar bodies, 105mm projectile bodies, field wire, radio dry cells, PF and hamlet radios, construction steel and bridge spans [X-008]. I sent it to CINCPAC, and on 1 March 1965 the Aid Compliance Board made it a condition of the counterpart-fund release: a quarter of the requirement in 1965, a third in 1966 and half from 1968 (EC008-2, POS008-1) [X-008]. The American ordnance staff at Long Binh objected that domestic cases cost about a fifth more than the American article and were unproven, and both objections were true in 1965 [X-008]. The rule held because the Board held, as it held for the land titles and the province chiefs (EV001-1, EV002-1).

The base also had a price in men. About 31,000–45,000 of its workers were of military age, and every one needed a deferment that the mobilization decrees of 1964 did not provide (F008-9) [M008]. The Directorate's deferment list became a market within a year: in Bien Hoa in the spring of 1965 a place on it cost about two months of a skilled worker's wage, paid to a clerk in the Directorate's personnel office, and the Commission Against Corruption opened its first case in the factories in May (EV001-1) [X-008]. The plants also took, at better pay, the electricians, machinists and radio technicians the Signal and Ordnance Corps had expected to draft. By July 1965 the communications plant had delivered about 5,000 PRC-10s and 6,000 hamlet radios, the battery works about 600,000 dry cells, and the wire mill about 40,000 kilometres of field wire, most of it to the territorial forces of the held belt [X-008].

The Front read the base wrongly and organised it rightly. A COSVN study captured in Bien Hoa in 1966 called the plants "American factories employing Vietnamese labour", which they were not; its proselytizing section had by then put cells into the arsenal's case lines and the battery works, and was collecting dues from workers whose brothers were in the ARVN (F008-17, F008-21) [X-008]. It was the first time since 1954 that the Front had an industrial working class in the South to organise, and it set about it as the Party in the North had been taught to.

## EV008-2 · Operation LONG SPAN: the floods of November 1964 · 6 Nov 1964 – 15 Feb 1965 · Route 1 and the coastal railway from Da Nang to Quang Ngai; Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai Provinces · NEW · 008
*Initiative: neither*

In the first week of November 1964 two typhoons and the rains behind them put the coastal plain of Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai under water, in the worst flood in central Vietnam in living memory; the dead were counted in thousands, by some Vietnamese accounts about 7,000, and Route 1 and the coastal railway were cut at dozens of points between the Hai Van pass and Quang Ngai [H]. The ARVN 2nd Division in Quang Ngai and the province garrisons of the coast were cut off from Da Nang by road, and so were the rice and the relief.

In this war the bridges came back from Bien Hoa, in an operation the Directorate called LONG SPAN. The fabrication shops held a standard 30-metre steel truss span and a 12-metre girder span, drawn in 1963 for the outpost and road programme, and the Directorate had about forty of them in stock when the water fell [X-008]. Between 14 and 30 November the shops cut and drilled about ninety more, and two Vietnamese Navy LSTs and three chartered coasters carried them from the Thu Duc yard to Da Nang and to the beach at Sa Huynh, where the 2nd Division's engineers and two Vietnamese contractor crews met them (EC008-1) [X-008]. Route 1 was open to the 2nd Division's trucks from Da Nang to Quang Ngai on 21 December, about six weeks after the flood and, by the Directorate's own estimate, two months sooner than spans ordered from Okinawa would have reopened it [X-008]. The railway took longer, as it would have in any case, and its last bridge south of Tam Ky was not back until 15 February.

The ninety-day road became the Directorate's term for its standard: any bridge the Front or the weather took on a national route was to be back in ninety days, and on Route 1 it usually was. It changed no battle in Phase I. Ba Gia was lost in May on ground the road did not decide (EV000-17). What it changed was the winter of 1964–65 on the coast: the rice of the Quang Ngai lowlands went north by truck in January instead of rotting in the district towns, the relief reached the flooded districts of Dien Ban and Duy Xuyen in December instead of February, and the Front's Region 5 committee, which had planned its winter around a coast cut off until the dry season, found the 2nd Division supplied by road [X-008]. On the historical course the flood was one of the reasons the coastal Centre was so nearly lost in the spring of 1965. Here it was a smaller one.

## EV002-2 · The first posts and the Front's answer · 1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 002,004,CP01,006
*Initiative: both*

The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night (EC002-1) [X-002]. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village [X-002]. From February the platoon beside the post carried M4A1 carbines with red-dot sights, drawn under IRON GATE (EV004-1); the constables, whom the rearmament did not cover, kept the carbines of 1950 [X-004]. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965 [M002]. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads (EC002-2) [M002]. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered [X-002].

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected (F002-12). A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night [X-002]. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966 [X-002]. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required (F002-8) [X-002].

Where the platoon was there, it now held. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about nine in ten held, against about four in five that the programme's design would have given them with the platoons' old weapons [X-004]. The case that the Delta's advisers told for a year was the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140 against a post whose PF platoon had zeroed its carbines in March. The platoon's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and the company's first assault, which on the Front's own experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause; the second was not pressed, and the company withdrew before the Regional Forces boat company from Ham Long arrived at 0320, leaving eleven dead on the bund and taking its wounded [X-004]. The Front learned that lesson faster than this headquarters expected. From December its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in, and, against the posts that held, the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market (F004-1, F004-2) [X-004]. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts [X-004].

The weapons the Front took changed with its targets. Of the roughly 300 weapons lost with the police posts, nearly all were the constables' old carbines; but across the countryside the outposts that did fall, the Popular Forces deserters who walked away with their arms, and the ARVN battalions ambushed in the summer of 1965 gave the Front between about 10,000 and 30,000 M4A1 carbines by the end of 1965, with their sights (F004-7) [X-004]. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and each one needed the Republic's own 5.56mm to fire.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not [X-002]. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed (F002-13) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

From the summer of 1964 the Special Branch's harvest had a second layer. The district centres that opened behind the posts took each arrest and each rallier and asked a different question of them: not who in this hamlet belongs to the Front, but who sends the hamlet's cadres their orders, who collects the tax above the village, and where the district committee's couriers sleep (EC006-1) [X-006]. Of the roughly 1,900 people the Branch arrested in the held belt by July 1965, about 260 were members of district or village party committees or their security and finance sections, against perhaps 100 that the Branch's own folders would have reached without the centres' charts [X-006]. The biometric enrolment caught its first case in December 1964 at Cho Moi in An Giang, a finance cadre released by a magistrate in August on a bribe paid through a cousin, who was rearrested in November under a new name with a new identity card and found by the kit in forty seconds (F006-3) [X-006]. The Commission prosecuted the magistrate's clerk in 1965. The Front's answer to the centres came, as its answer to the posts had, within a season: from January 1965 its district committees in the held belt began moving their offices out of the villages to the contested edge, and the Dinh Tuong clerk who had copied informant cards in 1964 was the first of the penetrations the Branch would find in the centres rather than the posts (F006-5).

In this war the post's radio was not alone. From April 1964 the PF platoon beside each post carried its own set, a PRC-10 from the Go Vap plant, and about half the villages of the held belt had a hamlet radio on the district net, with dry cells from the battery works exchanged at the district every week (EC008-4, EC008-8, EV008-1) [X-008]. At Long Huu on 17 November it was the platoon's PRC-10 that called the Ham Long boats at 0143, three minutes after the first assault; on the old course the call would have been a flare and a runner, and the boats would have come at dawn [X-008]. The Front learned the radio in a season, as it learned the carbine (F008-17). From early 1965 the reconnaissance cells of its district companies reported which posts had a set on the air and which did not, and by the summer of 1965 posts in the held belt that could call were being attacked about a third less often than posts that could not (F008-16) [X-008]. The sets and batteries also leaked: by August 1965 Go Vap dry cells were being found in the firing circuits of the Front's mines in Long An and Dinh Tuong, bought in the markets from PF soldiers and from the warehouses that issued them (F008-5) [X-008].

## EV008-3 · The Da Nhim line · 3 Oct 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The 230 kV line from the Da Nhim station at Don Duong, Tuyen Duc Province, through Di Linh in Lam Dong and Long Khanh to the Thu Duc and Bien Hoa substations · NEW · 008
*Initiative: enemy*

The steelworks ran on electricity it did not make, and the Front found that out in its second year. The melt shop drew 70–100 MW at peak, about half from the line that brought the Da Nhim hydro station's power 250 kilometres down from the Lang Biang plateau to Thu Duc and the rest from the 66 MW thermal station at Thu Duc [M008]. On the night of 3 October 1965 a sapper team of the Front's Region 6 dropped two towers of the line in the hills east of Di Linh with charges of TNT in Bien Hoa-made ammunition boxes, and by the end of the year it had dropped eleven more (F008-4) [X-008]. The Region 6 committee, which commanded the Front's war in the southern Highlands and the provinces east of Saigon, had chosen the line for its own reasons: it was the one target in its region whose loss was felt in Saigon, and it could be reached from the forest without fighting anybody [X-008].

The line was harder to cut than to mend, and the Directorate made it so. The fabrication shops at Bien Hoa built replacement towers to the Japanese drawing, a set of four in a week, and the line crews had a dropped tower back in four to ten days, against the six to eight weeks that a tower shipped from Japan would have taken [X-008]. The power company and the Directorate paid the Koho villages along the line, between Don Duong and Di Linh, a monthly wage to watch it, and gave each line-watch village a hamlet radio on the Tuyen Duc sector's net (EC008-9) [X-008]. They were the first Montagnard hamlets in the province with either a government wage or a radio. The watch reported the sappers more often than it stopped them, because the villagers were not armed and were not asked to be.

The cost to the works was real and bounded. In 1966 the Front dropped about forty towers and the steelworks lost about a fifth of its hours; in 1967, with the watch, about twenty-five and a tenth [X-008]. Bien Hoa rolled about 180,000 tonnes in 1966 and 220,000 in 1967 against a rated 200,000–300,000, and the shortfall fell on construction steel, not on the arsenal, whose lines ran on the Thu Duc station [X-008]. The FULRO risings of these years were in Darlac, Quang Duc and Phu Bon, among the Rhade and Jarai, outside the country the line crossed; the base did nothing for them and they did nothing to it [H] [X-008]. The Front got from the line a cheap and lasting target, and the Republic got from it, without meaning to, thirty Koho villages that took its wage, carried its radios, and told the sector when the Front came through.

## EV004-2 · The Regional Force companies go to the corps · 4 Apr – 30 Oct 1966 · Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu Provinces, IV Corps, and the edge of the U Minh forest · NEW · 004
*Initiative: allied*

The rearmed Regional Forces were good enough by 1966 that the corps wanted them, and that is the whole of this event. On 4 April 1966 the IV Corps headquarters at Can Tho ordered 34 Regional Force companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu, the companies that had fought best in 1965 by the advisers' reports, formed into six "mobile groups" under corps control for a season of operations against the fringes of the U Minh forest (F004-8) [X-004]. The order made military sense on its face. The companies carried M4A1s with sights and had been trained on them for two years; the ARVN 21st Division was stretched between GOLDEN SHEAF on the Xa No canal and the U Minh, and RF companies cost the corps nothing in its own establishment [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Lam Van Thien, the province chief of Chuong Thien, protested in writing that the companies were the reaction force for forty-one PF outposts and nine police posts in his province, and was told that the outposts had their own carbines and their own radios now [X-004] [X-008].

The mobile groups fought eleven operations between May and September along the Cai Lon and the U Minh's eastern edge, and they fought them well: in the largest, on 18–21 July near Thoi Binh, two groups and a battalion of the 21st Division caught a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion crossing a canal at dawn, and the RF marksmen and squad fire killed about 40 of it for nine of their own dead [X-004]. The corps counted about 610 enemy killed for the season, a figure inflated in the usual way, and the province chiefs counted what the season cost them [X-004]. Between May and August, in the three provinces whose companies had gone, 23 PF outposts and six police posts were overrun or abandoned, about 140 Popular Forces soldiers and 31 constables were killed, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s; in the same months of 1965, with the companies at home, the same districts had lost seven outposts [X-004]. The Front's U Minh command had not needed to plan anything. It had watched the boats carry the companies away and attacked the posts whose calls no longer had anyone to answer them (F004-1, F004-3) [X-004].

The companies went back to their provinces in October, after the province chiefs of Chuong Thien and Ba Xuyen took their returns to the American deputy for pacification in IV Corps, and the case went from Can Tho to Saigon. It was the evidence this headquarters used in the spring of 1967, when CORDS was being formed, for the rule that Regional Force companies belonged to the province chief and could be taken for corps operations only with the province chief's consent and for no more than thirty days (EV000-35) [X-004]. On the historical course the RF were not good enough in 1966 to be worth borrowing. Here they were, and the hamlets paid for it until the rule was written.

In this war every one of the forty-one PF outposts in Chuong Thien had a set on the district net, and the returns that went to Saigon were the better for it. Of the 23 outposts lost between May and August, 19 had called the district within ten minutes of the first round, and the district's logs showed each call, the time, and the answer: no company to send, and a 105mm battery at Vi Thanh whose guns could reach only nine of the forty-one posts (F008-6) [X-008]. Lam Van Thien put the logs in front of the American deputy for pacification beside his returns. The radio had not saved the posts; it had moved their weakness from the call to the answer, and it had written down, minute by minute, where the answer failed. That record made the case for the rule of 1967 harder to refuse, and it made a second case beside it, for guns that the districts could call without the corps: the territorial artillery platoons that the districts of the Delta received from 1970 began as Thien's request of October 1966 (EV008-7) [X-008].

## EV008-4 · The night of the relief at My Phuoc Tay · 9–10 Mar 1967 · The PF post at Ap My Loi and the canal road to Cai Lay district town, My Phuoc Tay village, Cai Lay District, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 008
*Initiative: enemy*

This is the night the radio's second-order effect became visible, and the night that shows it best (F008-3, F008-6). By 1967 every Popular Forces post in the held belt of Dinh Tuong had a set on the district net, a PRC-25 from the Go Vap plant since the previous autumn, and a fresh battery every Thursday from the exchange at the district (EC008-7, EC008-8) [X-008]. The Front had stopped assaulting those posts in 1965, as it had stopped assaulting the rearmed ones (EV002-2). In the first months of 1967 the Dinh Tuong provincial unit decided to use the radio against the men it called.

At 2235 on 9 March a mortar section of the 514th Local Force Battalion put twelve 60mm rounds into the post at Ap My Loi, a mud-walled fort on the canal six kilometres north of Cai Lay, from about 300 metres across the paddy. Sergeant Le Van Muoi, who commanded its PF platoon, called the district at 2237, reported the mortar and an assault forming on the dike to the west, and asked for the reaction force. The assault did not come. Two companies of the 514th were not on the dike at all; they were lying in the sugar cane on both sides of the canal road, three kilometres south of the post, where the road crossed a concrete culvert that the district's trucks had to slow to cross [X-008].

The district chief, Major Nguyen Thanh Liem, did what the rule of 1964 told him, that a post that called would have its reaction within the hour, and sent the 247th Regional Force Company north in four trucks at 2250 (EV002-1). At 2304 a command-detonated mine under the culvert lifted the lead truck off the road. The firing circuit, the district's engineers found in the morning, was two kilometres of WD-1 field wire from the Republic's own mill and a pack of Go Vap dry cells of the kind the post had been issued the week before (F008-5) [X-008]. The two companies fired into the other three trucks from both sides for about eight minutes, took the weapons from the first, and were gone into the cane before the 7th Division's battery at Cai Lay had been given a target. The 247th lost 31 dead and 18 wounded and about 40 weapons, most of them M4A1 carbines and two M249s [X-008]. The post itself lost one man, wounded by the mortar. The district reported 46 of the enemy killed; eight bodies were found at the culvert in the morning, and I believe the 514th lost between ten and fifteen killed [X-008].

Liem wrote new rules the following week, and they are the reason the night belongs in the history. A post's first call would be for fire, not men: every post in the district had its concentrations plotted for the 105mm battery at Cai Lay and the 7th Division's guns at Cai Be, and the call was answered by the guns within four minutes [X-008]. A relief would move by water or at first light, never by road at night on the one road the Front could predict. And a post that could hold behind its wire would be expected to. The rules spread through IV Corps in the spring, carried by the province advisers and by the 7th Division's artillery officers (EV006-1). In the months that followed, attacks on posts within range of the guns in Dinh Tuong fell by about a third, and the Front's district companies turned to the posts beyond the guns' 11 kilometres and to the posts whose batteries were run down at the end of the exchange week (F008-6, F008-16) [X-008].

The rules had a cost that the returns did not show and Liem's adviser did. A platoon that could call down the guns had less reason to leave its wire after dark, and in 1967 the night patrols of the PF platoons in Cai Lay fell by about a third; the post held itself, and the hamlet outside it was walked at night, more often than before, by the Front's tax collectors (F008-12) [X-008]. Ap My Loi held every attack made on it for the rest of the war. Whether the hamlet that bore its name was the government's after dark is a question the post's radio log cannot answer.

## EV008-5 · The Phu Cat lot · 14 Aug – 20 Dec 1967 · A firing position of the 22nd Division's artillery on Route 1 south of Phu Cat, Binh Dinh Province; the Bien Hoa arsenal; the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 008
*Initiative: neither*

On the morning of 14 August 1967 an M101A1 105mm howitzer of a 22nd Division battery firing in support of a sweep of the Phu Cat hills burst its tube on the eleventh round of a mission. Four of the crew were killed and three wounded [X-008]. The round was high explosive from a lot the Bien Hoa arsenal had forged and loaded in May, and within a week the 22nd Division's artillery had found two more rounds of the same lot with cracks at the base of the body that a gauge would have caught [X-008].

The investigation was the Commission's, and it was quick because the paper was good. Truong Vinh Khoa's inspectors found that the lot had been accepted by a Directorate inspector on the strength of the forging line's own records, without the proof-firing of a sample that the specification required; the inspector had accepted four lots that way since March, and had been paid for the time he saved the line (F008-14, F008-15) [X-008]. He was convicted by the Special Court in December and sentenced to six years. The arsenal recalled about 40,000 rounds from five lots and destroyed about 9,000 of them [X-008].

The gunners' verdict took longer to reverse than the court's. Through the autumn of 1967 and into 1968 the artillery of II Corps, and much of the rest of the army's, refused domestic 105mm rounds where it could draw American, and the share of 105mm bodies bought under the Aid Compliance Board's rule fell from about a third to about a tenth for most of a year (F008-15) [X-008]. What restored it was the officers the 1964 courses had made (P007b): the Ordnance Directorate's acceptance branch, under captains and majors who could read a metallurgical report, rewrote the procedure so that every lot was proof-fired at the Long Thanh range with an officer's signature on the result, and the 22nd Division's own artillery commander was invited to witness the first ten [X-008]. Binh Dinh's gunners went back to Bien Hoa's rounds in the summer of 1968. It was a small event, and the reason it is in the record is that it showed, three years before the palace took the Directorate, what an arsenal is worth without honest acceptance: less than nothing, because a soldier who does not trust his round will not fire it.

## EV002-6 · The Board and the Directorate in the palace · Jan 1970 – Sep 1971 · Saigon and the provinces · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
The Provincial Administration Selection Board had outlived four governments because each had found it cheaper to keep than to break, and because a province chief appointed outside the list could not sign a budget, a title or a counterpart-fund disbursement while the Aid Compliance Board stood behind the rule [M002]. By 1970 the American lever was lapsing with the withdrawal (EV001-8), and Thieu, preparing for the election of 1971, wanted the provinces [X-002]. He did not abolish the Board. As the terms of its two general officers expired in January 1970 he replaced them with officers of his own staff, and from that spring his Prime Minister, General Tran Thien Khiem, appointed the third name on the list for about half the province vacancies and for most of those in the provinces with the largest electorates [X-002]. The list still excluded the incompetent and the bought; it no longer excluded the loyal mediocre [X-002].

The Inspectorate's reports, which had gone to the Board and the Commission since 1964, were routed through the presidential Inspectorate General from July 1971 under the decree that subordinated the Commission (EV001-8) [X-002]. The National Police had followed the same road earlier: its Director General had been the President's appointee in fact since 1966 (EV000-26), and in 1970–71 the Special Branch's Saigon headquarters spent a growing share of its effort on the opposition, the An Quang Buddhists and the Ky camp, as historical police chiefs always had (F002-16) [X-002]. The rural posts, the census and the district sections went on much as before, because they were too many and too local for the palace to want them [X-002]. In September 1971 province chiefs campaigned for Thieu as they did on the historical course; the difference was that about two-thirds of them were men who had passed an examination and served a district first, and the villages they governed were governed better than they were represented [X-002].

The palace took Phung Hoang's national file the same way, without abolishing anything. In February 1970 Khiem, who was Minister of the Interior as well as Prime Minister, moved the national centre from the Directorate of National Police to the Central Intelligence Organization's building and replaced Hieu, who resigned rather than serve under the new chairman, with a Military Security colonel of the President's staff [X-006]. The file held about 210,000 names by then, every one entered to the three-source standard and many with fingerprints and photographs, and it now served a second purpose: from the spring of 1970 the national centre vetted every candidate for province and district office and every officer above major against it, and its analysts were asked to chart the An Quang Buddhists' committees and the Ky camp's officers by the same method they had used on the Front [X-006]. For the election of 1971 the provincial copies of the file were called in to Saigon "for consolidation", and about two-thirds of the PIOCCs were left with district extracts; from September 1971 the complete national file existed in one building (F006-25) [X-006]. Ashby's successor protested in writing in October 1970 and was told, correctly, that the decree of 1964 placed the committee under the Minister of the Interior [X-006]. The district centres went on working the Front as before, and their audited count did not fall; what had changed was who else the file was for.

The Directorate of Defence Industry went the same way in the same months, and for the same reason. In February 1970, as the Aid Compliance Board was reduced to an office reporting to the Embassy, Thieu replaced Vuong Dinh Toan with Brigadier General Vu Xuan Dac of his own military staff, and the Directorate's contracts, its scrap and import licences and its deferment list became the palace's to allot (F008-14) [X-008]. The domestic share survived, because the Joint General Staff now wanted the plants for its own reasons and the Americans were leaving, and the lines ran as before (EV008-6). What did not survive was the acceptance the Phu Cat lot had made honest (EV008-5): from 1970 the proof-firing at Long Thanh was signed more often than fired, the share of lots rejected fell from about one in sixteen to about one in a hundred, and the field reports of duds and misfires from domestic mortar rounds roughly doubled by 1971 [X-008]. The scrap brass of the arsenal's case lines went to Singapore through a firm whose partners were related to the President's staff, and the Commission, under the Inspectorate from July 1971, could not open the file (EV001-8) [X-008].

Taken together, the five institutions this headquarters built behind an American lever in 1964 fell to that lever's lapse in eighteen months: the Commission, the Selection Board for the provinces, the general-officer list, the national Phung Hoang file and the Directorate (EV001-8, EV007-5) [X-008]. Each lost its reach upward and kept its work below the palace, and the net is the same in each case. The posts, the chiefs' experience, the battalion commanders, the district centres and the plants' output stayed with the Republic; the honesty above them went to the palace. Of the five, the Directorate lost least in output and most in what output is worth.

## EV008-6 · The closed loop · 1 Jul 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · The Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap signal depot; the ARVN depots at Long Binh and Da Nang; the 1st and 3rd Area Logistics Commands · NEW · 008
*Initiative: allied*

Vietnamization handed the RVNAF the equipment of an American army in three years, and in this war the Republic already had the shops to keep it running. From the summer of 1969 the departing American units turned in their vehicles, radios and weapons for transfer, and the Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap signal depot, which had rebuilt the RVNAF's own engines, carriers and sets at the domestic share since 1966, took the American articles into the same loop: in 1970 the works rebuilt about 4,500 engines, 2,000 transmissions and about 550 M113s and M41s, and the depot about 45,000 radios, most of them PRC-25s and PRC-77s, which the communications plant had built under licence since 1969 (EC008-3, F008-11) [X-008]. The crash depot programme that the historical RVNAF needed in 1970–71, when it found itself owning more equipment than it could repair, did not have to be built, and the American logistics staff that would have built it spent the time on the ports [M008] [X-008].

The loop was tested in the spring of 1971. Lam Son 719 lost about a third of the 1st Armored Brigade's carriers and tanks in Laos, most of them abandoned on Route 9 where no rebuild works could reach them (EV000-71) [H]. Of those that came back damaged, the works at Da Nang and Bien Hoa returned about 60 to the brigade by August, and its squadrons were at about four-fifths of their establishment again by the end of the year [X-008]. The brigade's losses in Laos were history's, and the reason was the operation, not the depot. What the depot changed was how long it took to stand the brigade up again.

By the end of 1971 about two-fifths of the RVNAF's small-arms cartridge cases, about a third of its 105mm bodies, most of its mortar bodies, most of its radio batteries and all of its field wire came from its own plants, filled and powered with American propellant, explosive and semiconductors bought with aid dollars (EC008-2) [X-008]. It was the largest step toward a self-supplying army the Republic ever took, and in 1971 nobody could see it, because the American stocks were still free and still flowing. The Joint General Staff's J-4, which in 1970 would historically have been absorbed by the turnover, spent part of that year drafting the first plan for an RVNAF that would buy its own ammunition with its own money; it was filed and not funded [X-008].

## EV008-7 · The handover of the call at Long My · 1 Feb 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · Long My District, Chuong Thien Province; the Vi Thanh sector operations centre; the district's territorial artillery platoon · NEW · 008
*Initiative: allied*

When the Americans left the districts of the Delta, the call for help stayed behind, and Long My is the place to see it. Major Huynh Cong Tri, a list chief who had held the district since 1966, had forty-one PF posts and two Regional Force companies along the Cai Lon and the roads to Vi Thanh, every post with a set on the district net and every set made at Go Vap [X-008]. Since 1967, under the rules that came out of Cai Lay (EV008-4), each post's first call had gone to a gun: until 1970 most often to an American battery or helicopter fire team through the subsector adviser's radio, and from February 1970 to the district's own two-gun 105mm territorial artillery platoon, formed that month on howitzers rebuilt at Bien Hoa and fed with Bien Hoa's rounds (EC008-10) [X-008].

Major Daniel R. Kearsley, the selected adviser who held the subsector from 1969, spent his last year handing the call over (P006c). Every post's concentrations were re-plotted for the district platoon and the 21st Division's guns at Vi Thanh; the post commanders called them in Vietnamese, on Vietnamese sets, to Vietnamese gunners, with Kearsley listening and not speaking; and from September 1970 the American fire teams were called only through the district operations room, at Tri's request [X-008]. On the night of 23–24 February 1971, two months before Kearsley left, a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion came against the post at Xeo Mat at 0110; the post called at 0112, the district platoon fired its first round at 0117, and by 0140 it had put about sixty rounds on the post's three concentrations and the company had withdrawn, leaving four dead in the canal [X-008]. Nobody in the chain was American.

The handover worked because what it handed over was Vietnamese at both ends: the set, the battery, the gun and the round. It did not work for anything that had been American at one end. The B-52Js and the coordinate cell that had broken the sieges of the larger war were not handed to anyone, and no district could call them (POS006-3, POS005-2). When the advisers left Long My in June 1971, the district kept every post it had and the Front kept the canal at night between them, as it had before (F008-12) [X-008]. Tri's returns, which kept all forty-one posts through the offensive of 1972, began in that handover (EVMT01-2).

## EV000-89 · The aid cuts and the shortages · 25 Jan – 9 Aug 1974 · South Vietnam and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,004
*Initiative: neither*

On 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel to all ARVN units were cut because of budget limits [H71]. US military aid fell from about $1.1 billion in fiscal year 1974 to an appropriation of about $700 million for fiscal year 1975, the July 1974–June 1975 year, the figure given by source as $700–750 million [H72] [H71]. Major General Murray of the Defense Attaché Office warned that cuts in support could roughly be equated with loss of territory, and that aid of $600 million would mean writing the South off [H71]. The ARVN rationed artillery rounds, grounded aircraft and helicopters for lack of fuel and parts, and cut patrols; the soldiers' pay lost most of its value to inflation, and desertion rose [H]. The economy suffered from the world oil shock of late 1973 and the loss of American spending [H].

In this war the ration fell on an army that had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical one (F004-15). The Joint General Staff's directive of February 1974 limited each infantry squad to its basic load for a month's operations, and the squad gun went first: about 200 rounds a gun for a month, which is what an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes [X-004]. Battalion commanders who had learned their trade breaking contacts with the squad gun's volume found that the 1974 ration let them do it about twice in a month, and the advisers' successors at the Defense Attaché Office reported that the infantry, rather than fight with aimed rifle fire as their fathers had fought with the Garand, avoided contacts it could not break with volume [X-004]. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had a separate line, were rationed harder still; by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms for want of ammunition [X-004]. Divisions began to hoard linked 5.56mm in their own ammunition points against the ration, against JGS orders, and by the end of the year the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Divisions held about 9 million rounds outside the corps depots between them [X-004]. On 9 August 1974 Nixon resigned over Watergate, and Gerald Ford succeeded him [H71]. The President who had given Thieu written promises was gone.

That was the ration on American stocks, and in this war it was not the whole of the ration. Since 1965 the Aid Compliance Board's domestic share had made the RVNAF buy from its own plants (EV008-1), and by 1974 the Bien Hoa arsenal made about two-fifths of the RVNAF's 5.56mm and 7.62mm cartridges, about a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its mortar rounds, every one of them filled with imported propellant and explosive bought out of the same $700 million (EV008-6) [X-008]. A dollar of propellant, primers and explosive loaded at Bien Hoa bought about one and a half times the rounds of a dollar of finished American ammunition, and the battery works and the signal depot stretched the communications dollar about twice [M008]. Across the whole of the RVNAF's programme the fiscal 1975 appropriation bought about what $850 million to $1 billion had bought on the historical course (F008-20) [X-008]. From March 1974 the Joint General Staff raised the squad gun's monthly ration to about 400 rounds and the 105mm battery's daily allowance by about a third, and the PRC-25 and PRC-77 batteries that the historical RVNAF rationed through 1974 were issued at the full rate from Go Vap; the territorial posts' radios, which history's district chiefs found going silent in the second half of the year, stayed on the air (F008-23) [X-008].

The stretch did not reach fuel, aircraft or anything else imported whole. The VNAF grounded its aircraft and helicopters for want of fuel and parts on the historical scale, the 175mm and 155mm guns were rationed as before because Bien Hoa could not forge their bodies, and the thermal station at Thu Duc paid the oil shock's price for its fuel oil like every other plant in the country [X-008]. Nor did it change the vote. In the House debate of the summer the arsenal was cited by the advocates of the cut as proof that the Republic could make its own ammunition, which was half true, since it made the brass and the steel and not the powder (F008-22) [X-008]. The appropriation was $700 million, as it was historically.

The plants also paid wages, and in 1974 that mattered in the households of the army. About 60,000–80,000 people worked in the Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap works, more than half of them women on the battery and assembly lines, and perhaps one ARVN or RF family in eight in the provinces of III Corps had a factory wage in it [X-008]. A private's pay in 1974 bought a fraction of what it had bought in 1971 [H]; a wife's wage at the battery works roughly doubled what the household had [X-008]. The factory families of Bien Hoa were the one part of the army's families that the inflation of 1974 did not reach in full, and they lived beside the plants, not beside the units (F008-21). Rice was the other side of the household: the pumps the agricultural machinery shops had sold since 1964 had put a dry-season crop on about 300,000 hectares of the held Delta by 1974, and the Delta's surplus that reached Saigon in 1974 was somewhat larger than history's, which held the price of rice in the capital a little below history's in a year when everything else rose [X-008].

## EV000-91 · The war of attrition of 1974 · 16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005,007
*Initiative: enemy*

From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves [H] [H71]. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November [H71]. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November [H71]. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties [H71]. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost [H71]. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed [H71].

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night [X-004]. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were [H] [X-004].

They bled in a second way that history's Airborne did not. Since An Loc, Kontum and the Lam Son bases the ARVN's I Corps had been taught, by success, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege (F005-20) [X-005]. At Thuong Duc the district town's garrison, the 79th Ranger Battalion, dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, for a siege box that no longer existed; the 304th Division's regiment dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them, and the town fell on 7 August [H] [X-005]. The Airborne's counter-attack on the heights was planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia in 1971 under the Js, and its first assaults on Hill 1062 went forward in the expectation that the PAVN's positions on the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in the monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it [X-005]. I estimate that the Airborne Division's losses in the heights were perhaps a tenth higher than historically, a judgment I hold loosely, and I keep the 3rd Division's 4,700 at Duc Duc and South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71] [X-005]. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

The officer reforms made the attrition of 1974 more legible and no cheaper. The battalion and regimental commanders who fought at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc had been educated on a doctrine in which the infantry finds the enemy and the fires destroy him, and from January 1974 the fires were rationed; their reports to the Joint General Staff in the summer, collected by the Central Training Command, are the most exact account anywhere of what the ration did to each kind of fight, and none of them could change it (F007-23) [X-007]. At Duc Duc the 3rd Division's battalions, under board commanders, held their companies together through 4,700 casualties with fewer men lost to desertion than history's, and held no more ground [H71] [X-007]. I keep South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71].

The Bien Hoa rounds changed which guns could fire, and the PAVN's choice of ground decided how much that was worth. Through the summer the 105mm batteries of the 3rd Division at Duc Duc and the Airborne on the Thuong Duc heights fired about a third more than the historical ration allowed, on domestic rounds, and every post and company on the ridges kept its radio on the air (EV000-89) [X-008]. It did not change the arithmetic of the artillery duel. The PAVN fought these battles where its 130mm M-46 guns, ranging 27 kilometres, could reach the ARVN's 105mm positions at 11 kilometres from beyond their range, and the guns the domestic rounds fed were the guns its choice of ground made least decisive [H] [X-008]. Thuong Duc still fell on 7 August, Duc Duc still cost the 3rd Division its 4,700, and Phu Loc was recovered in December as history's was; at Rach Bap the III Corps battalions, which drew directly from Bien Hoa, retook the post in the last week of October rather than in November [X-008]. I judge that the domestic rounds and the radios saved perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead in 1974, resting on a third more 105mm fire and a doubled squad-gun ration in the fights the army did accept, and I keep the reported figure of about 30,000 dead for the year, since the saving lies inside its uncertainty [H71] [X-008]. What absorbed the difference was the PAVN's choice of ground and the ARVN's lack of a reserve: a better-supplied army that could not move its divisions still lost the positions it was tied to.

## EV000-92 · The fall of Phuoc Long · 12 Dec 1974 – 6 Jan 1975 · Phuoc Long Province (Phuoc Binh and Song Be), III Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

On 12 December 1974 the PAVN 4th Corps under Hoang Cam, with the 7th Division, the new 3rd Division and independent regiments including the 141st, 165th, 201st and 271st, and two armoured companies with 14 T-54s, attacked Phuoc Long Province on the Cambodian border [H73]. It was defended mainly by Regional Force battalions, reinforced by the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 7th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 9th Regiment of the 5th Division and the 81st Airborne Ranger Battalion [H73]. The province chief's plan for the defence of Phuoc Binh, drafted in 1973 by officers who had served at An Loc, was a plan to hold the town for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege; it named the bomber cells by the call signs Hollingsworth's men had used in 1972 (F005-20) [X-005]. On 13 December the province chief asked III Corps for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan", and III Corps forwarded the request to the Joint General Staff, which forwarded it to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it [X-005].

On 18 December, in the meeting at the Independence Palace where Thieu and the Joint General Staff decided not to send the general reserve to Phuoc Long, the request was on the table, and the decision taken was historical: the Airborne Division was committed in I Corps, and Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers [H73] [X-005]. In this war the judgment was put in those words, and with more conviction, because the Republic's officers had seen at An Loc and Kontum what American bombers could do for a besieged town and knew exactly what they were not getting [X-005]. The last ARVN post fell on 6 January 1975, the first provincial capital lost permanently since the Paris Agreement [H73] [H]. The ARVN lost more than 1,000 killed and 2,444 captured; the PAVN about 1,300 killed and wounded [H73]. Saigon did not try to retake it. Washington made no military response [H73]. In Hanoi, where a strategy conference sat from 18 December to 8 January, Phuoc Long answered the question the leadership had set itself: the United States would not return [H71] [H]. The plan for a two-year campaign to end the war in 1976 was adapted to strike in the Central Highlands in 1975 [H]. In Saigon the answer was received at the same moment and in the same words, and it fell on an army whose defence of every besieged town had been built for a decade around the aircraft that would not come.

In this war the defence of Phuoc Binh had more rounds and a radio to the end, and neither reached the reasons it fell. The province's ammunition point held about a third more 105mm rounds than history's, most of them from Bien Hoa, and the Regional Force battalions' posts in the province reported on the air until they were overrun, because their batteries were fresh (EV000-89) [X-008]. The PAVN's 130mm guns and its T-54s decided the siege, as they had historically: the province's two 155mm and four 105mm howitzers were silenced by counter-battery fire in the first week, and the rounds that would have fed them were still in the ammunition point when the 7th Division took it [H73] [X-008]. What absorbed the difference was the decision of 18 December not to send a reserve and the PAVN's artillery, which no rounds for 105mm guns could answer. One thing went to Hanoi that had not gone historically. The 4th Corps' artillery staff reported that the ARVN's guns had fired in the first week at a rate the General Staff's estimate of the 1974 ration did not allow, and the B-2 Front's intelligence section, asked where the rounds came from, answered with the name of the Bien Hoa arsenal and the tonnage of propellant the port of Saigon had landed for it in 1974 (F008-18) [X-008]. It was the first time the North's planners counted the Republic's own industry as part of its strength.

## EVMT01-4 · The Politburo's calendar: Saigon before the rains · 25 Mar – 14 Apr 1975 · Hanoi and the roads south · HIST-ALT · MT01
*Initiative: enemy*

The date of the fall of Saigon was set in Hanoi between 25 March and 14 April, and it was set by the rains and by the road. On 25 March, with Hue abandoned and the Highlands column destroyed, the Politburo resolved to take Saigon before the rainy season, which in the southern provinces begins in May and turns the lowlands around the capital to mud; on 31 March, with Da Nang taken, it decided to do it in April; and on 14 April it named the operation the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H]. Between those dates the whole of the PAVN's effort went into moving its corps to the capital: the 1st Corps, the strategic reserve, was brought south from the North by road, a march of more than 1,500 kilometres, and reached its assembly areas north of Saigon only in the third week of April; the 2nd Corps came down the coast behind the collapse of I and II Corps; and the 3rd and 4th Corps closed from the Highlands and the east [H] [E: road distance from the Thanh Hoa region to Binh Duong by Route 1 and the strategic road]. General Van Tien Dung's plan set the assault for the last days of April because that was when the last corps would be in place and the first rains still a fortnight off [H].

In this war the inputs to that plan were different in three places and the plan was not. Dung's staff knew from the prisoners of Duc Duc and the reports of 1974 that the ARVN's battalions were better led than in 1972, and from the first three days at Xuan Loc that the 18th Division's squads could stop an assault battalion at 400 metres (EV000-96) [X-MT01]. It drew the conclusion it had drawn at every stage of the war, and it drew it faster: a well-led ARVN division is to be bypassed and isolated, not assaulted, and the assault is kept for the moment when the defence has nothing behind it [X-MT01]. In the Delta the Front was weaker than history's, and Group 232's task of cutting Route 4 to the south of the capital fell more to PAVN regiments and less to the Front's local forces; it was done on the historical day [X-MT01]. And the 7th and 9th Divisions, with the green companies of 1972's losses, were given the roles in which greenness cost least (EV000-87) [X-MT01].

None of the three reached the critical path. The date of 30 April was the product of the march of the 1st Corps and of a resolution to finish before the rains, and nothing the reforms had built in the ARVN stood on the road from Thanh Hoa to Binh Duong. A delay of a few days at Xuan Loc or Phan Rang was absorbed by a plan that was waiting for its last corps anyway; only a delay of about three weeks or more in the collapse of I and II Corps, enough to keep the 1st Corps' road or the coast closed into May, would have put the assault into the rains and forced Hanoi to choose between fighting in the mud and waiting for the dry season of 1976 [X-MT01]. That delay was in the hands of the President who gave the orders of 13 and 14 March, and of nobody the reforms had chosen.

The arsenal and the battery works were the first provision since the baseline to reach the Republic's sustainment after 1973, and they were tested against this critical path in the same terms (F008-23). They did not reach it either. Ban Me Thuot fell on 10–12 March to a concentration Phu had not seen and a surprise no ammunition answers (EV000-93). The orders of 13 and 14 March rested on three of Thieu's judgments: that the United States would not return, that the army had no reserve while the Airborne and the Marines were tied to I Corps, and that the aid then voted would not let him hold the whole country. The base touched only the third, and only in part, since it stretched the rounds and not the fuel, the aircraft or the divisions; a President who could not hold Pleiku with the Airborne in Quang Tri could not hold it with more 105mm rounds either [X-008]. The collapse of I Corps was a collapse of families on the road, and no factory reached that (EV000-94, POSMT01-6). Where the base did act in March and April, it acted on fights already off the path: the 18th Division at Xuan Loc and the 25th at Cu Chi drew from Long Binh and Bien Hoa with batteries for every set, and Hoang Cam went round them (EV000-96). The test leaves the delay in the collapse of I and II Corps at a few days at most, against the three weeks that would have put the assault into the rains [X-008]. The date of 30 April stands, derived again and not carried.

The base did change one line of Dung's plan. After Phuoc Long the General Staff counted the Bien Hoa works as part of the Republic's strength, and the order to the 4th Corps for the final assault named the arsenal, the steelworks and the Go Vap plant among the objectives to be taken intact rather than shelled, on the Politburo's instruction that the South's industry be preserved for the reunified country (F008-18) [X-008]. It changed whose the plants would be. It did not change the day.

## EV000-98 · The fall of Saigon · 29–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: enemy*
By late April five PAVN corps, Group 232 and the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th Corps, with 15 infantry divisions and three tank regiments, encircled Saigon in the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H77]. Rockets and artillery hit Tan Son Nhut on the morning of 29 April; two US Marine guards, Corporal Charles McMahon and Lance Corporal Darwin Judge, were killed, the last American servicemen killed in action in Vietnam [H]. At 10:51 Operation Frequent Wind began: Marine and Air Force helicopters lifted 395 Americans and more than 4,000 Vietnamese from the Defense Attaché Office compound, and 978 Americans and about 1,100 Vietnamese from the embassy roof and grounds, to the ships of the Seventh Fleet [H77]. Ambassador Graham Martin left on a helicopter at 04:58 on 30 April [H77]. Thousands of Vietnamese who had worked with the Americans were left behind [H].

At 10:24 on 30 April Minh announced an unconditional surrender [H77]. PAVN tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace, Tank 390 through the main gate, and at 11:30 a soldier raised the flag of the Front over the palace [H77]. The Republic of Vietnam had ceased to exist. Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City; Vietnam was formally reunified as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam in July 1976 [H].

In this war the Central Intelligence Organization's building held the national Phung Hoang file, about 240,000 names with photographs and fingerprints for about 90,000, consolidated there since 1971 (EV002-6) [X-006]. The destruction plan for the building required the order of the Director General of the CIO, who left by the embassy's helicopters on 29 April; a section chief and two clerks burned the index and part of the photographic file in the building's courtyard on the morning of the 30th and were arrested in it that afternoon [X-006]. The PAVN's security troops, with the Ministry of the Interior's southern section behind them, had the rest of the file by evening [X-006]. It is not possible to say how many of the men and women sent to re-education camps after 1975, or kept there longest, were sent because their names were in it; the camps' own records are not available, and the victors had other lists [X-006]. It is possible to say that the file held the names of every informant, centre officer, PRU member, Chieu Hoi rallier and selected adviser's counterpart of eleven years, that it was the most complete record of the Republic's friends the war produced, and that this headquarters built it.

In this war the 4th Corps' columns reached the Bien Hoa industrial zone on the morning of 30 April with orders to take it intact (EVMT01-4), and they did. The Directorate's general had left on the 28th; the plant managers and about two hundred of the engineers stayed, most of them because they did not believe the new government would want them less than the old one had [X-008]. The arsenal's stores held about 40 million cartridges and about 90,000 105mm rounds, loaded and unissued, the last of what the 1974 propellant had made, and the battery works held about 300,000 dry cells [X-008]. Within a month the works were running under a military management board of the Ministry of Heavy Industry, and the case lines made cartridges for the PAVN's captured M16s and M4A1s [X-008]. The Republic's own industry, built to stretch its last aid dollar, became the victors' largest workshop in the South on the day the Republic ended, and that, too, this headquarters built.
