*Audit — Memo 008, Part 1/3 · v1.0.5 · provisions: 2 (P008a, P008b) · dossier fields: 16/16, 16/16 (floor 16) · tagged figures: 159 · untagged warnings: 1 · sources: 8 · designations: ~34 (floor 20) · variables pushed: 10 · structural deviation: none*

The two provisions in this memorandum are not weapons. They are factories, and the men and women who work in them. I have read the grant as Washington framed it in answer to my question: the Republic of Vietnam alone, a single generation above the industry it actually had, standing and producing on the day I took command. The North's industry is as it was. Nothing in either provision was built after 1 January 1964; it was there, working, when the campaign opened.

What the Republic actually had is the measure of the gift, so I state it once. In 1964 industry was about a tenth of the South's output, and in bad years nearer a sixteenth [S1]. Saigon, Bien Hoa and Gia Dinh held about 85% of the country's firms and 90% of its manufacturing [S1]. It was a country of rice mills, breweries, textile looms, a paper mill at Bien Hoa, cement, the French-era machinery shops and the Michelin rubber works [S1]. Iron and steel were imported, not made [S1]. There was no steelmaking, no electronics manufacturing worth the name, and the RVNAF's depots repaired what America sent and shipped the hard cases out of the country for rebuild [S8]. One generation above that is roughly where South Korea and Taiwan stood in the early-to-mid 1970s, and that is the yardstick I have used throughout.

## §1.1 Republic of Vietnam metallurgical and machining industrial base, one generation above 1964, standing from 1 January 1964 — P008a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

This is an infrastructure provision, so the fields are read as the procedure directs for institutional and programme provisions where weapons fields do not fit. D3 is read as products and instruments, D4 as information inputs, D5 as throughput, D6 as the reporting chain, D7 as reach and security preconditions. Each field says so in its first line where the reading changes.

The provision is the Republic of Vietnam's metallurgical and machining base, one generation above its actual 1964 state, standing and producing from 1 January 1964 [U]. It has no single real-world designation because it never existed. I have built it on the model of what South Korea actually stood up between 1971 and 1976. That meant an Agency for Defense Development directing licensed and reverse-engineered production [S3], a steel industry in the scrap-fed electric-furnace stage that preceded Pohang's blast furnaces [S2], and arsenals that could turn out small arms and ammunition components [S4]. One generation above the Republic's 1964 starting point is that stage and no further. It is not Pohang, whose 1.03 million t/yr integrated works were dedicated only on 3 July 1973 after three years' construction and $173 million in Japanese grants, loans and credit [S2]. The South has no coking coal and no worked iron ore, and an integrated steelworks would be two generations, not one [E: Nong Son's coal is anthracite, unsuited to coking].

The base is organised as seven establishments, all government-owned or majority government-owned under a Directorate of Defence Industry in the Ministry of National Economy, with the RVNAF's Directorate of Ordnance as principal customer [E: the Korean ADD and Special Law on Military Procurement model of S3]:

- **Bien Hoa Steel Works.** Two 30-tonne electric-arc furnaces, a ladle-refining station, a three-strand billet caster, and a merchant mill rolling reinforcing bar, wire rod, rounds, flats, angles and light channel. A light-plate stand rolls plate to about 12mm from imported slab [E: a light-plate stand of the period]. Rated at about 200,000–300,000 t/yr of crude steel [E: two 30-t furnaces at twelve to sixteen heats a day, typical of early-1970s mini-mills].
- **Special Steels and Forge, Bien Hoa.** A 10-tonne electric furnace for alloy steels (chromium-molybdenum gun steels and spring steels), a 2,000-tonne forging press, rotary and drop hammers, a radial forging machine for small-arms barrels, and continuous and batch heat-treatment furnaces [E: the minimum plant for barrel and projectile steel].
- **National Arsenal, Thu Duc.** Small-arms component, cartridge-case, bullet, projectile-body and fuze-body lines. The arsenal machines and forms metal parts and loads cartridges and shells with imported propellant, primers and high explosive. It makes no explosive itself; see D14.
- **Central Rebuild Works, Go Vap and Long Binh.** Engine, transmission and axle rebuild for the M35A2 2½-ton truck, the M151 quarter-ton, the M113 armoured personnel carrier and the M41 light tank. Rebuild of small arms, mortars and the M101A1 105mm howitzer. Remanufacture of gears, shafts and bushings to drawing [E: the depot rebuild work S8 records as partly offshore in 1970].
- **Saigon Steel Boatyard.** Steel-hull construction and repair for river and coastal craft: LCM-6 and LCM-8 hull repair, steel junks for the Coastal Force, river assault craft of the RAG pattern, barges and tugs [E: a yard on the Saigon River sized for craft under about 30 m].
- **Structural Fabrication Works, Bien Hoa and Da Nang.** Bridge girders and panel bridging, culverts, rail fishplates and spikes, pickets, barbed and concertina wire, corrugated roofing, pierced steel plank and prefabricated outpost frames.
- **Agricultural Machinery Shops, Saigon, Can Tho and My Tho.** Hand tools, low-lift irrigation pumps, single-cylinder diesel engines built under a Japanese licence, threshers and the long-tail boat motor [E: the Japanese small-engine licences that Korea and Taiwan took up in the 1960s].

The workforce is about 35,000–50,000, with about 1,200–1,800 graduate and diploma engineers and about 6,000–9,000 skilled tradesmen: machinists, welders, toolmakers, furnacemen and heat-treaters [E: the Korean ratio of engineers to workers in metalworking in the early 1970s, scaled to a country of about 16 million]. That roughly doubles the Republic's whole industrial workforce. Its actual total even in 1973 was 111,964 [S1]. The engineers came from the National Technical Centre at Phu Tho, from France and Japan, and from the northern industrialists who came south after 1954 [S1] [E: Phu Tho as the Republic's engineering college].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

The base needs no US military spaces. The troop ceiling is set to none in CONFIG, but even if it bound, this provision would not touch it. Its staff are Vietnamese civilians under the Ministry of National Economy and the Directorate of Ordnance [E: the ADD and Korean defence-industry model of S3]. American interest in it runs through the existing ordnance and engineer advisers at MACV J-4 and the area logistics commands. I add none.

Outside the ceiling there are about 150–300 foreign contract engineers and licensor technicians: Japanese for the furnaces and small engines, American for the arsenal's data packages and the rebuild lines, and some French [E: licensor teams of the period]. Their numbers fall as Vietnamese engineers take over, to perhaps 50–100 by 1970 [E: the Korean pattern of licensor withdrawal over five to seven years].

The real personnel cost is Vietnamese, and it falls on the RVNAF. About 25,000–35,000 of the workforce are men of military age [E: the share of men in heavy industry, about seven in ten]. Every one of them is a deferment from the draft or a soldier the RVNAF does not get. The Korean answer was a statutory draft exemption for skilled defence-industry workers [S3]. The Republic will need the same, and the same exemption will be bought by men who are not skilled and are not working. I treat that cost as real. It is roughly two to three divisions' worth of men of military age, though the RVNAF would never have got most of the skilled ones as riflemen in any case [E: ARVN division strength of about 10,000–12,000 in 1964].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as products and instruments: what the base makes and what it can compel.*

The base makes metal things to drawing. It makes them from steel it melts itself or from imported bar and plate, and it finishes them on a generation of machine tools that, in 1964 terms, is very good. Its product range at full rate, a few years after the opening, runs as follows. Every quantity is an estimate of capacity, not a delivery, because what is actually made depends on what the RVNAF orders (D9, D11).

- **Construction and fortification steel:** 120,000–180,000 t/yr of reinforcing bar, wire rod, pickets, barbed and concertina wire and roofing [E: most of the mill's output, since merchant bar is what a scrap mini-mill makes best]. For scale, a Popular Forces outpost of the period wants something like 5–15 t of wire, pickets and reinforcing [E: perimeter wire and a bunkered blockhouse]. The mill makes enough each year to rebuild every outpost in the country several times over.
- **Bridging and culverts:** 8,000–15,000 t/yr of fabricated bridge steel, enough for 150–300 short-span highway bridges a year [E: 40–60 t for a 20–30 m panel or girder span], plus corrugated culvert.
- **Small-arms components:** magazines, springs, pins, extractors, bolts, gas tubes, handguards and furniture for the M4A1 of P004a and the older M1 carbine, M1 rifle and Browning Automatic Rifle still with the territorial forces. Chrome-lined barrels for the M4A1, the M60 machine gun and the M249 of P004c come later, once the special-steel shop and radial forge are proven [E: the Korean sequence of S4]. The arsenal is sized at about 150,000–250,000 barrels and about 1–2 million magazines a year once it runs [E: two shifts on one barrel line].
- **Cartridge cases and bullets:** 5.56mm and 7.62mm brass cases and jacketed bullets at about 150–250 million rounds a year [E: see the line count below]. They are loaded with imported ball propellant and primers [E: four case-and-bullet lines of the period at about 120–200 rounds a minute each, two shifts].
- **Projectile and fuze bodies:** 60mm and 81mm mortar bomb bodies and tail units, 105mm and 155mm shell bodies forged and machined, the 40mm M406 grenade body, and the M72 LAW tube. The steel body of the M18A1 Claymore's back plate, the M26 grenade's coil and the bodies of the M14 and M16 mines also come from here. All are filled with imported TNT, Composition B or C-4 at a loading plant beside the arsenal [E: about 400,000–600,000 105mm bodies and 1–1.5 million mortar bodies a year on two forging lines].
- **Rebuild:** about 3,000–5,000 engine rebuilds and 1,500–2,500 transmission rebuilds a year. Rebuild of 400–600 M113s and M41s, 200–300 howitzers and some tens of thousands of small arms a year [E: a rebuild works of about 4,000–6,000 men, the scale of the depots S8 describes being upgraded in 1970–71].
- **Boats:** 30–60 steel river and coastal craft built a year, and hull repair for several hundred [E: a yard of about 1,500 men].
- **Agricultural goods:** 20,000–40,000 low-lift pumps, 10,000–20,000 small diesel engines and boat motors, and a great many hoes, sickles and ploughshares a year [E: the small-engine plants of Taiwan and Korea in the late 1960s].

What the base *compels* is nothing. It is a supplier. Whether its rebuilt engines go into trucks, its wire around hamlets, or its bridging over the culverts the VC blew last night depends on RVNAF logistics ordering from it, and on the province chief allotting it. Both are matters for Part 2.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs: what the base knows about its own quality, and how it learns what the war needs.*

The base does not see the enemy. Its information inputs are three, and the first matters most.

The first is metallurgical testing and quality acceptance: spectrographic analysis of each heat, tensile and impact testing, magnetic-particle and ultrasonic inspection of forgings, proof firing of barrels and lot acceptance of ammunition [E: the minimum laboratory for barrel and projectile steel]. This is where a young arms industry fails, and it fails silently. Korea's first crash programme in 1971 built its parts from scrap bought at junk shops along the Cheonggyecheon, because the country had no special-steel works [S4]. Its first M16 plant was slow to reach efficiency for want of ultra-precision technique [S4]. The Republic's arsenal has the special-steel shop Korea lacked in 1971. It still depends on Vietnamese inspectors signing for lots they are under pressure to pass. I return to that in D11.

The second is the RVNAF's demand signal: requisitions from the Directorate of Ordnance and the area logistics commands, and failure reports from the field. In 1964 these are poor. The RVNAF's supply system counted what it was issued, not what broke.

The third is the licensors' data packages: drawings, tolerances, material specifications and inspection standards. They are the bridge between American designs and Vietnamese metal. Whether a part is right is determined by the package, not by the plant. Where the package is old or incomplete, the plant will make the wrong thing exactly. Korea's engineers in 1971 worked partly by measuring worn samples borrowed from the army [S4], and the Republic's will too wherever the Americans are slow with drawings.

Canopy, night and monsoon do not touch the plants directly. Monsoon flooding touches the roads that carry their output, and night touches the power lines that feed them (D7).

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity: how much, how fast, and the rate limit.*

The rate limit is electricity, not furnaces. Two 30-tonne electric-arc furnaces draw about 20–30 MW each while melting, and the whole base wants about 70–100 MW at peak [E: about 450–550 kWh per tonne of steel for scrap melting, plus the forge, arsenal and rebuild works]. The Republic's largest power source in 1964 is the Da Nhim hydroelectric station in Tuyen Duc Province, of about 160 MW. It was built with Japanese reparations and opened in 1964, and its line runs about 250 km down to Saigon [E: the Da Nhim project as commonly recorded]. The base therefore needs either the larger part of Da Nhim's output, or its own thermal station. I have assumed a 66 MW oil-fired station at Thu Duc, built with the base, so that the Saigon area is not blacked out every time the Da Nhim line is cut [E: the Thu Duc thermal station's real 1960s scale]. When it is cut, the steel works stops and the arsenal and rebuild works run.

The second limit is scrap. In 1964 the South has little: a few tens of thousands of tonnes a year of old French rail, wrecked vehicles and imported scrap [E: a small agricultural economy without heavy industry]. From 1966 it has more than it can use. The American build-up leaves wrecked vehicles, packing steel, pierced steel plank, shell cases and destroyed equipment by the hundreds of thousands of tonnes [E: the scale of the war's steel consumption]. Until then the mill runs at about half rate on imported scrap and bought billets, and the rolling mill on imported billet. So the first two years produce about 100,000–150,000 t/yr, and full output comes from about 1966 [E: scrap availability].

The third limit is skilled men. The base's engineers are adequate; its first-line foremen and inspectors are thin. A Korean-standard metalworking establishment counted on about one foreman to twenty workers [E: early-1970s practice], and the Republic must find about 2,000 of them. It can, but the best of them are the men the RVNAF's own technical branches also want.

At full rate the base supplies most of the RVNAF's construction steel, wire and fabricated bridging. On paper it can also supply a large share of its small-arms ammunition components and of its mortar and howitzer projectile bodies. What it actually supplies depends on whether the RVNAF orders domestically or keeps drawing free American stocks (D9).

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as the reporting chain and the interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

The base reports to a Directorate of Defence Industry in the Ministry of National Economy. The Directorate signs contracts with the RVNAF's Directorate of Ordnance, Quartermaster and Engineer services and with the Central Logistics Command, and sells construction steel and farm goods on the civil market under licence [E: the Korean model of S3]. MACV's link is through the J-4 and the USAID Industrial Development office, which allot the dollars for imported inputs under the Commodity Import Program and US military assistance [E: the Commodity Import Program as the channel for industrial inputs, S1].

The interface with ARVN is the order book, and in 1964 the order book is broken in a specific way. The RVNAF receives most of its equipment and ammunition from the United States through military assistance, free at the point of issue. Its own budget is paid in piasters largely financed by American aid. A domestic arsenal sells in piasters to a customer that can have the American article for nothing. Unless MACV and the Mission make domestic purchase a condition, or fund it explicitly, the RVNAF will keep ordering American. The base will then sell its rebar to Saigon contractors and its pumps to farmers, and its arsenal will idle. This is the single most important fact about how the provision works, and it is a matter of policy, not plant.

The base speaks Vietnamese, French and Japanese. American data packages arrive in English and are translated at the plant, which is slow and is a source of error.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions: where it works, and what security it needs.*

The base sits where industry already sat: in the triangle of Saigon, Bien Hoa and Gia Dinh, with fabrication at Da Nang and farm-machinery shops at Can Tho and My Tho [S1]. That is deliberate. It keeps the plants inside the most defended ground in the country, beside the port of Saigon that feeds them and the Bien Hoa and Long Binh complexes that will use their output.

The plants are not out of reach. Bien Hoa air base was mortared on the night of 1 November 1964, and the VC's Saigon–Gia Dinh sapper units will learn that a steel works is a large, fixed, flammable target [E: the historical sapper attacks on Long Binh and Bien Hoa]. A furnace shop is hard to damage permanently with mortars, but a transformer yard is easy. A 30 MW furnace transformer takes six to twelve months to replace from Japan or Europe [E: lead time of large transformers]. The base needs a defended perimeter, spare transformers held in reserve, and a guard force, which I put at a Regional Force battalion and the plant's own armed guards [E: a guard force for a large fixed site].

Its inputs and outputs travel the country's worst roads. The Da Nhim line crosses the Central Highlands and was cut repeatedly. Scrap from up-country bases and bridging steel for down-country repairs move on Route 1, Route 4 and the rail line, each interdicted [E: the historical interdiction of these routes]. Bridging steel is useful only where an engineer company can install it under guard.

The agricultural shops reach the farmer through the market, and the market reaches only as far as the hamlets the GVN holds by day and the traders can travel. In a Front-held district the long-tail motor and the pump are taxed by the VC like everything else.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The base is a net importer of inputs and a net saver of imports. On balance it saves the port tonnage, but it does not save foreign exchange as much as its output suggests.

What it imports: alloying metals (ferro-chrome, ferro-molybdenum, ferro-manganese, nickel), graphite electrodes for the furnaces, refractories, coke and lime, some scrap and billet in the first two years, brass strip or copper and zinc for cartridge cases, machine tools and their spares, cutting tools, and all propellant, primers and high explosive [E: an EAF mini-mill and arsenal's import bill]. Fuel oil for the thermal station adds about 120,000–150,000 t/yr [E: a 66 MW station at moderate load]. By weight, these inputs are about a quarter to a third of the steel products they become [E: scrap is local from 1966]. By value, they are about 40–60% of the output [E: graphite electrodes, ferro-alloys and machine-tool spares are dear].

What it cannot get from the RVNAF or the Vietnamese economy: graphite electrodes, ferro-alloys, propellant, high explosive, primers, precision bearings and machine-tool spares. Every one of these comes by sea through Saigon and is paid in dollars, almost all of them American aid dollars.

Specialist personnel are D1's tradesmen, plus a few hundred metallurgists and quality engineers who are the real bottleneck [E: the scarcity of metallurgists in the region].

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

*Read as what it depends on Americans and foreigners for, and what happens when they leave.*

The base is already Vietnamese, so Vietnamization does not apply in the usual sense. The question is what it needs from outside to keep running, and the honest answer is dollars, data packages and filling.

The base does the most good exactly when the Americans have gone. In 1974 and 1975 the RVNAF rationed rifle ammunition, artillery rounds, batteries and spare parts. Congress cut military aid to about $700 million for fiscal 1975 against about $1.4 billion asked [H]. A domestic arsenal cannot erase that cut. It can stretch it: an aid dollar that buys propellant and explosive to fill a Vietnamese shell body buys more rounds than a dollar that buys the finished American round [E: the metal and machining content is roughly half the cost of a 105mm round]. My estimate is that domestic production of bodies, cases and spares stretches each ammunition and parts dollar by about 1.4–1.8 times [E: the share of value added in country]. That is the provision's war-shaping effect, and Part 3 will have to carry it into 1974–75.

If the licensors leave, the base keeps running but stops improving. If the Americans stop funding inputs, it runs down in months on the arsenal side and continues on the construction side, because scrap and power are local. The arsenal holds about three to six months of propellant and explosive [E: a prudent stock], and that is its real endurance.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The base performs to specification under five conditions. Power arrives. Scrap and imported inputs arrive. The RVNAF orders from it. Its quality inspectors are honest. And its output reaches the field. Two of those are physical; three are institutional.

It degrades in four ways:

- **When the Da Nhim line is cut,** steel production stops and the thermal station carries the arsenal and rebuild works alone.
- **When the port of Saigon is congested,** as it was through 1965–66 with ships waiting weeks to discharge, inputs queue behind American cargo [E: the historical port congestion of 1965–66].
- **When the RVNAF draws American stocks instead of ordering,** the arsenal idles.
- **When security in Bien Hoa and Gia Dinh fails,** the plants close. The Tet attacks of 1968 hit exactly this ground [E: the historical Tet fighting in Saigon and at Bien Hoa and Long Binh].

Over weeks, the base sustains its rated output only if the furnace shop is kept supplied with electrodes and refractories, which are consumed fast. A furnace shop that runs short of electrodes stops without warning [E: about 3–5 kg of electrode per tonne].

### D11 · Failure modes

Four failures matter, and the last is the most likely.

**Sabotage of power or plant.** A cut line stops the melt shop for days. A destroyed furnace transformer stops it for months. The arsenal and rebuild works run on the thermal station's output in the meantime. Partial failure looks like reduced shifts; total failure needs a transformer from overseas.

**Quality failure.** The arsenal makes a lot of barrels from steel with a bad heat-treat, or cartridge cases that split, or shell bodies with laps that crack on firing. A bad lot of barrels shows up as burst or bulged barrels months later in the field. A bad lot of shell bodies shows up as an in-bore premature that kills a gun crew. The danger is not that the plant cannot make good parts. It is that inspectors rated on output will pass bad ones. The force's protection is lot acceptance by an independent inspectorate with American advisers, and proof-firing of every barrel [E: standard arsenal practice]. Korea's slow M16 start shows the learning curve is real [S4].

**Diversion.** Steel, wire, cement-reinforcing bar and especially brass and copper are valuable. They leak to the black market and some reaches the Front, whose engineers need wire, pipe and plate for mines and rocket tubes [E: VC workshops' use of scavenged metal]. Where the province chief allots wire and pickets to outposts, some of it is sold.

**Starvation of orders.** The RVNAF keeps drawing American stocks, so the arsenal runs at a fraction of capacity. Its best engineers drift to the civil market, and in 1973, when the RVNAF needs it, it is a shell. This is the most likely failure, because it requires no enemy action and no mistake, only the path of least resistance.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The curve here runs the wrong way for a weapon: the base is weakest in 1964 and strongest in 1968–72. Month one is a standing base without wartime scrap, without the RVNAF's habit of ordering from it, and with foreign technicians still running the hard processes. Month twelve is a base with Vietnamese foremen who have run their furnaces for a year. Year five is a mature base drawing on the American war's scrap.

What declines first, if anything does, is the machine tools' accuracy. Spares and cutting tools are imported, and a plant that saves dollars by running worn tooling makes worse parts without anyone noticing. The second is electrode and refractory stock in a congested port. The third, from 1973, is the foreign-exchange allowance for inputs.

I put the base at about 50–60% of rated output in 1964, about 80% by 1966 and 90% or more from 1967, subject to orders [E: scrap and skills].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is the RVNAF's actual logistics of 1964–75. Steel, wire, bridging and spares were imported. Rebuild was done partly in country and partly offshore, by contract, in Okinawa, Japan and elsewhere [S8] [E: the offshore rebuild programmes of the period]. In 1970–71 the RVNAF spent $30.6 million upgrading its rebuild depots for the coming surge of overhauls [S8].

Against that, the margins are as follows. Bridging and fortification steel goes from ship-dependent to local, with turnaround cut from months to weeks [E: order to delivery for US-shipped construction steel against a Bien Hoa mill]. Engine and transmission rebuild moves wholly in country, cutting the RVNAF's not-mission-capable time for want of rebuilt assemblies by perhaps a half [E: the time spent waiting on offshore rebuild]. Ammunition and small-arms components shift from wholly imported to about half the value added in country (D9). Against Korea's real arms industry of 1972, the Republic's base is about equal in metalworking and better in special steel, since it has the shop Korea lacked [S4]. It is about seven to nine years ahead of where the Republic's real industry ever got.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P008a -->
This base makes metal, not war materiel. It cannot make propellant, high explosive or primers. Every cartridge, shell and mine it produces is filled with imported chemicals, so it stretches the ammunition supply but cannot replace it. It makes no fuel or lubricants, no aircraft, no engines larger than small diesels, no artillery tubes beyond rebuild, no tanks and no armour plate beyond light plate. It does not make the 2026 M4A1 upper receiver of P004a to its original tolerance, only magazines, springs, small parts and, after its barrel line is proven, chrome-lined barrels. It does not find, fix or kill anyone, and it touches neither half of the find-and-fix problem. It does not secure a single hamlet: it makes the wire, and the wire is worth what the Popular Forces behind it are worth. It is only useful if the RVNAF orders from it, and in a war where the American article is free, it will not do so unless made to. It depends on imported electrodes, alloys and machine-tool spares paid in aid dollars, and on power lines that run through contested country.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P008a -->
It denies the Front the long repair times of a country that waits on ships: blown bridges, cut rail and wrecked outposts come back in weeks instead of months. After 1973 it denies Hanoi part of the bet that an American aid cut would starve the RVNAF of parts and ammunition. It does not deny him sanctuary, infiltration or initiative, and its own scrap, wire and brass are a new source for his workshops through the black market.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P008a -->
This is a slow provision, and its weight falls late. In 1964–67 it builds bridges and outposts faster, keeps more trucks and carriers running, and gives the farmer a pump. All of that is useful and none of it changes a battle. From 1968 it shortens the RVNAF's repair cycle and puts its construction and bridging on its own feet. In 1973–75 it may matter a great deal, because it stretches every aid dollar for ammunition and parts by something like half again, at exactly the moment the dollars run short. It will do that only if American policy forces the RVNAF to buy from it during the years when American supply is free. Otherwise the arsenal starves and is a shell when it is needed. It is a real step toward an RVNAF that can supply itself. It is not self-sufficiency, because propellant, explosive, fuel and every major weapon still come from abroad.

## §1.2 Republic of Vietnam electronics manufacturing base, one generation above 1964, standing from 1 January 1964 — P008b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

This too is an infrastructure provision, read in the institutional sense (D3 products, D4 information inputs, D5 throughput, D6 reporting chain, D7 reach and security).

The provision is the Republic of Vietnam's electronics manufacturing base, one generation above its actual 1964 state, standing and producing from 1 January 1964 [U]. In 1964 the Republic made no electronics. Radios were imported, and repair was what the Signal depot in Saigon and the town's repair shops could manage [S1] [E: the Republic had no electronics plants]. One generation above that is South Korea's electronics sector of the early 1970s. Korea's first radio was assembled by GoldStar in 1959 [S5]. Its electronics exports were $3.6 million in 1966, it targeted $100 million by 1971, and it passed its Electronics Industry Promotion Law in 1969 [S5]. Its firms did licensed assembly, built passive components and consumer sets, ran foreign-owned transistor assembly-and-test plants, and by 1972 maintained Hawk and Nike Hercules missiles [S3]. That is the level granted: assembly and components, licensed military radios, batteries and cable, and serious repair, but no semiconductor wafer fabrication and no design of complex systems.

The base is organised as seven establishments, under the same Directorate of Defence Industry as P008a, with the RVNAF's Signal Directorate as principal customer [E: the Korean defence-industry model of S3]:

- **Thu Duc Communications Plant.** Licensed assembly and test of military VHF-FM radios. In 1964 that means the AN/PRC-10 manpack and the AN/GRC-9 HF set. From 1965 it means the AN/PRC-25 manpack and the AN/VRC-46 and AN/VRC-12 family vehicle sets, and from 1968 the AN/PRC-77 [E: licence following each set's US service entry, S6, S7].
- **Village and Hamlet Radio Shop, Thu Duc.** A simple, rugged VHF-FM handheld and base set for hamlets, village offices and outposts, of the HT-1 class the Americans bought for the Strategic Hamlets [E: the hamlet-radio programmes of the early 1960s].
- **Battery Works, Bien Hoa.** Zinc-carbon dry cells, including the BA-386 pack for the AN/PRC-25 and AN/PRC-77, and later magnesium cells. Also lead-acid vehicle and storage batteries [S6] [E: magnesium cells follow from 1968].
- **Wire and Cable Mill, Bien Hoa.** WD-1/TT field wire, telephone cable, building wire and power cable. It draws copper rod from imported cathode, using steel strand from P008a [E: a wire mill fed by P008a].
- **Components and Printed Circuits, Saigon.** Resistors, capacitors, coils and transformers, printed-circuit boards, connectors and cable assemblies [E: Korean component makers of the period, S5].
- **Transistor Assembly and Test Plant, Thu Duc.** Foreign-owned, on the pattern of the American semiconductor firms that opened in Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong in 1964–67. It packages and tests imported dice into discrete transistors and diodes; it does not make the dice [E: the offshore assembly plants of that period].
- **Signal and Electronics Rebuild Depot, Long Binh.** Depot repair and rebuild of radios, generators, telephone switchboards, AN/PPS-5 ground-surveillance radar modules, field-artillery fire-direction equipment and test sets. It also does bench repair of helicopter avionics line-replaceable units [E: the depot work S8 records as partly offshore].

Alongside these, a consumer radio and television assembly line sells on the civil market, financing the plants and training workers [E: Korea's consumer-electronics route, S5]. The workforce is about 20,000–35,000, mostly young women on assembly and test, as in Korea and Taiwan, with about 600–1,000 engineers and about 3,000–5,000 technicians [E: the assembly-industry ratios of S5 scaled].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

There are no US military spaces; the ceiling is none in any case. The American interface is the existing signal advisers at MACV J-6 and the US Army Strategic Communications Command's depot advisers [E: licensor teams of the period]. Outside the ceiling are about 100–200 licensor engineers and technicians from American radio makers, Japanese component makers and the foreign transistor assembler, falling to about 40–80 by 1970 [E: licensor teams of the period].

The Vietnamese personnel cost is lighter than P008a's in military manpower, because most of the assembly workforce are women. About 6,000–10,000 men of military age are in the technician and engineer grades [E: about a third of the workforce]. These are the very men the RVNAF's signal corps wants. The Republic will need the Korean draft exemption for them too [S3].

### D3 · Weapons and payload

*Read as products and instruments.*

The products, at full rate, a few years after the opening:

- **Military manpack and vehicle radios:** about 15,000–25,000 a year [E: one assembly-and-test line of the period on two shifts]. The PRC-25 runs on 30–75.95 MHz in FM with about 920 channels and is solid-state except for one valve in the final amplifier [E: the set's published characteristics, with S6 for the band's top]. The PRC-77 replaces that valve with a transistor and can carry the KY-38 NESTOR voice encryption [S6]. The Republic's plant builds both to the American data package but cannot build NESTOR (D14).
- **Village and hamlet radios:** about 20,000–30,000 a year [E: this memorandum's design of the base]. A simple VHF-FM set on the territorial-force net, so a hamlet chief, a village office and a Popular Forces outpost can call the district.
- **Batteries:** about 1.5–3 million radio dry cells a year, plus lead-acid batteries [E: a battery works on the Korean scale]. The PRC-25's battery lasted two to three hours of heavy use and several days of light [S7]. A territorial force with radios at every outpost and platoon burns these by the million. In 1974–75 they were one of the items the RVNAF rationed.
- **Field wire and cable:** 60,000–100,000 km of WD-1/TT a year [E: a wire mill on the Korean scale]. The roads, hamlets and base camps of a country at war eat wire.
- **Components and repair parts:** the parts for all of the above, and the common parts for the sets the Americans supply.
- **Rebuild:** 30,000–50,000 radio rebuilds and repairs a year, and depot repair of AN/PPS-5 modules, generators, switchboards and avionics units [E: a depot of about 2,500–3,500].
- **Consumer goods:** 200,000–400,000 transistor radios and some thousands of television sets a year, sold on the civil market [E: the Korean consumer sector of S5 scaled].

Like P008a, it compels nothing. It is a supplier.

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

*Read as information inputs.*

The base learns from three sources: its own test and acceptance benches; the RVNAF Signal Directorate's requisitions and failure reports; and the licensors' data packages. As in P008a, the danger is acceptance testing under pressure to ship.

The products, though, do touch canopy, night and monsoon, and the provision's value lives there. A VHF-FM manpack's line-of-sight range shrinks sharply under triple canopy and in broken ground. In the field the PRC-25 needed the 10-foot long-range antenna, and the Special Forces and recon teams built "jungle antennas" to reach anyone at all [S7]. Monsoon damp ruins handsets and connectors [S7]. At night, a radio's value is that it is the only way an outpost can be heard. The plant does nothing about propagation physics. What it does is put more sets in more hands, with batteries to run them and a depot near enough to repair them.

### D5 · Performance

*Read as throughput and capacity.*

The rate limits are three. The first is imported transistors and dice, then crystals, then skilled test technicians [E: no chip or crystal plant is granted]. The plant makes passive parts, wire and dry cells from mostly local labour on imported zinc, manganese dioxide and copper. It cannot make semiconductors or quartz crystals in volume, so every radio's heart arrives by air or sea from America, Japan or Hong Kong. A month's interruption of those imports stops radio output and leaves battery and wire output untouched.

The second is the US licence. The PRC-25 enters American service in Vietnam in 1965 [S7] and the PRC-77 in 1968 [S6]. Before the US Army fields a set, the Republic's plant does not build it, because no data package is released. I have assumed licensed production of each from the year after its US fielding [E: the lag of foreign licences in the period, such as Brazil's PRC-77 in 1970, S6]. So in 1964 the plant builds the older PRC-10 and the hamlet radio. From 1966 it builds the PRC-25, and from 1969 the PRC-77.

The third is the RVNAF's allocation. At 15,000–25,000 manpacks a year, the plant could put a radio in every Popular Forces platoon in about two years. In 1964 there were roughly 4,000–5,000 platoons; by 1970 there were over 8,000. A third year adds every Regional Force company's second set, and by the fourth year the regular forces' shortfalls are filled [E: the territorial forces' strength of the period].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

*Read as the reporting chain and the interface with the GVN and RVNAF.*

The plant answers to the Directorate of Defence Industry and contracts with the RVNAF Signal Directorate and the Ministry of Interior, which owns the hamlet and village radio net through the province chiefs [E: the hamlet-radio nets of the early 1960s]. The order-book problem of P008a applies here too: the American PRC-25 is free and the Vietnamese one costs piasters. In radios, though, the gap is less sharp. In 1965–67 American supply of PRC-25s went first to American units and left the RVNAF and the territorial forces with PRC-10s and less [E: the historical priority]. The local plant fills exactly that gap, which makes it likelier to be used.

The products are fully interoperable with American nets, because they are American designs: same band, same channels, same handset. That means a Popular Forces outpost with a Vietnamese-built PRC-25 can reach an American artillery fire-direction centre, if the net plan allows it. It also means the enemy's captured PRC-25s reach the same nets, as they did historically.

### D7 · Domain conditions

*Read as reach and security preconditions.*

The plants sit inside the Saigon–Bien Hoa–Thu Duc triangle, with the same exposure as P008a's (D7 there). The rebuild depot is at Long Binh. Electronics plants draw a few megawatts, not tens, and run on the Saigon grid and their own diesel generators [E: assembly plants draw a few MW]. Their security problem is not mortars. It is the workforce: thousands of workers inside a plant whose output the Front wants badly.

The products' reach is the countryside, and their value depends on who holds it. A radio in a hamlet the GVN holds at night is a lifeline. A radio in a hamlet the Front holds at night is the Front's radio, its battery a booby-trap power source [S7]. It is also a monitor on the district net, for which the Front kept a large and skilled listening service [E: the historical VC and PAVN technical reconnaissance units].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The base imports dice, crystals, copper cathode, zinc, manganese dioxide, ferrites, test equipment and some plastics [E: assembly-industry inputs]. By value about half of its output is imported content, rising toward two-thirds for the military radios and falling toward a third for batteries and wire [E: assembly-industry practice]. Unlike P008a, its inputs are light. A year's transistors for 25,000 radios fit in one cargo aircraft, so it is little burden on the port and none on the roads [E: assembly-industry inputs].

What the RVNAF cannot supply: the semiconductors, the licences, the test equipment and its calibration, and, until about 1968, enough trained technicians for the rebuild depot [E: technician training of two to four years]. The calibration laboratory needs American standards and regular checks against them.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

*Read as what it depends on outside the Republic.*

As with P008a, the base is Vietnamese from the start. Its dependencies are semiconductors and licences, which come from outside, and dollars to buy them.

Its strongest effect, like P008a's, comes after 1973. The RVNAF's real communications in 1974–75 ran short of batteries, repair parts and depot repair, and units cut their radio use to save batteries [E: the rationing of 1974–75]. A battery works and a rebuild depot in country cut those shortages to the semiconductor and crystal content, which is small in tonnage and value. On my estimate this base stretches the aid dollar for communications by about two times and nearly ends the battery shortage [E: the share of value added in country]. That last effect is not small: an outpost that cannot afford to run its radio is an outpost that cannot call for help.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The base performs to specification when semiconductors and crystals arrive, its test benches are calibrated, its workforce shows up, and the RVNAF orders from it. It degrades when imports are interrupted, which stops military radio output first, and when acceptance is corrupted, which gives bad radios to the field. It also degrades when its trained technicians are drafted or leave for the better-paid American bases, where radio technicians were in high demand [E: US base pay for local technicians].

Its products perform as the PRC-25 and PRC-77 do: to perhaps 5–8 km on the short antenna in open country, much less under canopy, more with the long antenna or a relay [E: the PRC-25's typical working range]. Their batteries last as S7 describes.

Over weeks, the hard limit is batteries in the field, not radios. A Popular Forces platoon with a PRC-25 on listening watch every night burns a battery in about three to five nights [E: the duty cycle of a nightly watch].

### D11 · Failure modes

Four failures, two of them insidious.

**Leakage to the enemy.** The VC prized captured PRC-25s and their batteries. Radios made in Thu Duc and issued to Popular Forces in contested hamlets will be lost, sold and captured in their thousands [E: historical VC capture of radios]. Each one becomes a VC radio and a listening post on the district net. Batteries leak through the market. This is a cost the provision imposes on itself, and its size is a question for Part 2.

**Bad lots.** A run of sets with poor solder joints, or a lot of batteries that fail early, will go unnoticed until the field reports. The field reports slowly.

**Import interruption.** No dice means no radios. The plant holds about three to six months of semiconductors [E: a prudent stock], so this is a slow failure, not a sudden one.

**Allocation failure.** The sets go to headquarters, district staffs and favoured units, not to Popular Forces platoons. Or they go to Popular Forces in hamlets that are already safe, not the ones that need them. That is the likely failure, and it is a policy failure rather than a plant failure.

### D12 · Degradation curve

The base is weakest in 1964, when it can build only PRC-10s and hamlet sets, and strongest from 1968, when it builds the PRC-77 and runs a mature rebuild depot. What declines first under stress is calibration and test discipline, because they cost time and do not show. The second is the stock of semiconductors, in a port or airfield queue. From 1973 it is the foreign-exchange allowance.

I put military radio output at about 50% of rated capacity in 1964–65, constrained by the PRC-10 design and the hamlet set [E: licence timing in D5]. It reaches about 80–90% from 1966 once the PRC-25 is licensed, subject to orders [E: licence timing in D5]. Battery and wire output is at full rate from the opening [E: licence timing in D5].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The benchmark is the RVNAF's actual communications. The regular forces were short of modern radios until about 1968–69. The territorial forces were worse off: they relied on PRC-10s, older sets and the hamlet radios for most of the war, and many Popular Forces platoons had no radio at all in the early years [E: the historical equipping priorities]. All radios, batteries and repair parts came from America. Depot repair was partly offshore until the 1970–71 upgrade [S8].

Against that, the margins: radios for every Popular Forces platoon about four to five years earlier than they historically had them [E: D5's throughput against the historical equipping]. The battery supply moves from wholly imported to wholly local in its bulk. Radio repair turnaround falls from weeks or months to days [E: an in-country depot against offshore rebuild]. Against Korea's real electronics industry, the Republic's base is about the 1972 Korean level [S5]. It is roughly a decade ahead of anything the real Republic had.

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P008b -->
This base assembles and repairs; it does not invent or fabricate chips. It makes no semiconductor dice and no quartz crystals in volume, so every radio depends on imports. It builds only American-licensed sets, each about a year after the US Army fields it: PRC-10s in 1964–65, PRC-25s from about 1966, PRC-77s from about 1969. It does not build encryption (NESTOR and all COMSEC equipment stay American-controlled), radar, avionics, sensors, satellite equipment, or anything resembling the 2026 systems of earlier provisions. It repairs radar and avionics modules; it does not make them. It does nothing about canopy, terrain or monsoon propagation. A Vietnamese-built PRC-25 has the same range as an American one. It does not find the main force or identify the VCI. It does not secure a hamlet: it lets the hamlet call for help, which is worth what the help is worth. Radios and batteries issued in contested hamlets will leak to the enemy.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P008b -->
It denies the Front the silent outpost. A Popular Forces post that can call the district, the artillery and the reaction force at night is harder to overrun and its defenders are harder to frighten into leaving. After 1973 it denies Hanoi the RVNAF's communications collapse that a battery and parts famine would bring. It gives the Front more captured radios and batteries than before.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P008b -->
Of the two provisions this is the more important, and it is important in a narrow, specific way. It puts a working radio and a battery in Popular Forces outposts and hamlets four or five years earlier than history did, and keeps them working with a depot and a battery works in country. The night war in the hamlets is decided by whether a squad behind wire can call for help and whether help comes, and this is the half of that equation that was missing. It also keeps the RVNAF's communications alive in 1974–75, when the real force rationed batteries. It does nothing for finding the enemy, builds no system Hanoi need fear, and depends wholly on imported semiconductors. Its value in any hamlet is set by whether the artillery and the reaction force answer the call. Where they do, I expect the territorial forces to be measurably harder to break at night. Where they do not, it is a radio that reports the hamlet's fall.
