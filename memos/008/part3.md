## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I inherited on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war being lost by a state that had just killed its President. In this war the state was not as empty as the one Harkins handed over. On the first day it decreed land to the tiller and a commission against corruption, a rural police and chiefs chosen by examination, and a promotion board for its army, and it went into the year with seven classes of educated officers in its companies and a coordinating centre ordered for every district. It also owned something no government in Saigon had owned before: a steelworks, an arsenal and rebuild works at Bien Hoa, and a radio plant, a battery works and a wire mill at Go Vap, with fifty or eighty thousand people working in them. Of all the things that stood on that first day, the factories were the least visible and the slowest. This memorandum is about how slow, and about the one year in which slowness turned out not to matter.

For fourteen months the plants had more capacity than customers. No Vietnamese quartermaster would pay piasters for a cartridge case the Americans gave him free, and the arsenal sold its brass to the police while the Army drew from American stocks. The steelworks rolled wire and pickets for the posts of the held belt at half its rating, because the country had not enough scrap. What saved the plants from becoming a monument was a rule, and I am glad I signed it. From March 1965 the Aid Compliance Board made the RVNAF buy a fixed share of eight kinds of materiel from its own plants before it drew a single American round: a quarter in 1965, half from 1968 (EV008-1). The American ordnance staff were right that the domestic cases cost more and were unproven. They were wrong that it did not matter.

The first effect was in the hamlets, and it was a radio. From the spring of 1964 the Popular Forces platoon beside each police post carried its own set, and from 1966 a PRC-25 from Go Vap with a fresh battery every week. On the night of 17 November 1964 at Long Huu the platoon's radio brought the boats from Ham Long three minutes after the first assault, and the Front's company withdrew before they came (EV002-2). The Front learned the radio as quickly as it had learned the carbine. By the summer of 1965 its district companies were attacking the posts that could call about a third less often than the posts that could not, and nobody's statistics showed the attacks that were never made. In November 1964 the worst flood in memory had taken the bridges of Route 1 between Da Nang and Quang Ngai, and the spans had come back from Bien Hoa on landing ships; the coast road was open in six weeks rather than three months (EV008-2). Ba Gia was still lost in May. The provision does not win battles. It shortens repairs.

The main-force war of 1965–67 went as it went. Plei Me and the Ia Drang, Masher, Con Thien and Dak To were fought on the enemy's terms, on the borders and in the Highlands where nobody needed a factory. The factories were fighting another war underneath. The Front found that the steelworks ran on power from Da Nhim, 250 kilometres up on the Lang Biang plateau, and from October 1965 its Region 6 sappers dropped the towers of the line in the hills east of Di Linh. Bien Hoa answered by building replacement towers in a week and paying the Koho villages along the line to watch it, with a hamlet radio in each (EV008-3). The steelworks lost a fifth of its hours in 1966 and a tenth in 1967. Thirty Montagnard villages got a government wage and a radio for the first time, which is not nothing and not much.

The radio's second effect was the one I had warned about in Part 2, and on the night of 9 March 1967 the Front showed me it had read the same page. At Ap My Loi, north of Cai Lay, a mortar section of the 514th Local Force Battalion fired twelve rounds into a PF post, and two of its companies lay in the sugar cane three kilometres south, where the canal road crossed a culvert. The post called. The district chief sent the reaction force in trucks, as the rule of 1964 required. The mine under the culvert was fired with field wire from the Republic's own mill and dry cells from its own battery works, and the 247th Regional Force Company lost 31 dead in eight minutes (EV008-4). Major Liem's answer the following week was the right one: a post's first call would be for fire, not for men, and reliefs would move by water or at dawn. Attacks on posts within range of the guns fell by a third. The Front went for the posts beyond the guns and the posts whose batteries had run down. And the platoons that could call down the guns stopped leaving their wire at night, so that the hamlets outside the posts were walked after dark more often than before by the Front's tax collectors. The post held itself. Whether the hamlet was ours after dark is the question the radio cannot answer.

The same lesson was written in Chuong Thien the year before in a different hand. When the IV Corps headquarters borrowed the province's Regional Force companies for the U Minh in 1966, every one of the forty-one PF posts had a radio, and nineteen of the twenty-three that fell had called the district within ten minutes of the first round. The district had nobody to send (EV004-2). The radio had moved the post's weakness from the call to the answer, and it had written down where the answer failed. Those logs, laid beside the province chief's returns, made the rule of 1967 that the companies belonged to their provinces, and the request for guns the districts could call for themselves. And at Phu Cat in August 1967 a 105mm round from a Bien Hoa lot burst in the tube and killed four gunners of the 22nd Division, because an inspector had been paid to sign without firing a sample (EV008-5). The gunners of II Corps refused domestic rounds for most of a year, and the arsenal learned that a round the soldier does not trust is worse than no round.

Tet came on the historical night and was decided where it was decided historically, in Saigon, Hue, Khe Sanh and Washington. The posts of the held belt called, and where the reaction forces were defending the district towns nobody came; the towns held at the walls, for reasons the record had already given. In 1969–71 the factories did their most important work and the least noticed. Vietnamization handed the RVNAF an American army's equipment, and the rebuild works at Bien Hoa and the signal depot at Go Vap, running since 1966, took it into a loop that the historical army had to build in a crash programme. By the end of 1971 the RVNAF made about two-fifths of its own small-arms cartridges, a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its batteries (EV008-6). In Long My the subsector adviser spent his last year handing the call for fire to a district gun platoon on Bien Hoa howitzers, and on the night of 23 February 1971 a post called, the district's guns answered in five minutes, and nobody in the chain was American (EV008-7). That is what a handover looks like when both ends of it belong to the Republic. It did not work for anything American at one end, and the bombers that had broken every siege since Con Thien were handed to nobody.

And then the lever lapsed, as it lapsed for everything this headquarters had built behind it. In February 1970 Thieu put a general of his own staff over the Directorate, as he put his own men over the Selection Board, the general-officer list, the Phung Hoang file and the Commission in the same eighteen months (EV002-6). The plants went on producing; the domestic share survived because the Joint General Staff now wanted it. What went was the honesty of the acceptance that Phu Cat had made honest: proof-firing signed more often than fired, rejections falling from one lot in sixteen to one in a hundred, and duds in the field doubling. I do not think any of the five institutions was destroyed. I think each of them lost the part of itself that reached upward, and the Directorate lost least in output and most in what output is worth.

1972 was the last year of free American supply, and the arsenal's weight was invisible in it. The Easter Offensive was decided by American air power and by the orders of corps commanders, and in the autumn the Americans refilled the RVNAF's depots to the brim before the agreement froze them. The base's year was 1974.

On 25 January 1974 the ration fell. In history the squad guns were cut to about 200 rounds a month, the batteries for the PRC-25 were rationed, and the territorial posts' radios went silent one by one through the second half of the year. In this war the Bien Hoa arsenal filled two-fifths of the small-arms requirement, a third of the 105mm and most of the mortars, from propellant and explosive bought out of the same appropriation, and the battery works filled almost all of the batteries. From March the squad gun's ration was about 400 rounds and the 105mm battery's daily allowance a third higher, and the posts stayed on the air (EV000-89). The $700 million of fiscal 1975 bought about what $850 million to $1 billion had bought historically. I want that stated at its real weight: this is the first provision in eight memoranda that reached the Republic's sustainment after the Americans left, and in the attrition of 1974 it saved perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead and kept its communications alive. It did not reach fuel, aircraft or the big guns, and it did not change the vote. In the House the arsenal was cited by the advocates of the cut as proof the South could make its own ammunition, which was half true, since it made the brass and not the powder.

The fights of 1974 show both halves. At Duc Duc and on the Thuong Duc heights the ARVN's 105s fired a third more than the ration allowed, and the PAVN fought where its 130mm guns could reach them from beyond their range. Thuong Duc still fell on 7 August and Duc Duc still cost the 3rd Division 4,700 men (EV000-91). At Phuoc Long in December the province's ammunition point held a third more rounds than history's, its posts reported on the air until they were overrun, and the guns those rounds were for were silenced by counter-battery fire in the first week. The reserve was not sent on 18 December for the historical reasons, and the town fell on 6 January (EV000-92). One thing went north that had not gone before. The 4th Corps' gunners reported that the ARVN had fired at a rate their estimate of the ration did not allow, and the B-2 Front's intelligence section, asked why, answered with the name of the Bien Hoa arsenal. Hanoi had read the Republic as an army that would starve when American supply stopped. It now had to read it as an army that would go hungry more slowly.

It did not need to. The decisions that ended the Republic were taken in March 1975 on grounds the factories never reached. Ban Me Thuot fell to a concentration Phu had not seen. Thieu's orders of 13 and 14 March rested on his conviction that the Americans would not return, on the absence of a reserve while the Airborne and the Marines were tied to I Corps, and on the aid then voted; the base touched only the last of these, and only the part of it that was brass and steel. The I Corps that dissolved toward its families on the road to Da Nang was not short of rounds. I have tested this provision against the critical path the meta-review set, and the honest answer is that it delays the collapse of I and II Corps by a few days at most, against the three weeks that would have pushed the assault on Saigon into the rains (EVMT01-4). The 18th Division at Xuan Loc and the 25th at Cu Chi had batteries for every set and rounds for their guns, and Hoang Cam went round them.

Dung's plan changed in one line. After Phuoc Long the General Staff counted the Bien Hoa works as part of the Republic's strength, and the 4th Corps' order for the final assault named the arsenal, the steelworks and the Go Vap plant among the objectives to be taken intact. On the morning of 30 April they were. The arsenal's stores held about 40 million cartridges and 90,000 105mm rounds, the last of what the 1974 powder had made, and within a month the case lines were making cartridges for the PAVN's captured rifles (EV000-98). Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. The Republic's own industry, built to stretch its last dollar, became on that day the victors' largest workshop in the South.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The provision's effect in Phase I is in three places, and none of them is a battle. The first is the radio at the post: the PF platoon's own PRC-10 completes the middle term of the rule that a hamlet is held only by security resident in it, with a means of calling help and a reaction that comes in the dark (F008-1, POSTNA-2). The night at Long Huu on 17 November 1964 is the case. The platoon's rifles broke the first assault; its radio brought the boats; on the old course the call would have been a flare, and the boats would have come at dawn (EV002-2). The second is the bridge: the November flood on the coast cost the 2nd Division a winter of isolation on the historical course and six weeks here, because the spans were made at Bien Hoa rather than ordered from Okinawa (EV008-2, EC008-1). The third is the rule that made the base matter at all. Without the domestic share of March 1965 the arsenal would have sold its brass to the police for ten years and been a shell in 1974; with it, the RVNAF became a customer, and the base had a reason to stay proven (F008-2, EV008-1).

I put the weight of Phase I plainly. It is real and small. The posts held their nine attacks in ten because of the carbines and the platoons; the radio made the tenth less often a massacre and the reaction faster, and its larger effect, the attacks the Front never made on posts that could call, begins in the summer of 1965 and is the subject of Phase II (F008-16). None of Binh Gia, Ba Gia or Dong Xoai turned on a radio at a police post or a bridge span, and they are unchanged.

The base cost the Republic men it needed in its first year. About 31,000–45,000 of the plants' workers were of military age, the deferment list was a market within months, and the Signal and Ordnance Corps lost to the plants the technicians they had expected to draft (F008-9, EV008-1). Against an army that the musters had shown to be about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls (EV001-4), that was not a trivial price.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front read the radio within a season and read the factories wrongly. Its district companies in the held belt were ordered by the spring of 1965 to report which posts had a set on the air, and attacks moved to the posts that did not, to the constables on the road, and to the mine on the path to the morning market (EV002-2, F008-3, F008-16). COSVN's proselytizing section called the plants "American factories", which they were not, and organised their workers as if they were Party cadre in Nam Dinh, which some of them became (F008-17, F008-21). The leakage began at once: by August 1965 the batteries of the posts were in the firing circuits of the Front's mines in Long An and Dinh Tuong, bought in the markets from PF soldiers and from the warehouses that issued them (F008-5). The enemy's large decisions of the phase, the commitment of the PAVN regiments in the autumn of 1964 and the summer offensive of 1965, were taken in Hanoi for reasons no factory touched, and the record of them stands.

### Causal chains

No chain of this memorandum terminates in Phase I. **C008-2** begins here, with the PF platoon's radio at the post (EV002-2), and lands in Phase IV.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a held belt whose posts can call, an enemy who has begun to attack the ones that cannot, and an arsenal with its first customer. Phase IV inherits the bridge spans and the rebuild works that will close Vietnamization's loop. Phase VI inherits the domestic share, which is the only reason the plants exist in 1974 as more than buildings. The Front inherits a working class in Bien Hoa and a supply of batteries.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is where the radio's second-order effects stop being predictions and become events. The first-order effect is the one the Front hid from us: posts in the held belt that could call were attacked about a third to a half less often than those that could not, and the gain appears nowhere except as attacks not made (F008-16, POS008-2). The second-order effects are three, and each is on the ground in a dated event.

The first is the bottleneck moving from the call to the answer. In Chuong Thien in 1966 every post had a radio and nineteen of the twenty-three that fell had called within ten minutes; the corps had taken the companies that would have answered (EV004-2, F008-6). The radio did not save them. It made the failure legible, and the logs made the rule of 1967 and the case for district guns.

The second is the Front attacking the relief rather than the post, which is the event I narrate at length in this phase because it shows the downstream analysis true of the world (F008-3). At My Phuoc Tay on 9 March 1967 the post called, the reaction came by road as the 1964 rule required, and the 514th Local Force Battalion killed 31 of it at a culvert with a mine fired by the Republic's own wire and batteries (EV008-4, F008-5). Liem's answer, the call for fire first and the relief by water or at dawn, was right, and the guns did what trucks could not. The posts within range of the guns were attacked a third less often.

The third is the perverse effect of the right answer. A platoon that could call down the guns had less reason to leave its wire, and night patrols in Cai Lay fell by a third while the Front's tax collectors walked the hamlet past the post (F008-12, EV008-4). I set this against the gain honestly, as the meta-review asked: the posts were held more cheaply and the hamlets outside them were not held better, and on balance the night belt is somewhat stronger at the posts and no stronger between them. That is why I leave night control at its band.

Two lesser mechanisms run alongside. The Front's sappers took a fifth of the steelworks' hours in 1966 by dropping the Da Nhim towers (EV008-3, F008-4), which bound the construction steel and not the arsenal (F008-8). And the Phu Cat lot of August 1967 cost the arsenal its gunners' trust for a year, until officers educated to read a metallurgical report rewrote acceptance (EV008-5, F008-15, P007b). The main-force war of the phase is unchanged: the battles were on the borders, the enemy chose them, and no factory reached a single one of them.

The rural economy moved a little in the held Delta, where the pumps the machinery shops sold against the new titles put a dry-season crop on owner-cultivators' fields; it is the one place the base touches rice before 1974, and it compounds the land programme where the night is held (F001-4, EV008-1).

### Enemy response and initiative

The Dinh Tuong provincial unit's decision to use the radio against the men it called is the Front's best answer to this provision in the whole war, and it was cheap: a mortar section, two companies, a mine and a culvert (EV008-4, F008-19). The Region 6 committee's choice of the Da Nhim line was taken for its own reasons, that the line was the one target in its region whose loss was felt in Saigon and that it could be reached without a fight (EV008-3). It is the closest thing in the phase to a decision this headquarters did not provoke, and I do not mark it as the memorandum's initiative because the steelworks provoked it. The expensive side of the Front's adaptation was elsewhere: a post that falls with its radio and its batteries is an arsenal for the Front's guerrillas, and every post that now held was an arsenal it did not capture (F008-19).

### Causal chains

**C008-3** [months] The steelworks at Bien Hoa draws half its power down the Da Nhim line from the Lang Biang plateau (EV008-1) → the Front's Region 6 sappers drop the line's towers from October 1965, choosing the one target in their region whose loss is felt in Saigon (EV008-3, F008-4) → the Directorate pays the Koho villages between Don Duong and Di Linh a wage to watch the line and gives each a hamlet radio on the Tuyen Duc sector's net → by 1967 thirty Koho villages are the only Montagnard hamlets in the province with a government wage and a radio, and their reports of the sappers' movement are the sector's best warning of the Front in the Da Nhim valley; a Saigon factory has done more for the government's presence among the Koho than any programme aimed at them.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits posts that call for fire first and stay inside their wire, which matters on the night of Tet only where the reaction forces were not already defending the towns. Phase IV inherits the Chuong Thien logs and Liem's rules, which become the territorial artillery platoons and the handover of the call (EV008-7), and the Phu Cat acceptance procedure, which the palace will hollow out in 1970 (EV002-6). Phase VI inherits an arsenal whose gunners trust it again.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- null P3 -->
The provision does not change 1968's events, for three reasons. Tet was fought in the towns and cities, where telephones and military radios already stood and where the enemy, not the call, chose the battle; the record already credits the rifles and the board's battalions with what changed at the district walls. The posts of the held belt called on 31 January, and where the reaction forces were defending the district towns nobody came, which is the Chuong Thien lesson again and not a new event. And 1968 was the peak of free American supply: the June mobilization's new PF platoons drew radios from Go Vap as well as American stocks, inside an expansion the record already holds (EV000-51). Phase II's end vector did not move, so no ripple arrives.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

Phase IV is where the base does its most important work, and it is invisible in the phase. The closed loop is the case (EV008-6, F008-11). The historical RVNAF received an American army's equipment in 1969–71 and could not keep it running without a crash depot programme; here the rebuild works and the signal depot had run at the domestic share since 1966, and the American articles went into a loop that already existed. By the end of 1971 the Republic made about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, a third of its 105mm rounds, most of its mortar rounds and batteries and all of its wire, filled and powered with imported materials (F008-7). The bottleneck of self-sufficiency has moved, as Part 2 predicted, from capacity to the order book and now to the dollars for propellant and semiconductors that will be voted in 1974.

The handover of the call at Long My is the second mechanism, and it is the clearest demonstration in the record of what a handover can and cannot be (EV008-7, F008-1). It worked because both ends were Vietnamese: the set and the battery from Go Vap, the howitzers rebuilt at Bien Hoa, the round from the arsenal, the gunners and the post commanders. It is the selected adviser's handover of the radio (POS006-3) completed, and it is the reason Long My kept all forty-one of its posts through 1972 (EVMT01-2). It did not, and could not, hand over the B-52J.

The third mechanism is the capture of the Directorate, and I judge it with the four institutions that fell beside it, as the meta-review asked (EV002-6, F008-14). The net in both directions is this. The plants' output survived the palace, and so did the domestic share, because the Joint General Staff now wanted the plants and the Americans were leaving; that is the larger half, and it is the half that matters in 1974. Honest acceptance did not survive: rejections fell from one lot in sixteen to one in a hundred and duds in the field roughly doubled, and the scrap went to Singapore. I put the cost at perhaps a tenth to a fifth of the base's 1974 value lost to bad lots and diversion, and the gain at the rest. Of the five institutions captured in 1970–71, the Directorate is the one whose captured form was still worth most to the Republic.

I leave the self-sufficiency band at the end of 1971 where it was. The band measures what the RVNAF can do without American supply and air at the phase's end against the historical state, and at the end of 1971 its dependence on American air, deepened by memorandum 005, weighs against a domestic share that no one can yet see at work because American supply is still free. The gain is real and below a band in this year. It becomes visible in 1974.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's answer to the handover was the one it had made since 1967: it did not assault posts the district guns could reach, and it held the canal between them at night (EV008-7, F008-12). In the factories COSVN's cells, now six years old, collected dues and information and did not strike, because a strike would have closed the plants whose workers they were organising; the Front's interest in the plants was in what they would be after the war, earlier than Hanoi's (F008-21). Hanoi itself did not read the base in these years. Its estimates of the RVNAF's sustainment, as far as the record shows them, counted American deliveries and nothing else (F008-18).

### Causal chains

**C008-2** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV002-2) The PF platoon's own radio puts every held-belt post on the district net, and the Front stops assaulting the posts that can call → at My Phuoc Tay in March 1967 it attacks a post to ambush the relief the call brings, and Major Liem makes the post's first call a call for fire and moves reliefs to water and dawn (EV008-4, F008-3) → the Chuong Thien logs of 1966 and the Cai Lay rules of 1967 make the districts' case for guns of their own, and from February 1970 the Delta's districts receive two-gun territorial artillery platoons on Bien Hoa howitzers (EV004-2) → when the subsector advisers leave in 1970–71, the call for fire passes from American to Vietnamese hands with nothing to hand over but practice, and Long My's posts are answered on the night of 23 February 1971 by a chain with no American in it (EV008-7); a Front ambush at a culvert became the Republic's best-prepared handover.

**C008-4** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase II, EV008-5) A bribed inspector's lot bursts a 105mm tube at Phu Cat in August 1967 and the gunners refuse domestic rounds for a year (F008-15) → officers of the 1964 courses rewrite acceptance around proof-firing at Long Thanh, and the gunners return in 1968 → the Directorate passes to the palace in February 1970 with the Board, the list, the file and the Commission, and proof-firing becomes a signature (EV002-6, F008-14) → in 1974, when the domestic rounds are a third of the 105mm the army fires, perhaps a tenth to a fifth of the base's stretch is lost to duds and diverted brass, and the arsenal enters its decisive year with its output intact and its gunners' trust spent by men who never fired a round.

### Downstream

Phase V inherits a closed loop that nobody needs in a year of free American resupply. Phase VI inherits the plants, the domestic share, the territorial guns on Vietnamese sets, and an acceptance system the palace has emptied.

### Clock

The base costs nothing on the American clock and buys nothing on it. Vietnamization's American budget is a little smaller for the depot programme that was not built, and no member of Congress knew it. If anything, the plants made Vietnamization's claim to be working slightly more true than history's and no more believed; the tolerance of 1969–71 was spent by My Lai, Cambodia, Kent State and the casualty lists, none of which a factory reached (POSCP01-1). No band moves.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- null P5 -->
The base does not change 1972. The year's battles were decided by American air power at An Loc and Kontum and by the orders of corps and division commanders at Quang Tri, neither of which a factory reached. Its sustainment, which is the base's only lever, was not binding in 1972: the Americans resupplied the RVNAF freely through the offensive and refilled its depots in the autumn before the agreement froze them. The rebuild works returned carriers and radios faster than history's, inside a resupply that already exceeded what the army could use. The PF posts of Long My held through the year, which the record already shows (EVMT01-2). Phase IV's end vector did not move.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

The provision does what I said it would in 1974 and nothing in 1975, and both halves deserve their full weight. The strongest positive effect is on the ground in a dated event: from March 1974, when the Joint General Staff raised the squad gun's ration to about 400 rounds and the 105mm allowance by a third on Bien Hoa's output, the RVNAF's communications and ammunition did not go dark as history's did, and the $700 million of fiscal 1975 bought about what $850 million to $1 billion had bought historically (EV000-89, F008-23, F008-20). At Duc Duc and Thuong Duc the guns fired a third more and the posts stayed on the air (EV000-91). I judge that this saved perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead in 1974, resting on a third more 105mm fire and a doubled squad-gun ration in the fights the army accepted; it is a judgment on a rate and has been shown at work at Duc Duc and Rach Bap, and it lies inside the uncertainty of the reported 30,000. This is the first provision in eight memoranda to reach one of the three conditions the meta-review set for moving the rung, sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation (POSMT01-1).

The bottleneck moved where Part 2 said it would, to the dollars for propellant, explosive and semiconductors, and above them to the things the base could not make (F008-7). It made no fuel: the VNAF was grounded on the historical scale. It forged no 155mm or 175mm bodies: the big guns were rationed as before. It made no aircraft and no divisions. And the stretch did not change the vote: the arsenal was cited by the cut's advocates as proof the South could supply itself, which is the counter-directional finding at work (F008-22). The appropriation was $700 million and the April 1975 request of $722 million was refused as historically (EV000-97).

The critical path of 1975 is where the provision is tested hardest, and I state the test as the meta-review asked (EVMT01-4). Ban Me Thuot fell to a concentration and a surprise, not to a shortage. The orders of 13–14 March rested on three judgments of Thieu's: that the Americans would not return, that he had no reserve while the Airborne and Marines held I Corps, and that the aid voted would not let him hold everything. The base touched only the third, and only the brass-and-steel part of it; it added no division and no aircraft, and a President who could not hold Pleiku without the Airborne could not hold it with more 105mm rounds. The I Corps that dissolved toward its families was not short of rounds (POSMT01-6). The 1st Corps' march from Thanh Hoa crossed no ground the factories reached. The delay the base buys in the collapse of I and II Corps is a few days at most, against a threshold of about three weeks. Its effects in April fell on fights the PAVN bypassed, at Xuan Loc and Cu Chi (EV000-96), and they are reported as such.

At the end of 1974, before the last offensive, the Republic was not history's, and the end vector measured after the fall cannot show it. The held belt of the Delta and the Saigon region was held at night more firmly than history's, and its posts were on the air with fresh batteries when history's were going silent. The army's battalions were better led and its divisions and corps no better. Its ammunition position was better by about a third to two-fifths in the categories Bien Hoa made and unchanged in fuel and the heavy guns. Its dependence on American air, deepened by memorandum 005, was unchanged and unanswerable. On self-sufficiency I would put the Republic at the end of 1974 at about the historical state, the gain in sustainment offset by the loss in air, which is why no vector moves even where the fall did not erase it.

I state the PAVN's 1972 losses in this war against the historical figure before the rebuilding, as the meta-review requires: about 7,000–10,000 killed and wounded above the historical count of about 100,000, a tenth more, made good in men within a season of 1973 and in experienced cadre more slowly (EV000-87) [X-MT01]. This provision adds nothing to them. The rebuilding ran on the historical schedule, and the corps were formed on their historical dates.

### Enemy response and initiative

<!-- initiative --> The decision that set the last campaign was the Politburo's, taken on its own reading of American politics and not provoked by anything this headquarters did. At its conference of 30 September to 8 October 1974, with Le Duan presiding and Nixon two months out of office, the Politburo resolved on a two-year plan to finish the war in 1975–76, on the judgment that the United States, having let its President fall, would not send its forces back; the General Staff under Van Tien Dung was ordered to plan the 1975 campaign in the Central Highlands. It was driven by Watergate, by the American vote on aid, and by the Party's own calendar, and it was taken while the Republic's battalions were still holding Rach Bap and the Thuong Duc heights. Tran Van Tra, for the B-2 Front, argued in the same months for the attack on Phuoc Long as a test of the American response, against caution in Hanoi, and won. The Soviet and Chinese supply behind both decisions was a variable with its own politics: by most estimates Soviet deliveries of heavy weapons and fuel to the North rose in 1974 as American aid to the South fell [E: the pattern given in most accounts of 1973–75; no figure in this record]. Nothing the base did reached Moscow's or Beijing's reasons.

The PAVN's reaction to the base itself came late and went to the point (F008-18). At Phuoc Long the 4th Corps' gunners found the ARVN firing above their estimate of the ration, the B-2 Front's intelligence section named the Bien Hoa arsenal, and the General Staff began, for the first time, to count the Republic's own industry as part of its strength (EV000-92). Its answer was not to change the campaign but to change its objectives: the 4th Corps was ordered to take the plants intact (EVMT01-4). Hanoi's judgment of 1974 that the South would be forced into a poor man's war was less true than history's, and it did not need to be true. The PAVN chose its ground under its 130mm guns, as it had chosen it since 1964 (EV000-91).

### Causal chains

**C008-1** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV008-1) The Aid Compliance Board makes the RVNAF buy a share of its ammunition bodies, batteries, wire and radios from its own plants from March 1965 (F008-2) → the arsenal's lines run, are proven and survive Phu Cat and the palace, so that in 1974 they fill two-fifths of the small-arms requirement and a third of the 105mm from imported powder (EV008-5, EV008-6, EV002-6) → the squad gun's ration doubles in March 1974 and the 105mm batteries at Phuoc Long fire at a rate the PAVN's estimate of the ration does not allow (EV000-89, EV000-92) → the B-2 Front names the arsenal, and the General Staff's order for the final assault makes the Bien Hoa works an objective to be taken intact, so that on 30 April the plants built to stretch the Republic's last dollar become the victors' first industry in the South (EV000-98).

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1).** **C004-5** of memorandum 004 ran: the ARVN built around the belt-fed squad gun and abundant American ammunition → the ration of January 1974 silences the squad gun first and leaves battalions avoiding the contacts they cannot break by volume → division commanders hoard about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm against the ration → the hoards are abandoned intact in March 1975. In the war as now written it resolves differently at its second link: the ration is about 400 rounds a gun from March, not 200, the squad gun is rationed and not silenced, and the battalions of I Corps break contacts about four times a month rather than twice (EV000-89). The third and fourth links hold for a reason the chain did not name. The hoarding was driven by the division commanders' distrust of the corps depots, not by scarcity alone, and with more 5.56mm flowing from Bien Hoa they hoarded the surplus; the hoards were of the same order and were lost with I Corps as before (EV000-94). The chain was right about where the rounds ended and wrong about why.

### Downstream

There is no later phase. The plants pass to the Ministry of Heavy Industry in May 1975 and make cartridges for the PAVN's captured rifles (EV000-98). The workers of Bien Hoa, whom COSVN organised from 1965, and the engineers who stayed, are the South's first state industrial workforce under the new government. That is beyond this exercise's end and I note it only because the factories outlived the state that built them.

### Clock

The clock in 1973–75 is the appropriation, and the base changed what it bought, not what was voted (F008-20, F008-22). The fiscal 1975 vote was $700 million as historically; the stretch made it worth about $850 million to $1 billion of history's, which was still well short of the $1.45 billion the administration had asked for [H]. The April 1975 request was refused, and nothing in the factories touched the reasons. The arsenal's one effect on the American clock ran the wrong way: it gave the advocates of the cut an argument, and it was half true. American tolerance does not move by a band.

## §4.H Historical comparison

The cumulative force has changed the Republic's interior a great deal and its end not at all, and this memorandum adds to the interior in a new category: things the Republic made for itself.

Against history, the Republic of this war owned from 1964 a steelworks, an arsenal, rebuild works, a radio plant and a battery works, and from 1965 an army made to buy from them. The PF posts of the held belt had radios four to five years before history's and kept them on the air with domestic batteries; the Front attacked them about a third to a half less often, invisibly, and answered the posts that called by ambushing the men who came. The coast road came back from the flood of 1964 in six weeks. Vietnamization's equipment went into a rebuild loop that existed rather than a crash programme. In 1974 the Republic's last aid dollar bought a third to two-fifths more ammunition in the categories it could make, the squad gun's ration was doubled, and the posts' radios stayed on the air when history's went silent.

Lives are the hardest part to state and I state them as judgments on rates. Across 1964–72 the radio at the post, by the third to half fewer attacks on held-belt posts that could call, saved perhaps 3,000–7,000 territorial soldiers and constables, shown at work at Long Huu, Chuong Thien and Cai Lay and offset in part by the ambushes of the relief (EV002-2, EV004-2, EV008-4). In 1974 the domestic rounds saved perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead (EV000-91). The American dead are unchanged by any figure I can defend.

What has not changed is the end. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, the plants are taken intact, and the margin the base bought in 1974 was spent on fights that did not decide the war.

By region, as the meta-review requires. In the Delta the effect is largest and is shown at Long Huu in 1964, in Chuong Thien in 1966, at My Phuoc Tay in Cai Lay in 1967 and at Long My in 1971: posts that called, a Front that learned to attack the relief, and a district that handed its call to Vietnamese guns. Rice moves a little there, where the pumps sold against the titles put a dry-season crop on about 300,000 hectares of the held Delta by 1974. On the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps the effect is the bridges of Quang Nam and Quang Ngai in 1964 and the Phu Cat lot in Binh Dinh in 1967; the PF radios did nothing for the hamlets of northern Binh Dinh that the PAVN 3rd Division took back in 1972, which the record already holds. In the Highlands the effect is thin and specific: thirty Koho villages along the Da Nhim line with a wage and a radio from 1966 (EV008-3); the Rhade and Jarai of FULRO's country, in Darlac, Quang Duc and Phu Bon, got nothing from the base, and their grievances were not of a kind a factory addresses. In the Saigon belt the effect is the plants themselves, at Bien Hoa, Thu Duc and Go Vap: 60,000–80,000 wages, a working class the Front organised from 1965, and in 1974 perhaps one soldier's family in eight in III Corps with a factory wage in it.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state is unchanged: rung (a), collapse, with Saigon falling on 30 April 1975.

**The date, tested.** The window is 27 April to the first days of May, derived and not carried. The mechanisms in the cumulative force that pull the fall later are the better battalions at Duc Duc, Xuan Loc, Phan Rang and Cu Chi, the PAVN's greener divisions, a stronger held belt in the Delta, and now an army whose ammunition and radios did not go dark in 1974. Those that pull it earlier are a President more certain his army could not hold alone and an army more dependent on American fire it no longer had. This memorandum's mechanism acts on the fights of 1974 and on the fights the PAVN bypassed in April 1975. None of the later-pulling mechanisms reaches the critical path: Ban Me Thuot, the orders of 13–14 March, the dissolution of I Corps toward its families, and the 1st Corps' march against the rains. The delay this provision buys in the collapse of I and II Corps is a few days at most against a threshold of about three weeks (EVMT01-4). The earlier-pulling and later-pulling mechanisms both act on decisions and fights that the enemy's calendar absorbed, and the date that results is the historical one.

**Why not rung (b).** The ladder's overlap stands: the historical fall, twenty-five months after the last American combat troops left, satisfies the words of rung (b), and the exercise keeps (a) and (b) apart by the date alone (DIRMT01-1). The date has not moved, so the rung has not.

**What this memorandum does to the rung conditions.** The meta-review named three conditions under which the rung could move: an air arm the Republic owns, sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation, and the choice of the corps commanders (POSMT01-1). This is the first provision to reach the second, and it reaches it in part: brass, steel, batteries and wire, not powder, fuel, aircraft or the heavy guns. The other two conditions are untouched. The Republic of 1975 still had no air force that could break a siege, and its corps were still commanded by the President's choices. A Republic with the base and an air arm it owned would have been a different test; with the base alone, it is a better-supplied army that loses the same war on the same day.

**Running sums.** American lives saved across all memoranda remain a judgment of about 2,500–5,000, mostly 1965–69, five to nine percent of the historical dead; this memorandum adds nothing I can defend, and the sum is below a band (POSMT01-5). South Vietnamese territorial lives saved before 1973 now stand at about 13,000–32,000 across memoranda 004 and 008, resting on the rates of the posts that held (memorandum 004's nine in ten against four in five) and the attacks not made (this memorandum's third to half); with memorandum 007's third fewer battalions broken in 1966–67, this is the largest sub-band accumulation in the record and it lies on the territorial and ARVN effectiveness variables, which it would move by a band in 1965–67 if it were measured rather than judged. In 1974 the domestic rounds add about 1,000–2,500 to the Republic's lives saved, inside the uncertainty of the reported figure.

**The 1974 vote and the 1975 refusal,** treated as events a provision can change. This provision changed what the vote bought and not the vote, and it gave the cut's advocates an argument (F008-20, F008-22). I do not think any provision that leaves the Republic dependent on American air could have changed the vote of 1974; one that made the Republic visibly cheaper to sustain might have, and this one made it cheaper and less visible. The refusal of April 1975 came after the Highlands and I Corps were gone and was not about ammunition.

**Phase redraw.** I propose none. The base's weight falls in 1974, inside Phase VI as drawn; the shape of the war has not changed.

## §5 Delta ledger

**Forces gained.** P008a, the metallurgical and machining base: about 200,000–300,000 tonnes of crude steel a year at full rate, an arsenal for cartridge cases, bullets, mortar and 105mm bodies, engine, vehicle and weapons rebuild works, a steel boatyard, bridge and fortification fabrication, and agricultural machinery shops, with 35,000–50,000 workers. P008b, the electronics base: PRC-10, PRC-25 and PRC-77 manpacks and vehicle sets, hamlet radios, dry cells, field wire, a transistor assembly plant and a signal rebuild depot, with 20,000–35,000 workers. Both arrive 1 January 1964 and are captured intact on 30 April 1975.

**Institutions gained and lost.** The Directorate of Defence Industry (1964; captured by the palace February 1970). The domestic share enforced by the Aid Compliance Board (1 March 1965; survives the Board's decline because the Joint General Staff wants it). Proof-fired lot acceptance (1968; hollowed out 1970). Territorial artillery platoons in the Delta's districts (February 1970).

**Events.** New: EV008-1 to EV008-7. Altered: EV002-2, EV004-2, EV002-6, EV000-89, EV000-91, EV000-92, EVMT01-4, EV000-98.

**Variables moved, by band.** No chapter end vector moves. The provision's pushes act inside bands: RFPF effectiveness and night control at the held-belt posts in 1964–71 (the gain at the post offset in part by the platoons' withdrawal behind their wire, F008-12); ARVN self-sufficiency, invisible until 1974 and offset at the end of 1974 by the dependence on American air; logistics distribution and engineers, the bridges and the rebuild loop; the rural economy in the held Delta; corruption, through the deferment list and the palace's Directorate; inflation, slightly lower through factory wages and domestic production.

**New categories.** Domestic war industry. The Republic's own working class as a field for the Front's organisers.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- The Republic owns an industrial base from 1964 and an army made to buy from it from 1965; by 1974 it makes about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, a third of its 105mm rounds, most of its mortar rounds and nearly all of its radio batteries and wire, from imported powder and semiconductors (EV008-1, EV008-6, EV000-89).
- The last aid dollar stretches: the $700 million of fiscal 1975 buys about what $850 million to $1 billion bought historically, the squad gun's ration doubles from March 1974, and the RVNAF's communications do not go dark in 1974–75. This is the first provision to reach sustainment that survives the 1974 appropriation, and it saves perhaps 1,000–2,500 of the Republic's dead in 1974 (F008-23, EV000-91).
- Every PF post in the held belt has a radio four to five years early and keeps it on the air, and the Front attacks the posts that can call about a third to a half less often, which no statistic shows (EV002-2, F008-16).
- The call for fire is handed to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets before the advisers leave, and the districts of the Delta keep their posts through 1972 on that handover (EV008-7).
- Repair is measured in weeks, not months: the coast road after the 1964 flood, the Da Nhim towers, and the rebuild loop that makes Vietnamization's crash depot programme unnecessary (EV008-2, EV008-3, EV008-6).
- The Front has a new target and a new constituency: the relief column it ambushes with the Republic's own wire and batteries, and a working class in Bien Hoa it organises from 1965 (EV008-4, EV008-1).

### What has not changed

- The date and the rung. The base acts on the fights of 1974 and on fights the PAVN bypassed in 1975, and buys a few days at most against the three weeks that would have pushed the assault into the rains. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975 (EVMT01-4).
- The decisions of March 1975: Thieu's orders of 13–14 March rested mostly on the absence of American air and of a reserve, which no factory supplied, and I Corps dissolved toward its families, which no factory reached (back-reference: memoranda 005 and MT01).
- The vote: the appropriation of 1974 and the refusal of 1975 are history's, and the arsenal gave the cut's advocates an argument (F008-22).
- Fuel, aircraft, the heavy guns, propellant and explosive all still came from abroad; the VNAF was grounded on the historical scale.
- The main-force war: no battle from Binh Gia to Dak To turned on a factory, and the enemy still chose every fight.
- The palace: the Directorate fell to the lapse of the American lever with the four institutions before it, and kept its output while losing its honesty (EV002-6).
- The hamlet outside the post: the platoon that could call down the guns stayed behind its wire, and the hamlet after dark was not held better than before (F008-12).
