# Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964 [H40], none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year [H40]. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon, and at Binh Gia, Song Be, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they destroy ARVN battalions in set-piece battles. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

One thing in this phase is new. On the first day of 1964 the junta promulgates a land reform that ends rent on tenanted rice land, gives the land free to the man who works it and confirms to him whatever the Viet Minh or the Front had already given, together with an independent commission against corruption backed by an American power to suspend aid. Neither touches the main force, and the double collapse in Saigon and in the field runs much as before. What changes, slowly and only where the government holds the hamlets, is what the government's arrival means to a tenant: by the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled [X-001], the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the first musters have shown the RVNAF that it is about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls [X-001]. The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

A second change runs beside the first. The same decrees of 1 January gave the Republic a rural police and a system of choosing its province and district chiefs by examination, from a ranked list, for at least two years (EV002-1). Khanh's coup tested both within a month and both survived it (EV000-1). By July 1965 about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have been appointed through the list, and the Special Branch has begun to take the Front's village committees on evidence; the Front has killed about 410 constables, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security (EV002-2) [X-002]. None of it touches the main-force war or Saigon's coups.

A third change runs from the same first day, and it is a change in what this command can see rather than in what the Republic is. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks begin flying in January 1964, and the enemy's rear stops being a matter of guesswork. The coastal picket, TIDE TABLE, sees the trawlers of the North's maritime group every time they run south and closes the sea route within a year (EV003-1); Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months before it did historically (EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats near the destroyers, Pierce Arrow is not flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the real attack of 2 August alone (EV000-3). The SA-2 sites are mapped as they are built, and Washington still declines to strike them (EV000-15). None of it touches the coups in Saigon, the summer offensive or the July decision.

A fourth change is in the hands of the men who fight. From January 1964 the ARVN, the Rangers, the Airborne, the Marines and the Regional and Popular Forces draw modern carbines with red-dot sights, a belt-fed squad gun in every regular squad and a marksman's rifle beside it (EV004-1). The territorials who hold the hamlets at night are no longer outgunned by the guerrillas who come over the wall, and in the held belt more posts hold through the night (EV002-2). The battles of the summer of 1965 are still lost, at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, because the enemy still chooses where and when to fight; they are lost at a smaller price, and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out (EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18). The outposts that fall give the Front the same carbines.

## EV001-1 · The Land-to-the-Tiller Decree and the Commission Against Corruption · 1 Jan 1964 · Saigon · NEW · 001
On 1 January 1964 the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh, with Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho's cabinet, promulgated two decree-laws drafted with the US Mission over the preceding weeks (P001a, P001b). The first ended the collection of rent on tenanted rice land from the coming main-season harvest, made every present tiller the provisional owner of the land he worked up to 3 hectares in the Delta and 1 hectare in the Central Lowlands, set the landlord's retention of tenanted land at zero and his compensation at 2.5 times the land's annual paddy yield, 20% in cash and the rest in eight-year bonds indexed to the paddy price, and confirmed by name every distribution the Viet Minh or the Front had made to the man now tilling the land [M001]. The second created the Commission Against Corruption under a Commissioner appointed for six years, Judge Truong Vinh Khoa of the Saigon Court of Appeal, a Special Court for Corruption, a Pay and Muster Verification Service, and a Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board whose American co-chair, the Deputy Chief of Mission, could suspend aid to a named command, importer or province and whose concurrence was needed to remove the Commissioner [M001].

Minh read the land decree on Radio Saigon that evening; its first sentence was the confirmation of the Front's distributions [X-001]. Within a week the Vietnamese Information Service's loudspeaker teams and leaflets had carried it to the district towns of the Delta and the coastal Centre. The landowners' associations of Saigon and Can Tho protested to the council within days, and the protest was carried by officers of landed families who sat on it [X-001]. Two USAF-contracted survey aircraft began photographing the rice lands from Tan Son Nhut on 13 January, tied to ground control laid by an Army topographic detachment from the old French triangulation; they finished the Delta in early April and the coastal Centre in May [X-001].

## EV002-1 · The Police and Provincial Administration decrees; the first list · 1 Jan – 27 Apr 1964 · Saigon · NEW · 002
On 1 January 1964, in the same session that promulgated the land and anti-corruption decrees (EV001-1), the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh promulgated two more decree-laws drafted with the US Mission and the aid mission's Public Safety Division (P002a, P002b). The first rebuilt the National Police around the village: a resident post of 8 to 12 constables in every village the government held, a Police Special Branch with sole charge of intelligence on the Viet Cong infrastructure, a Police Field Force to be raised at once rather than in 1966, a national identity card and family census, a pay and career statute, and magistrate review of every arrest within 72 hours, with detention without trial capped at two years and reviewed every six months [M002]. The Directorate General was given to Vo Dinh Cuong, a career inspector of the old Sûreté who had served in the police under the French and under Diem and had kept out of Nhu's political service, for a fixed term removable only for cause [X-002]. The second decree created the Provincial Administration Selection Board, seven members under Judge Dinh Quang Tuyen of the Court of Cassation, and an Inspectorate of Provincial Administration, and made unlawful the appointment of any province chief, district chief or deputy for administration who was not taken from the Board's ranked list [M002].

The two decrees were read on Radio Saigon on the evening of 1 January after the land decree, and drew less notice than it did; the landowners protested the land decree, and the officers who would lose their provinces said nothing yet, because there was nothing yet to lose [X-002]. The British Advisory Mission under R. G. K. Thompson, in Saigon since 1961, lent four Special Branch officers from the Federation of Malaysia to write the Special Branch's first manuals; the first 2,500 recruits a month entered the police training centres at Vung Tau and Da Lat from February [H] [X-002]. The Board's written examination was sat on 2 March at the National Institute of Administration by about 1,640 officers and civil servants, of whom about 1,000 were judged credible after the three-day assessments held through March and April [X-002].

On 27 April 1964 the Board published its first list in the official gazette: ranked candidates for 31 posts, the 6 province chiefs and 25 district chiefs whom the Inspectorate's first sweep had found worst, nine of them in Khanh's "acting" appointments of February (EV000-1) [X-002]. Khanh's government appointed 27 of the 31 from the first name on each list, 3 from the second and 1 from the third; it refused no list [X-002]. By 31 December 1964 about 95 of the Republic's roughly 290 chiefs had been appointed through the list, a third of the whole, and the Board's register of candidates had become the first record the Republic had ever kept of what its officers could actually do (F002-5) [X-002].

## EV003-1 · Operation TIDE TABLE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · The II Corps coast from Qui Nhon to Cam Ranh Bay (Binh Dinh, Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa Provinces) and the Ca Mau coast of An Xuyen Province · NEW · 003
The first Global Hawk orbit went up from Clark Air Base on 6 January 1964 and took station off the II Corps coast, about 60 nautical miles out between Qui Nhon and Cam Ranh Bay, with its radar's maritime mode sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep (EC003-3) [M003]. The operation was named TIDE TABLE. Its quarry was the steel-hulled trawlers of about 100 tons run south from Haiphong and the Do Son peninsula by the PAVN's maritime transport group, Group 759, which became Group 125 of the PAVN Navy in the course of 1964 [H]. Its hunters were few: the Vietnamese Navy's Sea Force, with a handful of PC-461-class submarine chasers and PCE escorts out of Qui Nhon and Nha Trang, the motorised and sail junks of the Junk Force's coastal divisions, and, when the Seventh Fleet lent them, a US destroyer or two off the coast [H]. Before January nobody had been looking for the trawlers at all; the coast is about 2,000 kilometres long and carried tens of thousands of fishing junks [M-TNA].

The first track came on the night of 18–19 February. A contact moving at a steady ten knots on a course that had begun in the Gulf of Tonkin two days earlier, running 80 nautical miles offshore past Quang Ngai, turned west at dusk toward the Phu Yen coast; it did not stop, did not fish and did not change speed [X-003]. The exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut flagged it at 1940, and the Vietnamese Navy's coastal command at Nha Trang sent a PC-461 from her patrol line off Tuy Hoa. The radar held the trawler for seven hours, so the patrol craft did not have to search; she had only to steam. She came up with the trawler at 0310 about four miles off Vung Ro Bay, under a low overcast and in a moderate northeast swell. The trawler's crew ran her toward the beach and fired her demolition charges, as Group 759's standing orders required; the forward hold blew, the after hold did not [H] [X-003]. A boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of cargo before the fire reached it: Chinese Type 56 carbines and assault rifles, B-40 (RPG-2) launchers and their rockets, 82mm mortar rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm ammunition [X-003]. Seven of the crew were taken from the water, two of them wounded; nine died in the explosion or drowned [X-003]. The Vietnamese lost one sailor killed and four wounded to small-arms fire from the beach, where a VC reception party had waited with porters [X-003].

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven contacts that the cell judged to be trawlers, on the II Corps coast and on the Ca Mau peninsula, where the landings went into the mangrove creeks of the Bo De and the Cua Lon [X-003]. Four were intercepted: two scuttled or blew up, one was taken nearly intact off Binh Dinh in April by the destroyer USS *Edsall*'s boarding party with a Vietnamese liaison officer, and one ran into the Bo De ahead of a junk division and landed most of its cargo [X-003]. Six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon, which the radar saw as a reversal of course at about the time the trawler's own radar would have detected the patrol [X-003]. One landed unobserved in May, on a night when the orbit's aircraft had diverted to Clark with a generator fault and the relief was four hours late [X-003]. The casualty figures above are counts of bodies and prisoners and are reliable; the cargo tonnages are estimates from what was recovered and from the prisoners' statements of their loads, and carry a margin of about a third [E: recovery and interrogation].

The prisoners told the rest. Interrogated at Nha Trang, the crew of the April capture described the landing points, the signal lamps, the reception parties and about two years of earlier voyages, and the Vietnamese Navy found two of the landing beaches in Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa from their directions [X-003]. TIDE TABLE did not starve the Front; the tonnage it stopped in six months was a small fraction of what moved by sea and through Laos. What it ended was the cheapest and most direct route by which heavy weapons reached the coastal provinces of II Corps and the lower Delta, and it ended it by being seen, which is the subject of the next event in this chapter (EV003-2) (F003-17).

## EV004-1 · Operation IRON GATE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Saigon port and Tan Son Nhut; the Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the division training centres and the province RF/PF training camps of IV and III Corps, then countrywide · NEW · 004
The first ship of the rearmament, MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez*, berthed at the Khanh Hoi wharves in Saigon on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines, their Aimpoint CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249 light machine guns and 320 M110A2 marksman rifles, and about 380 tonnes of 5.56mm and 7.62mm ammunition [X-004]. MACV called the issue Operation IRON GATE, because the first rule of it was that no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate in exchange. The second rule was the zeroing range. Every company that drew carbines drew them by name against its muster roll, fired each one at 25 metres until the dot sat on the point of aim, and handed in its M1 Garands, M1 and M2 carbines, BARs and Thompsons before it left (EC004-2) [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn, chief of the small-arms new-equipment training team, set the course at five days for an infantry company and three for a Popular Forces platoon, and refused to shorten it when the corps commanders asked [X-004].

The order of issue was the order of the war's danger. The ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions in the Delta and the Regional and Popular Forces of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac provinces drew first, in January and February; the Airborne Brigade, the Marine battalions and the Ranger battalions of the general reserve in February and March; I and II Corps from April [X-004]. Khanh's coup of 30 January stopped the issue for nine days and cost more: three of the division commanders who had sent their companies to the ranges were replaced, and their successors recalled the companies to garrison the province towns against a counter-coup before the course was finished (F004-5) [X-004]. Venn's teams counted about 2.4 million Vietnamese training man-days in the first half of 1964 and reckoned that about a fifth of them were lost to the coup and its aftermath, in units that drew carbines and went back to their posts without zeroing them [X-004].

By 30 June about 310,000 carbines had been issued, about 180,000 of them to the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles to the regular infantry; the marksmen's course at Quang Trung, three weeks long, had graduated its first 1,100 [X-004]. The depots took back about 290,000 old weapons [X-004]. About a third of them went, at the Directorate's insistence and over this headquarters' objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia, who were not covered by the rearmament and wanted something better than shotguns; the rest were crated at the Go Vap arsenal [X-004]. On the ranges of the Delta the change was visible in an afternoon. A Popular Forces platoon at Can Duoc that had fired its Garands at a 100-metre target and hit it about once in four shots put most of its rounds into the same target by the third morning with carbines its smallest men could hold steady, and asked to be allowed to keep the spent cases as proof to show the village [X-004]. The Front's province committee in Long An reported the new weapons to the region in February and called them a "trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty"; it ordered its local-force companies to capture one for study, and the first was taken on 22 March in an ambush at Rach Kien [X-004].

## EV000-1 · Khanh's coup · 30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002
Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh without firing a shot [H8]. He acted with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau [H8]. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were put under house arrest on a charge of plotting a French-backed neutralist settlement, and Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho was detained [H8]. The one death was Major Nguyen Van Nhung, Minh's aide, executed after the takeover [H8]. Khanh kept Minh as a figurehead chief of state [H8]. The generals were tried in closed session on 28–29 May and found guilty only of "lax morality" [H8].

The coup was not caused by the decrees of 1 January; it was the product of the generals' rivalries and Khanh's ambition, and it came on the historical date [X-001]. But the reforms shaped what followed. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay gave Khanh their quiet support in the hope of a government that would let the decrees lapse (F001-16) [X-001]. In February Khanh asked Commissioner Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals; Khoa replied that the Commission had no evidence against them and would bring no case without it [X-001]. Khanh moved to replace him, and the American co-chair of the Aid Compliance Board refused the concurrence the decree required [X-001]. The generals went before a military court instead, on the "lax morality" charge of the historical record.

Washington was surprised but quick to back the new man; President Johnson sent Khanh a personal note on 2 February [H8], which in this war carried the condition, at the urging of the US Mission and this headquarters, that the United States would support no government that repealed either decree (F001-15) [X-001]. Khanh, who needed American backing more than he needed the landlords, accepted it and kept both. The coup mattered less for what Khanh did than for the precedent it set: a second violent change of government within three months, carried out by officers against officers, and accepted by the United States. Khanh moved, as every coup-maker had, to place his men in the provinces, and the decree of 1 January now stood in his way (EV002-1). In March, Secretary of Defense McNamara visited and endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 of 17 March 1964 committed the United States to the survival of an independent, non-communist South Vietnam [H]. In June 1964 General William C. Westmoreland, Harkins's deputy since January, took command of MACV, and General Maxwell D. Taylor replaced Henry Cabot Lodge as ambassador in July [H].

In this war Khanh's coup was the first test of the police and provincial decrees, and it came thirty days after them, before the Board had published a list. On the morning of 30 January Khanh's officers detained the new Director General of National Police, Vo Dinh Cuong, as they had detained the police chief on the historical course, and in the first week of February Khanh named "acting" chiefs in eleven provinces and districts [X-002]. The US Mission and this headquarters treated the two decrees as part of the condition Johnson's note attached to the land and anti-corruption decrees: the Aid Compliance Board gave notice that a province whose chief was not taken from the list would receive no counterpart funds once the first list was published, and Cuong was released on 2 February and restored to his office (F002-16) [X-002]. Khanh kept the Board, because he had no candidate for its chairmanship and saw a reputation for reform in it, and kept his acting chiefs until the list replaced nine of the eleven in April and May; the other two placed well enough to be appointed from it [X-002]. It was a partial failure of the reform's first test and a partial success, and it set the pattern that held until 1970: the Board survived each government because the government that inherited it found it cheaper to keep than to break.

## EV002-2 · The first posts and the Front's answer · 1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 002,004
The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night (EC002-1) [X-002]. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village [X-002]. From February the platoon beside the post carried M4A1 carbines with red-dot sights, drawn under IRON GATE (EV004-1); the constables, whom the rearmament did not cover, kept the carbines of 1950 [X-004]. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965 [M002]. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads (EC002-2) [M002]. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered [X-002].

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected (F002-12). A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night [X-002]. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966 [X-002]. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required (F002-8) [X-002].

Where the platoon was there, it now held. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about nine in ten held, against about four in five that the programme's design would have given them with the platoons' old weapons [X-004]. The case that the Delta's advisers told for a year was the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140 against a post whose PF platoon had zeroed its carbines in March. The platoon's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and the company's first assault, which on the Front's own experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause; the second was not pressed, and the company withdrew before the Regional Forces boat company from Ham Long arrived at 0320, leaving eleven dead on the bund and taking its wounded [X-004]. The Front learned that lesson faster than this headquarters expected. From December its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in, and, against the posts that held, the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market (F004-1, F004-2) [X-004]. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts [X-004].

The weapons the Front took changed with its targets. Of the roughly 300 weapons lost with the police posts, nearly all were the constables' old carbines; but across the countryside the outposts that did fall, the Popular Forces deserters who walked away with their arms, and the ARVN battalions ambushed in the summer of 1965 gave the Front between about 10,000 and 30,000 M4A1 carbines by the end of 1965, with their sights (F004-7) [X-004]. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and each one needed the Republic's own 5.56mm to fire.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not [X-002]. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed (F002-13) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

## EV001-2 · The first titling teams · 16 Mar – 31 Dec 1964 · An Giang, Phong Dinh, Vinh Long, Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 001
The first 40 mobile titling teams of about 25 men each took the field on 16 March 1964, working outward from province capitals and main roads into villages the district could reinforce by day, each escorted by a Regional Forces company armed with M1 and M2 carbines and M1918A2 automatic rifles [X-001]. The first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen in An Giang on 21 April, in the Hoa Hao country where the Front was weakest, and the first in a contested district at Phung Hiep in Phong Dinh on 11 May [X-001]. By July 70 teams were in the field, the most the provinces could escort without stripping their daytime reaction forces (F001-6) [X-001]. They moved by road, in the LCM-6 and LCVP craft of the River Assault Groups and by hired sampan, and to villages beyond both by CH-21C and UH-1B helicopters when the operations staff would release them [X-001].

By 31 December 1964 titles had been issued for about 210,000 hectares to about 170,000 families [X-001], about a fifth of the target, almost all of it in hamlets held by the government at night or contested only lightly: An Giang, the riverine districts of Phong Dinh and Vinh Long along the Bassac, the inner districts of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and the coastal plain around Hoi An and Tam Ky in Quang Nam [X-001]. The rent stop held at the main-season harvest of December 1964 in most districts the government controlled; landlords' agents collected under Regional Forces escort in parts of Vinh Long, Vinh Binh and Dinh Tuong, which led to the first suspension a few months later (EV001-5) [X-001]. Twenty-three team members were killed by mines and ambush during the year [X-001]. The figure the Mission reported to Washington each month was titles issued; this headquarters reported it with the warning that it measured the government's paperwork, not the peasants' allegiance (F001-10).

## EV001-4 · The dawn musters · 7 Apr – 31 Dec 1964 · ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions and the Regional and Popular Forces of the Delta, then countrywide · NEW · 001
The Pay and Muster Verification Service's first 20 teams began unannounced dawn musters of the ARVN 21st Division's 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments around Bac Lieu and Soc Trang on 7 April 1964, and of the 7th and 9th Divisions in Dinh Tuong and Vinh Long in May; by the autumn 60 teams were mustering about 480 units a month across all four corps [X-001]. Each man was enrolled on an identity card with his photograph and fingerprints, and from August the Service's paymasters paid the enrolled units of IV Corps at the parade rather than through their commanders [X-001]. By 31 December about 41,000 names had been struck from the rolls of a force carried at about 514,000, about 8% [X-001]; the rate ran from about 4% in the Airborne and Marine battalions to about 15% in some Regional Forces companies of the Delta [X-001]. These are counts of men present against names on the roll, not estimates, and they are the best figures this headquarters had ever had for the army it advised.

Washington read them as a smaller army. Secretary McNamara, visiting in May, was briefed on the first returns and told the President that the RVNAF's real strength was below its reported strength by a margin that would grow as the musters spread (F001-9) [X-001]. In the units mustered twice, the second muster found few ghosts, because commanders had struck them before the teams came (F001-11) [X-001]. The Commission opened 412 cases by the end of the year, almost all against company and battalion commanders and province finance officers, and brought 61 to trial [X-001].

## EV001-3 · The Can Giuoc killings · 9 Jun – 14 Jul 1964 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · NEW · 001
In May 1964 titling teams from Tan An began work in the villages of Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, the rice districts of Long An between Saigon and the sea, where the survey of December 1963 had found most of the strategic hamlets destroyed and the Front's infrastructure intact [H] [X-001]. The province chief, under pressure from Saigon for a showcase close to the capital, sent the teams into hamlets the government held by day only and posted the claimants' names in the village offices, as the procedure then required (EC001-4) [X-001]. Each team worked under the escort of a Long An Regional Forces company that withdrew to its post at dusk.

On the night of 9 June armed propaganda teams of the Can Giuoc district committee, with the Long An provincial security section and screened by a company of the provincial 506th Local Force Battalion, entered three hamlets of Phuoc Lai village, read out names from the posted list, and shot four men who had claimed titles and the village land committee's secretary [X-001]. Over the following five weeks the same pattern was repeated in eleven hamlets of the two districts: by 14 July 37 title holders and 9 members of village land committees had been killed, and several hundred families had brought their titles back to the district office at Can Giuoc rather than keep them in the house [X-001]. The Regional Forces companies, posted in mud forts along Route 5 and the canal, did not move at night, and no reaction force came (POSTNA-2). The figures are this headquarters' count from district and province reports, cross-checked by the American province adviser, and are a floor [X-001].

The killings were the land programme's first lesson in what it did not change (F001-8). Teams stopped posting names in hamlets not held at night, and titles in contested hamlets were thereafter held at the district office until the hamlet was secured or the owner asked for his. A COSVN directive captured near Duc Hoa in August told district committees not to obstruct the titling, to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper", to keep collecting the Front's agricultural tax, and to make examples only where a hamlet's people began to inform (F001-10) [X-001]. In Can Giuoc the titling was suspended until the following spring. It was the first evidence that the reform's value in any district was exactly the value of the security behind it (F001-4).

## EV000-2 · The battle of Nam Dong · 6 Jul 1964 · Nam Dong CIDG camp, about 50 km west of Da Nang near the Laotian border · HIST · 000
At 02:30 on 6 July 1964 a force that the Americans estimated at about two battalions of Viet Cong and PAVN, some 800–900 men [H4], attacked the CIDG camp at Nam Dong in the mountains west of Da Nang, near the Laotian border. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-726 under Captain Roger H. C. Donlon, 12 Americans in all, with one Australian warrant officer of the Australian Army Training Team Vietnam, 311 Montagnard strikers, 50 Nung mercenaries and seven Vietnamese Special Forces [H4]. The attack opened with mortar fire on the command post and a sapper assault on the main gate; the outer perimeter was penetrated and many strikers deserted or went over to the attackers [H4].

The inner perimeter, held by the Americans and the Nungs, survived about five hours of close fighting until the attackers withdrew at dawn [H4]. Two Americans and the Australian adviser were killed; 58 strikers and Nungs died and 57 were wounded, and more than 100 strikers defected [H4]. Fifty-five to sixty-two attackers' bodies were counted around the wire [H4], a figure more reliable than most body counts because the dead lay inside a perimeter the defenders held. Donlon, wounded several times, became the first American awarded the Medal of Honor in Vietnam [H4]. Nam Dong is the pattern of the border camp war: an isolated garrison of irregulars whose loyalty is uncertain, a night assault by a force that has chosen its moment, no reinforcement before daylight, and a defence that survives on the quality of a handful of men.

## EV000-3 · The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution · 2–11 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 30–31 July 1964 South Vietnamese commando boats under the covert OPLAN 34A programme, run by MACV's Studies and Observations Group, raided the North Vietnamese islands of Hon Me and Hon Ngu [H7]. On 2 August the destroyer USS *Maddox*, on a DESOTO signals intelligence patrol under Captain John J. Herrick, was attacked in daylight by three P-4 torpedo boats of the 135th Torpedo Squadron [H7]. The *Maddox* fired, four F-8 Crusaders from USS *Ticonderoga* attacked the retiring boats, and the North Vietnamese lost four killed and six wounded with three boats damaged; the *Maddox* was struck by one machine-gun round and suffered no casualties [H7]. Johnson, as historically, chose a warning rather than retaliation, and ordered the patrol resumed with USS *Turner Joy* in company [H7].

At Admiral Sharp's request I moved the Global Hawk orbit from the II Corps coast to the Gulf on 3 August (EC003-7). There were no SA-2s in the North and nothing that could reach 60,000 feet, and the aircraft took station about 40 nautical miles east of Hon Me, with the destroyers, the coast from Hon Matt to the mouth of the Song Ma and the open Gulf to the east in its radar's maritime mode [M003]. On the night of 4 August the *Maddox* and *Turner Joy*, in rough weather with thunderstorms, fired for about two hours at radar contacts they took to be a second attack [H7]. The watch in the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut that night was Major Harold Kessling's. His radar log showed the two destroyers and their manoeuvres, a cluster of fishing junks about 30 nautical miles to the west, rain cells moving across the Gulf, and no fast small craft within 40 nautical miles of the ships at any time between 2000 and 2330 local [X-003]. P-4 boats making attack runs at 40 knots are good radar targets even in a sea state of three and four, and the radar had tracked the three boats of 2 August from their base on the same set [X-003]. Herrick cabled that the "entire action leaves many doubts" [H7]. Kessling's report reached MACV at about midnight Saigon time and CINCPAC an hour later, which in Washington was the late morning of 4 August, while McNamara and Sharp were still trying to establish what had happened [X-003].

What followed was Washington's decision, and it went the other way from history. Historically Johnson had decided on retaliation within thirty minutes of the first reports [H7]. Here McNamara told him in the early afternoon that the ships' reports were contradicted by a radar that had watched the whole engagement, that Herrick himself doubted them, and that the Navy could not confirm an attack [X-003]. Johnson held the strike order and, at about 1900, cancelled it [X-003]. Operation Pierce Arrow was not flown. The 64 sorties against the torpedo-boat bases and the Vinh oil storage did not happen; Lieutenant (jg) Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Lieutenant (jg) Everett Alvarez was not shot down and taken prisoner that day [H] [X-003].

The resolution was sent anyway. Johnson had carried a draft since May, an election was three months away, and the attack of 2 August was real [H]. On 5 August he asked Congress for authority to repel armed attack against US forces and to prevent further aggression, citing the 2 August attack on a US destroyer in international waters and Hanoi's support of the war in the South, and he said nothing of the night of the 4th [X-003]. The debate ran three days longer than history's: Senator Wayne Morse, tipped about the 34A raids, asked why the destroyer had been near Hon Me at all, and Senator Gaylord Nelson's amendment against a land war was again declined by Senator Fulbright on the administration's assurance that no such war was intended [H] [X-003]. Congress passed the Southeast Asia Resolution on 10 August, 414–0 in the House and 85–3 in the Senate, in the words of the historical text [X-003]. Johnson signed it on 11 August. It was again the only legislative authority for the war that followed, but it rested on one attack that had happened rather than on two of which one had not, and the radar log of 4 August sat in the files at Tan Son Nhut and Honolulu as a record of a false alarm, not as the concealed proof of a pretext. B-57B bombers were moved to Bien Hoa in the aftermath [H3], within mortar range of the countryside around it.

## EV000-4 · The Vung Tau Charter crisis and the September coup attempt · 16 Aug – 14 Sep 1964 · Saigon, Hue and Da Nang · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002
Khanh tried to use the Tonkin crisis to make himself president under a new constitution, the Vung Tau Charter of 16 August 1964 [H] [H10]. Students and the Buddhist movement led by Thich Tri Quang answered with demonstrations and riots in Saigon, Hue and Da Nang, and Khanh conceded the repeal of the charter [H10]. At a Military Revolutionary Council meeting on 26–27 August he promised to resign, but the generals could agree on no successor, and a triumvirate of Khanh, Minh and Khiem was formed to rule for two months; Khanh dominated it [H10]. Khiem was sent abroad as ambassador to Washington and Minh on a "goodwill tour" [H10].

Before dawn on 13 September Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc, with about ten battalions recruited among them and four moving up from the Mekong Delta, seized key points in Saigon and announced a new government on the radio while Khanh took refuge in Da Lat [H10]. The coup collapsed within a day. US officials publicly backed Khanh; Air Commodore Nguyen Cao Ky, commanding the Vietnamese Air Force, flew jets low over the city and brought loyal marines and paratroopers into Tan Son Nhut, and General Nguyen Chanh Thi supported him; Phat and Duc backed down on the morning of 14 September [H10]. No one was killed [H10]. In this war the coup-makers had a programme as well as grievances. General Duc, the IV Corps commander, had learned in August that the Commission had opened a case on the ghost payrolls the musters had found in his corps, and Phat's broadcast of 13 September promised to "suspend the land decree pending review" and to dissolve the "foreign-controlled" Aid Compliance Board [X-001]. It was the first time a coup had announced a policy toward the countryside, and the policy was the landlords' (F001-16); the same broadcast promised to "restore the authority of the corps commanders over the provinces", which meant the abolition of the Selection Board [X-002]. Its failure within a day attached the reforms to the side that won: the Young Turks who had saved Khanh took up the land decree as their own cause, and Ky spoke of it in his first public statements as an argument for their claim to govern (F001-15) [X-001].

The lasting result was a new power centre: the younger officers, the "Young Turks" around Ky and Thi, who had saved Khanh and now knew they could unmake him [H10]. The war in the countryside was left to commanders whose first concern was the next coup.

The police played a part in this coup that it had never played before. The Special Branch, which watched the generals under its statute as well as the Front, reported to Cuong on 11 September that battalions of the 7th Division were being readied at My Tho without orders, and Cuong passed the report to Khanh and to the US Mission the same night [X-002]. The warning did not stop the coup, which came on schedule, but on 13 September the Saigon police stayed in their stations and the Field Force companies in their barracks, on Cuong's order, rather than joining either side, and the Mission's knowledge of the movement two days in advance let Ambassador Taylor tell the coup-makers within hours that the United States would not recognise them [X-002]. A police that was not one faction's instrument had raised the cost of a coup by a little, and the plotters' programme of abolishing the Board had made the Young Turks who defeated them its defenders, as it had made them the land decree's (F002-16).

## EV000-5 · The Hop Tac programme · Sep 1964 – mid-1965 · Saigon and the surrounding provinces · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002
In September 1964, at the urging of the US Mission and Westmoreland, the government launched Hop Tac ("cooperation"), a plan to pacify a ring of territory around Saigon outward in phases, the "oil spot" concept applied to the capital region [H] [H25]. The priority was explicit: concentrate resources on the Saigon area and accept little progress elsewhere for the time being [H25]. By then the trend elsewhere was plain. Ambassador Taylor's mission reported on 6 October that the provinces rated unsatisfactory for pacification had risen from 7 in July to 14 in August, and named the diversion of resources to Hop Tac as one possible cause [H25]. The mission nonetheless recommended unanimously that Hop Tac be given full support [H25].

The programme never reached its schedule. Its ARVN units were repeatedly pulled away by coups and coup alerts, its Vietnamese chain of command changed with each government, and the Viet Cong infrastructure in Gia Dinh, Long An and the provinces north of the capital was left largely intact [H]. Hop Tac carried the titling teams into the inner ring of Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, and by mid-1965 about 38,000 hectares there had been titled [X-001]; in the hamlets of the inner ring the Front's tax collectors found fewer families willing to pay without coercion, and the province advisers' reports of volunteered information rose from a trickle to a few dozen reports a month [X-001]. The provincial reform reached the ring at the same time: Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An received province chiefs from the list between May and July 1964, and about 160 police posts were settled in the inner districts by mid-1965, with a Special Branch section in each district town [X-002]. In the inner ring of Gia Dinh the Special Branch broke two of the Front's village committees and the courier line that ran from the Hoc Mon rice fields into the city's northern wards, and the hamlets around the posts were held at night through the coups of 1964–65 even when the ARVN battalions assigned to Hop Tac were pulled away (F002-1) [X-002]. That did not save the programme. By mid-1965 Hop Tac was effectively moribund [H]. Its failure foreshadowed the problem of the whole war: pacification required a stable government and a security force that would stay in the hamlets at night, and in 1964 the Republic of Vietnam had neither.

## EV000-6 · Hanoi commits the regulars; the Trail and the first air war in Laos · Sep 1964 – Apr 1965 · North Vietnam, the Laotian panhandle and the Central Highlands · HIST-ALT · 000,001
The Ninth Plenum of the Lao Dong Party Central Committee, in December 1963, had resolved to seek a decisive military victory in the South before the United States could intervene in strength [H]. Through 1964 the Politburo under Le Duan acted on it. Infiltration down the Trail, managed since 1959 by the PAVN's Group 559, shifted from regrouped southerners to northern-born conscripts, and in the autumn of 1964 the first complete PAVN regular regiments were ordered south [H]. The 95th Regiment of the 325th Division arrived in Kontum Province in December 1964; the 101st Regiment followed in February 1965 with the division commander, Colonel Nguyen Huu An; the 18th Regiment came a few months later [H22]. The division was then broken up among the B3 (Central Highlands) Front and Military Region 5 [H22]. These were the first regular North Vietnamese formations committed to the war, and by the US estimate of 1965 about 4,500 PAVN troops and 300 tons of materiel moved through Laos each month [H24]. Both figures are intelligence estimates of their time; the movement was disciplined, at night, under canopy and through a country that US ground forces could not enter.

The decision to commit the regulars had been taken before the land decree existed, and it was not caused by it. The decree gave it a second argument. Party reporting from the Delta through 1964 described the "land trick" as the most dangerous of the enemy's measures, because it threatened the movement's base in exactly the provinces where it was strongest, and Le Duan's line that victory must come before the Americans and before the political base eroded drew strength from it (F001-12) [X-001]. The schedule of the 325th Division's regiments was set by their training and by the Trail, not by the reform, and the regiments came when they would have come [X-001].

The American answer in Laos began on 14 December 1964 with Operation Barrel Roll, a small programme of armed reconnaissance by US aircraft over northern and eastern Laos [H24]. On 3 April 1965 the 2nd Air Division opened Operation Steel Tiger against the Trail in the southeastern panhandle [H24]. Both were bound by the fiction of Laotian neutrality under the Geneva Accords of 1962, by the ambassador in Vientiane's control of targets, and by the bar on ground operations across the border. The Trail at this stage was still largely foot and bicycle traffic with truck routes being built; its capacity was growing faster than anything flying over it could reduce.

## EV003-2 · The trawlers turn back; the Sihanoukville route opens · Sep 1964 – Jun 1965 · Haiphong and the Gulf of Tonkin; the II Corps coast; Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Cambodia · NEW · 003
Hanoi did not give up the sea at once. After TIDE TABLE's first half-year Group 125 paused through the summer of 1964, and in September, while the Global Hawk orbit was still held in the Gulf of Tonkin after the August crisis, it sent two trawlers south [X-003]. One landed its cargo at Vung Ro on the night of 21–22 September in a gap the Gulf watch had opened on the II Corps coast; the other was tracked off Binh Dinh after the orbit returned, and was intercepted on 9 November by the Vietnamese Navy escort *Chi Lang* and a Junk Force division off the Tam Quan coast, where her crew beached and burned her [X-003]. Sixteen of her crew were killed or drowned and four captured; the junk sailors lost two killed [X-003]. Those are counts, and reliable. In December a third trawler reversed course off Quang Ngai at dusk when a Seventh Fleet destroyer closed her, and went home [X-003].

The decision to change routes was taken in Hanoi in December 1964 and January 1965. Party records captured later in this war describe the Central Military Commission's conclusion that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night" and that the route was no longer worth the ships and the men [X-003]. The alternative was already prepared. Sihanouk, who had renounced American aid in November 1963 and was moving toward China, would break diplomatic relations with Washington on 3 May 1965 [H]. Through the winter Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators in Phnom Penh arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville, the port the French had built at Kompong Som, with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and Cambodian trucking contractors to carry the cargo inland along Route 4 to Kompong Speu and thence to the border base areas [H] [X-003]. Historically this arrangement took shape in late 1966 [H]. Here the first arms cargo for the Front came ashore at Sihanoukville under a Chinese flag in late June 1965, about eighteen months early, and was trucked to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak by night [X-003].

Two things followed from it that did not happen historically. The trawler that an Army helicopter pilot, Lieutenant James Bowers, found by chance unloading at Vung Ro Bay on 16 February 1965, with about 100 tons of arms aboard, was not there to be found [H] [X-003]; there was no trawler on the II Corps coast that month. And Market Time, which the Navy opened on 11 March 1965 historically because of Vung Ro [H], opened on the same date here because of TIDE TABLE's record, with a picture of what it was looking for and, increasingly, nothing to find [X-003]. The weapons the sea route would have landed in 1965 still reached the Front, more slowly and by a longer road, and from mid-1965 the enemy's principal line of supply to the southern half of the country ran through a neutral port that no American could stop or seize (F003-3).

## EV000-7 · The attack on Bien Hoa air base · 1 Nov 1964 · Bien Hoa air base, Bien Hoa Province · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On the night of 31 October–1 November 1964, two days before the American presidential election, a Viet Cong mortar team fired on Bien Hoa air base, about 30 km northeast of Saigon, for about thirty minutes [H2]. The B-57B Canberras moved there after the Tonkin crisis were parked wingtip to wingtip on the ramp. Five B-57Bs were destroyed and 13 damaged; with helicopters and other aircraft, as many as 30 aircraft were destroyed or damaged [H2] [H3]. Four Americans were killed and 72 people wounded [H2]. The attackers escaped.

In this war one of the rounds fell on the most expensive aircraft in the theater. The two U-2S were kept in open-fronted earth-and-sandbag revetments on the north side of the field, beside the exploitation station; the second aircraft was in maintenance that night [M003]. An 81mm round burst on the revetment wall, and fragments holed its left wing, the fuel cells inside it and the forward equipment bay [X-003]. No one in the detachment was hurt. The contractor field engineers patched the structure, and a replacement wing panel and sensor components came from the United States by C-133 [X-003]. The aircraft was out of service for seven weeks, from 1 November to 20 December, and for those seven weeks the theater had one U-2S, one sortie on most days and none when it was in maintenance [X-003]. The daily deep look over the Laotian panhandle, where the first trucks of the dry season were moving, lapsed to twice a week [X-003].

It was the first of at least 475 attacks on the primary air bases in South Vietnam during the war [H2]. Ambassador Taylor and the Joint Chiefs pressed for reprisal bombing of the North; Johnson, on the eve of the election, declined [H]. I asked for and got a covered, steel-arched shelter for each U-2S, built by the Navy's Seabees by February 1965, and a Regional Forces company for the hamlets of the Bien Hoa and Di An approaches where the mortar team had set up [X-003]. The Global Hawks at Clark were untouched, which was why they were there (EC003-4). The attack exposed what American airpower in South Vietnam would cost: every base needed a ground perimeter that the ARVN could not or would not hold, and the question of who would guard the bases was the question that would bring the first US combat troops ashore four months later.

## EV000-8 · The Brink Hotel bombing and the purge of the High National Council · 19–24 Dec 1964 · Saigon · HIST · 000
In the week before Christmas the Young Turks, with Khanh's acquiescence, dissolved the High National Council, the civilian body set up to give the government a constitutional face, and arrested several of its members [H10]. Ambassador Taylor summoned the officers and dressed them down; Khanh answered by attacking Taylor publicly, and relations between the US Mission and the junta reached their low point [H10]. The civilian prime minister, Tran Van Huong, who had held office since early November, was left without authority [H].

At 17:45 on 24 December a charge of about 90 kg of explosive in a car parked beneath the Brink Bachelor Officers' Quarters in central Saigon exploded during the evening gathering in the officers' bar [H20]. Two Americans were killed, Lieutenant Colonel James R. Hagen and a Navy Department civilian, David M. Agnew; the wounded are given variously as 38 American officers and 25 Vietnamese civilians, 58 people, or 98, and are best stated as about 60–100 [H20]. Two Viet Cong operatives, disguised as a driver and an ARVN major, placed the car and escaped [H20]. Taylor and Westmoreland urged reprisal strikes; Johnson again refused, doubting that the public would accept Viet Cong responsibility and saying that he had "never felt that this war will be won from the air" [H20]. Within six weeks he had changed his mind.

## EV000-9 · The battle of Binh Gia · 28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
Binh Gia, a Catholic village of northern refugees in Phuoc Tuy Province east of Saigon, was the site of the Viet Cong's first large set-piece battle [H1]. The attackers were the 271st and 272nd Regiments (then called the 1st and 2nd), the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, with the 80th Artillery Detachment and local battalions of the B-2 Front, an estimated 1,800 men [H1]. They took the village on 28 December and then waited for the relief. The ARVN committed the 30th, 33rd, 35th and 38th Ranger Battalions and the 4th Marine Battalion of the general reserve, with two artillery platoons and a section of M24 tanks, about 4,300 men [H1], lifted and supported by the US 118th Aviation Company and the gunships of the 68th Assault Helicopter Company [H1]. Every one of those battalions had carried the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 since the spring (EV004-1); the attackers carried Type 56 and SKS rifles, RPD guns and recoilless rifles [X-004].

The relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal, the artillery badly positioned and air support thin, as they were historically [H1]. The rifles did not change the generals' plan, and the plan was the problem. On 30 December a 68th Company gunship was shot down near Quang Giao and its four crewmen killed [H1]. On 31 December the 4th Marine Battalion, going to recover the bodies, was ambushed in the rubber by the 271st at a range of 20 to 40 metres, as it was historically, and its commander, Major Nguyen Van Nho, was killed in the first minutes [H1]. What followed was different. The battalion's squad guns, three to a company platoon, put a base of fire into the trees that the Marines' BARs could not have sustained, and the companies, instead of being cut apart in the order they had marched, closed into two perimeters within about a quarter of an hour and fought out of the rubber at dusk carrying most of their wounded (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 60–70 killed and 90 wounded, against 122 killed and 71 wounded on the historical course [X-004]. Captain Donald Cook, the Marine adviser, was wounded and captured going back for a wounded Marine, as he was historically, and died in captivity [H1]. MACV counted allied losses from 28 December to 6 January at about 140 killed, 220 wounded and 45 missing [E: the historical 201 killed, 192 wounded and 68 missing, adjusted for the 4th Marines' smaller loss and the larger number of wounded who came out alive]. MACV reported four helicopters shot down [H1]. The VC claim of 1,783 killed and captured is no more credible here than it was historically; their own dead, of whom at least 32 were confirmed historically, I put at about 120–200, because the Marines' marksmen and squad guns killed men in the rubber whom the 271st could not carry away [E: the 4th Marines' after-action count of about 90 bodies left on the ground, and the usual ratio of dead removed to dead left].

The significance was still the form. A Viet Cong formation had held ground for four days against armour, artillery and helicopters, chosen the field, ambushed the general reserve on the road it had to use, and withdrawn intact [H1]. The 271st took the honorific "Binh Gia Regiment" [H1]. US observers concluded, as they did historically, that the ARVN could not defeat main-force units of this kind without American firepower; what they could not say, as they said historically, was that a Marine battalion of the general reserve had been destroyed. The 4th Marines were back in the line in February.

## EV000-10 · The fall of Khanh · 27 Jan – 25 Feb 1965 · Saigon · HIST · 000
On 27 January 1965 Khanh and the Armed Forces Council removed Prime Minister Huong after another wave of Buddhist protest [H]. On 16 February, to stop Khanh installing a prime minister of his own choosing, the council appointed Dr Phan Huy Quat, a civilian physician and former foreign minister [H23]. On 19–20 February Colonel Pham Ngoc Thao and General Lam Van Phat attempted another coup; it failed, but in its confusion the Armed Forces Council, with American encouragement, voted Khanh out [H23] [H10]. He left the country on 25 February with the title of roving ambassador [H]. Thao, who went into hiding, was a communist agent, as later became clear [H].

Quat's civilian cabinet governed under the oversight of a military council in which Ky and Thieu were now the strongest figures [H23]. The government that would receive the American ground commitment was a caretaker administration whose authority depended on generals who had just shown that they would remove anyone, and whose own legitimacy with the population of the countryside was close to nil.

## EV000-11 · Pleiku, Qui Nhon and Flaming Dart · 7–11 Feb 1965 · Camp Holloway and Pleiku, Pleiku Province; Qui Nhon, Binh Dinh Province; southern North Vietnam · HIST · 000
At 01:50 on 7 February 1965, while the President's national security adviser McGeorge Bundy was in Saigon and Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin was in Hanoi [H], about 300 men of the 30th Company of the VC 409th Sapper Battalion, reinforced by local engineers and a local force company and issued four 81mm mortars, attacked the US Army helicopter base at Camp Holloway and the advisers' compound at Pleiku [H5]. Seven Americans were killed and 104 wounded; 10 aircraft were destroyed and 15 damaged [H5]. Bundy recommended reprisal from Saigon, and about twelve hours after the attack 49 aircraft from USS *Coral Sea* and USS *Hancock* struck the barracks at Dong Hoi just north of the 17th parallel, the first strike of Operation Flaming Dart [H5].

On 10 February the VC blew up the enlisted men's billet at Qui Nhon, killing 23 Americans [H5]. On 11 February about 160 US and Vietnamese aircraft struck the barracks at Chanh Hoa and Chap Le in the southern North [H5]. Flaming Dart was still framed as reprisal for specific attacks, but Bundy's report of 7 February argued for "sustained reprisal" against the North as the best available way to affect the war, and the administration accepted it [H]. Pleiku is the hinge of the phase: within four weeks the reprisal policy had become a continuing bombing campaign.

## EV000-12 · Rolling Thunder begins · 2 Mar 1965 · North Vietnam, south of the 20th parallel · HIST · 000
On 2 March 1965 Operation Rolling Thunder opened with a strike by 104 USAF aircraft, F-105s, F-100s and B-57s, on the ammunition storage area near Xom Bang, while 19 RVNAF A-1 Skyraiders struck the naval base at Quang Khe [H9]. It was conceived as a graduated campaign: a slowly rising pressure on the North, moving northward a step at a time, meant to signal resolve, raise the cost of supporting the insurgency and bring Hanoi to negotiate, while avoiding Chinese or Soviet intervention [H].

The constraints were part of the design. Targets were chosen in Washington, approved individually by the President and the Secretary of Defense at the weekly meetings of the "Tuesday" group, which also fixed timing, numbers of aircraft and ordnance [H9]. Strikes were barred within 30 nautical miles of Hanoi and 10 of Haiphong and in a 30-mile buffer along the Chinese frontier [H9]. The North was divided into route packages assigned to the Air Force and the Navy [H9]. North Vietnam began the campaign with about 1,500 anti-aircraft guns and a few dozen MiG-17s; the United States estimated more than 5,000 guns within a year [H9]. Through 24 December 1965 the USAF flew 25,971 sorties and dropped 32,063 tons and the Navy flew 28,168 sorties and dropped 11,144 tons, at a cost of 180 US aircraft [H9]. The campaign that began on 2 March would last, with pauses, until November 1968.

## EV000-13 · The Marines land at Da Nang · 8 Mar – 6 May 1965 · Da Nang, Quang Nam Province · HIST · 000
On 8 March 1965 Battalion Landing Team 3/9 under Lieutenant Colonel Charles E. McPartlin came ashore over Red Beach 2 northwest of Da Nang, an hour late because of surf, and the first C-130s carrying BLT 1/3 under Lieutenant Colonel Herbert J. Bain landed from Okinawa at about 13:00; the airlift halted and resumed, and 1/3 was complete by 12 March [H15]. The 9th Marine Expeditionary Brigade under Brigadier General Frederick J. Karch numbered nearly 5,000 [H15]. Its mission was the security of Da Nang air base, which Rolling Thunder now needed and the Bien Hoa attack had shown to be vulnerable [H14]. The Joint Chiefs' orders were that the Marines "will not, repeat will not, engage in day-to-day actions against the Viet Cong" [H15].

The restriction lasted three weeks. At a National Security Council meeting on 1 April the President approved the use of Marines "in active combat under conditions to be established and approved by the Secretary of Defense" and an increase of 18,000–20,000 in US support forces [H15]. In April further battalion landing teams arrived [H15]; on 6 May the brigade became the III Marine Amphibious Force [H14]. The enclave concept, US forces holding coastal bases and patrolling outward, was the first form of American ground strategy and lasted only until the summer. By the end of 1965 III MAF would number more than 38,000 [H15].

## EV000-14 · The bombing of the US embassy · 30 Mar 1965 · Saigon · HIST · 000
On 30 March 1965 a car packed with about 300 pounds of plastic explosive was parked outside the US embassy; a Vietnamese policeman who argued with the driver was shot by a second VC who drove up alongside, before the charge exploded [H12]. Twenty-two people were killed, two Americans, among them Barbara Robbins of the CIA, 19 Vietnamese and a Filipino serving in the US Navy, and 183 were wounded [H12]. Johnson rejected calls for a specific reprisal; Rolling Thunder was already running [H12]. Congress voted one million dollars for a new, fortified embassy, which opened on 29 September 1967 [H12]. The bombing confirmed that the VC could strike anywhere in Saigon and that its urban networks were intact after a decade of police effort.

## EV000-15 · The Thanh Hoa Bridge and the northern air defences · 3 Apr – 27 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong), Thanh Hoa Province, and the Hanoi region · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 3 April 1965 a 79-aircraft package led by Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner, 46 F-105s with F-100 escorts, RF-101Cs and KC-135 tankers, attacked the Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa; 16 F-105s carried AGM-12 Bullpup missiles and the rest 750 lb bombs [H16]. The bridge was hit repeatedly and stood; traffic stopped for a few hours; an F-100 and an RF-101 were lost [H16]. On 4 April 48 F-105s returned with 750 lb bombs. Eight MiG-17s of the VPAF 921st Fighter Regiment, half acting as decoys, attacked the F-105s and shot down two, killing Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson; a third F-105, Captain Carlyle Harris's, was lost and its pilot captured [H16]. The VPAF lost three MiG-17 pilots that day; how each was lost is disputed [H16]. The bridge, struck by more than 300 bombs, still stood; it would stand until laser-guided bombs dropped its span in 1972 [H16]. In this war the U-2S's radar pass over Ham Rong at dawn on 5 April told CINCPAC what the RF-101s had been lost trying to see: both spans standing, the approaches cratered, and rail traffic moving again within the day [X-003].

The missiles were seen coming. On 22 March the U-2S's radar change detection showed a new clearing southeast of Hanoi with the six-pointed pattern of revetments and access roads that every Soviet SA-2 site follows, and the ASIP signals payload heard the first emissions of the SNR-75 Fan Song fire-control radar on 18 April [X-003]. By 24 July the U-2S had mapped eleven prepared sites around Hanoi and Haiphong and judged, from emissions and vehicle movements, that four of them were occupied on any given day [X-003]. The Joint Chiefs asked to strike the sites under construction; McNamara and his deputies declined, as they had historically, for fear of killing Soviet technicians [H] [X-003]. On 24 July an SA-2 Guideline missile shot down an F-4C of the 47th Tactical Fighter Squadron near the munitions depot at Dien Bien Phu, the first US aircraft lost to a surface-to-air missile; Captain Roscoe Fobair was killed and Captain Richard Keirn captured [H9] [H]. The flight had been warned that morning that a site west of Hanoi was occupied; the missile came from a battery that had moved overnight [X-003].

The retaliation, Operation Spring High on 27 July, went differently. Historically the F-105s were sent against two sites, at least one of them empty, through guns massed around them, and lost aircraft for nothing [H]. Here the U-2S's pass of 26 July showed the eastern site with dummy launchers of bamboo and its missiles gone, and the guns doubled around it; the strike was re-targeted on the one occupied site, went in low from the west, destroyed two launchers and the Fan Song van, and lost two F-105Ds, one pilot killed and one recovered [X-003]. Within five months the North's air defence had become an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles, and the restricted zone around Hanoi had become its sanctuary; what had changed was that every part of it was on a map in Saigon before it fired.

## EV001-5 · The Vinh Long suspension · 6 Apr – 11 May 1965 · Vinh Long Province · NEW · 001
At the December 1964 harvest landlords' agents collected rent in four villages of Vinh Long's Tam Binh and Vung Liem districts under the escort of Regional Forces platoons the province chief had lent them, and tenants who refused were taken to the district headquarters [X-001]. The Commission documented 26 cases from the tenants' complaints to the land court on circuit and from the American province adviser's reports, and on 2 February 1965 sent them to the Ministry of the Interior with 30 days to act [X-001]. Nothing was done; Saigon was occupied with the fall of Khanh. On 6 April the Aid Compliance Board suspended the release of counterpart piasters to Vinh Long Province (EC001-8) [X-001]. On 28 April the Quat government replaced the province chief, and on 11 May, after the Commission certified that the escorts had ended and the collected rents had been returned, the Board restored the funds [X-001].

It was the only suspension the Board made against a province in the war's first two years, and it worked as designed: the next province chief stopped the escorts, and in the rest of the Delta the threat was enough [X-001]. It also cost what this headquarters had warned it would cost (POSTNA-6). Liberation Radio broadcast for a month that the "American masters" had dismissed a "puppet" who displeased them, and in Saigon the officers who resented the Board pointed to the same fact [X-001].

## EV000-16 · The battle of Song Be · 10–12 May 1965 · Song Be (Phuoc Long province capital) and Phuoc Binh, Phuoc Long Province · HIST · 000
The 173rd Airborne Brigade, the first US Army combat formation in the country, began arriving at Bien Hoa and Vung Tau on 5 May 1965 to guard the air bases [H]. Five days later the VC opened their summer offensive. In the early hours of 11 May B-2 Front forces, identified variously as the 763rd Regiment with the 840th Battalion or as elements of the 271st–273rd Regiments, with two sapper platoons and artillery, attacked Song Be, capital of Phuoc Long Province, 119 km northeast of Saigon, and the district town of Phuoc Binh [H13]. The defence rested on the 36th Ranger Battalion, about 250 men, about 150 territorials, and about three dozen American advisers, mostly of Special Forces Detachment B-34 under Lieutenant Colonel Alton E. Park, who was badly wounded; Major Mitchell A. Sakely took over [H13]. Gunships of the US 197th Aviation Company supported them, and a relieving Ranger battalion reached the town by midday on 11 May [H13].

The town was held. The ARVN lost 58 killed, 96 wounded and 83 missing; five Americans were killed and 13 wounded; a B-57 was shot down; civilian dead are given as 12–80 [H13]. The advisers counted 297 VC dead, and MACV estimated up to 1,000 VC killed and wounded, which is an estimate of the kind A13 warns against [H13]. The communist history claims 1,389 allied killed, including 30 Americans, which is fiction [H13]. Song Be was a battle the government won, narrowly and with American firepower; the next two were not.

## EV000-17 · The battle of Ba Gia · 28–31 May 1965 · Ba Gia, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 29 May 1965 the VC 1st Regiment of the forming 2nd Division (40th, 60th and 90th Battalions, with the 45th Independent Battalion and local force battalions), directed by PAVN Major General Chu Huy Man, overran the outpost at Loc Tho near Ba Gia, about 10 km from Quang Ngai, and ambushed the ARVN 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry Regiment as it came to help [H11]. On the historical course the battalion was destroyed. On this one it was cut in two on the road from Ba Gia and lost its headquarters company and most of one rifle company in the first half hour; the other three companies, each with a squad gun in every squad and a marksman in most, formed a perimeter on the low ridge north of the road and held it until dark, and about 280 of the battalion's men came back to Quang Ngai over the next two days [X-004]. Major General Nguyen Chanh Thi, commanding I Corps, sent a task force under the 51st Regiment: its 2nd Battalion, the 3rd Marine Battalion, the 37th and 39th Ranger Battalions, two 105mm artillery battalions and an M113 squadron [H11]. On 30 May the 39th Rangers fought the VC 45th Battalion on Mount Chop Non, and the 2nd Battalion, 51st and the Marines were hit near Phuoc Loc; US fighter-bombers and UH-1 gunships, delayed because forward air controllers could not find the fight, prevented a rout [H11]. On the night of 30–31 May the 3rd Marines held a moat line through repeated assaults, with nine M249s across the line firing under illumination from the 105s, and their marksmen picking off the 40th Battalion's machine-gun teams setting up in the paddy dikes beyond the moat at 300–400 metres (EC004-6) [X-004]. The 39th Rangers were reduced to about two companies, against one historically [X-004]. The VC withdrew at dawn on 31 May.

I put ARVN losses at about 75 killed, 110 wounded and 190 captured or missing, against the US Army's historical figures of 107 killed, 123 wounded and 367 captured or missing; the individual weapons lost, 384 historically, I put at about 230, most of them M4A1 carbines with their sights [E: the historical return less the 1st Battalion's survivors and the smaller Ranger loss]. About 150 VC bodies were found, against 84 historically, and MACV's estimate of up to 826 VC casualties is no more reliable than it was [X-004]. Ba Gia was still a defeat. The VC had overrun the outpost, broken a battalion and held the field, and by 3 June they controlled much of northern Quang Ngai, because the ARVN pulled its battered task force back to the province capital [H11]. On 5 July they overran Ba Gia again, and the ARVN abandoned the post [H11]. In Washington it was read as evidence that the ARVN was being worn down faster than it could be rebuilt, not, as historically, as proof that a regimental task force could be wiped out; the distinction mattered to the 51st Regiment and not to the July decision. The 230 carbines went to the 2nd Division's local battalions, who were firing 5.56mm taken from the dead within the month (F004-7) [X-004].

## EV000-18 · The battle of Dong Xoai · 9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation [H6]. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers [H6]. The CIDG companies and the militia carried the carbines and Garands they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach them; the 111th RF Company and the Americans carried M4A1s (F004-1) [X-004]. The camp was largely overrun during the night, as it was historically, from the CIDG sectors inward, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters, where the 111th Company had fallen back with its carbines, until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out [H6] [X-004]. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overrun, losing about half its men against nearly all of them historically [X-004]; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it in the rubber [H6].

The Airborne fight on 12 June is the clearest measure in this war of what the squad weapons could and could not do. Historically about 150 of the 470 came back. On this course the battalion's lead company was lost in the opening fire as it was historically, but the three companies behind it each turned their squad guns into the trees and held, and about 270 men fought their way to the landing zone at Thuan Loi over the afternoon, bringing about 60 wounded [X-004]. The 271st had chosen the place, the hour and the range, and it won; the Airborne battalion was broken, though not wiped out [X-004]. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had [H6].

I put the allied losses at about 300 South Vietnamese killed, 210 wounded and 150 missing, and 18 Americans killed as historically, against 416 killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing on the historical course; about 300 civilians were killed and 2,500 made refugees, as historically [X-004] [H6]. More than 300 VC bodies were found historically; I put the figure here at about 400, with the same scepticism about the allied estimate of 625 more [X-004]. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured [H6]. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact [H6]. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had about two reserve battalions left, against one historically [X-004]. That was not enough to change Westmoreland's request of 7 June, which rested on the PAVN regiments entering the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and it did not change the answer.

## EV000-19 · Quat resigns; the Thieu–Ky government · 12–19 Jun 1965 · Saigon · HIST · 000
On 12 June 1965 Prime Minister Quat, deadlocked with the chief of state Phan Khac Suu over a cabinet reshuffle, handed power back to the military [H23]. On 14 June the generals formed a National Leadership Committee with Major General Nguyen Van Thieu as its chairman and chief of state, and on 19 June Air Vice Marshal Nguyen Cao Ky became prime minister as chairman of the Central Executive Committee [H] [H23]. American officials expected it to last no longer than its predecessors. It lasted the war, in the sense that Thieu would govern until April 1975; but it began as a junta of officers in their thirties and forties with no political base outside the armed forces [H].

## EV000-20 · The first Arc Light strike · 18 Jun 1965 · Near Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · HIST · 000
On 18 June 1965 Strategic Air Command flew its first B-52 mission of the war. Thirty B-52s of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings were launched from Andersen Air Force Base, Guam [H18] [H19]. Over the sea, while circling to meet their KC-135 tankers, two B-52Fs collided and fell into the sea, killing eight crewmen [H18]. Twenty-seven aircraft struck a suspected Viet Cong base area near Ben Cat, about 40 miles north of Saigon, with 750 lb and 1,000 lb bombs [H18] [H19]. Ground follow-up found little: the enemy had left, or had not been there in strength [H]. The mission was widely ridiculed as a sledgehammer swung at flies [H]. Within five months B-52s would be striking within a few kilometres of US troops in the Ia Drang; Arc Light would continue until August 1973 and fly about 126,615 sorties [H17]. What its first strike showed is the find-and-fix problem in its purest form: unlimited striking power, aimed at a target located by intelligence that was hours or days old.

## EV000-21 · The July decision · 28 Jul 1965 · Washington · HIST · 000
On 7 June 1965 Westmoreland asked for a major reinforcement to stop what he judged a coming collapse; by 22 July the President had authorized US combat strength of 44 battalions [H11]. On 28 July, at a midday press conference rather than in an address to Congress, Johnson announced that US forces in Vietnam would rise immediately from 75,000 to 125,000, with more to follow as requested, that monthly draft calls would rise over time from 17,000 to 35,000, and that the Airmobile Division would deploy [H21]. The reserves were not called up and no new taxes were sought [H]. The decision was presented as a continuation of policy and made, in effect, without a public debate. Its strategy, Westmoreland's three-phase concept of halting the losing trend in 1965, taking the offensive in high-priority areas in 1966 and destroying the enemy's main forces and base areas thereafter, committed the United States to a war of attrition against an enemy who controlled his own losses by deciding when to fight.

## How the phase ends
By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has destroyed ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security forces it needs. One thing is different from the historical summer of 1965: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's [X-001]. Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

The police and the chiefs have begun to change the same districts. About 1,100 village posts are settled, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure in the villages around the posts has begun to leave them (EV002-2) [X-002]. The posts that fell were the posts settled ahead of security, and the Front has learned to kill constables before it has learned that the new chiefs are staying.

This command enters Phase II able to see the enemy's rear and not his regiments. The sea route to the coast is closed and the Cambodian route open; the Trail's first truck roads are on the U-2S's maps; the North's air defences are on its electronic order of battle; and the Southeast Asia Resolution rests on an attack that happened. The war in the hamlets and the main-force war under the canopy are exactly as hidden as before.

The rifles are in the hands of the RVNAF from the first quarter of 1964 and in all of it by the end of 1964 (EV004-1). Posts with their Popular Forces platoon now hold about nine times in ten, and the Front has turned to the posts without them, the constables on the roads, the CIDG camps and the militia, and to the mortar and the mine (EV002-2) [X-004]. The general reserve enters Phase II weakened but whole, and the Front enters it with between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines [X-004].
