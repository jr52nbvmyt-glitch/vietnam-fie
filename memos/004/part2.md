## §2 Part A — Integration

These three weapons change who wins the fight once it starts, and nothing else directly. That sentence governs everything below. The M4A1 with its red dot, the M249 at the centre of the squad and the M110A2 in the marksman's hands act only inside a contact, and in this country the enemy still decides most contacts. Where the war is a fight at the wall of an outpost at two in the morning, the provision is a large improvement. Where the war is the search for a regiment under the canopy, or the identification of the tax collector in the market, it is no improvement at all.

### Functional systems

**Hamlet security** is the system the provision joins most heavily and strengthens most. The Regional and Popular Forces are the men who hold the hamlets after dark, and in the historical war they held them with M1 and M2 carbines, M1 Garands and the odd M1918A2 automatic rifle against a local force that was re-equipping with the AK-47, the SKS and the RPD from 1964 [M004·S7]. The territorials received the M16 only at the end of 1969 [M004·S7]. Under this provision a Popular Forces platoon in a Dinh Tuong hamlet in March 1964 carries thirty-round magazines and a sight that puts the first round where the man is looking, and it is not outgunned by the company that comes for it. That is the provision's centre of gravity.

**Fixing an enemy who chooses whether to fight** is strengthened at the small-unit level and only there. A squad with a belt-fed M249 and nine carbines can establish fire superiority in the first minute of a meeting engagement, hold the enemy in contact long enough for artillery or an air strike to arrive, and survive the ambush that historically destroyed ARVN columns in their first ten minutes. It does not make the enemy accept the fight in the first place.

**The advisory effort** is joined in an unexpected way. The rearmament gives every adviser a practical reason to visit every outpost: to check zero, batteries, cleaning and ammunition. A weapon that must be zeroed and accounted for is a door into units that advisers historically never saw.

**Sustainment** is burdened at the margin and relieved at the margin. One calibre, 5.56mm, replaces the .30-06, the .30 carbine and the 7.62mm NATO in the individual and squad weapons, and a man carries about twice as many rounds for the same weight [M004]. Against that, linked 5.56mm and match 7.62mm are new lines at squad level, and the whole flow must reach about 5,000 Popular Forces platoons and 800 Regional Forces companies over roads the enemy cuts [D].

**Strike** is touched only by the marksman's rifle, which puts precise fire on the enemy's machine gunners and leaders at 300–800 metres in open country [M004]. It is a squad weapon, not a fire-support system, and I record the contact for completeness.

**Find, interdict and subvert** are not joined. The provision finds no one, interdicts nothing, and identifies no cadre. Newly viable, in a limited sense, is one thing: a hamlet post that can survive the first two hours of a night attack on its own fire, which makes the settled-post rule of the police programme work more often.

### Compound pairings

The pairings that produce compound effects are with the district government built in memoranda 001 and 002, not with the reconnaissance aircraft of memorandum 003.

**The settled post and the rearmed platoon.** The police programme's rule is that no village post is settled until its Popular Forces platoon sleeps in the same hamlet and a reaction force can reach it within the hour (EC002-1) [M002]. The chronicle records that four in five posts attacked under that rule held, and that the posts lost were almost all settled without the platoon [X-002]. This provision does not touch the constables, who keep their carbines, but it rearms the platoon that stands beside them, and the platoon is what holds the post. The rule and the rifle are each worth less alone.

**The muster and the serial number.** The pay and muster verification service counts every soldier physically about twice a year [M001]. From 1964 it can count his carbine too: serial number to soldier, sight to carbine, magazines and rounds against issue. A carbine on the muster roll is much harder to sell than a carbine on a depot ledger, and the Commission Against Corruption, which already has the power to search and seize, can follow the missing ones.

**The merit-selected chief and the ammunition.** The district chief commands the territorials and controls the district's ammunition [M002]. A chief chosen from the list, who stays two or three years, is the man who decides whether the outposts get their resupply on time or whether it is sold in the district town. Rifles distributed through a venal chief are an arms market; rifles distributed through a competent one are a defence.

**The titled family and the armed son.** Where the land programme has delivered titles, the Popular Forces platoon is increasingly recruited from families who own the fields it guards [M001]. This provision gives those men a rifle that is not outgunned by the guerrillas who would take the land back. The land programme gives the rifle a reason; the rifle gives the title a defence.

**The reconnaissance aircraft.** I looked for a compound pairing with the U-2S and the Global Hawk and found none of weight. The aircraft find movement in the open; the rifles act inside a contact at 10–30 metres. The marksman's rifle and the radar both work best in open paddy and on the coastal plain, but they are not coupled: the radar's tracks go to police checkpoints and strike aircraft, not to squads.

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

The provision sits at the very end of the chain and does nothing for its beginning. A PAVN regiment under the canopy in the Do Xa or War Zone D is exactly as hard to find in 1964 as it was without the provision, and the record's position that finding is never claimed as fixing holds here with more force (POS003-1). A rifle cannot be pointed at a regiment no one has located.

What it changes is the moment after contact. In the historical war the main force's characteristic victory over the ARVN was the ambush of a column or a relieving force: the relief road at Binh Gia at the turn of 1964–65, the 1st Battalion of the ARVN 51st Regiment destroyed coming to the help of the post near Ba Gia in May 1965 [H]. In each the enemy won the first ten minutes with automatic fire from cover against men carrying Garands and carbines, and the survivors never regained the initiative. An ARVN squad with an M249 and nine M4A1s returns fire at three to four times the volume of a Garand squad [M004], and a column that survives its first ten minutes holds the ground long enough for the artillery, the A-1 Skyraiders and the helicopters to arrive. The enemy who sprang the ambush then has a choice he did not have before: break contact early, with less to show for it, or stay and be hit from the air.

That is a real improvement in fixing, and it is narrow. It applies only where the enemy has already chosen to engage. It makes an ARVN unit a better anvil; it does not make the hammer arrive faster, and it does not stop the enemy from breaking contact by night and going back across the border. The main-force chain moves a little at its last link and not at all at its first.

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

The provision does not touch the identification of the infrastructure at all. The tax collector, the security cadre and the district committee are not hiding behind a treeline at 400 metres; they are in the hamlet, and no rifle, sight or machine gun tells a constable who they are.

Its only contribution is indirect and real. The police and the Special Branch can work only in villages held at night (POS002-1), and the night is held by the Popular Forces platoon. A platoon that holds longer keeps the constable alive to complete his census and his night check, and keeps the Special Branch's informants from being killed when the post falls. The rifle protects the people who identify the infrastructure; it identifies no one.

### Dependence on SECURE

Almost everything this provision does in the countryside depends on hamlet security, and in one sense it is a hamlet security provision. Its value in a hamlet is exactly the value of the platoon that carries it, at night, in the hamlet: a carbine in the hands of a Popular Forces platoon that sleeps in the district town protects nothing (POSTNA-2). The rifle does not make the platoon present, does not make it awake, does not make it fight, and does not bring the reaction force that arrives in the dark. What it does is make a present, awake platoon that chooses to fight much harder to overrun. I want that dependence stated plainly, because the temptation in this headquarters will be to count rifles issued as hamlets secured.

### The ADVISE cluster

This provision feeds the advisory effort more than it starves it. It removes a grievance the advisers historically spent years trying to answer: the ARVN and the territorials knew they were outgunned by the AK-47, said so, and watched American units and then the Korean divisions receive the M16 first [M004·S7]. Their equipment was a daily statement of where they stood in American priorities. Here they receive the modern rifle on the same day as the Americans, and the statement reverses.

It also gives the advisers a practical way in. The zeroing visit (EC004-2) puts an American with a Vietnamese instructor at every outpost every five to six weeks, checking each carbine's zero, battery and cleanliness and counting magazines and rounds against issue. That visit is training, inspection and accounting in one, and it shows the adviser things about a platoon that no district report would: whether it is at strength, whether its rounds are where they should be, whether its men sleep in the hamlet.

What it costs the advisory effort is attention in 1964, when the junta is changing every few months and the advisers have much else to do, and about 250 American spaces for training, repair and the territorial training centres [M004]. It does nothing for the officer corps, which remains the deepest problem the advisers face (G-4). A well-armed battalion led by a commander who will not leave the road is a well-armed battalion on the road.

### Employment concepts

The concepts that settle are small-unit drills, not operations. Four live concepts belong to the carbine and the squad gun together, two to the marksman's rifle; four were tried and dropped.

#### EC004-1 · The outpost wall at night

The Popular Forces platoon of about 30–35 men holds a hamlet post with a mud-and-log wall, a bunker at each corner and an AN/PRC-10 to the district [E: PF platoon strength and post design of the period]. At dusk the platoon leader checks each man's sight is on and his magazines are full; the CompM5 is left on permanently, so the battery is not a nightly task [M004·S3]. The men carry a basic load of about 210 rounds each, about 7,000 for the platoon, and the post holds a reserve of 2,000–5,000 more in a bunker [E: basic load per M004, post reserve a command decision]. Two men are awake at each corner in rotation; the rest sleep in the post.

The engagement sequence is the one the Front uses against outposts: sappers cut the wire between one and three in the morning, a 60mm mortar or B40 rockets open on the bunkers, and an assault company comes out of the paddy at 20–50 metres [E: the pattern of VC outpost attacks]. The platoon fires aimed semi-automatic into the assault under hand flares, and in short bursts at the breach. At a disciplined rate the platoon's ammunition lasts about two to three hours of intermittent fighting; on automatic it lasts about twenty minutes [E: about 12 aimed rounds a minute per engaged man against sustained automatic fire]. The radio call goes to the district within the first minute, and the post's survival depends on a reaction force by road, canal or helicopter, or an AC-47, arriving inside that window. The cycle is nightly, seven nights a week, for the life of the war.

The minimum viable package is the platoon, the radio, the reserve ammunition and a reaction force within the hour, which is the settled-post rule of the police programme extended to every post (EC002-1). Without the reaction force the platoon holds two or three hours instead of one and then runs out, which is a longer death unless the enemy decides the price is too high, which on the record he often does.

#### EC004-2 · The zeroing round

A team of an American small-arms adviser, a Vietnamese instructor and an armourer visits each Popular Forces platoon and Regional Forces company in its own hamlet or camp, never pulling the unit back to a training centre. The team confirms each man's zero on a 25-metre range scraped out behind the post, replaces dead batteries and broken sights, cleans and gauges the bolts, and counts carbines, sights, magazines and rounds against the issue record and the muster roll. A team does two posts a day by jeep, sampan or helicopter, about 40 a month [E: travel and range time]. With about 120 teams working against about 4,800 platoons and 800 companies, each unit is seen about every five to six weeks [D] (from 168,000 PF and 96,000 RF in 1964 [M004·S9] at about 35 and 120 men a unit [E: platoon and company strengths of the period]).

It depends on the muster service (P001b) for the roll it checks against, and on the district chief (P002b) for its escort and for acting on what it finds. Without the muster service it degrades to a maintenance visit that cannot prove where a missing carbine went. Without escort it reaches only the posts on main roads, which are the posts that least need it.

#### EC004-3 · The squad base of fire

The ARVN rifle squad of about ten men moves with its M249 gunner and assistant at the centre of the file, carrying 600–800 linked rounds between them [E: gunner's 200-round pouch and two or three in the assistant's load]. At contact the gun is in action within seconds, at the rapid rate of 100 rounds a minute for the first minute and the sustained rate of 50 thereafter [M004·S6], while the riflemen either assault or break contact under its cover. The gun's 600–800 rounds last about 12–16 minutes at the sustained rate [D], long enough to hold contact until the platoon's M60 and the company's 60mm mortars come in and, in the historical tempo, until the first artillery or air arrives [E: response times of the period].

ARVN battalion operations run three to five days out and two to three days in refit, so a squad fights this sequence perhaps two to four times a month in an active province [E: operational tempo of ARVN divisions of the period]. The package is the gun, its two-man team and the linked ammunition; resupply by helicopter or by the company's porters is what lets the sequence repeat. Without resupply the gun becomes a heavy rifle firing from magazines after a quarter of an hour.

#### EC004-4 · The column's first minute

The counter-ambush drill on roads. Every truck and M113 in a column carries squads with their carbines and squad guns, and the drill is that on the first shot every squad dismounts on the side away from the fire, the gun goes into action within thirty seconds, and the riflemen fire aimed rounds into the treeline while the column commander calls for artillery and air [E: modern counter-ambush drill]. The point is to win the first two or three minutes, which is where the historical ambushes of 1964–65 were decided.

It depends on training and on the column having artillery within range and air on call; without them, the drill wins the first minutes and then holds a perimeter around burning trucks, which is better than dying in the trucks and still a defeat. It applies to the relief column, the convoy, the road-clearing patrol and the land programme's escort companies on their way out to a village.

#### EC004-5 · The marksman's overwatch

In paddy, on the coastal plain and in the open delta country, the squad's designated marksman takes up a position on a dike, a tomb mound or a hamlet edge with the squad leader as spotter while the squad crosses open ground. His targets are the enemy's machine gunners, RPG gunners, snipers and the man giving orders in the treeline 300–800 metres away [M004], and the suppressor makes him hard to locate. A marksman fires perhaps a few dozen rounds a day [E: SDM employment] and does this whenever his squad moves across open ground, which in the Delta is every day of an operation.

The minimum package is the marksman, a spotter and a squad that understands what he is for. Without a trained marksman (first trained men about three months after issue [M004]) the rifle is a heavy semi-automatic in an untrained man's hands and the overwatch does not exist. Under canopy the concept does not apply at all.

#### EC004-6 · The perimeter under the flares

At firebases, district towns and battalion night positions the marksmen of each company are placed on the perimeter bunkers with the illuminated reticle of the TANGO6T, firing under flares and artillery illumination at sappers forming in the wire and at the enemy's own snipers on the surrounding hills; the M249s are sited for grazing fire along the wire [M004·S5]. The marksman works the nights of every attack and by day watches the ground the sappers will cross. It depends on illumination: without flares, moonlight or the burning of something, the marksman sees no further than anyone else, because the force has no night-vision devices [M004].

#### EC004-7 · Marksmen pooled as battalion sniper teams (abandoned)

Several ARVN and American battalion commanders collected their squads' M110A2s into a battalion sniper section of eight to twelve two-man teams, to hunt the enemy on the trails around their areas. It failed for three reasons. Under the canopy there were no targets beyond 30 metres; the squads lost their overwatch in exactly the open country where they needed it; and two-man teams sent out alone in contested country were killed or captured with their rifles. By the end of the first year the rifles were back in the squads. The constraint it surfaced is that this provision is a squad weapon in a war whose long shots occur in the squad's own fights.

#### EC004-8 · Issue through the district depot (abandoned)

The first territorial issue in early 1964 went in bulk to district headquarters for the districts to hand out, as the historical carbines had been. Within months advisers found sights for sale in the markets of Cholon and My Tho, carbines unzeroed in the posts, and districts that had issued half their allocation and stored the rest against need or sale [E: the pattern of territorial supply in the period]. The issue was changed to the zeroing round: the carbine handed to the man, zeroed in front of him and entered against his name on the muster roll. The constraint it surfaced is that the supply chain to the territorials runs through the honesty of the district.

#### EC004-9 · Volume of fire as doctrine (abandoned)

The first ARVN instruction taught squads to answer contact on automatic, to win fire superiority by volume. Men who had carried the eight-round Garand emptied a thirty-round magazine in three seconds, and a platoon was out of ammunition before the enemy had finished deploying. Automatic fire was taken out of the rifleman's drill except at the wall of a post in the last seconds of an assault; volume became the squad gun's job and aimed fire the carbine's. The constraint it surfaced is ammunition at the end of a cut road.

#### EC004-10 · The squad gun to the outposts (abandoned)

Some province chiefs diverted M249s from the ARVN to their Regional Forces posts, on the reasonable argument that the post needed a belt-fed gun more than the squad did. Linked ammunition and spare barrels did not reach the posts, the guns burned out or ran dry within months, and at least some were lost with the posts. The guns were recalled to the squads. The constraint it surfaced is that the provision gives the territorials the carbine and not the squad gun, and that a heavy weapon at an isolated post is only as good as the road to it.

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

The enemy's firepower advantage at close range does not vanish; it moves to the targets the provision did not rearm and to the weapons it does not touch.

**F004-1** (SECURE) The attacks move to the men who still carry carbines. The provision covers US forces, the RVNAF and the Regional and Popular Forces; it does not cover the National Police, the Police Field Force, the Civilian Irregular Defense Group or the hamlet self-defence militia [M004]. A local-force company that has learned that a rearmed Popular Forces post is expensive goes looking for the post that is not: the police post settled without its platoon, the CIDG camp with its strikers' carbines and M1918A2s, the census team's escort of constables, the hamlet whose only defence is a dozen self-defence men with shotguns. The Front's own directive of March 1964 already put the constables above the Popular Forces as a target [X-002], and this provision widens that preference into a doctrine. In the record's terms the cost is moderate: the police programme loses more constables in its unsupported posts, and the CIDG camps in the Highlands become the softest standing targets in the country. The remedy is the settled-post rule enforced without exception and the CIDG rearmed by a later decision; until then, the displacement is real.

**F004-2** (SECURE, FIX) The enemy moves from the rifle fight to the stand-off weapon, the satchel and the mine. A rifle assault on a rearmed post costs him more men than it did, so he substitutes 60mm and 82mm mortars fired from 1,500 metres, B40 rockets at the bunker slits, sappers with satchel charges in place of the infantry wave, and mines and booby traps on the paths the platoon patrols by day [E: the enemy's historical repertoire]. None of these is answered by a better rifle. What the platoon's intelligence loses is the warning: an assault company forming in a treeline is often seen or heard, a mortar team firing four rounds from a rice dike and leaving is not, and a mine is invisible until it kills. In the main-force war the same substitution appears as the enemy fighting from bunkers, which a rifle does not break, and initiating contact only at ranges and in places where his RPD and RPG fire can win the first minute before our squad gun answers. The displacement is moderate: it raises the enemy's ammunition requirement and lowers the casualties he takes, and it hands our men a war of wounds from fragments in place of a war of rifle fire.

### Bottleneck migration

The provision does exactly what bottleneck analysis predicts: it removes firepower as the binding constraint on the outpost and the squad, and exposes the constraints that firepower had been masking.

**F004-3** (SECURE) At the outpost, the binding constraint moves from firepower at the wall to three things firepower never addressed: whether the platoon is present after dark, whether the ammunition is there, and whether a reaction force arrives within the hour. Before the provision, an outpost attacked by a local-force company with automatic weapons was often lost in the first hour because its Garands and carbines could not stop the assault at the wall. After the provision, the post that is attacked holds the first hour and runs short in the second or third, and whether it survives the third hour is decided by the district's reaction and the ammunition in the bunker. Take the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, overrun on 3 October 1964 three weeks after it was settled without its platoon [X-002]. With the provision, the post that fell there still falls, because the platoon was not there to carry the rifles. The binding constraint in that hamlet was always presence, and the provision exposes it rather than relieving it. This is the most useful sentence in this memorandum for the provinces: the bottleneck is now who sleeps in the post and who comes when it calls, and the rifles have made that unmistakable (POSTNA-2). Major.

**F004-4** (FIX) In the main-force war the binding constraint moves from fire superiority in the first minutes of a contact back to where it has always really been: finding the enemy and the enemy's control of whether battle is joined (POSTNA-3). The historical ARVN battalion often lost the first minutes of an ambush and with them the fight. The rearmed battalion holds the first minutes and then faces the enemy's real advantage, which is that he chose the place and will leave when he likes. The constraint after the provision is the hammer: helicopters, artillery within range, air on call, and a reserve that can reach the enemy before he breaks contact at dusk. In the Delta that constraint is the helicopter hours; in the Highlands it is the canopy. Moderate.

### Own-force opportunity cost

The provision is cheap in American spaces and expensive in Vietnamese training time in the worst year to spend it.

**F004-5** (ADVISE, SUSTAIN) The American cost is about 470 spaces for training, repair and the territorial training centres [M004], which against a theater with no ceiling is negligible. The port and road cost is modest: about 5,000 tonnes of packed 5.56mm in the first year [M004], a fraction of a single month's general cargo through Saigon by 1966 [E: the port's throughput of the period]. The real cost is Vietnamese: about 600,000 men to be trained on a new rifle for three to five days in 1964 [M004], about two to three million man-days, or about 1% of a year's RVNAF man-days [D]. It falls in the year of Khanh's coup, the Vung Tau Charter crisis and the September coup attempt, when units were being moved for political reasons and the training centres were also absorbing the Police Field Force [M002]. Every Regional Forces company pulled into a training centre for a week is a district without its reaction force for that week. That is why the zeroing round (EC004-2) brings the training to the post, and it is why the territorial rearmament takes until well into 1965 to reach the posts that most need it. What is not done in 1964 is a few weeks of other training in every ARVN battalion. Moderate, and front-loaded: by 1966 the cost is replacements and repair, not a rearmament.

### Counterfactual erasure

Four things that happened in the historical war do not happen in this one, and the attention they consumed is freed.

**F004-6** (ADVISE) The ARVN's four years of being outgunned, the M16 crisis of 1967 and the territorial rearmament of 1968–69 are erased. From 1964 to mid-1968 the historical ARVN infantry fought AK-47s with Garands and carbines, and knew it [M004·S7]; that gap is gone. The M16 reliability crisis that killed Marines beside their jammed rifles on Hills 861 and 881 in the spring of 1967 [H], and the congressional inquiry it produced, does not occur: the Marines carry a mature design with a chrome-lined barrel and the right powder from the first day. The rifle line of the Improvement and Modernization programme of 1969, in which the territorial forces were finally rearmed with the M16 [H], is erased, and the attention of the programme's designers moves to its other lines: artillery, helicopters, the training base. What the territorials' commanders did with the five years they spent asking for a modern rifle, they now spend on something else, and so does the enemy's propaganda, which loses the outgunned government soldier as a theme. Moderate: real and visible, but it erases a deficit that was one of several and not the war's deepest.

### Perverse effect

Three perverse effects, of which the first is the largest and the last the smallest.

**F004-7** (SECURE) Every outpost lost, every column destroyed and every deserter who takes his carbine with him arms the Front with an M4A1, a red-dot sight that runs five years on its battery and the 5.56mm it fires [M004·S3]. The local force and the guerrillas historically armed themselves largely from captured government weapons [M004]. In the historical battle at Ba Gia in May 1965 the ARVN lost 384 individual and 10 crew-served weapons in four days [H]; in this war those 384 are carbines with sights. If the RVNAF and territorials lose weapons at a few percent of their holdings a year in the bad years of 1964–65, that is on the order of 10,000–30,000 carbines in enemy hands by the end of 1965 [E: a loss rate of 1–3% of about 600,000 carbines a year over two years]. There is a twist. A captured M4A1 fires only 5.56mm, which only we supply, so the local force that rearms from our posts must keep feeding its new carbines from our ammunition: captured, bought from a corrupt depot clerk or carried away from a fallen post. The Front's local force becomes a customer of our supply line, which gives the muster service and the Commission a trail to follow (F004-14) and gives the enemy a reason to keep the old Chinese and Soviet calibres for its main force. Moderate, because the numbers are a small fraction of what the force holds, and because the same losses historically armed the enemy with carbines and Garands that were themselves good enough for a guerrilla.

**F004-8** (SECURE) A Regional Forces company made effective is pulled out of its district. The rearmed Regional Forces company, with modern carbines and a better fire discipline, looks to a corps commander short of infantry like a light infantry company, and the corps commanders historically used Regional Forces companies in their operations and on their convoy escorts whenever they could. The province chief who had one good reaction company for his district loses it to a two-week sweep, and the outposts that depended on it are alone on the nights it is away. The record already notes the same perverse effect for a successful district chief promoted away from his district (F002-10); this is the military version, and it is moderate, because the merit-selected chiefs have the standing to resist it and the corps commanders the power to override them.

**F004-9** (SECURE) The M855A1 round goes through walls. Built to penetrate 3/8-inch steel at about 400 metres [M004·S10], it passes through thatch, bamboo and the mud-and-wattle walls of a Delta house as if they were not there. A night fight in a hamlet, a Popular Forces platoon firing at muzzle flashes among the houses, puts rounds through the walls of the houses behind the target, and the families inside die. The .30 carbine's round, for all its faults, often stopped in the first wall. The effect is small in numbers and large in each hamlet where it happens, and it falls on exactly the population this command most needs. Minor, and to be answered by fire-control training in the zeroing round and by the rule that the platoon fights from the wall, not among the houses.

### Anticipation

**F004-10** (FIX, SECURE) The enemy declines the fights the rifles would win, and the improvement becomes invisible. This is the most important second-order effect of the provision and it runs exactly as it ran for the police in memorandum 002. A Viet Cong district committee that sees its local-force company lose half a platoon at the wall of a rearmed post does not attack that post again; it attacks the police post down the canal (F004-1), or the post whose platoon sleeps in the district town, or it waits for the monsoon. The rearmed post is not attacked, and an outpost not attacked generates no report. The province's statistics show fewer outposts overrun, which is real, and fewer contacts, which looks like inactivity. In the main-force war the same anticipation shows as fewer ambushes of rearmed ARVN battalions in the places where they move, and more ambushes of the convoys and posts that are softer. The provision's best results are therefore the ones this headquarters cannot count. Worse, the metrics it can count will mislead in both directions: a rearmed battalion that fights its few contacts better will report more enemy dead, and the temptation to treat that body count as evidence of progress must be refused at its source (A13). An ARVN battalion that kills more of the enemy in the fights the enemy chose has improved its fighting; it has not changed who chooses. Major, because it is the mechanism by which the provision's real effect is hidden and its false effect counted.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F004-11** (SECURE, SUBVERT) The enemy detects the provision at once, reads its military meaning correctly within a season, and reads its political meaning wrongly for a year. Detection is the first contact and the first captured carbine; the rifles are in thousands of hands by March 1964, and a captured M4A1 with its sight reaches a COSVN military staff within weeks. The military reading is right and fast: COSVN's conclusion is that the government's soldiers are no longer outgunned at close range, that the rifle assault on a post now costs more, and that the answer is to attack the posts that are not rearmed, to bring the mortar and the B40 forward, to put sappers in place of assault waves and to fight from bunkers. That doctrine propagates from COSVN to province committees in a few months and to a district cell by the end of the season, and it applies everywhere the government has a post.

The political reading is wrong at first. Hanoi reads a rearmament of the RVNAF on this scale as the precursor of a larger American commitment, which it is not by itself, and as evidence that the Americans intend the ARVN to fight a long war, which is closer. For some months in 1964 the Front overrates the rearmed ARVN and is more cautious against its battalions than their leadership deserves. The error corrects itself as soon as the district committees see that a better rifle has not made a platoon go out at night or a battalion leave the road. What the enemy gets right that hurts us is the dependence: every round the rearmed force fires comes from the United States, and every post is only as good as its last resupply. Cut the road, besiege the post, and wait. Moderate.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F004-12** (SECURE, INTERDICT) The adaptations are cheap for the Front and moderately expensive for Hanoi, and on balance the exchange slightly favours us. Choosing softer targets costs the Front nothing. Mines and booby traps are made locally from duds and scrap at almost no cost. Sapper training costs time and the best young men, perhaps six months for a sapper and a year for a sapper leader [E: the enemy's sapper training of the period], and those men are drawn from the units that would otherwise have assaulted the posts. The expensive part is the stand-off weapon: mortar rounds and rockets are heavy, come down the Trail or through Sihanoukville, and raise the tonnage the external supply must carry. A local force that fights posts with 60mm and 82mm mortars and B40s instead of rifles needs perhaps two to four times as many tonnes of ammunition per attack [E: the weights of mortar and rocket rounds against rifle ammunition]. Against the historical requirement of about 20 tons a day for the whole South (G-9) the increment is small, but it is not nothing, and it lands on the most constrained part of the enemy's system. The rearmament cost us about 600,000 carbines and five thousand tonnes of ammunition in the first year; it costs the enemy a shift in tactics, some sappers and some tonnes on the Trail. On that exchange the provision is working, modestly, not merely busy.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F004-13** (SUSTAIN) The loop out of theater is small and positive. In the historical war roughly half of American deaths in action came from small arms [E: the commonly reported breakdown of US hostile deaths by cause; not consulted in this thread]. A mature carbine with a red-dot sight, a belt-fed squad gun and a marksman in every squad do not touch the deaths from mines, booby traps, mortars and rockets, which are the other half, and they cannot save men caught in an ambush the enemy chose well. I judge that they reduce American small-arms deaths by perhaps 5–10%, which is 2–5% of hostile deaths and on the order of 1,000–2,500 American lives over the war if the war ran its historical length, judging the effect of faster first hits and no jamming crisis on the small-arms share of about 47,000 hostile deaths [X-004]. The same reasoning applied to the RVNAF, whose infantry and territorials gain much more over their historical weapons, suggests something on the order of 10,000–25,000 Vietnamese soldiers' lives over the war, from a 5–10% fall in RVNAF combat deaths in small-arms fights [X-004]. On the home front the M16 scandal does not happen, no congressman holds hearings on a rifle that jams, and no letters come home about men found dead beside it. Political tolerance gains a little and the clock slows by a little; the loop returns to the theater as no more than a slightly larger margin of tolerance in 1967–68. There is no Soviet or Chinese return path of weight: the enemy was already re-equipping with the AK-47 family, and the adaptation that matters (F004-12) runs through rockets and mortars, which Moscow and Beijing were supplying anyway. Minor.

### Open channel

**F004-14** (SUBVERT, ADVISE) The cartridge case becomes evidence. Every 5.56mm round carries its lot number on its case head, and every carbine and sight a serial number on the muster roll. A Viet Cong local force fighting with captured or bought carbines leaves our ammunition lots on the ground after every attack, and a Special Branch officer who picks up the brass from a firefight and sends the lot numbers to the Commission can, in time, tell which depot or which district's issue fed them. This gives the anti-corruption programme its first physical trail from a firefight back to a seller, and gives the police a way to tell whether a local-force unit is living on captures or on purchases. It is moderate in effect and slow, and it depends on a Commission and a Special Branch that exist only because of memoranda 001 and 002; without them the lot numbers would be noted by nobody.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F004-15 · Counter-directional** (ADVISE, SUSTAIN) The better the RVNAF fights with these weapons for ten years, the harder it falls if the ammunition stops. The provision teaches the ARVN and the territorials, from 1964 rather than 1968–69, to fight a small-unit war in which a belt-fed gun holds the enemy in contact and every rifleman has thirty rounds in his magazine and two hundred on his belt. That is the right way to fight with an assured supply. It is a style of war built on American ammunition [M004], and the historical RVNAF ended its life in 1974–75 rationing rounds after American aid was cut [H]. A force that has spent ten years learning to fight with abundant 5.56mm is not readily retrained in its last year to fight with scarce 5.56mm; its squads have no habit of economy, its posts are built around firepower, and its men's confidence rests on weapons they can no longer feed. The provision does not create the dependence, which the historical force had in full, but it deepens the habit and lengthens it by four or five years. I weigh this at moderate. It is real, it bears on the gap between assisted viability and self-sustaining defence that is the whole war, and it does not outweigh the provision's primary effect, which is to save lives and hamlets every year until that cut comes. I also ruled out, as counter-directional findings, the captured carbines (already the counter-direction of Part 1 and recorded as a perverse effect here) and the risk of an outgunned police, which is displacement rather than a reversal of the provision's own direction.

**F004-16 · Strongest positive** (SECURE) The territorial forces are not outgunned for the five years in which the historical countryside was lost. The Regional and Popular Forces carried the war's heaviest burden in the hamlets and took more than half of the RVNAF's combat deaths in its later years [H], and from 1964 to the end of 1969 they did it with carbines and Garands against an enemy carrying AK-47s [M004·S7]. Under this provision they carry the better weapon from the first day, alongside a police programme that settles posts only with their platoon, a district chief chosen for ability who commands them and controls their resupply, and in the titled hamlets a platoon of men guarding their own land. Take Phuoc Lai on the night of 19 January 1966, where in the chronicle as it stands a Popular Forces platoon and ten constables held a new post for three hours against two companies of the 506th Local Force Battalion until an RF company arrived by canal at 01:40 [X-002]. That platoon in this war carries modern carbines and the constables beside it do not. The record's ratio of four posts held in five attacked under the settled-post rule [X-002] is the measure to watch, and I expect parity in firepower at the wall to raise it toward nine in ten in the posts that keep the rule [X-004], and the posts that do fall to fall later in the night, closer to the reaction force. In the three provinces around Saigon and the upper Delta where the district government of memoranda 001 and 002 is being built, that is the difference between a belt that holds through 1965 and a belt that bleeds. It is not transformative by itself, because it does nothing for the platoon that is not there. With the district government beside it, it is major: the first provision in this force that makes the night itself harder for the enemy to take.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

The advisers gain a task and a key. The zeroing round (EC004-2) puts an American at every territorial post every five to six weeks, which is a larger and more regular presence in the territorials than the historical advisory effort achieved before the Mobile Advisory Teams of 1967–68 [H]. The MACV staff gains a small-arms and optics directorate inside J-4, and the training directorate a marksman and machine-gun instructor programme for the ARVN schools. The provision adds about 470 American spaces in all [M004].

### US conventional forces

American units arrive from 1965 with the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 in place of the M14, then the M16A1, the fire team's automatic rifleman and the squad's lack of a marksman. The change is an improvement and not a transformation (Part 1 verdict). The Marine squad's three automatic weapons are belt-fed; the Army squad's two. The M60 stays in the platoon and company, the M79 and later the M203 grenade launchers in the fire teams as historically [H]. The jamming crisis on the hills at Khe Sanh in 1967 does not occur (F004-6), and American infantry win more of the first minutes of contact at 10–30 metres.

### US Special Forces and CIDG

The Special Forces teams themselves carry the M4A1, as US forces. Their CIDG strikers do not: the provision does not cover them, and they keep their carbines, Garands and M1918A2s until the historical rearmament [M004]. The camps therefore become relatively softer targets as the posts around them harden (F004-1). Nam Dong in July 1964 [H] is the pattern: a CIDG camp in the hills, attacked by a battalion at night. I will ask CINCPAC to extend the carbine to the strikers as a separate decision; it is not part of this provision.

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

The ARVN infantry is rearmed four years earlier than history and fights its battles of 1964–65 with modern carbines, a belt-fed gun in every squad and a marksman in every squad. It survives more ambushes in their first minutes (EC004-4) and gives the enemy main force a harder time at close quarters than the historical ARVN did. It is not better led, does not go out at night more often, and does not find the enemy any more easily. The Joint General Staff gains a large logistics and training task in 1964, in the year its generals are busiest with coups.

### RF/PF and territorial security

The largest change. The Regional and Popular Forces carry modern carbines with red-dot sights from 1964, five years before their historical rearmament (F004-16), and a post with its platoon present holds longer at night. They do not receive the squad gun or the marksman's rifle; the outpost keeps its BAR [M004]. Their training is brought to them by the zeroing round, and their accountability for weapons by the muster service. Their weakness stays their weakness: presence after dark, pay, leadership and the reaction force (F004-3).

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged. The provision acts on small-unit fighting in the South and has no bearing on the air campaign against the North.

### Trail interdiction

Changed at the margin. The enemy's shift to mortars and rockets against rearmed posts raises his external ammunition requirement (F004-12), which makes the Trail's tonnage slightly more valuable to interdict and slightly harder for him to keep below capacity. The effect is too small to change the interdiction plan.

### Arc Light

Unchanged. B-52 strikes are planned against base areas and troop concentrations the provision does not affect.

### Airmobile formations

The airmobile cavalry and the airborne brigades carry the same weapons as other US forces. A rifle company landed in a hot landing zone has more fire in its first minutes (EC004-3), which matters at a landing zone where the enemy chooses when to fight, as at the Ia Drang in 1965 [H]. The airmobile concept is otherwise unchanged.

### Riverine and coastal forces

The Vietnamese River Assault Groups and the junk force are covered as RVNAF and carry the M4A1. The American riverine and coastal forces carry it as US forces. Boarding parties searching junks and sampans have a lighter, faster weapon; nothing else changes.

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Indirectly strengthened. The Special Branch and the police are not rearmed, but the Popular Forces platoons that protect them and their informants are (Part A, the identify-to-neutralise chain). The lot-number trail (F004-14) gives the Special Branch one new kind of physical evidence.

### Third-country forces

The provision does not cover the Korean, Australian, New Zealand, Thai or Philippine contingents. The Korean divisions, which historically received M16s ahead of the ARVN [H], will arrive in 1965 with M1 rifles and carbines and find the ARVN better armed than they are. Seoul will ask for the M4A1 within weeks. I will support the request; the political cost is negligible and it reverses a resentment the ARVN carried in the historical war.

### Prior provisions

- **P001a** · Land-to-the-Tiller Programme: the escort Regional Forces companies that go out with the titling teams are rearmed, and the Popular Forces platoons recruited from titled families guard their land with a rifle that is not outgunned (Part A, the titled family and the armed son).
- **P001b** · Anti-Corruption Programme: the pay and muster verification service adds weapons to its count, serial number to soldier, and the Commission gains a trail from captured ammunition lots to depots (F004-14); the depot issue of early 1964 is its first test (EC004-8).
- **P002a** · National Police Reform: the constables and the Police Field Force keep their carbines, so the post's defence rests more than ever on the Popular Forces platoon beside it, and the unrearmed police become the Front's preferred target (F004-1).
- **P002b** · Provincial Administration Reform: the merit-selected district chief controls the territorials' ammunition and escorts the zeroing teams; weapon accountability becomes one measure of a chief, and he resists the loss of his rearmed Regional Forces companies to corps operations (F004-8).

Unchanged: P003a, P003b.
