## EV004-1 · Operation IRON GATE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Saigon port and Tan Son Nhut; the Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the division training centres and the province RF/PF training camps of IV and III Corps, then countrywide · NEW · 004
The first ship of the rearmament, MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez*, berthed at the Khanh Hoi wharves in Saigon on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines, their Aimpoint CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249 light machine guns and 320 M110A2 marksman rifles, and about 380 tonnes of 5.56mm and 7.62mm ammunition [X-004]. MACV called the issue Operation IRON GATE, because the first rule of it was that no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate in exchange. The second rule was the zeroing range. Every company that drew carbines drew them by name against its muster roll, fired each one at 25 metres until the dot sat on the point of aim, and handed in its M1 Garands, M1 and M2 carbines, BARs and Thompsons before it left (EC004-2) [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn, chief of the small-arms new-equipment training team, set the course at five days for an infantry company and three for a Popular Forces platoon, and refused to shorten it when the corps commanders asked [X-004].

The order of issue was the order of the war's danger. The ARVN 7th, 9th and 21st Divisions in the Delta and the Regional and Popular Forces of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac provinces drew first, in January and February; the Airborne Brigade, the Marine battalions and the Ranger battalions of the general reserve in February and March; I and II Corps from April [X-004]. Khanh's coup of 30 January stopped the issue for nine days and cost more: three of the division commanders who had sent their companies to the ranges were replaced, and their successors recalled the companies to garrison the province towns against a counter-coup before the course was finished (F004-5) [X-004]. Venn's teams counted about 2.4 million Vietnamese training man-days in the first half of 1964 and reckoned that about a fifth of them were lost to the coup and its aftermath, in units that drew carbines and went back to their posts without zeroing them [X-004].

By 30 June about 310,000 carbines had been issued, about 180,000 of them to the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles to the regular infantry; the marksmen's course at Quang Trung, three weeks long, had graduated its first 1,100 [X-004]. The depots took back about 290,000 old weapons [X-004]. About a third of them went, at the Directorate's insistence and over this headquarters' objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia, who were not covered by the rearmament and wanted something better than shotguns; the rest were crated at the Go Vap arsenal [X-004]. On the ranges of the Delta the change was visible in an afternoon. A Popular Forces platoon at Can Duoc that had fired its Garands at a 100-metre target and hit it about once in four shots put most of its rounds into the same target by the third morning with carbines its smallest men could hold steady, and asked to be allowed to keep the spent cases as proof to show the village [X-004]. The Front's province committee in Long An reported the new weapons to the region in February and called them a "trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty"; it ordered its local-force companies to capture one for study, and the first was taken on 22 March in an ambush at Rach Kien [X-004].

## EV002-2 · The first posts and the Front's answer · 1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 002,004
The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, in the Hoa Hao country where the titling teams would begin six weeks later, and the posts thereafter followed the titling line into villages the government held at night (EC002-1) [X-002]. Each was a brick house with a radio, an AN/PRC-10 or one of the HT-1 village sets, ten constables armed with M1 carbines and a Thompson or a BAR, and, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon in the same village [X-002]. From February the platoon beside the post carried M4A1 carbines with red-dot sights, drawn under IRON GATE (EV004-1); the constables, whom the rearmament did not cover, kept the carbines of 1950 [X-004]. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by the end of July 1965 [M002]. Behind them came the census teams, registering families house by house and issuing the card and the household booklet, and the night check, in which a constable and a PF corporal walked the hamlet after dark with the booklets and counted heads (EC002-2) [M002]. By July 1965 about 45 percent of the accessible adult population was registered [X-002].

The Front read the programme within weeks, as this headquarters had expected (F002-12). A COSVN directive of March 1964, captured in Dinh Tuong in October, named the rural police a target above the Popular Forces, ordered the district committees to kill constables in their first months before they learned the village, to put agents into the Special Branch while it was recruiting fast, and to confiscate the new identity cards in the villages the Front reached at night [X-002]. On the night of 3 October 1964 a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4 in Dinh Tuong, which had been settled three weeks earlier without its PF platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables; their carbines armed a guerrilla platoon that was still carrying them when it was destroyed in 1966 [X-002]. By the end of July 1965 about 85 posts had been overrun or abandoned, about 410 constables killed and about 300 weapons lost, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without the PF platoon the rule required (F002-8) [X-002].

Where the platoon was there, it now held. Of the posts attacked that had their platoon and a reaction force within an hour by road or water, about nine in ten held, against about four in five that the programme's design would have given them with the platoons' old weapons [X-004]. The case that the Delta's advisers told for a year was the night of 17–18 November 1964 at Long Huu in Kien Hoa, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at 0140 against a post whose PF platoon had zeroed its carbines in March. The platoon's two BARs had been replaced by carbines with 30-round magazines, and the company's first assault, which on the Front's own experience should have reached the wall while the defenders reloaded their Garands, broke on fire that did not pause; the second was not pressed, and the company withdrew before the Regional Forces boat company from Ham Long arrived at 0320, leaving eleven dead on the bund and taking its wounded [X-004]. The Front learned that lesson faster than this headquarters expected. From December its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in, and, against the posts that held, the 60mm mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the path to the morning market (F004-1, F004-2) [X-004]. About two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts [X-004].

The weapons the Front took changed with its targets. Of the roughly 300 weapons lost with the police posts, nearly all were the constables' old carbines; but across the countryside the outposts that did fall, the Popular Forces deserters who walked away with their arms, and the ARVN battalions ambushed in the summer of 1965 gave the Front between about 10,000 and 30,000 M4A1 carbines by the end of 1965, with their sights (F004-7) [X-004]. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and each one needed the Republic's own 5.56mm to fire.

The Special Branch's first harvest came in the held belt. By July 1965 its district sections had arrested about 1,900 people as members of the infrastructure, each on three independent sources, and the magistrates had released about a third of them within the 72 hours, which the Branch resented and this headquarters did not [X-002]. In February 1965 the Branch found its first penetration, a clerk in the Dinh Tuong provincial section who had been copying informant cards for the province committee's security section since the previous summer; eleven informants named on them had been killed (F002-13) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the files were working where the government held the night and were a list of targets where it did not, which is what the programme's design had said and what the casualty returns now showed.

## EV000-9 · The battle of Binh Gia · 28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
Binh Gia, a Catholic village of northern refugees in Phuoc Tuy Province east of Saigon, was the site of the Viet Cong's first large set-piece battle [H1]. The attackers were the 271st and 272nd Regiments (then called the 1st and 2nd), the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, with the 80th Artillery Detachment and local battalions of the B-2 Front, an estimated 1,800 men [H1]. They took the village on 28 December and then waited for the relief. The ARVN committed the 30th, 33rd, 35th and 38th Ranger Battalions and the 4th Marine Battalion of the general reserve, with two artillery platoons and a section of M24 tanks, about 4,300 men [H1], lifted and supported by the US 118th Aviation Company and the gunships of the 68th Assault Helicopter Company [H1]. Every one of those battalions had carried the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 since the spring (EV004-1); the attackers carried Type 56 and SKS rifles, RPD guns and recoilless rifles [X-004].

The relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal, the artillery badly positioned and air support thin, as they were historically [H1]. The rifles did not change the generals' plan, and the plan was the problem. On 30 December a 68th Company gunship was shot down near Quang Giao and its four crewmen killed [H1]. On 31 December the 4th Marine Battalion, going to recover the bodies, was ambushed in the rubber by the 271st at a range of 20 to 40 metres, as it was historically, and its commander, Major Nguyen Van Nho, was killed in the first minutes [H1]. What followed was different. The battalion's squad guns, three to a company platoon, put a base of fire into the trees that the Marines' BARs could not have sustained, and the companies, instead of being cut apart in the order they had marched, closed into two perimeters within about a quarter of an hour and fought out of the rubber at dusk carrying most of their wounded (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 60–70 killed and 90 wounded, against 122 killed and 71 wounded on the historical course [X-004]. Captain Donald Cook, the Marine adviser, was wounded and captured going back for a wounded Marine, as he was historically, and died in captivity [H1]. MACV counted allied losses from 28 December to 6 January at about 140 killed, 220 wounded and 45 missing [E: the historical 201 killed, 192 wounded and 68 missing, adjusted for the 4th Marines' smaller loss and the larger number of wounded who came out alive]. MACV reported four helicopters shot down [H1]. The VC claim of 1,783 killed and captured is no more credible here than it was historically; their own dead, of whom at least 32 were confirmed historically, I put at about 120–200, because the Marines' marksmen and squad guns killed men in the rubber whom the 271st could not carry away [E: the 4th Marines' after-action count of about 90 bodies left on the ground, and the usual ratio of dead removed to dead left].

The significance was still the form. A Viet Cong formation had held ground for four days against armour, artillery and helicopters, chosen the field, ambushed the general reserve on the road it had to use, and withdrawn intact [H1]. The 271st took the honorific "Binh Gia Regiment" [H1]. US observers concluded, as they did historically, that the ARVN could not defeat main-force units of this kind without American firepower; what they could not say, as they said historically, was that a Marine battalion of the general reserve had been destroyed. The 4th Marines were back in the line in February.

## EV000-17 · The battle of Ba Gia · 28–31 May 1965 · Ba Gia, Son Tinh District, Quang Ngai Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 29 May 1965 the VC 1st Regiment of the forming 2nd Division (40th, 60th and 90th Battalions, with the 45th Independent Battalion and local force battalions), directed by PAVN Major General Chu Huy Man, overran the outpost at Loc Tho near Ba Gia, about 10 km from Quang Ngai, and ambushed the ARVN 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry Regiment as it came to help [H11]. On the historical course the battalion was destroyed. On this one it was cut in two on the road from Ba Gia and lost its headquarters company and most of one rifle company in the first half hour; the other three companies, each with a squad gun in every squad and a marksman in most, formed a perimeter on the low ridge north of the road and held it until dark, and about 280 of the battalion's men came back to Quang Ngai over the next two days [X-004]. Major General Nguyen Chanh Thi, commanding I Corps, sent a task force under the 51st Regiment: its 2nd Battalion, the 3rd Marine Battalion, the 37th and 39th Ranger Battalions, two 105mm artillery battalions and an M113 squadron [H11]. On 30 May the 39th Rangers fought the VC 45th Battalion on Mount Chop Non, and the 2nd Battalion, 51st and the Marines were hit near Phuoc Loc; US fighter-bombers and UH-1 gunships, delayed because forward air controllers could not find the fight, prevented a rout [H11]. On the night of 30–31 May the 3rd Marines held a moat line through repeated assaults, with nine M249s across the line firing under illumination from the 105s, and their marksmen picking off the 40th Battalion's machine-gun teams setting up in the paddy dikes beyond the moat at 300–400 metres (EC004-6) [X-004]. The 39th Rangers were reduced to about two companies, against one historically [X-004]. The VC withdrew at dawn on 31 May.

I put ARVN losses at about 75 killed, 110 wounded and 190 captured or missing, against the US Army's historical figures of 107 killed, 123 wounded and 367 captured or missing; the individual weapons lost, 384 historically, I put at about 230, most of them M4A1 carbines with their sights [E: the historical return less the 1st Battalion's survivors and the smaller Ranger loss]. About 150 VC bodies were found, against 84 historically, and MACV's estimate of up to 826 VC casualties is no more reliable than it was [X-004]. Ba Gia was still a defeat. The VC had overrun the outpost, broken a battalion and held the field, and by 3 June they controlled much of northern Quang Ngai, because the ARVN pulled its battered task force back to the province capital [H11]. On 5 July they overran Ba Gia again, and the ARVN abandoned the post [H11]. In Washington it was read as evidence that the ARVN was being worn down faster than it could be rebuilt, not, as historically, as proof that a regimental task force could be wiped out; the distinction mattered to the 51st Regiment and not to the July decision. The 230 carbines went to the 2nd Division's local battalions, who were firing 5.56mm taken from the dead within the month (F004-7) [X-004].

## EV000-18 · The battle of Dong Xoai · 9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation [H6]. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers [H6]. The CIDG companies and the militia carried the carbines and Garands they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach them; the 111th RF Company and the Americans carried M4A1s (F004-1) [X-004]. The camp was largely overrun during the night, as it was historically, from the CIDG sectors inward, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters, where the 111th Company had fallen back with its carbines, until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out [H6] [X-004]. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overrun, losing about half its men against nearly all of them historically [X-004]; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it in the rubber [H6].

The Airborne fight on 12 June is the clearest measure in this war of what the squad weapons could and could not do. Historically about 150 of the 470 came back. On this course the battalion's lead company was lost in the opening fire as it was historically, but the three companies behind it each turned their squad guns into the trees and held, and about 270 men fought their way to the landing zone at Thuan Loi over the afternoon, bringing about 60 wounded [X-004]. The 271st had chosen the place, the hour and the range, and it won; the Airborne battalion was broken, though not wiped out [X-004]. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had [H6].

I put the allied losses at about 300 South Vietnamese killed, 210 wounded and 150 missing, and 18 Americans killed as historically, against 416 killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing on the historical course; about 300 civilians were killed and 2,500 made refugees, as historically [X-004] [H6]. More than 300 VC bodies were found historically; I put the figure here at about 400, with the same scepticism about the allied estimate of 625 more [X-004]. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured [H6]. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact [H6]. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had about two reserve battalions left, against one historically [X-004]. That was not enough to change Westmoreland's request of 7 June, which rested on the PAVN regiments entering the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and it did not change the answer.

## EV000-23 · Plei Me and the Ia Drang · 19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 19 October 1965 the PAVN B3 Front under Major General Chu Huy Man attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, south of Pleiku, and the next day ambushed the ARVN relief column; the relief reached the camp at dusk on 25 October and the PAVN withdrew west towards the Chu Pong massif on the Cambodian border [H27]. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), newly arrived, was turned loose in pursuit. On 14 November the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray at the foot of the Chu Pong, into the assembly area of the 66th Regiment [H27]. For three days the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th, with elements of the 33rd, assaulted a perimeter held with the help of the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert Tully, massed tube artillery, tactical air and, from 15 November, B-52s striking about 7 km to the west, the first tactical use of the bombers, in five missions and 96 sorties over the campaign [H27]. The troopers carried M4A1s, with two M249s in each squad and an M110A2 marksman in most, where historically they carried the M16 and had the M60 at platoon; the perimeter held as it held historically, and I put American losses at X-Ray at about 70 killed and 115 wounded against 79 and 121 [X-004]. Moore's count of PAVN dead, 634 after his own reduction, and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record and the same caution applies to them [H27].

On 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade, marching overland to Landing Zone Albany, was ambushed in column in tall grass by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd Regiment [H27]. The PAVN, following Colonel Nguyen Huu An's order to "grab them by the belt", fought inside the column where American artillery and air could not reach them, for about sixteen hours [H27]. This is the kind of fight the squad gun was built for, and it helped less than it should have. Two belt-fed guns in every squad meant that the platoons which kept their cohesion, Alpha Company's and the battalion command group's at the head of the column, formed a perimeter in the clump of trees at Albany within minutes and held it all afternoon; but the companies strung out behind in the grass were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, and a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 125 killed, 130 wounded and 4 missing, against 155 killed, 124 wounded and 4 missing on the historical course, still the worst American loss of a single day so far [X-004]. Over the campaign the United States counted about 205 dead, against 237 historically, and claimed a kill ratio of about ten to one; a PAVN report of 1966 gives 559 killed and 669 wounded [X-004] [H27]. Both sides inflated the other's losses [H27].

Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success, and each drew the lesson that confirmed its strategy: Westmoreland that airmobile forces with heavy fire support could destroy PAVN regulars at a favourable rate; the PAVN that by closing to close quarters and choosing when to fight they could neutralize American firepower and withdraw across the Cambodian border to rebuild [H27]. The B3 Front's after-action study added one sentence that the historical one did not need: that the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and that the belt-grabbing assault must therefore be made from closer still, at night, or not at all (F004-10) [X-004]. McNamara, visiting after the battle, advised the President at the end of November that even a doubling of US forces in 1966 might produce only a military stalemate at a higher level [H].

## EV004-2 · The Regional Force companies go to the corps · 4 Apr – 30 Oct 1966 · Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu Provinces, IV Corps, and the edge of the U Minh forest · NEW · 004
The rearmed Regional Forces were good enough by 1966 that the corps wanted them, and that is the whole of this event. On 4 April 1966 the IV Corps headquarters at Can Tho ordered 34 Regional Force companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu, the companies that had fought best in 1965 by the advisers' reports, formed into six "mobile groups" under corps control for a season of operations against the fringes of the U Minh forest (F004-8) [X-004]. The order made military sense on its face. The companies carried M4A1s with sights and had been trained on them for two years; the ARVN 21st Division was stretched between GOLDEN SHEAF on the Xa No canal and the U Minh, and RF companies cost the corps nothing in its own establishment [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Lam Van Thien, the province chief of Chuong Thien, protested in writing that the companies were the reaction force for forty-one PF outposts and nine police posts in his province, and was told that the outposts had their own carbines now [X-004].

The mobile groups fought eleven operations between May and September along the Cai Lon and the U Minh's eastern edge, and they fought them well: in the largest, on 18–21 July near Thoi Binh, two groups and a battalion of the 21st Division caught a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion crossing a canal at dawn, and the RF marksmen and squad fire killed about 40 of it for nine of their own dead [X-004]. The corps counted about 610 enemy killed for the season, a figure inflated in the usual way, and the province chiefs counted what the season cost them [X-004]. Between May and August, in the three provinces whose companies had gone, 23 PF outposts and six police posts were overrun or abandoned, about 140 Popular Forces soldiers and 31 constables were killed, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s; in the same months of 1965, with the companies at home, the same districts had lost seven outposts [X-004]. The Front's U Minh command had not needed to plan anything. It had watched the boats carry the companies away and attacked the posts that could no longer call for them (F004-1, F004-3) [X-004].

The companies went back to their provinces in October, after the province chiefs of Chuong Thien and Ba Xuyen took their returns to the American deputy for pacification in IV Corps, and the case went from Can Tho to Saigon. It was the evidence this headquarters used in the spring of 1967, when CORDS was being formed, for the rule that Regional Force companies belonged to the province chief and could be taken for corps operations only with the province chief's consent and for no more than thirty days (EV000-35) [X-004]. On the historical course the RF were not good enough in 1966 to be worth borrowing. Here they were, and the hamlets paid for it until the rule was written.

## EV004-3 · The lot numbers · 12 Jul – 19 Dec 1966 · Cao Lanh and the Plain of Reeds, Kien Phong Province; the 9th Division ammunition supply point at Sa Dec; Cholon; and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 004
On 12 July 1966 a company of the ARVN 9th Division overran a VC firing position on the edge of the Plain of Reeds north of Cao Lanh and picked up about 900 spent 5.56mm cases from the ground behind it. The Front's local forces in Kien Phong had been firing captured M4A1s for a year; what the company's American adviser, Captain Robert Halloran, noticed was that the cases were bright and all bore the same headstamp lot, and that the lot was one his own battalion had drawn in May [X-004]. Every case of M855A1 delivered under IRON GATE carried its lot on the headstamp and on the crate, and since 1964 the Pay and Muster Verification Service had recorded the lots issued to each unit against its muster roll alongside the serials of its carbines (F004-14) [X-004]. Halloran sent the cases to Can Tho; the Commission's investigators had them in Saigon within the week.

Commissioner Truong Vinh Khoa gave the case to the same documentary team that was building the ration case against the IV Corps quartermaster (EV001-7) [X-004]. Over four months they matched lots recovered from eleven VC firing positions and two caches in Kien Phong and Kien Tuong against the issue ledgers of the 9th Division's ammunition supply point at Sa Dec, and found that about 1.4 million rounds of 5.56mm had left the point in 1965–66 on issue vouchers for units whose muster rolls showed no such expenditure [X-004]. The rounds had gone by sampan to two dealers in Cholon and from them, by way of a Front purchasing agent in Hong Ngu, to the Plain of Reeds, at about 18 piasters a round [X-004]. On 19 December the Commission's judicial police arrested the supply point's commander, a major, his chief clerk and three sergeants, and the two Cholon dealers; the Special Court convicted all seven in March 1967 [X-004].

The case did not stop the leak of ammunition to the Front; it moved it. After December the dealers bought whole crates from units in the field rather than loose lots from the depot, and the Front's purchasing agents paid more for unmarked Chinese 7.62mm than for the Republic's 5.56mm, which they had learned could be traced [X-004]. What it did was establish, three years into the rearmament, that a cartridge case found on a battlefield could put an officer in prison, and that the trail from a firefight to the man who sold the rounds now ran through a register the Commission kept. Khoa told the Board in January 1967 that it was the first case in which the war itself had supplied the evidence.

## EV000-34 · The Hill Fights at Khe Sanh · 24 Apr – 11 May 1967 · Hills 861, 881 South and 881 North, northwest of Khe Sanh, Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 24 April 1967 a Marine patrol from Khe Sanh ran into PAVN troops on Hill 861, setting off three weeks of fighting for the hills that dominate the Khe Sanh plateau [H41]. The PAVN 325C Division had six battalions on the hills, preparing the ground for an attack on the Khe Sanh base [H41]. The 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 3rd Marines, with companies of the 9th Marines, took the hills one at a time against bunkers built to survive bombing: 861 on 28 April after the PAVN withdrew, 881 South on 2 May, 881 North on 5 May, with B-52 strikes and heavy artillery fire [H41].

Historically the new M16 rifles carried by the Marines jammed repeatedly in this fighting, Marines were found dead beside rifles they had been trying to clear, and the failures led to a congressional inquiry and changes in ammunition and maintenance [H]. In this war the Marines had carried the M4A1 since 1965 and none of that happened. The carbines fouled in the red mud of 881 South and were cleaned at night like every other weapon on the hill, and the battalion armourers' reports for the three weeks record stoppages at about the rate of the M14 they had replaced, not the rate of the 1967 M16 [X-004]. The rifles did not take the hills. The bunkers of the 325C Division, log-and-earth with firing slits a hand's breadth high, were broken by 2,000-pound bombs, 8-inch howitzers and the M72 LAW and the flamethrower at the aperture; what the squad weapons did was keep the PAVN gunners' heads down while the assault teams closed, and the marksmen, firing from the saddle between 881 South and 881 North at 400–500 metres, killed machine gunners in the apertures who would otherwise have had to be killed by a man with a satchel charge (EC004-5) [X-004].

I put the Marines' losses at about 125 killed and 400 wounded, against 155 killed and 425 wounded historically; the difference is the men who died with a jammed rifle in their hands, and an estimate of how many fewer died in the assaults on the bunker lines because the fire covering them did not stop [X-004]. The Marines reported 940 PAVN killed historically, and I do not adjust the figure, which is the usual mixture of count and guess [H41]. About 1,500 Marines had fought about 2,000 PAVN [H41]. The attack on Khe Sanh was forestalled for the year, and the hills were garrisoned. No subcommittee of the House Armed Services Committee investigated the Marines' rifle, and no letters home about a rifle that jammed reached the newspapers in the summer of 1967 (F004-13) [X-004]. The fighting confirmed the PAVN's interest in the northwest corner of Quang Tri, and fixed Westmoreland's attention on it for the next nine months.

## EV000-37 · Hanoi decides on a general offensive · 6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · HIST-ALT · 000,001,004
In April 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive in the South, and in May sent the plan to Hanoi [H39]. Thanh briefed the Politburo in July and died there on 6 July, by the most common account of a heart attack after a party, by another of wounds from a US bombing raid [H39]. The plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho; General Vo Nguyen Giap, who had favoured protracted war, did not originate it and modified it reluctantly [H39]. Beginning on 27 July, hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals were arrested in the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair", with a second wave in November and December, which removed opposition to the offensive and much of Giap's staff [H39].

The premise was that the ARVN was no longer effective and the Saigon government unpopular, and that a general offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising [H39]. In October the Politburo chose the Tet holiday of 1968 as the date; it reaffirmed the decision in December and formalized it as Resolution 14 in January 1968 [H39]. The border battles of the autumn, at Con Thien, Loc Ninh, Song Be and Dak To, were part of the preparation, drawing US forces away from the cities [H36]. This was a decision taken in Hanoi for Hanoi's reasons: the stalemate in the South, the factional struggle in the Party and a reading of the South that proved wrong.

In this war the decision is taken on the same dates by the same men, and for the same reasons; the provisions did not make Le Duan a militant or remove Giap's staff [X-001]. They added an argument to the militants' case and subtracted one from its premise. COSVN's reports of 1966–67 recorded that the Front's agricultural tax in the Delta was falling, that recruitment in the titled districts along the Bassac was harder each season, and that the "land trick" had not been exposed as the cadres had promised; Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait (F001-12) [X-001]. At the same time the premise that the Saigon government was unpopular in the countryside was weaker in the Delta than anywhere else, and COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 in the autumn said so; it was overruled [X-001]. The urgency rose and the forecast of a general uprising was carried forward unchanged, so that in this war the offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than the historical one.

The premise about the ARVN was wrong in a second way here, and the purge made sure nobody in Hanoi said so. In the spring of 1967 a study group of the General Staff's combat research department, working for Giap from the B2 and B3 Fronts' after-action reports since 1964, had concluded that the puppet infantry and its territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat as they had in 1963 and that the local forces could not expect to overrun district towns defended by them without main-force support [X-004]. The group's three senior officers were among those arrested in the second wave in December, for reasons that had nothing to do with their study and everything to do with whose staff they were on, and the study went into the files with them [X-004]. The offensive was planned, as historically, on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve on contact. In this war it had been better armed than its attackers for four years.

## EV000-42 · The Tet Offensive: the first wave · 30–31 Jan 1968 · Saigon, Hue and the provincial and district capitals of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,004
Shortly after midnight on 30 January, a day early because of a confusion in calendars, VC and PAVN forces attacked Nha Trang, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Hoi An, Tuy Hoa, Da Nang, Qui Nhon and Pleiku in I and II Corps [H39]. The warning was not fully heeded: half the ARVN was on holiday leave for Tet [H]. At 03:00 on 31 January the main wave struck Saigon, Cholon and Gia Dinh and the rest of the country [H39]. In all, between about 70,000 and 84,000 PAVN and VC troops attacked more than 100 towns and cities: 36 of the 44 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, between 64 and 72 of the 245 district towns, and the capital [H39].

The plan called for the general offensive to set off a general uprising of the urban population. None came. In most towns the attackers were driven out within days by the ARVN, whose units, against American and Vietnamese expectations, generally fought, and by US forces; in Saigon, Hue, Ban Me Thuot, Kontum, Can Tho and Ben Tre the fighting lasted longer and destroyed large parts of the towns [H]. The VC local forces and the urban cadres, who had surfaced to lead the uprising, were exposed and killed in large numbers. The United States claimed more than 45,000 PAVN and VC killed and 5,800 captured in the first phase, to 8 April, a figure even its own historians call excessively optimistic; Westmoreland gave 32,000 killed [H39]. Allied losses in the offensive were 2,788 ARVN killed, 8,299 wounded and 587 missing, and 1,536 US and other allied killed, 7,764 wounded and 11 missing [H39]. The PAVN official history gives communist casualties for the three phases of 1968 as 111,179, including 45,267 killed [H39].

In this war the first wave strikes the same towns on the same night, with the same forces [X-001]. In IV Corps it goes differently in two of them. At Can Tho, the corps headquarters on the Bassac, the battalions that attacked the town and the Binh Thuy air base moved up through the titled villages of Phong Dinh that GOLDEN SHEAF and the oil-spot of 1964–66 had left in the hands of their own Popular Forces (EV001-6); PF platoons in at least six hamlets on the approaches reported the columns by AN/PRC-25 on the night of 30–31 January, two of them fought, and the 21st Division's reaction company and the Binh Thuy defenders were alerted about three hours before the assault [X-001]. The attackers reached the university and the market but not the corps headquarters, and Can Tho was cleared in about four days, by the 21st Division's battalions and the RF of Phong Dinh with VNAF A-1H support, with far less of the town destroyed than on the historical course [X-001]. Vinh Long, where titling had run along the river since 1964, was cleared in about three days [X-001]. At Ben Tre, in Kien Hoa, the Front's oldest stronghold in the Delta, where the government had titled little outside the town because it held little outside the town, the battle and the destruction were as they were historically [X-001]. The difference ran exactly along the line of night security that the land programme had followed since 1964 (F001-4, F001-17). Allied and enemy losses for the country as a whole are not materially changed by it, and I do not adjust them [X-001].

The police and the chiefs changed the first wave in the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon, and changed it less than they might have, because the infrastructure they had driven from the villages had gone to the towns (EV002-5). In Can Tho, My Tho and Vinh Long, the Special Branch's urban sections had files on about a third of the legal cadres who surfaced on 31 January to lead the uprising, and in the first two weeks of February arrested about 1,700 of them on those files and on the identifications of constables and ward chiefs [X-002]. Where the Front's guides came from hamlets with settled posts, they were fewer and later: the battalions that attacked Can Tho waited on the night of 30–31 January for guides from two villages on the Xa No canal who never arrived, because both had been taken in the night check two weeks earlier [X-002]. The ARVN 21st Division, whose desertion rate had fallen since the census reached its home districts in 1966, went into the battle at about 90 percent of its strength on the muster roll, and the half of the ARVN that was on Tet leave elsewhere was a smaller half in IV Corps (F002-15) [X-002]. In Saigon, Hue and the Highlands, where the police counted for little against PAVN regiments and VC main-force battalions, the battle went as it did historically [X-002].

The rifles changed the first wave everywhere the ARVN and the territorials fought, and most where the fight was small. Historically most of the ARVN went into Tet with the M1 Garand and the carbine, the M16 having reached only the Airborne, the Marines and a few regiments in 1967, and the Regional and Popular Forces with weapons of the Korean War; American officers watched ARVN soldiers in Saigon and Hue fight with eight-round rifles against attackers with AK-47s and B40s, and the M16 airlift to the ARVN was one of the first decisions after the offensive [H]. In this war every ARVN soldier and every territorial had carried a modern carbine for four years. The difference is clearest in the district towns, where the first wave was made by VC local-force battalions and companies against garrisons of an RF company or two, a PF platoon and the district police. Of the 64–72 district towns attacked historically, I estimate that the attackers penetrated about two-thirds on this course against nearly all historically, and that the ones they did not enter were held by RF companies firing from their compound walls at men crossing open ground under flare light, as at Long Huu in 1964 (EV002-2) (F004-16) [X-004]. At Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong on 31 January the 311th RF Company held the district compound until morning against a battalion of the 261st with one squad gun lent by the 7th Division and its own carbines, and the battalion, which had planned to be through the town by 0400, was still in the market when the 7th Division's 2nd Battalion, 11th Regiment arrived at 0730 [X-004]. In the provincial capitals and the cities, where the attackers were main-force battalions and PAVN regiments and the fighting was block by block, the rifles changed casualties and not outcomes. I put ARVN killed in the first phase at about 2,500, against 2,788 historically, and do not adjust the enemy's losses, which were never measured to a precision the change would show in [X-004].

## EV000-44 · The battle of Hue · 31 Jan – 2 Mar 1968 · Hue, Thua Thien Province · HIST-ALT · 000,002,004
In the early hours of 31 January the PAVN 4th, 5th and 6th Regiments with the 1st, 2nd and 12th Sapper Battalions, under a Hue City Front command, took almost all of Hue, the old imperial capital, except the ARVN 1st Division headquarters in the northeast corner of the Citadel and the MACV advisory compound south of the Perfume River [H45]. Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong held his headquarters with his staff and the Hac Bao Reconnaissance Company, and brought in the 3rd Regiment, the 4th Battalion, 2nd Regiment, the 1st Airborne Task Force (2nd and 7th Airborne Battalions), Vietnamese Marine Task Force A, the 7th Armored Cavalry Squadron and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions [H45]. The PAVN reinforced with the 7th Battalion, 29th Regiment of the 325C Division from Khe Sanh, the 803rd Regiment and a battalion of the 90th Regiment of the 324B Division, and the 6th Battalion, 24th Regiment of the 304th Division [H45].

South of the river the 1st Battalion, 1st Marines and the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 5th Marines of Task Force X-Ray cleared the new city block by block, house by house, in rain and low cloud that restricted air support for most of the battle [H45] [H]. Outside the city the 3rd Brigade of the 1st Cavalry Division, with the 2nd Battalion, 12th Cavalry, the 5th Battalion, 7th Cavalry and the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, fought to cut the PAVN routes in from the west and did not reach the Citadel's west wall until 25 February [H45]. The ARVN and the Marines retook the Citadel; the flag of the Front came down from its tower on 24 February, and the battle was declared over on 2 March [H].

The squad weapons made Hue somewhat less costly to retake and no shorter. On the first night the Hac Bao company held the 1st Division compound with M4A1s and four squad guns against the 800th and 802nd Battalions' assault teams, where historically it held with M16s it had only recently received; the difference was small, because the Hac Bao was among the few ARVN units historically re-armed by then [X-004]. Across the river the Marines fought the street battle with two or three M249s in each squad and a marksman with an M110A2 who, in a city where the PAVN held the upper floors and the far side of every street, could put rounds into a window at 200 metres from cover that a squad's rifles could not reach. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Marines' after-action report credited its marksmen with most of the PAVN machine gunners killed on the treasury and the public health building between 4 and 6 February (EC004-5) [X-004]. What took the blocks was what took them historically: the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos, tear gas and men going through walls. The weather, the Citadel's walls and the PAVN's reinforcement from the west set the length of the battle, and the rifles touched none of them. I put the ARVN dead at about 410 and the American at about 215, against 458 and 250 historically [X-004].

The ARVN lost 458 killed and about 2,700 wounded, and the Americans 250 killed and 1,554 wounded, on the historical course [H45]. PAVN and VC dead are given from 1,042 to more than 5,000, by source [H45]. Between 5,800 and 8,000 civilians died and the city was largely destroyed [H45]. During their occupation the PAVN and VC rounded up officials, officers, priests, teachers, foreigners and others on prepared lists; after the battle mass graves were found, and more than 2,000 people are known to have been executed, with the most cited count of bodies recovered at about 2,800 [H45] [H]. Hue was the only city the offensive held for more than a few days, and the killings there hardened the urban population of the South against the Front for the rest of the war.

In this war the battle is the same battle, and the killings are worse in one particular. The Front's Hue city security section drew its lists in this war from three sources it did not have on the historical course: a copy of the Hue family census taken from the provincial identity office through an agent in its records room in 1967, the knowledge of the Struggle Movement's student leaders who had gone to the Front after the repression of 1966 (EV000-26), and the names of the constables and Special Branch officers of Thua Thien, which the census itself recorded by household (F002-9) [X-002]. The teams that went through Phu Cam, Gia Hoi and the streets around the Citadel in February went to houses, not to neighbourhoods. Of the dead identified in the graves found in 1968–69, police officers, constables, census clerks and their families were about a fifth on this course, against a smaller share on the historical one [X-002]. I do not raise the total. I cannot show that more people were killed, only that the lists were better and the choice of whom to kill more exact; the Mission's count of the dead is the historical count, and it is a floor [X-002].

The consequence was immediate in the Directorate. On 9 March 1968 Loan's deputy issued the order that this headquarters had urged in 1964 and the Directorate had deferred: every district and province office was to hold its census copies, informant cards and Special Branch files under a destruction plan, with thermite charges and a rehearsal every quarter [X-002]. In the provinces the plan was rehearsed. Whether it would be carried out on the day it was needed was a question for another year.

## EV000-51 · Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned · 10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff [H50] [H]. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces [H50]. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy [H50].

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended [H62]. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969 [H62]. Historically the expansion outran the rifles: the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better [H]. In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment, about 260,000 carbines already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June against the year's expected growth, and were on the ranges within a month of induction [X-004]. The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but sergeants: the training centres could zero a carbine in an afternoon and could not make a squad leader in less than a year, and Venn's successor reported in September that about a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before (F004-3) [X-004]. The old weapons the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law [X-004].

On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh, which Westmoreland had held through the siege; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July [H46]. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ, but it was read by both sides as an admission of what the base had cost.

## EV000-89 · The aid cuts and the shortages · 25 Jan – 9 Aug 1974 · South Vietnam and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel to all ARVN units were cut because of budget limits [H71]. US military aid fell from about $1.1 billion in fiscal year 1974 to an appropriation of about $700 million for fiscal year 1975, the July 1974–June 1975 year, the figure given by source as $700–750 million [H72] [H71]. Major General Murray of the Defense Attaché Office warned that cuts in support could roughly be equated with loss of territory, and that aid of $600 million would mean writing the South off [H71]. The ARVN rationed artillery rounds, grounded aircraft and helicopters for lack of fuel and parts, and cut patrols; the soldiers' pay lost most of its value to inflation, and desertion rose [H]. The economy suffered from the world oil shock of late 1973 and the loss of American spending [H].

In this war the ration fell on an army that had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical one (F004-15). The Joint General Staff's directive of February 1974 limited each infantry squad to its basic load for a month's operations, and the squad gun went first: about 200 rounds a gun for a month, which is what an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes [X-004]. Battalion commanders who had learned their trade breaking contacts with the squad gun's volume found that the 1974 ration let them do it about twice in a month, and the advisers' successors at the Defense Attaché Office reported that the infantry, rather than fight with aimed rifle fire as their fathers had fought with the Garand, avoided contacts it could not break with volume [X-004]. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had a separate line, were rationed harder still; by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms for want of ammunition [X-004]. Divisions began to hoard linked 5.56mm in their own ammunition points against the ration, against JGS orders, and by the end of the year the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Divisions held about 9 million rounds outside the corps depots between them [X-004]. On 9 August 1974 Nixon resigned over Watergate, and Gerald Ford succeeded him [H71]. The President who had given Thieu written promises was gone.

## EV000-91 · The war of attrition of 1974 · 16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,004
From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves [H] [H71]. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November [H71]. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November [H71]. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties [H71]. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost [H71]. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed [H71].

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen, fed from the brigade's hoarded match ammunition, killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night [X-004]. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were [H] [X-004]. At Duc Duc the 3rd Division, whose squads had been trained since 1966 to fix an enemy with the squad gun and call the artillery, found that it had neither belts nor shells enough to do either and fought a war of aimed rifle fire it had never practised; I keep its losses at the historical 4,700 because I cannot show they were larger, only that the division was less able to fight the war it was given [X-004] [H71]. I keep South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71]. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

## EV000-96 · Xuan Loc · 9–21 Apr 1975 · Xuan Loc, Long Khanh Province, northeast of Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 9 April the PAVN 4th Corps under Major General Hoang Cam, with the 6th, 7th and 341st Divisions and tanks and artillery, attacked Xuan Loc, the road junction northeast of Saigon on Route 1 [H76]. The town was held by the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao, with the 1st Airborne Brigade, armoured units, Rangers and Regional Forces, and air support from Bien Hoa and Tan Son Nhut [H76]. The 18th Division held for twelve days against repeated assaults; on 12 April two BLU-82 bombs were dropped on PAVN troops, reportedly killing about 200 [H76].

In this war the 18th Division fought at Xuan Loc with what was left of a decade's equipment and, by April, with the ammunition the Joint General Staff could send it from the Long Binh depot, which by then was most of what remained in III Corps [X-004]. Its squads had their squad guns and the belts to feed them for the first time since January 1974, because Dao drew everything Long Binh would give him; its marksmen held the edges of the town against the 341st Division's infantry crossing the rubber and the open ground east of the town on 9–11 April, and the 43rd Regiment's account of the first three days records the PAVN assault battalions going to ground at 400 metres under fire they could not locate [X-004]. It held for twelve days, as it held historically [H76]. The ammunition that might have let it hold longer had gone in March, in the divisional hoards at Hue, Da Nang and Qui Nhon that were abandoned when I Corps and the coast collapsed: about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm and most of the match 7.62mm in the country, captured intact (F004-15) [X-004].

The PAVN changed its approach, bypassed and isolated the town, and on 19 April the Joint General Staff ordered the withdrawal; Xuan Loc was abandoned on 21 April, and the 3rd Battalion of the 1st Airborne Brigade was destroyed in the withdrawal at Suoi Ca [H76]. The PAVN claimed 2,036 ARVN killed or wounded and 2,731 captured and admitted 460 killed and 1,428 wounded; Americans estimated about 5,000 PAVN casualties and 37 tanks destroyed [H76]. I do not adjust these figures; the squad weapons raised the price of each assault and the PAVN answered, as it did historically, by not making more of them [X-004]. On 17 April Phnom Penh fell to the Khmer Rouge [H]. Xuan Loc was the ARVN's last stand, and showed what a well-led division could still do; it bought twelve days.
