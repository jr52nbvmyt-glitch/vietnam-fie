## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I took over on the first day of 1964 was being lost in the hamlets, and the men losing it were carrying the wrong rifles. That second fact was never the first cause of anything, and I do not pretend now that it was. But it was true, and the soldiers knew it before the generals did. A Popular Forces platoon behind a mud wall in Long An with eight-round Garands and a couple of tired BARs was facing a local-force company with automatic weapons, and every man in the platoon understood the arithmetic of the first thirty seconds. In this war that arithmetic changed on the first day, in the same week as the land decree, the police decree and the first flights of the aircraft that see the enemy's rear. Those four things together are the war I now command, and this memorandum is about the last of them and the least of them.

The first months of 1964 were spent on ranges. The ships came into the Khanh Hoi wharves from January with carbines, sights, squad guns and marksman rifles, and the issue went out under one rule: no new weapon leaves the depot until the old one comes in, and no carbine goes to a post until it has been zeroed with the man who will carry it. The Delta drew first, because the Delta was where the war was being lost, and the RF and PF of Long An and Dinh Tuong were on the ranges in January, beside the titling teams' first surveyors and the first constables of the rural police. Khanh's coup at the end of the month cost us nine days and a fifth of the training, as every coup cost something; companies went back to garrison the province towns with carbines they had not zeroed. By the summer about 310,000 carbines were in Vietnamese hands. The old Garands and carbines came back through the gate, and about a third of them went out again, over my objection, to the police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia, who were not covered and wanted something. I lost that argument and was right to lose it gracefully. I was also right on the substance, and the Front proved it within a year.

The first thing the rifles did was let the outposts hold. In the held belt, where the police posts were settling beside Popular Forces platoons and the titles were being issued under their protection, a post with its platoon now held about nine times in ten when it was attacked, where the programme's design had promised four in five. The night at Long Huu in November 1964, when a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund and broke on thirty-round magazines that did not pause to reload, was told and retold in every district office in Kien Hoa. It is the strongest positive effect of everything in this memorandum, and it is invisible in every statistic this headquarters kept, because a post that is not overrun produces no report. I record it here at its full weight. The men who hold the hamlets at night were not outgunned for the five years in which, historically, the countryside was lost to men who outgunned them.

The second thing the rifles did was teach the Front where else to go. It learned in a season, faster than I had planned for. From December 1964 its district companies stopped assaulting posts that had their platoons and went for everything that did not: posts settled ahead of security, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps, and the militia carrying the Garands we had handed in. Against the posts that held it brought the 60mm mortar from three hundred metres and the mine on the path to the morning market. Two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts. And every post that did fall, every deserter who walked away with his weapon, and every battalion ambushed in the summer gave the Front our carbines, between ten and thirty thousand of them by the end of 1965. They were the best rifles any guerrilla in the country carried, and they fired our ammunition.

The summer of 1965 came as it came historically, because the rifles did not change the one thing that decided it: the enemy chose where to fight. At Binh Gia at the turn of the year the 271st Regiment took the village and waited on the road for the general reserve, and the general reserve drove down it. The 4th Marine Battalion was ambushed in the rubber at thirty metres as it was historically, and its commander died in the first minutes as he did historically. The difference was the next quarter of an hour. Three squad guns to a platoon put a base of fire into the trees, and the companies closed into perimeters instead of being destroyed in the order they had marched. About 65 Marines died instead of 122, and the battalion was in the line again in February. At Ba Gia in May the 1st Battalion, 51st Infantry was cut in two instead of wiped out, and at Dong Xoai in June about 270 of the 7th Airborne Battalion's 470 men fought their way back from Thuan Loi, where historically about 150 came. These were still defeats. The Viet Cong held the field at each, and the lesson Hanoi drew, that it could choose the field and the ARVN would keep driving to it, was the right lesson whatever our soldiers carried. Westmoreland's June request rested on the PAVN regiments coming into the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and the July decision was taken on the same day, for the same reasons, as it was historically. I did not expect the rifles to change it and they did not.

The Americans came with the same weapons, and for them the change was smaller and fairly exact. At X-Ray the perimeter held as it held historically, and at Albany the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry was ambushed in the elephant grass and lost about 125 men instead of 155. The head of the column formed a perimeter in minutes behind its squad guns; the companies strung out behind it could not see each other at twenty metres, and a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends. That sentence is the honest limit of this whole provision in the main-force war. The B3 Front's study of the Ia Drang noted that the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and concluded that the belt-grabbing assault must be made closer still, at night, or not at all. It was made closer.

The Marines on 881 South in May 1967 did not die beside rifles they were trying to clear. In this war there was no M16 crisis, no subcommittee, no letters home about a rifle that jammed, and about thirty fewer dead in the Hill Fights. Over the whole war I believe the rifles saved between about 1,000 and 2,500 American lives, and between 10,000 and 25,000 South Vietnamese, though I sign those estimates knowing they are estimates. They are a few percent of what this war cost. They are also real men, and I do not think a commander who has signed the letters is entitled to call that small.

The rearmament made its own trouble in Phase II, in ways that show how a good thing moves a bottleneck rather than removing it. The Regional Forces of the western Delta were good enough by 1966 that the IV Corps headquarters borrowed thirty-four of their companies for a season against the U Minh. They fought well, and the forty-one outposts of Chuong Thien that had depended on them for a reaction force lost twenty-three posts in four months. The Front had not needed to plan anything; it had watched the boats carry the companies away. The province chiefs' returns came to Saigon, and when CORDS was formed in 1967 it carried a rule that this war wrote from its own losses: the Regional Force company belongs to its province, and the corps borrows it for thirty days with the province chief's consent or not at all. In the same year the Commission convicted the commander of the 9th Division's ammunition point at Sa Dec and two Cholon dealers, on evidence the war itself supplied: the lot numbers on cartridge cases picked up behind a Viet Cong firing position in the Plain of Reeds, matched against the issue ledgers the muster service had kept since 1964. A cartridge case on a battlefield could now put an officer in prison. The leak of ammunition to the Front did not stop; it moved from the depots to the units in the field.

Hanoi decided on the General Offensive in the summer and autumn of 1967 for Hanoi's reasons, and in this war one of those reasons destroyed a piece of evidence it needed. A study group of the General Staff's combat research department had concluded in the spring, from three years of after-action reports, that the southern army's infantry and territorials no longer broke in close combat and that the local forces could not take district towns from them without main-force support. Its senior officers were arrested in December in the second wave of the Revisionist Anti-Party purge, for whose staff they served on rather than for what they had written, and the study went into the files with them. The offensive was planned, as it was historically, on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve on contact.

It did not dissolve historically, and here it had been better armed than its attackers for four years. Tet came on the same night, against the same towns, with the same forces; the cities were the cities, and Hue was a month of street fighting in the rain whatever our squads carried. The Marines' marksmen killed machine gunners in the windows of the treasury from two hundred metres, and the 106s, the Ontos and men going through walls took the blocks, as they did historically. The difference was in the district towns, which the first wave attacked with local forces against an RF company or two and a PF platoon behind a compound wall. About a third of those attacks never got inside the wall. At Cai Lay the 311th RF Company held the district compound all night against a battalion that had planned to be through the town by four in the morning. With the land programme's Popular Forces reporting the columns at Can Tho and the Special Branch's files in the Delta towns, it made the first wave in IV Corps a shorter and less destructive thing than it was historically. In Washington it changed nothing. The President's decision of 31 March was taken on the same day for the same reasons, because the American clock was spent by the progress campaign's collapse and by the casualty lists, and the rifles had moved neither by enough to show.

After Tet the gap the rifles had opened over history began to close from the other side. The historical ARVN received the M16 in 1968 and its territorials in 1969, and from then on the difference between this army and that one was the squad gun, the marksman and four years' practice with both. The mobilization of June 1968 armed its new territorials from stock within a month, and found that the constraint on the expansion was sergeants, not rifles. Through Vietnamization, the incursion, Lam Son 719 and the Easter Offensive, the rifles are present and do not decide anything. Quang Tri still fell in May 1972 because its defence failed at the top; An Loc and Kontum still held because the B-52s held them. A belt-fed gun at An Loc made each assault on the perimeter cost the PAVN more, and the PAVN's answer was its artillery, which no rifle touched.

The rifles came back into the story at the end, and in the wrong direction. The army that faced the ration of 1974 had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the abundance of American ammunition, four years longer than the historical one. When the Joint General Staff cut the squad to its basic load for a month, the squad gun went silent first: two hundred rounds a gun for a month, two minutes of fire. Battalions that had learned to break contacts with volume avoided contacts they could not break, and the marksmen's rifles went into the arms rooms for want of match ammunition. The divisions of I and II Corps hoarded linked 5.56mm against orders, about nine million rounds by the end of the year, and in March 1975 the hoards were lost intact at Hue, Da Nang and Qui Nhon, while the 18th Division at Xuan Loc fought on what Long Binh could still send. It held for twelve days, as it did historically. Its marksmen made the 341st Division's battalions go to ground at four hundred metres in the rubber east of the town. The PAVN bypassed it, as it did historically.

Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. Rung (a), collapse, on the historical day. I have now written four memoranda in which the Republic became a better state, a better-policed state, a better-informed command and now a better-armed army, and in which it fell on the same day. I do not find that discouraging so much as clarifying. Each of these provisions fixed something real. None of them touched the three things that decided the end: Hanoi's will and its sanctuaries, the American clock, and the Republic's dependence on an American supply line that Congress could close. The rifles made the war cheaper in lives on our side, in the hamlets above all, and they did it from the first week. They did not decide when the enemy fought, and they deepened the dependence that killed the Republic in the end.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

Phase I is where the provision does its most important work, and the work is at the outpost wall. The territorials were historically the most outgunned men in the country, and from January 1964 they are not; the post with its platoon now holds about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2). That is the strongest positive finding of Part 2 made true on the ground (F004-16), and it lands where the earlier provisions had prepared the ground for it. The land programme had given the Popular Forces of the titled villages something of their own to guard; the police programme had put a post beside them with a radio and a rule that no post was settled without its platoon. The rifle is what lets the platoon win the first thirty seconds. Each of the three was worth less without the others, and Long Huu on the night of 17–18 November 1964 is the case that shows all three at once: a titled village, a post with its platoon, a reaction force by water from Ham Long within two hours, and a local-force company that could not get over the bund.

The bottleneck moved exactly as Part 2 said it would (F004-3). Once the platoon could hold the wall, what decided the post was whether the platoon was in it after dark, whether its ammunition had come up the canal, and whether the reaction force came. The posts that fell in 1964–65 fell on those three, not on firepower.

In the main-force war the provision changed the price of defeat and not the fact of it (F004-4). Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai were all chosen by the enemy, all fought on ground he had prepared, and all lost. The squad gun's base of fire in the first minute of an ambush (EC004-4) is what turned the destruction of the 4th Marine Battalion into a mauling, the destruction of the 1st Battalion, 51st into a broken battalion, and 150 survivors of the 7th Airborne into about 270. The general reserve entered the summer of 1965 weakened and whole. That is a real gain for the RVNAF and changes nothing in Washington.

The opportunity cost was paid in the coup year (F004-5). About a fifth of the first half-year's training was lost to Khanh's coup and its aftermath, in companies that went back to their posts with carbines they had not zeroed, and the Delta's advisers spent the summer finding them.

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front's answer was quick and correct (F004-11). Its province committee in Long An reported the new weapons in February 1964, ordered one captured for study, and had it in March; by December its district companies had stopped assaulting posts with platoons and turned to everything the rearmament did not cover. The displacement was to the constables, the CIDG camps and the militia, and against the posts that held, to the mortar and the mine (F004-1, F004-2). The cost to the Front of this adaptation was small in men and real in time: the mortar and the mine do not take a post, and a post that is not taken still stands in the morning with its census and its titles.

What the Front got wrong for a year was the political meaning. Its cadres called the rifles a "trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty" and expected the territorials to sell them; some did, and more did not. What it got right, from the first month, was that every one of the new carbines needed our ammunition, and it began buying 5.56mm in Cholon in 1965.

### Causal chains

**C004-1** [months] The Popular Forces platoons beside the police posts draw modern carbines in the first months of 1964 (EV004-1) → posts with their platoon hold about nine attacks in ten, and the Front stops assaulting them (EV002-2) → the Front's district companies turn to the targets the rearmament left out: constables on the road, the CIDG camps, and the hamlet militia carrying the Garands the RVNAF had handed in → the old weapons that the Directorate insisted on issuing to the militia, rather than crating, become the Front's principal source of rifles in the held belt in 1965, so that the Republic's decision about what to do with its obsolete guns matters more to the guerrilla war in the Delta than the issue of the new ones.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits a general reserve that has not lost a battalion outright, territorials that are worth borrowing, a Front that has learned to go round the rearmed posts, and between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines in the Front's local forces, each one a customer for our ammunition. Phase III inherits district-town garrisons that have held their walls for four years. Phase VI inherits an army that has never fought without abundant ammunition, from its first year.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is the phase where the provision matters least to the outcome and most to the men in the American battalions, and the reason is the one Part 2 gave: in the main-force war the constraint is finding the enemy and his choice of battle, and the rifles are the hammer, not the anvil (F004-4). Albany is the clearest case. The 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry had two belt-fed guns in every squad, and about thirty more of its men lived than lived historically, because the head of the column formed a perimeter behind them in minutes. The companies behind it in the elephant grass were in knots that could not see each other at twenty metres, and the squad gun did nothing for them that an M16 would not have done. The PAVN chose the ground, the hour and the range, as Colonel Nguyen Huu An meant to.

The erasure of the 1967 M16 crisis is the cleanest gain of the phase (F004-6, F004-13). On 881 South the Marines' carbines fouled in the red mud and were cleaned at night like every other weapon; nobody died trying to clear one. The bunkers were taken as they were historically, by 2,000-pound bombs, 8-inch howitzers and the satchel charge, with the marksmen killing machine gunners in the apertures from 400–500 metres (EC004-5). About thirty fewer Marines died. No subcommittee sat. It is a small change in the war and a large one in the lives of three rifle companies.

The rearmament also produced the phase's two new events, and both are second-order. The IV Corps headquarters borrowed thirty-four rearmed Regional Force companies for its U Minh season in 1966, and the districts they left lost twenty-three outposts in four months (EV004-2). That is the perverse effect of Part 2 (F004-8) shown as a dated, named sequence: the RF were worth borrowing because the rifles had made them good, and the hamlets paid for the borrowing because the rifles had not made the Popular Forces into a reaction force. The rule CORDS wrote in 1967, that the RF company belongs to its province, is this war's answer. The second is the Commission's ammunition case of 1966 (EV004-3), the open channel of Part 2 (F004-14) made concrete: the muster roll that counted carbines against men also counted cartridge lots against units, and a handful of cases from the Plain of Reeds led to the ammunition point at Sa Dec.

### Enemy response and initiative

The PAVN's answer in the field was the one it gave historically, made closer. The B3 Front's study of the Ia Drang concluded that the belt-grabbing assault must be made from nearer still, at night, or not at all, and through 1966–67 the PAVN in the Highlands fought more of its battles at night and from closer than it did historically (F004-10). The cost of that adaptation was paid in the tempo of its attacks; the benefit was that the squad gun's advantage in a daylight meeting engagement was rarely offered to us. The Front in the Delta paid more for unmarked Chinese 7.62mm than for our 5.56mm once it learned the lots could be traced.

<!-- initiative --> The enemy's decisive act of the phase was taken for reasons of his own. In December 1967 the second wave of the Revisionist Anti-Party purge arrested, among hundreds of others, the three senior officers of a General Staff study group that had concluded from three years of combat reports that the southern infantry and territorials no longer broke in close combat and that the local forces could not take district towns from them without main-force support (EV000-37). They were arrested because they served on Giap's staff, in a factional struggle in the Party that had nothing to do with rifles or with anything this command did. The purge was driven by Le Duan's and Le Duc Tho's determination to remove opposition to the general offensive and to the line it represented. Its effect was that the one piece of analysis in Hanoi that might have changed the plan for the district towns was buried, and the offensive went forward on a reading of the ARVN that was more wrong in this war than it was historically.

### Causal chains

**C004-2** [months] The Regional Force companies of the western Delta are rearmed in 1964 and fight well in 1965 (EV004-1) → the IV Corps headquarters forms them into mobile groups for a season against the U Minh in April 1966 (EV004-2) → the forty-one outposts and nine police posts of Chuong Thien that depended on them for a reaction force lose twenty-three posts in four months, and the Front takes about 300 carbines from them → the province chiefs' returns reach Saigon and CORDS, formed in May 1967, adopts the rule that a Regional Force company belongs to its province and is borrowed for thirty days with the province chief's consent or not at all (EV000-35): a better rifle in the RF produces, by way of its own misuse, an institutional rule about who commands the territorials.

**C004-3** [war-shaping] The rearmament makes the ARVN and the territorials steadier in close combat from 1964 (EV004-1, EV000-9) → a General Staff study group in Hanoi concludes in the spring of 1967 that the local forces cannot take defended district towns without main-force support → the study's authors are swept up in the December purge for unrelated factional reasons, and the Tet plan sends local forces alone against the district towns (EV000-37) → about a third of those attacks fail at the compound walls on 31 January 1968, and the Front's district-level local forces and cadres in the Delta are destroyed in the first night rather than over the year (EV000-42): Hanoi's purge, not our rifles, decides that the rifles' advantage is met head-on by the Front's least replaceable units. The chain begins in this phase and lands in Phase III.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits an ARVN that has fought with modern weapons for four years, a Regional Force bound to its provinces, an American army with no rifle scandal behind it, and a Tet plan that assumes the ARVN will dissolve. Phase IV inherits a Commission that can trace ammunition by its lot, and a Front that has learned to buy what cannot be traced.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet is where four years of rearmament were tested against the enemy's whole local-force structure in one night, and the result was the one Part 2 predicted for the outposts, scaled up (F004-16). The cities went as they went historically, because main-force battalions and PAVN regiments fighting block by block are not decided by the infantry's rifles. The district towns did not. The first wave in the districts was made by local-force battalions and companies against an RF company or two behind a compound wall, and about a third of those attacks never got inside. Cai Lay is the case: the 311th RF Company held the district compound against a battalion of the 261st through the night, and the battalion that had planned to be through the town by four was still in the market when the 7th Division arrived. Historically the territorials met Tet with Korean War weapons and the ARVN with the Garand, and the M16 airlift to the ARVN was one of the first decisions after it.

At Hue the provision lowered the cost and not the length (F004-4). The Marines' marksmen killed PAVN machine gunners in the windows of the treasury from two hundred metres; the 106mm recoilless rifles, the Ontos and the weather decided the pace. About forty fewer ARVN soldiers and thirty-five fewer Americans died there than historically.

The mobilization of June 1968 shows the bottleneck migrating once more (F004-3). There were rifles for every new territorial within the month. There were not sergeants, and a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before. The rifles had removed the constraint the historical expansion faced and exposed the one beneath it.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response at Tet was the plan he had already made, and it was the purge, not anything we did, that made it a plan against an ARVN he had misread (C004-3). After the first wave the Front's regional commands drew the right conclusion faster than Hanoi did: the May and August offensives in the Delta were made against outposts and towns with mortars and rockets from outside the wire, not with assault companies against the walls, and the Front's local forces in IV Corps spent the summer rebuilding rather than attacking (F004-2) [X-004]. Its rearming from our stocks went on; by the end of 1968 most of its local-force companies in the Delta carried at least some captured M4A1s, and it bought their ammunition in the towns.

### Causal chains

**C004-4** [months] The Directorate issues about a third of the old weapons the RVNAF handed in to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the militia in 1964, over this headquarters' objection (EV004-1) → those weapons become the Front's easiest source of rifles in the held belt in 1965 (C004-1) → after Tet, with the territorials armed from stock, the remaining Garands and carbines go to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the mobilization law (EV000-51) → the self-defence militia of 1968–69, historically armed with whatever the regular forces had discarded, carries in this war the same rifles that the Front had already learned to take from hamlet militia in 1965, so the PSDF begins its life as a known target with a known yield: the cost of a 1964 decision about surplus weapons is paid again by a new force four years later.

### Downstream

From 1969 the historical ARVN catches up. The M16 reaches the regular divisions in 1968 and the territorials in 1969, and the gap this provision opened over history narrows to the squad gun, the marksman and the practice, which is why Phase IV and Phase V are stated nulls. The PSDF begins as a target. The Front's local forces in the Delta, gutted at the district walls on the first night, have less to rebuild with than historically, and the government's move into the countryside in the autumn finds the districts already emptied of them.

### Clock

The provision's effect on the American clock in 1968 is real, small and invisible in the decision that mattered. About thirty-five fewer Americans died at Hue and fewer in the first minutes of every contact, and there was no M16 scandal in the summer of 1967 to add a story of official failure to the progress campaign's collapse (F004-13). None of it moved the decision of 31 March, which was taken on the same day for the same reasons: the reinforcement request, the progress campaign's failure, and 14,592 American dead by hostile action in the year on the historical course, a figure the rifles lower by a few hundred at most [X-004]. The clock runs on the same dates.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- null P5 -->
The Easter Offensive is decided by armour, artillery, B-52s and the quality of corps command, and the provision touches none of them. Quang Tri falls in May because its defence collapses at the top; An Loc and Kontum hold because the bombers hold them. A belt-fed gun in each squad at An Loc made every assault on the perimeter dearer, and the PAVN answered with 130mm guns, which no rifle reaches. By 1972 the historical ARVN carries the M16 and the M60, and the gap left is too narrow to change any battle's result.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

Phase VI is where the provision's counter-directional finding comes due (F004-15). The army that met the ration of January 1974 had been built for ten years, four longer than the historical one, around the belt-fed gun, the thirty-round magazine and the abundance of American ammunition. When the Joint General Staff cut each squad to its basic load for a month, the squad gun went silent first, and battalions that had learned their trade breaking contacts with volume began to avoid contacts they could not break. The historical ARVN had the same habit; this one had it deeper and had a weapon in every squad that ate ammunition faster. The rifles did not cause the shortage. They made the army feel it more.

The bottleneck migrated for the last time, and into the depots (F004-3). Divisions hoarded linked 5.56mm against the ration and against orders, about nine million rounds in I and II Corps by the end of 1974, and in March 1975 the hoards were lost intact with Hue, Da Nang and Qui Nhon. At Xuan Loc the 18th Division fought on what Long Binh could still send. Its marksmen made the 341st Division's battalions go to ground at four hundred metres east of the town, and it held for twelve days, as it held historically. The PAVN answered as it did historically, by going round.

The provision's positive effect does not vanish in this phase; it becomes irrelevant to the outcome. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc the Airborne's marksmen and squad guns held Hill 1062 against three night assaults with a belt a gun. They held, and the PAVN's unrationed 130mm guns bled them.

### Enemy response and initiative

The PAVN's response to a better-armed ARVN in 1974–75 was the one it had made since 1968: fight it with artillery and go round it. The strategic raids of 1974 were designed to grind down the ARVN's reserves and its stocks, and they worked because the PAVN's artillery was supplied and the ARVN's was not. In 1975 the PAVN captured the ARVN's carbines, squad guns, marksman rifles and the divisional hoards of ammunition in I and II Corps intact, and used some of them in the last weeks of April.

### Causal chains

**C004-5** [war-shaping] From 1964 the ARVN is built around the belt-fed squad gun and abundant American ammunition, four years longer than the historical army (EV004-1) → the ration of January 1974 silences the squad gun first and leaves battalions that cannot break contacts by volume avoiding them (EV000-89) → division commanders in I and II Corps hoard linked 5.56mm in their own ammunition points against the ration and against Joint General Staff orders, about nine million rounds by the end of 1974 → in March 1975 the hoards are abandoned intact at Hue, Da Nang and Qui Nhon in the collapse of I Corps and the coast, while the 18th Division at Xuan Loc fights on what the Long Binh depot can still send (EV000-96): hoarding against a shortage puts the reserve exactly where the collapse takes it.

### Downstream

There is no later phase. The war ends on 30 April 1975 with the Republic's rifles, squad guns, marksman rifles and about nine million rounds of its ammunition in the PAVN's hands.

### Clock

By 1974 the American clock that mattered was the appropriation, not the casualty list, and the provision bears on it in one way: an army built around the squad gun needs more ammunition for the same fighting, so the $700 million of fiscal 1975 bought a ration that this army felt more sharply than the historical one did (F004-15) [H72]. There were no American combat casualties left to affect. Congress did not cut the aid because of rifles, and would not have restored it for them.

## §4.H Historical comparison

The cumulative force now differs from history in four ways, and this memorandum adds the fourth. The land programme, the police and the provincial chiefs, and the aircraft that see the rear changed the war's interior and the honesty of its accounts; the rifles change the men at the outpost wall and the price of every close fight on our side.

Concretely, against history. The territorials are not outgunned from 1964 to 1969, the five years in which, historically, the countryside was lost, and in the held belt a post with its platoon holds about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2) [X-004]. The general reserve loses no battalion outright in 1965: the 4th Marine Battalion at Binh Gia loses about 65 killed instead of 122, about 280 of the 1st Battalion, 51st come back from Ba Gia, and about 270 of the 7th Airborne Battalion come back from Thuan Loi instead of about 150 [X-004]. At Albany about 30 more Americans live, and in the Hill Fights about 30 more Marines, none of them beside a jammed rifle; there is no M16 scandal and no congressional inquiry [X-004]. At Tet about a third of the attacks on the district towns fail at the walls, and the Front's district-level local forces in the Delta are destroyed in the first night. Over the war I put the lives saved at about 1,000–2,500 American and 10,000–25,000 South Vietnamese [M004]. That margin is substantial in human terms, a few percent of the war's cost, and almost all of it is in the years 1964–68 before the historical army caught up.

Against these gains: the Front took 10,000–30,000 modern carbines from us by the end of 1965 and fired our ammunition from them for the rest of the war [X-004]; the rearmed Regional Forces were borrowed by the corps in 1966 and twenty-three outposts fell for it; and the army that met the ration of 1974 was more dependent on abundant ammunition than the historical one, and lost its hoards with I Corps. Every main-force battle is fought on the enemy's terms as it was historically. Tet's verdict in Washington, the decision of 31 March, the withdrawal, the Easter Offensive, the Paris Agreement, the aid cuts and the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 all stand.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The end state is unchanged: rung (a), collapse, on the historical timeline. The Republic falls on 30 April 1975.

The cumulative force now makes the Republic a better state in the hamlets it holds at night than it ever was historically, and its soldiers better armed than their enemies from the first year. That is not enough to reach rung (b), and the reason is the same one the three earlier memoranda gave, seen from a fourth side. Rung (b) needs a Republic that holds while American forces are present and falls only after they leave. This Republic holds where it is present, and it falls on the historical day after the Americans leave, so the difference between (a) and (b) is not about whether it can fight. It is about whether the war ends differently while the Americans are there: whether Hanoi's will, its sanctuaries or the American clock move. None of the four provisions touches any of them. The rifles make the price of every close fight lower on our side and give the enemy no reason to fight differently, except closer and at night and with artillery. Rung (c), assisted viability, would need an RVNAF that defeats a conventional offensive with American air power and supply. In 1972 it did that historically, and in this war it does the same, no better. The rifles have no bearing on whether the supply continues after 1973.

The distance between (c) and (d) is still the whole war, and the rifles widen it slightly in one direction. An army that has never fought without abundant American ammunition is further from self-sufficiency, not closer, and the counter-directional finding of Part 2 holds in the record (F004-15).

No phase redraw is proposed. The shape of the war is unchanged; its interior is changed in Phases I–III.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- The men who hold the hamlets at night are not outgunned from 1964, five years before the historical rearmament of the territorials. In the held belt, posts with their platoon hold about nine attacks in ten, and the land and police programmes are protected by garrisons that win the first thirty seconds. This is the largest effect of the memorandum, and no statistic this headquarters kept shows it.
- The general reserve survives 1965 whole. Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are lost at about half their historical cost, and no battalion is wiped out.
- At Tet about a third of the attacks on the district towns fail at the compound walls, and the Front's district-level local forces in the Delta are destroyed in the first night.
- The 1967 M16 crisis never happens: no Marines die beside jammed rifles on 881 South, no subcommittee sits, and there are no letters home about a rifle that jams. Over the war about 1,000–2,500 American and 10,000–25,000 South Vietnamese lives are saved, almost all of them before 1969.
- The Commission can trace ammunition from a battlefield to a depot by its lot number, and in 1966 it convicts the men who sold it. CORDS's 1967 rule that a Regional Force company belongs to its province is written from this war's own losses.

### What has not changed

- The enemy still chooses when and where to fight. Every main-force battle in the record is still fought on his terms, and the PAVN's answer to better rifles is to fight closer, at night, and with artillery.
- Neither half of the problem of finding the enemy is touched. The main force under the canopy and the infrastructure in the hamlet are as hidden as before.
- The Front rearms from our stocks: 10,000–30,000 carbines by the end of 1965, and our ammunition bought in the towns for the rest of the war.
- Tet's verdict in Washington, the decision of 31 March, the withdrawal, the Easter Offensive, the Paris Agreement and the aid cuts all fall on their historical dates.
- The army is more dependent on American ammunition, not less. The ration of 1974 falls on it harder, and its hoards are lost with I Corps.
- Saigon falls on 30 April 1975. Rung (a).
