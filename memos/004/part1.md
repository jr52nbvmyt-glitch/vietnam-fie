*COMUSMACV to CINCPAC · Saigon*

This provision puts a modern rifle in the hands of every American and South Vietnamese soldier and every man of the Regional and Popular Forces, a marksman's rifle in every infantry squad, and a belt-fed light machine gun at the heart of every squad's fire. I have filed it as three provisions because it is three instruments with three different jobs: the M4A1 carbine with the improved upper receiver and the Aimpoint CompM5 sight, which arms the whole force (P004a); the Knight's Armament M110A2 7.62mm rifle, one to a squad (P004b); and the M249 light machine gun in place of the squad's BAR or automatic rifle (P004c). The message gives no date, so all three arrive on the campaign's opening day, 1 January 1964 [E: the message gives no date; the default arrival in CONFIG], and all three are 2026 configurations.

I have had to read six things in the message, and I set them down here so that nothing later rests on a reading the reader cannot see.

First, "M4A1 URG-1" is the Upper Receiver Group – Improved, written URG-I: the Geissele upper with a Daniel Defense 14.5-inch barrel that US Army special operations fields in place of its older carbines [S1] [S2]. "Comp5" is the Aimpoint CompM5 [S3]. Second, there is no American service weapon designated "M249B". I read it as the M249 light machine gun in its 2026 Army configuration — the product-improved gun with the shorter barrel and collapsible stock — and I note that the Army selected the M250 to replace it in April 2022, so this is the gun the Army is leaving, not the one it is adopting [S6]. Third, the provision leaves the optic for the marksman's rifle to me and puts the same optic on the machine gun. I have chosen the SIG Sauer TANGO6T 1-6x24mm, the scope the Army selected for its Squad Designated Marksman Rifle [S5]. A squad fights in this country at 15 metres in bamboo one week and at 500 metres across a paddy the next; a variable scope that is a red dot at 1x and a marksman's glass at 6x serves both, and a fixed high-power scope would be useless in the first. The rifle carries the 7.62mm reticle and the machine gun the 5.56mm reticle of the same scope [S5]. Fourth, "at squad level only" means what it says: the M60 machine guns in the platoon weapons squads and the company stay, and so do the M1919 guns in ARVN heavy-weapons units. Fifth, the Regional and Popular Forces receive the carbine but not the marksman's rifle or the machine gun, which go to "US and ARVN infantry"; I count the Vietnamese Marine Corps and the US Marine Corps as infantry, and I do not count the National Police and its Police Field Force, the CIDG irregulars, or the Korean and Australian contingents, who keep their own weapons. Sixth, the standing condition that the GPS and military satellite constellations exist changes nothing here. None of these weapons uses a satellite, and none of them gains anything from one.

These are the first provisions in this war that the soldier carries. Everything before them has worked on the countryside's institutions or looked down on it from the stratosphere [M001] [M002] [M003]. These work at the distance that decides most fights in this country, which is the distance across a hamlet path.

## §1.1 M4A1 carbine, Upper Receiver Group – Improved, with Aimpoint CompM5 — P004a

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The weapon is the M4A1 carbine in 5.56x45mm NATO, fully automatic [E: M4A1 fire-control group], fitted with the Upper Receiver Group – Improved. The upper is built by Geissele Automatics under a US Special Operations Command contract [S1]. It carries a Daniel Defense 14.5-inch barrel of government profile with a 1:7 twist, a mid-length direct-impingement gas system, a free-floating Mk16 13.5-inch rail with M-LOK slots, and a SureFire SF4P four-prong flash hider pinned and welded to the barrel [S2]. The gas port is slightly overbored and the gas system lengthened for reliability and a softer recoil impulse, and the barrel is built for the M855A1 round [S1]. The lower receiver is the standard M4A1 lower from the Army's producers, Colt and FN America [E: the URG-I is an upper receiver group fitted to existing M4A1 lowers]. I read the provision as including the folding back-up iron sights that every fielded M4A1 carries, because a carbine whose only sight runs on a battery is not a service weapon [E: standard M4A1 issue; the URG-I sources consulted do not list sights].

The sight is the Aimpoint CompM5, a 1x red-dot reflex sight with a 2 MOA dot, weighing 148 g without its mount [S3]. It runs for 50,000 hours, more than five years, of constant operation on one battery [S3].

The ammunition is M855A1, the Army's lead-free Enhanced Performance Round, fielded for the M4 and M4A1 in June 2010 [S10].

In-service dates: the M4A1 is the US Army's standard carbine in 2026 [E: Army-wide conversion of M4 to M4A1 from the mid-2010s; not from a source consulted]; the URG-I replaced the SOPMOD Block II carbine across Army special operations [S2], from about 2019 [E: SOCOM contract reported in 2018 per S1, fielding the following year]; the CompM5 is a current Aimpoint product [S3].

Quantity. The provision says every M16, M1 Garand, M1 and M2 carbine and M14 in US and ARVN hands is replaced, and that the Regional and Popular Forces are supplied too [U]. In January 1964 the Republic's armed forces number about 514,000: about 250,000 regulars, about 96,000 Regional Forces and about 168,000 Popular Forces [S9]. The Americans in the country are advisers and support troops, about 16,000–23,000 through 1964 [H]. The first increment is therefore about 600,000 carbines and sights [E: 514,000 RVNAF per S9, the US advisory force, and a 10% float for losses, repair and training]. Through the war the requirement grows with the forces. By 1971–72 the RVNAF stands at about 1,048,000, of whom about 532,000 are territorials [S9], and the US force peaks at about 540,000 in 1968–69 [H]. The cumulative issue is about 1.7–1.9 million carbines and sights by 1972 [E: RVNAF growth per S9, US strength at peak and rotation, and a loss and float rate of about 10% a year]. Not every man in an air force or a navy carries a rifle, and some of that number will sit in armouries; I have not reduced it, because the provision replaces rifles, and the rifles on the historical books are the number to replace.

Organisation in theater. The carbine replaces the individual rifle one for one: the rifleman, the fire-team leader, the grenadier's personal weapon where he carries one, the outpost sentry, the truck driver. In the US rifle company it replaces the M14 and later the M16 [H]. In the ARVN infantry battalion and the Ranger, Airborne and Marine battalions it replaces the M1 Garand and the carbines [H]. In a Regional Forces company and a Popular Forces platoon it replaces the M1 and M2 carbines and the Garands, which are what those forces carry in this war's present chapters [M002] [H]. Issue runs through the ARVN ordnance depots at Saigon and the corps logistics commands, and through US channels for the advisory force and, later, US units [E: the period's RVNAF ordnance system].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

A carbine needs no crew; the man who carried the Garand carries the M4A1. The provision adds no Vietnamese spaces at all.

It adds American spaces in three places [E: no fielding plan exists; built from the size of the issue]:

- **New-equipment training.** About 120 spaces in the first year: teams that train ARVN and RF/PF instructors at the training centres in zeroing the red dot, the M4A1's maintenance, and the drills of a fully automatic carbine; afterwards about 30 remain [E: one team of about eight for each of the corps training centres, the national training centre and the regional training centres, plus the US units' own training].
- **Repair.** About 80 small-arms and optics repair technicians in the depots, for sights, bolts and barrels [E: one repair section to each corps logistics command and one at the Saigon depot].
- **Advisers.** About 50 added small-arms advisers to the Regional and Popular Forces training system, which in 1964 has almost none [E: historically Mobile Advisory Teams for the territorials came only from 1967–68 per S7].

That is about 250 American spaces [D]. The troop ceiling is set at none in this exercise, so these spaces do not count against a number, but each is still a man trained, rotated and paid; this is a small bill.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The carbine fires M855A1 5.56x45mm from 30-round magazines, semi-automatic and fully automatic [E: M4A1 fire-control group; STANAG magazine]. The basic load is seven magazines, 210 rounds [E: US Army basic load for the rifleman].

M855A1 has a copper slug and a hardened steel penetrator nearly twice the weight of the older M855's, and it penetrates 3/8-inch steel plate at about 400 metres [S10]. What matters in this country is less the steel plate than the bamboo, the paddy dike, the hut wall of wattle and mud, and the log over a bunker. M855A1 gives more consistent wounding performance at range than its predecessor [S10]; it will not go through a properly built log-and-earth bunker roof, and nothing this rifle fires will.

The rail takes a light and an aiming laser, and the barrel takes a suppressor [S1]; the provision gives none of these, and the historical force has no weapon lights or lasers of this kind [H]. The M203 grenade launcher of the historical force will not mount on this rail without an adaptor [E: the M203 was designed to clamp to the M16 and M4 barrel and handguard; the free-float rail needs an interface]. That is a fault I will have to fix: from 1969 the US grenadier carries an M203 on an M16A1 historically [H], and either he keeps a separate M16A1 for it or the depot makes an interface. Until then the M79 continues to serve as it did [H].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The CompM5 is not a sensor and does not find anything. It is an aiming device. What it changes is how fast and how surely a man who has seen a target puts a round on it.

**Triple canopy.** In true jungle the eye sees 20–30 metres and the fight happens at 10–15 metres, because the vegetation absorbs fire at longer distances [S8]. At that distance the red dot's virtue is speed: both eyes open, the dot on the target, no alignment of front post and rear aperture. It sees nothing through foliage that the eye does not.

**Night.** The dot has four night-vision-compatible settings and six daylight settings [S3]. The night-vision settings serve only a man looking through a night-vision device, and in 1964 the force has none: the first Starlight scopes reach the theater only in the following years [H], and they mount on a rifle in place of a sight, not behind it. What the red dot does give at night is a reticle that can be seen at all: in moonlight, at dusk, under parachute flares and in a burning village, the iron sight's front post vanishes against a dark target and the red dot does not. This is the CompM5's real advantage at night, and it is the condition in which outposts are attacked. It is not night vision; the man still must see his target.

**Monsoon.** The sight is submersible to 45 metres [S3], so rain and immersion in a canal do not flood it. Water on the lens blurs the dot without hiding it [E: reflex-sight behaviour in rain]. The flip-up lens covers keep mud off the glass on patrol.

### D5 · Performance

The M4A1 with a 14.5-inch barrel has a maximum effective range of about 500 metres against a point target and about 600 metres against an area target [E: published Army figures for the 14.5-inch M4A1; no source consulted in this thread]. M855A1 leaves that barrel at about 2,900–2,970 feet per second [E: published Army figure for the M4; not from a source consulted]. The cyclic rate on automatic is about 700–970 rounds a minute [E: Army figure for the M4A1]. The 2 MOA dot covers about 6 centimetres at 100 metres and about 30 centimetres at 500 metres [D].

Weight: the carbine with sight, mount and an empty magazine is about 3.6 kg, and about 4.1 kg with a loaded magazine [E: standard M4A1 of about 3.0–3.1 kg, the heavier free-float rail of S1, the 148 g sight of S3 and a mount of about 80 g, and about 0.48 kg for a loaded 30-round magazine]. The overall length with the stock collapsed is about 76 centimetres [E: M4 family length with 14.5-inch barrel], and the stock adjusts in length.

That last point matters more here than it would in Germany. The Garand weighs about 4.3 kg empty and is about 110 centimetres long [H]; it was built for a man of the American average, and the average Vietnamese soldier is smaller and lighter [H]. The carbine he gets fits him.

Protection: none, it is a rifle. Reliability: the M4A1 with the URG-I's mid-length gas system and chrome-lined bore [E: standard on M4A1 barrels] is a weapon of 2026, built after fifty years of learning what killed the M16 in 1967. It does not have the unchromed chamber and the fouling powder that jammed M16s at Hill 881 in April and May 1967 [H].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The carbine has no communications. Its interoperability is the point.

The American adviser, the ARVN infantryman, the Ranger and the Popular Forces sentry carry the same carbine, fire the same round, load the same 30-round magazine, and clean it with the same kit. In a mixed fight, a man who runs dry can take a magazine from the man beside him whatever his army. The M249 of P004c accepts the same magazines [S6]. In 1964 the force carries .30-06 for the Garand and the BAR, .30 carbine for the M1 and M2 carbines, and 7.62mm NATO for the M14 and the M60 [H]; after this provision the individual and squad weapons fire one calibre, 5.56mm, and the marksman's rifle and the platoon machine gun fire 7.62mm NATO, which the system already carries for the M60.

Talking to ARVN is a matter of manuals and instructors, not of hardware. The operator's manual and the zeroing procedure must be in Vietnamese before the carbines arrive [E: a translation task of weeks, not months].

### D7 · Domain conditions

This is a ground weapon; the ground conditions bind.

**Paddy.** A man who falls in a paddy fills the muzzle and the action with mud. The carbine must be cleared and, if mud has entered the barrel, cleaned before firing [E: any rifle; a barrel obstruction can burst it]. The M4A1 is no worse here than the M16A1 and better than the BAR, whose open action took in water and mud [E: comparison of the designs]. The red dot's lens cover keeps the glass clean.

**Canopy.** The carbine is short and handy in bamboo and secondary growth, where the Garand's length catches on vines [E: length comparison per D5]. Engagements are at 10–30 metres and almost never beyond 100 metres [S8]; the carbine's 500-metre effective range is irrelevant there and the speed of its sight is not.

**Laterite and dust.** In the dry season the highland roads raise red dust that fouls actions [H]. A direct-impingement rifle fouls its bolt carrier with its own carbon; it needs daily cleaning in the field [E: standard M4 maintenance]. That is a discipline, and the 1967 M16 crisis showed how quickly a force that is not issued cleaning kits and not taught to use them loses its rifles [H].

**Monsoon and humidity.** The barrel is chrome-lined and the finishes are modern, so corrosion is slower than on the Garand's parkerised steel, but humidity and salt air near the coast still rust exposed steel within days if the rifle is not oiled [E: tropical maintenance experience]. The sight is sealed [S3].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

The weapon is light; the logistics are about ammunition, batteries and spares.

**Ammunition.** In the first year the issue to the RVNAF and the territorials needs about 300–400 million rounds of 5.56mm: a basic load of 210 rounds for each of about 600,000 men, about 300 rounds each to zero and qualify, and a combat expenditure on top [E: issue quantity per D1, the US basic load per D3, and a qualification course of the period]. At about 12.3 grams a round, 300 million rounds weigh about 3,700 tonnes before packing and about 5,000 tonnes packed [E: M855A1 cartridge weight; packing at about a third again]. Under the exercise's rules munitions are unlimited at their basing [E: Standing Rule A2, unlimited munitions at basing], but every tonne still comes through Saigon, Cam Ranh Bay, Da Nang, Qui Nhon or Nha Trang and moves by an interdicted road net to the districts. The upside is that one calibre, 5.56mm, replaces three in the individual and squad weapons, and it is lighter per round than any of them [D]. A man carries about 210 rounds of 5.56mm for the weight of about 100 rounds of .30-06 [E: cartridge weights of about 12 g and 26 g].

**Batteries.** One battery runs the sight for over five years of constant operation [S3]. If the doctrine is to leave the sight on, the battery is a once-a-war issue for most sights, and the battery logistics, which could have been a running sore, nearly vanish [D]. I will order it so. The sight takes a single AAA cell [E: Aimpoint CompM5 battery; the source consulted gives the life but not the type].

**Spares.** Bolts, extractors, springs, magazines and sights wear and are lost. Magazines are the largest line: at a loss rate of one magazine per man per year, the force needs about 600,000 a year [E: field loss rate of magazines; no source]. Sights are lost to damage and theft; at a loss of about 5–10% a year the force needs 30,000–60,000 replacement sights a year from 1965 [E: optics loss rate; no source].

**Specialists.** Small-arms repairers for bolts and barrels, and an optics repair line, per D2.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** The RVNAF can do first-echelon maintenance and depot assembly of parts. It cannot make carbines, sights, magazines or 5.56mm ammunition; the country has no small-arms or ammunition industry [H]. The whole supply comes from the United States for the length of the war, and stops when US aid stops.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

There is nothing to transfer: the carbine is issued to the RVNAF from the first day, and most of the 600,000 go to Vietnamese hands [D].

The training pipeline is short. A soldier who has fired a Garand learns to strip, clean, load and zero the M4A1 and to use the red dot in three to five days [E: US Army new-equipment training for the M4 and red-dot sights]. The instructors are trained first, at the national training centre at Quang Trung and the regional training centres, and they train the units [H]. The RF and PF are the slowest to train, because in 1964 their training is short and their training centres are few [H]; the carbine will reach their outposts faster than the instruction in how to keep it running, unless the advisers of D2 go with it.

The long-run question is not the rifle but the supply line. Historically the ARVN fought its last campaign in 1975 short of ammunition because American aid was cut [H]. A force rearmed with M4A1s depends on American 5.56mm for every round, exactly as the historical force depended on American ammunition for the M16 [H]. Rearming it with a better rifle does not make it more self-sufficient; it makes it neither more nor less dependent than it was.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The carbine performs to specification in every condition this country offers, provided three things: it is cleaned daily, it is zeroed, and it has ammunition.

It performs best in the conditions where most of this war's killing happens: the sudden contact at 10–30 metres [S8], the ambush, the defence of an outpost wall at night, the house-to-house fight in a hamlet. In each of these the side that puts aimed rounds on the other first wins, and the red dot, the 30-round magazine and the fully automatic setting all favour the man who has them.

Its performance collapses when it is not cleaned (as the M16 did in 1967 [H]), when the battery dies and the man has not been taught his back-up iron sights, when the zero has been knocked off by a fall and no one has checked it, and when the outpost runs out of ammunition before relief arrives. The last of these is not the rifle's fault but is the commonest way an outpost dies.

Tempo over weeks: a carbine fired on automatic heats quickly, and a 2026 M4A1 fired at sustained full-auto rates degrades its barrel [E: M4A1 heavier-profile barrels were adopted for this reason]. The URG-I's government-profile barrel [S2] tolerates aimed semi-automatic fire indefinitely and long automatic bursts poorly. A rifleman who sprays his basic load in the first minute of a fight is out of ammunition in the second; fire discipline must be taught with the carbine, and for the ARVN soldier who has carried an eight-round Garand it is a new idea.

### D11 · Failure modes

The carbine fails in small ways first and large ways second.

**Partial failures.** The sight's battery dies or the sight is broken, and the man must use his folding iron sights. The zero shifts and the man shoots off target without knowing it. A magazine lip bends and the carbine feeds badly. Each of these is one man's failure, not the unit's, and each is found by the squad leader who checks.

**Systemic failures.** Cleaning lapses across a unit, and its rifles jam in the first fight after the rain, as the M16s jammed in 1967 [H]. Ammunition does not reach the outposts because the road is cut or the district sells it. The RF/PF platoon is issued carbines and no one shows it how to zero them, so the red dot is a toy. These are failures of the system, and they are where the carbine will actually fail.

**The captured carbine.** When an outpost falls, the Front takes its carbines, its sights and its ammunition. The sight runs for five years on one battery [S3], so a captured CompM5 serves the Front for the rest of the war. The VC local forces have historically armed themselves in large part from captured weapons [H], and from 1964 the weapons they capture will be M4A1s rather than carbines and Garands. Every outpost that falls hands the enemy a better rifle than the one it lost in the historical war. That is the counter-direction of this provision, and it is not small: it is the local force and the guerrilla who live on captured weapons, and the main force who carry AK-47s from the North [S7].

**During the window.** A unit whose carbines are down falls back on what it has. If the provision has replaced every Garand and carbine, there is nothing to fall back on but the weapons of the unit next door. I will hold a stock of the old weapons in the depots for the first year, not to issue, but in case the new supply chain fails [E: a command decision, not a specification].

### D12 · Degradation curve

Month one: the carbines are new, zeroed under supervision, with a fresh battery in every sight, and the units that received them first are those with advisers. Serviceability is near 100% [E: new issue].

Month twelve: the carbines are mechanically robust and their serviceability falls slowly, to about 90–95% in ARVN regular units [E: a year of tropical field use with first-echelon maintenance]. What declines first is not the weapon but the training: the zero, the cleaning discipline, the fire control. Units that have rotated men lose the ones the instructors trained; replacements arrive at the battalion having fired the carbine for three days at Quang Trung, and the squad leader must teach the rest. In the territorials, where training is thinner and advisory presence lighter in 1964–66 [S7], the carbine degrades fastest: by month twelve a significant fraction of RF/PF sights will be lost, sold, broken or out of zero [E: the RF/PF's historical maintenance and supply record; no number is offered]. Spares are deep because the supply line is American and the munitions are unlimited at basing [E: Standing Rule A2, unlimited munitions at basing]; what limits the force is distribution and command honesty, not production.

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The carbine displaces five weapons, and the margin is different against each.

- **M1 Garand.** Eight rounds in an en bloc clip, about 4.3 kg empty, semi-automatic only, iron sights [H]. The M4A1 carries 30 rounds [E: standard magazine], about 3.75 times as many before reloading [D], weighs about 0.7 kg less [D], fires automatic, and is about a third shorter [D]. The Garand's .30-06 round hits harder and penetrates more at long range [H]; in this country that advantage almost never arises [S8].
- **M1 and M2 carbines.** Light and handy, 15- and 30-round magazines, the M2 automatic [H]. The .30 carbine round has an effective range of about 200–300 metres and poor penetration of cover [H]. The M4A1 roughly doubles the effective range [D] and penetrates far better [S10], at a weight about a kilogram more [E: M2 carbine about 2.5 kg].
- **M14.** Twenty rounds of 7.62mm NATO, about 4.5 kg empty, long, hard to control on automatic [H]. The M4A1 is about a kilogram lighter [D], carries half as many rounds again [D], and a man carries about twice as many rounds for the same weight [E: cartridge weights of about 12 g for 5.56mm and 25 g for 7.62mm].
- **M16A1.** The M4A1's direct ancestor: same calibre, 20-round magazines in the early years, iron sights, and the 1967 reliability crisis [H]. The margin over the M16A1 is the reliability of a mature design, the red dot, M855A1 and a 30-round magazine; on the US side this is an improvement, not a transformation.
- **The enemy's AK-47.** Thirty rounds of 7.62x39mm, automatic, iron sights, robust, introduced to the enemy's forces in the South from 1964 [S7]. Against it the Garand and the carbine were outgunned, and the ARVN knew it [S7]. The M4A1 with a red dot is not outgunned by the AK: it is lighter, its sight is faster to use, and at 100 metres and beyond it is the more accurate weapon [E: comparison of the designs].

The size of the margin that matters is the number of hits in the first five seconds of a contact at 10–50 metres [S8]. I estimate that a trained man with a red dot gets about a quarter to a half more hits in those seconds than the same man with iron sights, and more in failing light [E: the general finding of military trials of red-dot against iron sights, not quantified in a source consulted]. Against the Garand-armed ARVN squad of 1964, an M4A1-armed squad puts out about three to four times the volume of fire before reloading [D].

The historical benchmark for what this means is the M16 issue of 1967–68: ARVN units with M16s were much more aggressive, and enemy casualties were higher opposite them than opposite units without it [S7]. This provision delivers that change from 1964, about four years earlier for the ARVN infantry and about five years earlier for the territorials [S7].

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P004a -->
**It does not find the enemy.** A rifle acts only in a contact, and the enemy still chooses most contacts [E: the historical record of who initiated engagements]; the main force under the canopy and the VCI inside the hamlet are exactly as hard to find as before. **It does not make a unit go out at night**, stay at its post after dark, or accept a fight its commander would avoid: whether the Popular Forces platoon sleeps in the hamlet or in the district town is a matter of leadership and pay. **It is not night vision**: the red dot can be seen in the dark, but the target must be seen too, and the force has no night-vision devices in 1964. **It does not outrange the enemy in the jungle**, where fights are at 10–30 metres. **It does not break a bunker.** **It does not make the RVNAF more self-sufficient**: every round is American. **It does not arm the National Police, the Police Field Force, the CIDG or the allied contingents.** Every outpost that falls gives the Front modern carbines, sights that run five years on a battery, and 5.56mm.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P004a -->
It denies the main force and the local force the firepower advantage that the AK-47 gave them over the ARVN, the Regional Forces and the Popular Forces from 1964 until those forces received the M16 in 1968–69. It denies the Front's propaganda the outgunned government soldier, and it denies the attacking company the outpost whose garrison has eight-round Garands and carbines.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P004a -->
This is a real improvement, concentrated where the war is decided: at night, at the wall of an outpost, in the first seconds of a contact. For the American forces it is an improvement and no more, because the M16A1 they historically carry is the same calibre and its 1967 crisis was fixable. For the ARVN infantry it closes, four years early, the firepower gap that history recorded and that ARVN soldiers felt. For the Regional and Popular Forces, who historically fought five years with carbines against AK-47s, it is the largest change: the territorials are the men who hold the hamlets at night, and an outpost that is not outgunned holds longer. What it does not do is just as plain. It does not find anyone, does not make an absent platoon present, does not touch the VCI, and every outpost lost arms the enemy with it. It improves the force inside its rung; I do not expect it to move the rung by itself.

## §1.2 Knight's Armament M110A2 rifle with SIG Sauer TANGO6T 1-6x24mm, one per squad — P004b

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The weapon is the M110A2 7.62x51mm semi-automatic rifle made by Knight's Armament Company of Titusville, Florida, the newest version of the M110 semi-automatic sniper system and a descendant of the SR-25 [S4]. It has a 20-inch free-floated match barrel, an upgraded integral suppressor, a 12-inch M-LOK handguard, a collapsible stock, ambidextrous controls and a more efficient gas system than its predecessor [S4]. Knight's Armament supplied the original M110 to the Army from 2007 to 2013; the M110A2 was shown in Army configuration in October 2021, was used at the Best Ranger Competition in April 2022, and can be bought by the Army under an open contract at Picatinny [S4]. It is a real rifle available to the Army, not a standard Army issue: the Army's squad marksman rifle is the Heckler & Koch M110A1 [S4].

The scope is the SIG Sauer TANGO6T 1-6x24mm with a 30 mm tube, first focal plane, the 762 Extended Range reticle in 0.2 mrad units, and illumination with nine daylight and two night-vision settings [S5]. The Army selected the TANGO6T for its Squad Designated Marksman Rifle and for Special Forces [S5]. I chose it for the reasons in the opening paragraphs: at 1x it is a red-dot sight for the jungle; at 6x it is a marksman's scope for the paddy.

The ammunition is 7.62mm NATO match and enhanced-performance ball: M118LR long-range match and M80A1 [E: the Army's standard 7.62mm loadings for the M110 family; not from a source consulted].

Quantity. The provision gives one rifle per squad in US and ARVN infantry [U]. In 1964 the ARVN has about 220,000 men [S9], and its infantry, Ranger, Airborne and Marine battalions number about 120 [E: about 9 divisions of about 9 battalions, plus about 20 Ranger, 5 Airborne and 4 Marine battalions, rounded]. At about 36 rifle squads per battalion that is about 4,300 squads [D]. With a float, the first increment is about 5,000 rifles and scopes [E: 4,300 squads, the US advisory force's few and 10% float]. By 1972 the ARVN has about 180 such battalions [E: ARVN growth to about 410,000 per S9 and the period's battalion count], and US Army and Marine manoeuvre battalions peaked at about 112 in 1968–69 [H]. The cumulative issue is about 13,000 rifles and scopes by 1972 [E: about 6,500 ARVN squads, about 4,000 US squads at peak, losses and float].

Organisation. One rifle to each rifle squad, carried by a designated marksman selected from the squad and trained at the training centres [U]. In the US Army squad he is one of the riflemen; in the Marine squad of three fire teams he is one of the fire-team members; in the ARVN squad he takes one of the rifle places [E: squad organisations of the period].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

One man per rifle, a member of the squad already counted. No Vietnamese spaces are added.

American spaces: about 60 marksmanship instructors in teams at the training centres and with US units, and about 60 match-rifle and scope armourers at the depots [E: marksman training of the US Army period and a repair line for a precision rifle]. That is about 120 spaces [D]. With no troop ceiling set, they count against nothing but the training base.

The real personnel cost is not American spaces but Vietnamese marksmen. A squad marksman is chosen for eyesight, steadiness and judgment, and trained for two to four weeks [E: US Army squad designated marksman courses of the 2020s]. Each squad gives up a good man to the role.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The rifle fires 7.62x51mm semi-automatically from a 20-round box magazine [E: SR-25 pattern magazine]. The marksman's load is about 100–140 rounds [E: five to seven magazines]. The rounds are M118LR for precision fire at range and M80A1 for general use [E: Army 7.62mm loadings; not from a source consulted].

The integral suppressor does three things that matter here [S4]. It cuts the muzzle flash at night, so the marksman does not give his position away with every shot. It reduces the report, so the enemy has more trouble locating him in a fight. And it reduces the blast that kicks up dust and leaves from the muzzle. It does not make the rifle silent: a supersonic 7.62mm round cracks as it passes [E: physics of supersonic ammunition].

The handguard takes a bipod [S4]; I will issue one with every rifle [E: a command decision].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The TANGO6T is an aiming device with magnification; it extends what the marksman sees.

**Triple canopy.** Under canopy the eye sees 20–30 metres [S8], and no magnification helps through leaves. At 1x the scope works like a red-dot sight with a field of view of about 106 feet at 100 yards [S5]. The marksman under canopy is a rifleman with a heavier rifle.

**Open ground.** In the paddy country, the coastal plain, the Delta, the hill country around Khe Sanh and the DMZ, the forest edge seen across a paddy, a hamlet's treeline seen from a dike, the 6x setting gives a field of view of about 21–22 feet at 100 yards [S5] and lets the marksman see a man's face, his weapon and his movement at 400–600 metres [E: 6x magnification]. That is where the rifle earns its weight. Less than 20% of the country is true primary jungle [S8], and a great deal of ARVN fighting happens in the Delta and the coastal plain [H].

**Night.** The illumination has two night-vision settings [S5], of no use to a force without night-vision devices. The illuminated centre helps in twilight and under flares, as the red dot does on the carbine. The 24mm objective lens gathers little light at 6x [E: exit pupil of 4 mm at 6x]; the scope does not make dusk into day. A suppressed marksman with a scope is still a capable shooter in moonlight at 100–200 metres [E: scope and moonlight].

**Monsoon.** The scope is waterproof to IPX-8, 20 metres [S5]. In heavy rain the 6x image is degraded by water on the lens and by the rain itself; the marksman's range in a monsoon downpour is set by what he can see, which may be 100–200 metres [E: visibility in tropical rain].

### D5 · Performance

The rifle is accurate enough for precision fire on point targets past 1,000 metres in trained hands [S4]. Its practical range for a squad marksman, not a sniper, is about 600–800 metres against a man [E: Army squad designated marksman employment]. The rifle weighs 10.5 lb, about 4.8 kg, in its default configuration [S4]; with the scope of about 626 g [S5], a mount, a bipod and a loaded magazine, it weighs about 6.5 kg [E: component weights]. It is 39.75–43.5 inches long, about 101–110 centimetres, depending on the stock [S4].

That is a heavy rifle for a man who weighs 55 kilograms and carries everything else a soldier carries [E: the average ARVN soldier's weight]. The marksman will be chosen partly for strength.

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The rifle has no communications. Its 7.62mm NATO ammunition is the same calibre as the M60 machine gun's, which the American and later ARVN platoons carry [H] [S7], though match ammunition is a separate line. The scope is the same model as the M249's [U], with a different reticle [S5]; the armourers repair one model.

The interface with ARVN is the training of marksmen and of squad leaders in using them. A squad leader who has never had a marksman will use him as a rifleman. The doctrine — that the marksman takes the enemy's machine gunner, his radio operator and his leaders at the ranges the rest of the squad cannot reach — must be taught in Vietnamese by instructors who have done it [E: a training plan].

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Paddy.** This is the rifle's best terrain: open, flat, with long sightlines from the dikes to the treelines [E: terrain]. A marksman on a dike or in a hamlet's edge commands a paddy for 500–600 metres in daylight [E: D4 and D5].

**Canopy.** Its worst terrain: the rifle is long and heavy, the scope adds nothing at 1x that a carbine's red dot does not, and the 20-round magazine is smaller than the carbine's 30 [E: standard magazines].

**Hills.** In the hills of Quang Tri, around Khe Sanh and along the DMZ, where the ground is broken by elephant grass and bare ridges, the rifle is useful at 300–700 metres [E: terrain].

**Firebase and outpost defence.** From a firebase perimeter or a hamlet outpost's bunker, a marksman covers the approaches and kills the enemy's sappers and leaders as they form for the assault. Under flare light, at 100–300 metres, this is where the suppressor and the scope combine [E: D3, D4].

**Monsoon.** The suppressor and the barrel need cleaning after heavy rain; the scope is sealed [S5].

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Ammunition.** About 5,000 rifles in 1964 [E: D1], each needing about 100–140 rounds as a basic load and about 500–1,000 rounds a year for training [E: marksman training], is 3–6 million rounds of 7.62mm in the first year [D], of which about half is match [E: training split]. That is about 100–200 tonnes [E: about 25 g per cartridge]. It is a small line, but it is a separate line, and match ammunition must not be confused with ball in the depots [E: ammunition management].

**Spares.** Barrels wear in the thousands of rounds, suppressors need cleaning and occasional replacement, and scopes are damaged and lost [E: precision-rifle maintenance]. A match rifle and its scope need a more skilled armourer than a carbine does; the depot armourers of D2 are the specialists.

**Batteries.** The scope takes one CR2032 [S5]. If the battery dies, the reticle is still etched on the glass [E: the TANGO6T's first-focal-plane reticles are etched; illumination is an addition], so a dead battery does not blind the scope.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** Everything: the rifles, the scopes, the match ammunition, the suppressors.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The rifle is issued to ARVN squads from the first day.

The training pipeline is the binding constraint. A marksman needs two to four weeks of training [E: D2], and the ARVN has no marksmanship school in 1964 [E: no such school appears in the historical order of battle]. The first increment of instructors will be trained by the American instructor teams of D2, and the first ARVN marksmen will come out of a school at Quang Trung or Duc My about three months after the rifles arrive [E: instructor training and a first course]. The rifles will sit in armouries or be used as heavy rifles until then. By the end of 1964 a majority of ARVN battalions will have trained marksmen in most squads [E: throughput of about 400–600 men a month across the training centres].

If the ARVN is left alone with them after the Americans leave, the rifles will keep working longest of anything in this provision: there are few of them, they are valued, and the marksmen are men the squad has chosen. The match ammunition and the spare barrels will run out with the aid.

### D10 · Employment envelope

The rifle performs to specification in daylight, in dry weather, in open ground, against an enemy who exposes himself at 200–800 metres, in the hands of a man trained to use it, with a squad leader who knows what it is for [E: D4–D9]. Those conditions are met on most days in the Delta and the coastal plain, and on few days in the highland forest.

Its performance collapses under canopy, where it is a heavy rifle with a small magazine; in heavy rain, where the marksman sees little; at night without illumination; and in the hands of a man chosen by favour or a squad leader who uses him as a rifleman. In a meeting engagement at 15 metres, the marksman is at a disadvantage against the AK-armed infantryman [E: a 20-round rifle of about 6.5 kg against a 30-round automatic].

Over weeks, the rifle tolerates continuous use; it does not wear fast at marksman rates of fire [E: a few hundred rounds a month]. The marksman's skill decays without practice.

### D11 · Failure modes

**The marksman is killed.** He is the squad's most valuable shooter and, when he is seen, its most obvious target: a scoped rifle is distinctive [E: in every war snipers and marksmen are targeted]. The squad loses its long-range fire and must find another man to train.

**The scope is knocked out of zero.** A fall, a blow, a jolt in a helicopter. The marksman shoots high or low without knowing it until he checks it on a range. A rifle that cannot be checked is a heavy carbine.

**The rifle fouls.** The suppressor fouls the action with more gas than an unsuppressed rifle [E: suppressed direct-impingement and piston rifles foul more]. It needs more cleaning, and if it is not cleaned it jams.

**The rifle is captured.** Captured, it is a dangerous weapon in a trained enemy marksman's hands. The enemy has his own trained snipers [H] and would put a captured M110A2 to good use as long as its 7.62mm lasts; the Front can capture 7.62mm from M60 positions too [H].

**Partial against total.** A squad whose marksman has been killed, or whose rifle has broken, has lost a capability it had for a short time; it is not weaker than a squad that never had one [D].

### D12 · Degradation curve

Month one: the rifles are in the armouries and the first marksmen are in training; capability is close to nil until the school produces them [E: D9].

Month three to six: the first marksmen reach their squads, and the rifles begin to work. Mission-capable rate of the rifles is high, about 95% [E: new precision rifles].

Month twelve: the marksmen of the first course are experienced; some are dead, wounded or promoted, and their replacements are fresher. The scopes have taken a year of jolts, and about 10–15% of them are out of zero or damaged [E: optics in field use; no source]. What declines first is the marksman's skill, which needs range time that a unit in the field seldom gets, and the zero checks that need a range at all [E: D10, D11].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The rifle displaces nothing at squad level, because the historical squad had no marksman [H]. The benchmark is the historical sniper: the US Army and Marine sniper with the M21 or the Remington M40 [H], scoped, in two-man teams at battalion level, few in number. The 9th Infantry Division's sniper programme of 1969–70 is the best-documented example [H].

Against that benchmark, the M110A2 provision does two things. It puts a scoped 7.62mm rifle in every squad, rather than in a sniper team at battalion, so its number is about ten to twenty times the historical snipers' [E: about 4,000–6,500 squads against a few hundred snipers]. But the marksman is not a sniper: he is trained for weeks rather than months, he works with his squad rather than independently, and he is employed at 300–600 metres, not beyond [E: D5, D9].

Against the squad's rifleman with an M4A1 and a red dot, the marksman adds precision at 300–800 metres and the suppressor's concealment [S4] [E: the M4A1's effective range of about 500 m]. Against the enemy's 7.62x39mm AK-47 at those ranges, he has a decisive advantage: the AK is not accurate past 300 metres [E: comparison of the designs].

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P004b -->
**It is not a sniper rifle in a sniper's hands.** One man in each squad, trained for weeks, using it within 600–800 m [E: squad-marksman employment]; not a hunter working alone beyond 1,000 m. **It is no help under canopy**, where the fight is at 10–30 m and the rifle is a heavy carbine with a small magazine. **It does not see at night**: the scope's illuminated reticle helps under flares and moonlight, but the force has no night-vision devices. **It works only after the marksmen are trained**, three months after the rifles arrive at the earliest. **It is a second calibre and a second ammunition line at squad level**: match 7.62mm, separately stocked. **The Regional and Popular Forces do not receive it.**
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P004b -->
It denies the enemy free movement at 300–800 m in the open [E: the marksman's practical range], where his snipers, machine gunners and leaders have historically been out of reach of the M16 and the AK alike. It denies his own snipers the uncontested ground around a firebase or an outpost, and it costs his sapper teams forming for an assault under flare light.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P004b -->
This is a narrow tool and, in its terrain, a good one. In the Delta, the coastal plain, the paddy country and around the firebases, a trained marksman in each squad kills the enemy's leaders and gunners at ranges nobody else in the squad can reach, and the suppressor keeps him alive to do it again. Under the canopy, where much of the main-force war is fought, it does nothing a carbine does not. It depends on training the ARVN does not yet have, on squad leaders who know how to use it, and on the marksman staying alive. Its effect on the war is real and small: it improves the squad's fire where the fight is in the open, and it does not change which side chooses the fight.

## §1.3 FN America M249 light machine gun with SIG Sauer TANGO6T 1-6x24mm, at squad level — P004c

### D1 · Designation, manufacturer, in-service date, quantity, unit organisation

The weapon is the M249 5.56x45mm light machine gun, the American version of the FN Minimi, built by FN America [S6]. I read the provision's "M249B" as the M249 in its 2026 Army configuration: the Product Improvement Program gun with a plastic stock, hydraulic buffer and single gas setting [S6], with the shorter 465 mm barrel and a collapsible stock [E: the configuration most fielded with US infantry in the 2010s and 2020s]. It was adopted in February 1982, entered Army service in 1984 and Marine Corps service in 1985 [S6]. In April 2022 the Army selected the SIG Sauer M250 to replace it, and the Marine Corps has partly replaced it with the M27 automatic rifle since 2010 [S6]; it remains in wide service in 2026 [E: the replacement is gradual].

The scope is the same SIG Sauer TANGO6T 1-6x24mm as on the M110A2 [U], with the DWLR-556 reticle for 5.56mm [S5].

The ammunition is linked M855A1, released for the M249 in December 2010 [S10], fed from 100- or 200-round soft pouches, or from the carbine's 30-round magazines in an emergency [S6].

Quantity. At squad level in US and ARVN infantry [U]. In the ARVN squad the M249 replaces the BAR, one gun per squad [E: the ARVN rifle squad of the period carried one BAR]. In the US Army squad it replaces the two fire teams' automatic rifles, two guns per squad; in the Marine squad of three fire teams, three [E: US squad organisations of the period]. On about 120 ARVN infantry, Ranger, Airborne and Marine battalions of about 36 squads each in 1964, rising to about 180 battalions by 1972, and up to about 112 US manoeuvre battalions at the 1968–69 peak, that is about 5,200 guns in 1964 [E: about 4,300 ARVN squads, the few for US advisers' units, a 10% float, and a stock for training] and about 17,000–19,000 cumulative by 1972 [E: about 6,500 ARVN squads, about 4,000 US squads at the peak with two or three guns each, losses and float].

Organisation. The gunner and an assistant gunner, both squad members [E: squad organisation]. The M60 in the platoon weapons squad and the machine guns at company and battalion are not replaced [U].

### D2 · Crew and complement; personnel spaces against the ceiling

Two men to a gun, gunner and assistant, both already in the squad [E: squad organisation]. No Vietnamese spaces are added.

American spaces: about 40 machine-gun instructors in teams at the training centres and with US units, and about 60 armourers for barrels, gas systems, bolts and feed trays at the depots [E: training and repair for a belt-fed weapon at this scale]. That is about 100 spaces [D], against no ceiling.

The Vietnamese cost is the choice of gunner. The BAR man in an ARVN squad is usually the strongest soldier in it, because the BAR is heavy [E: the period's practice]; the M249 is lighter than the BAR but its ammunition is not, and the gunner and his assistant together carry about 18–20 kg of gun, barrel and belts before their own kit [E: about 10 kg of gun and pouch per S6, a spare barrel, and 600 more linked rounds at about 13 g each]. The assistant gunner, who in the ARVN squad was a rifleman carrying spare BAR magazines, becomes a man whose whole job is to keep the gun fed and change its barrel. That is a squad member diverted from the rifle line, and in an under-strength ARVN squad of seven or eight men present for duty [E: ARVN squads historically ran well below establishment] it is a real price.

### D3 · Weapons and payload

The gun fires 5.56x45mm M855A1 from belts in 100- or 200-round pouches, or from STANAG magazines [S6]. The gunner's load is about 600–800 rounds, with more carried by the assistant and the squad [E: US Army practice]. The quick-change barrel lets the crew swap a hot barrel for a spare in seconds [S6]; one spare barrel goes with each gun [E: standard issue].

M855A1 from a 465 mm barrel penetrates as the carbine's does [S10], at slightly higher velocity [E: longer barrel than the carbine's 14.5-inch].

### D4 · Sensors: triple canopy, night, monsoon

The TANGO6T is a 1-6x24mm variable scope with a 30 mm tube and an illuminated first-focal-plane reticle, here the DWLR-556 for 5.56mm [S5]. It is an aiming device, not a sensor, and its value depends on what the gunner can see.

**Triple canopy.** At 1x, the gunner fires as with a red dot; the scope gives nothing through foliage. In the jungle, the M249 is fired at what the gunner sees or at where the fire is coming from, at 10–30 metres [S8].

**Open ground.** At 6x, the gunner sees targets at 400–700 metres and can put bursts on a treeline and observe the strike [E: magnification]; with the DWLR-556 reticle's holdovers, he can fire accurately at range [S5].

**Night.** The illuminated reticle helps under flares and in twilight [S5]; there is no night vision in 1964 [H]. The gun's muzzle flash, which the provision does not suppress, gives away the gunner's position in the dark [E: unsuppressed muzzle flash].

**Monsoon.** The scope is waterproof to 20 metres [S5]. The gun itself works wet but must be cleaned [E: belt-fed weapons in rain].

### D5 · Performance

The gun weighs about 7.5 kg empty and about 10 kg with a 200-round pouch [S6]. With the scope and mount it weighs about 8.3 kg empty [E: the scope's 626 g per S5 and a mount of about 150 g]. The cyclic rate is about 750–850 rounds a minute; the rapid rate is about 100 rounds a minute and the sustained rate about 50 [S6]. The effective range is about 700 metres against a point target with the 465 mm barrel, and the maximum range is 3,600 metres [S6].

### D6 · Communications, datalink, and talking to ARVN

The gun has no communications. It fires the same round and accepts the same magazines as the M4A1 [S6], so a gun whose belt runs out can be fed from the riflemen's magazines in a crisis. US and ARVN squads use the same gun with the same ammunition.

The interface with ARVN is training: fire discipline, barrel changes, belt management, and the use of a squad machine gun as the base around which the squad manoeuvres. The ARVN squad leader who has led with a BAR knows the principle; he must learn the rate at which a belt-fed gun eats ammunition.

The American adviser with an ARVN battalion can teach the gun because the US squad carries the same gun [U]. In a combined operation an American company and an ARVN company fight side by side with the same squad weapon, the same belts and the same magazines, and a resupply helicopter carries one kind of linked ammunition for both. That is a smaller logistic gain than it sounds, because the two armies' supply chains are separate [H], but on a hilltop at night with the helicopters grounded it is the difference between sharing belts and not.

### D7 · Domain conditions

**Paddy.** Belts trail in the water and pick up mud; the gun's feed tray and the belt links foul [E: belt-fed weapons in water]. The gunner must keep the belt in its pouch and out of the mud. In a paddy fight the gun's range and volume give the squad its base of fire across the open ground.

**Canopy.** The gun is heavy and long; the 465 mm barrel and collapsible stock help [E: compared with the 521 mm and fixed stock]. In the jungle the gun is a sustained-fire weapon at 10–30 metres [S8], which is what the squad needs in a meeting engagement or an ambush.

**The Delta.** In the Mekong Delta the squad moves along dikes and canals, wades and crosses by sampan; the gunner must keep his belts dry and his gun clear of the water, and a gun dropped in a canal must be stripped and dried before it fires reliably [E: belt-fed weapons in immersion]. The 465 mm barrel and collapsible stock make it handier in a sampan than the BAR's long barrel [E: length comparison].

**Hills and highlands.** The gun's weight on a man who climbs the highland ridges is a burden; the assistant carries more ammunition and the squad shares the belts [E: practice].

**Monsoon and humidity.** The gun rusts if not cleaned; the 2002 Natick survey found 30% of gunners said the M249 rusted easily, and the 2006 CNA survey found its stoppages the highest of any weapon at 30% of users [S6]. Those were in Iraq and Afghanistan, in sand. In the paddy and the monsoon the enemy is mud and water, and a gun that is not cleaned jams.

### D8 · Logistics footprint

**Ammunition.** A belt-fed gun eats ammunition. A gun firing at the sustained rate of 50 rounds a minute [S6] for ten minutes fires 500 rounds [D]; a gun in a long fight fires its gunner's whole load. Across about 5,200 guns in 1964 [E: D1], with a basic load of about 800 rounds and a year's training and combat consumption of about 3,000–5,000 rounds a gun, the first year needs about 20–30 million rounds of linked 5.56mm [E: basic load, training and expenditure]. That is about 300–450 tonnes before packing [E: about 12.3 g a cartridge plus links]. Linked ammunition is bulkier than boxed and needs its own packing [E: links and pouches].

**Spares.** Barrels: a gun that fires at high rates for long periods wears out barrels; a squad needs two barrels per gun and a depot stock [E: practice]. Bolts, gas pistons, feed trays, springs [E: maintenance].

**Specialists.** The armourers of D2.

**What RVNAF cannot supply.** The guns, the barrels, the linked ammunition, the spares.

### D9 · Transferability to ARVN

The gun is issued to ARVN squads from the first day. Training the gunner and the assistant takes about one to two weeks [E: US Army machine-gun courses], through the same training centres as the carbine. The ARVN has used the BAR for years and the M1919 machine gun [H]; the M249 is a lighter, faster-firing, belt-fed weapon of the same role.

Under Vietnamization the guns stay with the squads, and they keep working as long as linked ammunition, barrels and spare parts keep coming from the United States [E: the RVNAF has no small-arms industry]. When US aid stops, the guns are the first weapon to run dry, because they fire the most ammunition [D].

### D10 · Employment envelope

The gun performs to specification in a fight that lasts minutes, fired in bursts, by a crew that changes barrels and manages belts, with an ammunition resupply that reaches the squad. That is most fights.

Take an ARVN rifle squad of the 7th Division on a canal bank in Dinh Tuong at dusk, the kind of place and hour where this war's small fights happen [E: an illustration, not a recorded event]. A VC local-force platoon opens fire from a treeline 150 metres across a paddy [E: a typical Delta paddy width]. In the historical squad, the BAR man fires his 20 rounds, reloads, fires again, and the squad's carbines reach the treeline poorly or not at all [E: the BAR's magazine and the carbine's range]. With the M249, the gunner puts a 200-round belt into the treeline in measured bursts while the squad leader moves two men along the dike; the VC platoon, which had planned to fire for a minute and leave, must either fight a squad that is not pinned or break contact under fire. The gun has not found the enemy; the enemy chose the place and the time. But once he has chosen, the squad can answer him on equal terms.

Its performance collapses when the gunner fires at the cyclic rate for long periods and burns out barrels; when the belt fouls in mud; when the ammunition runs out before resupply; and when the gunner is killed, because the gun is the squad's main target [E: in every war].

Over weeks, guns that are fired often wear barrels and parts, and the depot must replace them [E: practice].

### D11 · Failure modes

**Stoppages.** The M249's stoppage record is the worst of the modern US small arms in survey [S6]. The commonest causes are dirt, worn parts and bad belts [E: maintenance]. A stopped gun is cleared in seconds by a trained gunner and in minutes by an untrained one.

**Barrels.** A barrel that is not changed overheats and loses accuracy, and in the extreme it fails [E: machine-gun barrels]. A crew without a spare barrel cannot sustain fire.

**Ammunition.** A gun that runs out is a heavy rifle without a magazine until it is fed from the riflemen's magazines [S6].

**The gunner.** He is the enemy's first target in any contact; his death silences the squad's base of fire until someone else picks up the gun [E: in every war].

**Partial against total.** A gun with a stoppage is down for seconds; a gun with a burned-out barrel and no spare is down for the rest of the fight; a gun with no ammunition is down until the resupply comes. The squad without its gun fights as the historical squad did with its BAR down: with rifles alone, which after this provision are M4A1s with 30-round magazines and full automatic, so a squad whose gun has failed is still better armed than the historical squad whose BAR was firing [E: comparison of squad firepower].

**Capture.** A captured M249 with its belts is a valuable weapon in the enemy's hands; the enemy has used captured machine guns throughout the war [H].

### D12 · Degradation curve

Month one: guns new, crews trained in the first courses, barrels fresh. Mission-capable rate near 100% [E: new issue].

Month twelve: guns that have been fired heavily have worn barrels and parts, and crews that have rotated have lost their trained gunners. Mission-capable rate about 85–90% in ARVN units [E: belt-fed weapons in tropical field use]. What declines first is the crew's training, then the barrels and the spare parts [E: maintenance]. A trained crew changes a barrel in seconds and fires in six- to nine-round bursts; an untrained replacement crew fires long bursts, burns barrels, and runs dry [E: machine-gun training doctrine]. The depots will hold barrels and parts in depth because they are American; what runs short at month twelve is the barrel that should have been in the squad and was left at battalion, and the trained gunner who rotated home or was killed. In the ARVN, where a battalion may spend months in the field without returning to a training centre, the crews trained in the first courses will carry the gun's whole reputation, and their losses decide how quickly it declines [E: ARVN replacement practice].

### D13 · Comparative benchmark

The M249 displaces the Browning Automatic Rifle in the ARVN squad and the automatic rifle in the US squad [U].

- **BAR M1918A2.** About 8.8 kg empty, 20-round box magazine, .30-06, rate of fire about 300–650 rounds a minute [H]. The M249 is about 1.3 kg lighter empty [D], fires from a 200-round belt, ten times the BAR's magazine [D], and fires a lighter round of which the gunner carries about twice as many for the same weight [E: cartridge weights of about 12 g for 5.56mm and 26 g for .30-06]. The BAR's .30-06 hits harder at long range; in this country that rarely matters [S8].
- **The US squad's automatic rifle.** The M14 or M16A1 on automatic, 20-round magazines [H]. The M249 fires a belt, sustains fire through barrel changes, and has a range of about 700 metres [S6], far more than an automatic rifle's.
- **The enemy's RPD.** The squad light machine gun of PAVN and VC main-force units: 7.62x39mm from a 100-round belt drum [H]. The M249 is a match for it, with a larger belt [S6] and a better sight [S5].

### D14 · Negative specification
<!-- card:negative P004c -->
**It is not a general-purpose machine gun.** It replaces the squad's automatic weapon, not the M60 in the platoon or company, which stays; it does not have the M60's reach and penetration [E: 7.62mm NATO against 5.56mm]. **It does not fight longer than its ammunition**: at the sustained rate of 50 rounds a minute a gun empties its gunner's load in about a quarter of an hour, and every round comes up an interdicted road. **Its stoppage record is the worst of modern US small arms in survey**; in mud and monsoon it needs cleaning. **It does not see at night.** **The Regional and Popular Forces do not receive it**: the outpost keeps its BAR.
<!-- /card -->

### D15 · What it denies the enemy
<!-- card:denies P004c -->
It denies the enemy's RPD-armed squads the advantage of a belt-fed gun against an ARVN squad with a 20-round BAR, and it denies the enemy assault the pause when the BAR reloads.
<!-- /card -->

### D16 · Honest verdict
<!-- card:verdict P004c -->
This is the most combat-significant of the three provisions at squad level. A belt-fed gun at the centre of each ARVN squad gives it a base of fire it has never had: it can hold an enemy in contact, cover a withdrawal, break an assault on a perimeter. In a meeting engagement or an ambush, the squad that has a belt-fed gun and uses it well wins more often. It is heavy, it eats ammunition, it jams if it is not cleaned, and its gunner is the enemy's first target. It does not find the enemy or decide when the fight happens. It makes the squads that fight better at fighting, which matters every day in this war, though it is not where the war's hardest problem lies.
<!-- /card -->
