## P1 · Overview

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. Two months earlier the coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the administrative continuity the Republic of Vietnam possessed. The Military Assistance Command, Vietnam, under General Paul D. Harkins, is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; the United States has about 23,300 military personnel in the country by the end of 1964 [H40], none of them formed into combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year [H40]. The fighting is done by the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, the Regional and Popular Forces, and the Civilian Irregular Defense Group camps of the US Special Forces, against a Viet Cong movement that has rebuilt itself since 1959 on the political infrastructure left from the war against France and on cadres regrouped to the North in 1954 and now returning down the Trail.

Phase I runs from January 1964 to the end of July 1965. Its shape is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon, the state comes apart: between January 1964 and June 1965 the government changes hands by coup or forced resignation repeatedly, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field, the Viet Cong move from the guerrilla phase to the mobile-war phase, forming regiments and then a division in the provinces around Saigon; at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve and its regiments in set-piece battles, and at Song Be the government holds a province capital only narrowly and with American firepower. In Hanoi, the Politburo decides that the moment for decisive victory has come and commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. The American response comes in two steps: from the air, beginning with the reprisal strikes of August 1964 and February 1965 and hardening into Rolling Thunder in March 1965; and on the ground, beginning with two Marine battalions sent to guard an airfield in March 1965 and ending with the President's decision of July 1965 to commit US ground forces to the war in strength.

Every one of the political constraints that will shape the war is drawn in this phase: the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong, the bar on ground operations in Laos and Cambodia, the reliance on presidential approval for targets in the North, and the decision to fight without calling up the reserves. So is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: the decision to close in, choose the time of battle, and keep his base areas beyond the border.

Five things in this phase are new, and all of them begin on its first day. Neither the coups in Saigon nor the summer offensive turns on any of them.

The first two belong to the Republic itself. On 1 January 1964 the junta decrees land to the tiller, confirming by name what the Viet Minh and the Front had already given, and an independent Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it (EV001-1); the same session gives the Republic a resident rural police with a Special Branch, and province and district chiefs chosen by examination from a ranked list (EV002-1). Khanh's coup tests both within a month and both survive it (EV000-1). By the end of July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the rent stop holds in most government-held districts, and the musters have shown the RVNAF to be about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls [X-001]; about 1,100 village police posts stand in the held belt, about two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the Front has killed about 410 constables, nearly all in posts settled ahead of night security (EV002-2) [X-002]. The reforms also give the coup-makers a new grievance and Hanoi a new reason to hurry.

The third is what this command can see. Two U-2S and four Global Hawks fly from January 1964. The coastal picket closes the trawler route within a year, and Hanoi moves its southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months before it did historically (EV003-1, EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats near the destroyers, Pierce Arrow is not flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passes on the real attack of 2 August alone (EV000-3).

The fourth is in the hands of the men who fight: modern carbines for the whole RVNAF and the territorials, and a belt-fed gun and a marksman in every regular squad (EV004-1). In the held belt a post with its Popular Forces platoon now holds about nine attacks in ten (EV002-2). Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy still chooses where and when to fight, but they are lost at a smaller price and no battalion of the general reserve is wiped out (EV000-9, EV000-17, EV000-18). The outposts that fall give the Front the same carbines.

The fifth waits on Guam: B-52Js carrying GPS-guided bombs and, from March 1964, a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut that measures the U-2S's imagery into targets for them (EV005-1). Washington refuses every request to use them over the North (EV000-15), and the first Arc Light strike of June 1965 flies without tankers and finds as little as history's did (EV000-20).

A sixth thing begins on the same first day and does not show until the second year. The decree of 1 January 1964 puts a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch and orders a coordinating centre in every province and district, built on the posts and files the police decree creates; the centres are staffed by the Special Branch, the Military Security Service and the sector S2, forbidden quotas, held to warrants under the police statute, and audited by the Commission (EV006-1). About 100 district centres are open by the end of 1964 and all of them by the end of 1965. In the same month the Army begins to select its field advisers by board, to train them for eight months in Vietnamese and in advising, and to keep them eighteen months; the first of them reach the subsectors and battalions in October. In March 1965 the centre at Hoc Mon breaks the district committee above the hamlets the police had already cleared, and the Front learns that leaving a village no longer puts its leaders out of reach (EV006-2).

A seventh thing began before the first day, and it is the only one that reaches back past it. Since 1957 every new officer of the ARVN, and every new American officer, has passed a branch course after commissioning and the courses of the 2026 continuum before each promotion, so that on 1 January 1964 seven such classes, about 10,000–14,000 ARVN officers, hold most of the platoons and companies of the army [M007]. The session of 1 January gives that army an Officer Selection and Promotion Board with written criteria, a rated evaluation report, central command lists for battalions and regiments, relief for cause, a path to commission for proven sergeants, and a ranked list from which generals and division commanders must be chosen, behind an American aid condition (EV007-1). Khanh's coup tests it in its first month (EV000-1). Its effect in this phase is in the companies, where the educated officers already stand, and in the first battalions the board fills; Ba Gia and Dong Xoai are still lost, because the enemy chose them and the generals planned the relief (EV000-17, EV000-18).

An eighth thing stood on the first day, and it was made of steel and copper. The Republic began the war with an industrial base a generation above its own: a steelworks, an arsenal and rebuild works at Bien Hoa, a radio plant, a battery works and a wire mill at Go Vap, placed under a Directorate of Defence Industry (EV008-1). For fourteen months it had more capacity than customers, until the Aid Compliance Board made the RVNAF buy a share of its ammunition bodies, batteries, wire, radios and steel from it from March 1965. Its first effects were small and real: the Popular Forces platoons beside the police posts carried their own radios from the spring of 1964, and the Front learned to attack the posts that could not call (EV002-2); the bridges the November floods took on the coast were back in six weeks rather than months (EV008-2). No battle of the phase turned on it.

A ninth thing stood in the ranks on the first day, and it was the plainest of them. Since 1957 every man who enlisted or was drafted into the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers and the Vietnamese Marines had passed a ten-week basic course on the 2026 American programme at Quang Trung or a division centre, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit a six-week abridgement of it; every American recruit since 1957 had passed his own service's 2026 course. On 1 January 1964 about 120,000–160,000 of the Republic's regular soldiers and about 60,000–100,000 of its territorials had been trained so, and the rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was put on the muster roll as a course number the Muster Service could check (EV009-1) [M009]. It changed no battle's choice of ground in the phase. It changed how the posts' first ten minutes went at Long Huu, how many of the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment came back from Thuan Loi, and how many wounded men in both armies were alive in the morning (EV002-2, EV000-18).

A tenth thing stood between the private and the officer, and it had been there since 1957 as well. Every NCO of the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers, the Vietnamese Marines, the VNAF and the VNN, and every sergeant and corporal of the Civil Guard and the Self-Defense Corps, had passed the course for his grade at the National NCO Academy at Nha Trang, a division school or a territorial centre before he could be promoted to the next, on a curriculum built on the American Army's of 2026; every American NCO had passed his own service's course for his grade under the same rule (EV010-1). It did not make more sergeants, and it took between 1,300 and 2,500 of the best ones to teach. It meant that the squad and the platoon, where the trained private met the educated lieutenant, were led by men schooled for the job, and that a corporal first class who had passed the leader's course could take an empty squad as a schooled man (F010-1, EC010-3) [X-010].

## EV000-9 · The battle of Binh Gia · 28 Dec 1964 – 1 Jan 1965 · Binh Gia, Phuoc Tuy Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01
*Initiative: enemy*

Binh Gia, a Catholic village of northern refugees in Phuoc Tuy Province east of Saigon, was the site of the Viet Cong's first large set-piece battle [H1]. The attackers were the 271st and 272nd Regiments (then called the 1st and 2nd), the nucleus of the forming 9th Division, with the 80th Artillery Detachment and local battalions of the B-2 Front, an estimated 1,800 men [H1]. They took the village on 28 December and then waited for the relief. The ARVN committed the 30th, 33rd, 35th and 38th Ranger Battalions and the 4th Marine Battalion of the general reserve, with two artillery platoons and a section of M24 tanks, about 4,300 men [H1], lifted and supported by the US 118th Aviation Company and the gunships of the 68th Assault Helicopter Company [H1]. Every one of those battalions had carried the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 since the spring (EV004-1); the attackers carried Type 56 and SKS rifles, RPD guns and recoilless rifles [X-004].

The relieving battalions were fed in piecemeal, the artillery badly positioned and air support thin, as they were historically [H1]. The rifles did not change the generals' plan, and the plan was the problem. On 30 December a 68th Company gunship was shot down near Quang Giao and its four crewmen killed [H1]. On 31 December the 4th Marine Battalion, going to recover the bodies, was ambushed in the rubber by the 271st at a range of 20 to 40 metres, as it was historically, and its commander, Major Nguyen Van Nho, was killed in the first minutes [H1]. What followed was different. The battalion's squad guns, three to a company platoon, put a base of fire into the trees that the Marines' BARs could not have sustained, and the companies, instead of being cut apart in the order they had marched, closed into two perimeters within about a quarter of an hour and fought out of the rubber at dusk carrying most of their wounded (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 60–70 killed and 90 wounded, against 122 killed and 71 wounded on the historical course [X-004]. Captain Donald Cook, the Marine adviser, was wounded and captured going back for a wounded Marine, as he was historically, and died in captivity [H1]. MACV counted allied losses from 28 December to 6 January at about 140 killed, 220 wounded and 45 missing [E: the historical 201 killed, 192 wounded and 68 missing, adjusted for the 4th Marines' smaller loss and the larger number of wounded who came out alive]. MACV reported four helicopters shot down [H1]. The VC claim of 1,783 killed and captured is no more credible here than it was historically; their own dead, of whom at least 32 were confirmed historically, I put at about 120–200, because the Marines' marksmen and squad guns killed men in the rubber whom the 271st could not carry away [E: the 4th Marines' after-action count of about 90 bodies left on the ground, and the usual ratio of dead removed to dead left].

The significance was still the form. A Viet Cong formation had held ground for four days against armour, artillery and helicopters, chosen the field, ambushed the general reserve on the road it had to use, and withdrawn intact [H1]. The 271st took the honorific "Binh Gia Regiment" [H1]. US observers concluded, as they did historically, that the ARVN could not defeat main-force units of this kind without American firepower; what they could not say, as they said historically, was that a Marine battalion of the general reserve had been destroyed. The 4th Marines were back in the line in February.

The sergeants did the rest. The 4th Marine Battalion's NCOs had all passed the courses for their grades at Nha Trang since 1957, and its platoon sergeants the senior course; when Major Nho fell, two of the four company commanders were hit in the same minutes, and the companies were taken over by their first sergeants, who had been taught in the leader's course to count their men, establish a base of fire and report before they moved (F010-1) [X-010]. Master Sergeant Nguyen Van Thuoc of the 2nd Company put his three squad guns on the trees and moved his platoons back by bounds; the companies closed into their two perimeters about five minutes faster than they would have done, and the second perimeter held the 271st's assault at 1640 without falling back into the first [X-010]. I put the battalion's loss at about 50–60 killed and 100 wounded, against the 60–70 killed and 90 wounded of the earlier course, and MACV's allied losses for the battle at about 130 killed rather than about 140; the difference is wounded men who came out because a sergeant counted them [X-010]. The battle's form, which the 271st chose, did not change (F010-8).

## EV000-18 · The battle of Dong Xoai · 9–13 Jun 1965 · Dong Xoai district town and CIDG camp, Phuoc Long Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,CP01,007,009
*Initiative: enemy*

Late on 9 June 1965 the VC 272nd Regiment, with a battalion of the 273rd, a 75mm recoilless rifle company, an 82mm mortar company, a flamethrower company and anti-aircraft machine guns, attacked the district headquarters and CIDG camp at Dong Xoai; the 271st Regiment lay in reserve in the Thuan Loi rubber plantation [H6]. The camp was held by Special Forces Detachment A-342, nine men of Navy Seabee Team 1104, three CIDG companies, the 111th Regional Force Company and militia, with six armoured cars and two 105mm howitzers [H6]. The CIDG companies and the militia carried the carbines and Garands they had always carried, because the rearmament did not reach them; the 111th RF Company and the Americans carried M4A1s (F004-1) [X-004]. The camp was largely overrun during the night, as it was historically, from the CIDG sectors inward, and the survivors held out in the district headquarters, where the 111th Company had fallen back with its carbines, until helicopters of the 118th Aviation Company lifted them out [H6] [X-004]. On 10 June the 1st Battalion, 7th Infantry Regiment (ARVN 5th Division), was landed at Thuan Loi and overrun, losing about half its men against nearly all of them historically [X-004]; the 52nd Ranger Battalion retook the town; on 11 June the 7th Airborne Battalion arrived with about 470 men, and on 12 June the 271st ambushed it in the rubber [H6].

The Airborne fight on 12 June is the clearest measure in this war of what the squad weapons could and could not do. Historically about 150 of the 470 came back. On this course the battalion's lead company was lost in the opening fire as it was historically, but the three companies behind it each turned their squad guns into the trees and held, and about 270 men fought their way to the landing zone at Thuan Loi over the afternoon, bringing about 60 wounded [X-004]. The 271st had chosen the place, the hour and the range, and it won; the Airborne battalion was broken, though not wiped out [X-004]. The 145th Aviation Battalion committed every aircraft it had [H6].

I put the allied losses at about 300 South Vietnamese killed, 210 wounded and 150 missing, and 18 Americans killed as historically, against 416 killed, 174 wounded and 233 missing on the historical course; about 300 civilians were killed and 2,500 made refugees, as historically [X-004] [H6]. More than 300 VC bodies were found historically; I put the figure here at about 400, with the same scepticism about the allied estimate of 625 more [X-004]. Second Lieutenant Charles Q. Williams and Construction Mechanic Third Class Marvin G. Shields received the Medal of Honor, Shields posthumously, the only Seabee so honoured [H6]. On 13 June Westmoreland committed the 1st Battalion, 503rd Infantry of the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Phuoc Vinh, the first commitment of US ground troops to a major battle; the VC had already gone, and the battalion returned on 18 June without contact [H6]. After Dong Xoai the ARVN in III Corps had about two reserve battalions left, against one historically [X-004]. That was not enough to change Westmoreland's request of 7 June, which rested on the PAVN regiments entering the Highlands as much as on the battalions lost, and it did not change the answer.

At Dong Xoai the board had not yet reached the 5th Division. The 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment was landed at Thuan Loi on 10 June on a division plan that put it on an open landing zone beside the rubber where the 271st was known to be waiting, under a battalion commander appointed by the division in 1962 [X-007]. The board reviewed the landing in July on its own motion, under the thirty-day rule, and in August relieved the battalion commander and the division operations officer who had written the plan, the first reliefs for cause in the ARVN's history that were not punishments for political disloyalty (EV007-2) [X-007]. The 5th Division's commander was untouched, and remained so, because he had been appointed before the ranked list existed. The night at the camp and the 7th Airborne's fight on 12 June went as told above [H6].

The basic course changed Dong Xoai at its edges and not at its centre. The CIDG companies, which the course did not reach, broke first, as they had historically and in this war before it (F009-6). The 111th RF Company, which had taken the territorial course as a company in 1961, fell back to the district headquarters in order and carried its wounded with it; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment, whose privates had all been through Quang Trung, was still landed on the plan its division wrote, on the open zone beside the rubber, and still lost about half its men, because no private's training reaches a landing zone chosen for him [X-009]. The difference was in the evening of 12 June, when the 7th Airborne's survivors came into Thuan Loi: of about 60 wounded they brought out, about 50 were alive the next morning, with tourniquets put on in the rubber by men who had never been taught first aid on the historical course and were taught it in basic here [X-009]. I put the allied killed at about 270 rather than about 300, a judgment on the wounded who lived and the companies that held, and I do not adjust the enemy's losses [X-009].

The sergeants changed Dong Xoai at the same edges. The 111th RF Company had passed the territorial courses for every NCO grade, and its platoon sergeants, not its officers, took it back to the district headquarters in order, a platoon at a time, while the CIDG companies, whose leaders the grant did not reach, broke around it (F010-6) [X-010]. On 12 June the 7th Airborne's three surviving companies were held together, after their captains were hit, by first sergeants and acting squad leaders who had all passed the leader's course, and about 290 men reached Thuan Loi rather than about 270 [X-010]. The 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment, was still landed on the open zone beside the rubber, on the division's plan, and no sergeant could change that (F010-8). I put the allied killed at about 255 rather than about 270 [X-010].

## EV010-1 · The academy at Dong De and the gate · 1 Jan 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The National NCO Academy at Dong De, Nha Trang, Khanh Hoa Province; the division NCO schools; the RF/PF training centres; the Joint General Staff, Saigon · NEW · 010
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the National NCO Academy at Dong De, on the hills above Nha Trang, began its eighth year as the school of the army's sergeants. It had been reorganised in 1957, a year earlier than historically, to teach every NCO grade a course before promotion, and since then every corporal first class of the ARVN and the territorial forces who wanted to be a sergeant had passed its six-week leader's course or the same course at a division school, every sergeant the four-week squad leader's course, every platoon sergeant the senior course, and the army's senior master sergeants a course of four to five months (M010·S8) [X-010]. Its senior instructor was Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc, a Tonkinese who had come south with the Commando School in 1954 and had taught every class since 1957; its senior American adviser from 1963 was Master Sergeant William H. Rourke, who had served as a platoon sergeant in Korea and had passed the senior course himself at Fort Benning [X-010]. The academy's barracks held about 3,300 students; the division schools and the territorial centres taught about as many again, and in 1964 the sequence put about 27,000–45,000 regular and territorial NCOs through a course (E03) [X-010].

The cost was paid in the year it could least be afforded. In June 1964, after the 1963–64 losses in the Delta, the Joint General Staff asked the academy and the division schools for a third of their instructors back for the general reserve; the Central Training Command refused it, as it refused the same request for the drill sergeants, and in August met it in part by sending 300 instructors of the 1957–59 classes to the Ranger and Airborne battalions as platoon sergeants and replacing them from the battalions' own sergeants (F010-11) [X-010]. In November, with the Armed Forces Council preparing a National Day promotion for the whole army, the Muster Service and MACV's training directorate argued that a grade for every man would put about 20,000 corporals first class into the sergeants' rank without the leader's course; the council promoted every private one grade and every NCO with his course one grade, and left the gate standing (F010-5) [X-010]. The academy's manuals, printed in Vietnamese at the Army Printing Office in Saigon, went into the packs of about 30,000 sergeants that year, and by December the first captured copies had reached the B-2 Front's military staff (F010-20) [X-010].

## P1 · How the phase ends

By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The decision to commit US ground forces in strength has been taken, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. On the other side, the Viet Cong have formed regiments and divisions in the provinces around Saigon and in the coastal lowlands, PAVN regular regiments are in the Central Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though none of the general reserve has been wiped out. In the countryside, the Viet Cong infrastructure administers, taxes and conscripts in a large share of the hamlets, and outside the held belt the government's pacification effort has nothing like the stable administration or the local security it needs. Inside it the summer is not the historical one: about 330,000 hectares of rice land have been titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most of the districts the government holds, and in those districts the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's [X-001]. Where the government does not hold the hamlets at night, nothing has changed except the promise.

In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta has just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Washington, the war has an open-ended authority from Congress and a decision on troops announced without the reserves, without a tax increase and without a declaration. In Hanoi, the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength and must now decide whether to fight them. It will: in October the PAVN regiments of the B3 Front will go after the camp at Plei Me, and the Ia Drang will follow. The war that Phase II inherits is a war between two armies that each believe they can outlast the other, fought over a population that neither government has secured.

About 1,100 village posts are settled, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, and the infrastructure has begun to leave the villages around the posts (EV002-2) [X-002]. The sea route to the coast is closed and the Cambodian route open, and the enemy's rear is on the U-2S's maps while his regiments under the canopy and his cadres in the hamlets are as hidden as before. The general reserve enters Phase II weakened but whole, and the Front enters it with between 10,000 and 30,000 of the Republic's carbines (EV004-1) [X-004]. On Guam the B-52Js hold about 1,400 measured targets in the cell's folders and authority to strike none of them in the North (EV005-1, EV000-15).

Phung Hoang ends the phase with about 140 district centres open, every one behind a settled post, and a method that has reached above the hamlet in one district on the edge of Saigon and in a dozen in the Delta (EV006-1, EV006-2) [X-006]. About 1,100 selected advisers are in the subsectors and battalions, speaking to their counterparts without interpreters; the rest of the field posts are still held on the old tour. The Front has begun to move its district committees out of the held belt, which costs it the villages' daily obedience and gains it the safety of the contested edge (F006-4).

By the end of July 1965 the board has filled about 70 of the army's roughly 190 manoeuvre battalion commands and relieved its first nine commanders; the rest are still held by men the generals put there, and about a third of all battalions are still commanded by captains, because the army is short of majors and the career-course gate makes none faster (EV007-1, F007-8) [X-007]. The companies are led almost everywhere by the classes of 1957–63. The generals are the generals of 1963, and every one of them has learned that the board can be bargained with and cannot be ignored.

The Directorate ends the phase with its plants at about half their capacity and the domestic share four months old; about 5,000 PF radios, 6,000 hamlet radios and 600,000 dry cells have gone to the held belt, and the Front's mines in Long An have Go Vap batteries in them (EV008-1, EV002-2) [X-008]. Route 1 on the coast has been reopened after the worst flood in memory, and the Front has an industrial working class in Bien Hoa to organise (EV008-2).

The army that enters Phase II has trained every private it has enlisted for eight years, and every territorial; it has kept about 2,000–3,000 of its best sergeants at the training centres to do it, in the two years it could least spare them, and the Muster Service has caught the centres that sold the course short (EV009-1) [X-009]. The general reserve was broken at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and came back from both with more of its men than on any course before this one; the battalions are steadier than history's by a band, though the enemy still chooses where they fight [X-009].

The NCO corps that enters Phase II has been schooled for its grades for eight years, regular and territorial alike, and about 1,300–2,500 of its best sergeants are teaching at Nha Trang, the division schools and the territorial centres, on top of the drill sergeants at Quang Trung (EV010-1, F010-11) [X-010]. The 4th Marine Battalion at Binh Gia and the 7th Airborne at Dong Xoai were broken and came back with more of their men than on either earlier course, because their sergeants took command when their officers fell (EV000-9, EV000-18). The enemy has the academy's leader's manual and has begun to teach its local forces the ARVN platoon's routine (F010-20).

## P2 · Overview

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967 [H40]. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed most. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 titled to about 640,000 families [X-001]. GOLDEN SHEAF, its first clearing operation in the western Delta, shows what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of what the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark (EV001-6, F001-4). The Commission survives the Buddhist crisis and the election behind the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 convicts its first general (EV001-7). With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case (EV002-4). The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its cadres into the towns (EV002-3, EV002-5), and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before (EV000-26). None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

The war of the enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch counts the Trail's trucks from December 1965 (EV003-3); the U-2S photographs the dispersed fuel drums before the POL strikes fly (EV000-27); and by March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree, from the radar's count of convoys out of Kompong Speu, that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4). The counts show the bombing raising the toll on the transit without lowering what arrives, and change no rule in Washington.

Every American and South Vietnamese infantryman carries a modern carbine, and every squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman. The Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle (EV000-23, EV000-34). The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by the corps, and the districts they leave pay for it (EV004-2). None of it changes who chooses the battle.

Above the Trail the B-52J is allowed against fixed crossings in the panhandle from April 1966 and cuts every bridge it can measure; the enemy answers with fords, causeways and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before (EV005-2). At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is measured every night and lifts three weeks early at a heavy price to the PAVN (EV000-36). It is the first siege the enemy loses badly, and Westmoreland draws a lesson from it that will cost the coastal hamlets.

Three changes of 1964 run through the phase without altering its battles. Phung Hoang's centres cover every district by the end of 1965 and break the Front's district administration in the held belt before Tet rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN takes the Can Giuoc committee itself, not only its security chief (EV002-4). The method spreads by its own success into the contested belt, where it becomes the historical Phoenix, and a PRU raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 makes "the American Phoenix" a press story three years early (EV006-4). The American battalions arrive and leave as units for twelve-month tours, trained together and commanded for the whole of them; they fight better in the contacts the enemy grants and get no more of them, and the enemy learns to aim at the week each one changes over, as it did at Dien Ban in August 1966 (EV006-3). And the field advisers, now chosen and trained and on eighteen-month tours, are the men who carry what the American battalions forget each year.

The officer reforms change the ARVN in this phase more than any provision before them. From the board's second season in 1965 the battalions are filled from its command list, their commanders serve eighteen months and are relieved when they fail, and by the end of 1966 about three in four of the army's battalion commanders are men a board chose, against about one in two competent commanders on the historical course (EV007-2) [X-007]. The enemy notices within a season and does what he does against every better unit: he declines to fight it, and lays his ambush for the battalion beside it, as the 272nd Regiment does on Route 13 in May 1966 (EV007-3, F007-18). The Buddhist uprising finds the board's commanders in I Corps on neither side (EV000-26), and in 1967 the ranked list gives the 25th Division its first commander chosen for competence, under the aid condition, while an agent in the board's secretariat gives COSVN every list it publishes (EV007-4). Above the battalions nothing moves: the corps are commanded by the same men, Westmoreland's strategy turns the ARVN to pacification as it did historically, and the generals still decide where the battalions go.

A fifth war runs under the fourth, in the factories. The steelworks at Bien Hoa rolls the pickets, wire and bridge spans of the countryside, at a fifth to a tenth below its rating because the Front has learned to drop the towers of the Da Nhim line (EV008-3). The radio plant puts a PRC-25 in every post of the held belt from 1966, and the Front answers the radio as it answered the carbine: it attacks the posts that cannot call, and at My Phuoc Tay in March 1967 it attacks a post in order to ambush the company that answers the call (EV008-4). The district chiefs answer with guns instead of trucks, and their platoons stay inside their wire. In Chuong Thien the posts call and nobody comes, because the corps has borrowed the companies (EV004-2). And at Phu Cat in August 1967 a bad lot of shell bodies teaches the army what an arsenal is worth without honest acceptance (EV008-5). None of it changes who chooses the main-force battle.

Under all of it, the private soldier of both armies is trained. Every ARVN recruit of the phase passes ten weeks at Quang Trung or a division centre before he joins his battalion, every territorial six weeks at his province's centre, and every American his service's 2026 course; from 1966 the drill-sergeant corps begins to return its sergeants to the battalions as platoon sergeants (EV009-1) [M009]. The effect is the one the officer reforms found: the better unit is attacked less, and the attack that is not made leaves nothing to count. The 272nd Regiment's ambush of the Bau Bang convoy breaks on trained Regional Force soldiers who cannot be trained out of riding their trucks (EV007-3). At the Ia Drang the troopers keep more of their wounded alive through the night at Albany (EV000-23). And the enemy answers the trained soldier as he answered the rifle, by moving his war to the weapon the course does not teach: in the lowlands south of Da Nang the mine and the booby trap become the principal killers of the Marines (EV009-2). The devaluation of June 1966 then shows what training does not buy: a trained private whose pay no longer feeds his family goes home with his training, and some of it reaches the Front (EV009-3).

Between the private and the officer, the sergeant is schooled too. Every ARVN and territorial NCO of the phase has passed the course for his grade at the academy at Nha Trang, a division school or his province's centre, and every empty squad in a regular battalion can be given to a corporal first class who has passed the leader's course; every American sergeant who wants a sixth stripe passes the Basic Leader Course first, at home or at MACV's academy at Bien Hoa (EV010-2). The squads hold their base of fire when their officers fall, as the 7th Division's did in the rice fields of Cai Be in November 1966 (EV010-3), and the posts of the held belt keep their listening posts out at night (F010-2) [X-010]. The enemy reads all of it from the academy's own manuals and fights the posts and the battalions that keep the routine less often (F010-19, F010-20).

## EV008-4 · The night of the relief at My Phuoc Tay · 9–10 Mar 1967 · The PF post at Ap My Loi and the canal road to Cai Lay district town, My Phuoc Tay village, Cai Lay District, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 008
*Initiative: enemy*

This is the night the radio's second-order effect became visible, and the night that shows it best (F008-3, F008-6). By 1967 every Popular Forces post in the held belt of Dinh Tuong had a set on the district net, a PRC-25 from the Go Vap plant since the previous autumn, and a fresh battery every Thursday from the exchange at the district (EC008-7, EC008-8) [X-008]. The Front had stopped assaulting those posts in 1965, as it had stopped assaulting the rearmed ones (EV002-2). In the first months of 1967 the Dinh Tuong provincial unit decided to use the radio against the men it called.

At 2235 on 9 March a mortar section of the 514th Local Force Battalion put twelve 60mm rounds into the post at Ap My Loi, a mud-walled fort on the canal six kilometres north of Cai Lay, from about 300 metres across the paddy. Sergeant Le Van Muoi, who commanded its PF platoon, called the district at 2237, reported the mortar and an assault forming on the dike to the west, and asked for the reaction force. The assault did not come. Two companies of the 514th were not on the dike at all; they were lying in the sugar cane on both sides of the canal road, three kilometres south of the post, where the road crossed a concrete culvert that the district's trucks had to slow to cross [X-008].

The district chief, Major Nguyen Thanh Liem, did what the rule of 1964 told him, that a post that called would have its reaction within the hour, and sent the 247th Regional Force Company north in four trucks at 2250 (EV002-1). At 2304 a command-detonated mine under the culvert lifted the lead truck off the road. The firing circuit, the district's engineers found in the morning, was two kilometres of WD-1 field wire from the Republic's own mill and a pack of Go Vap dry cells of the kind the post had been issued the week before (F008-5) [X-008]. The two companies fired into the other three trucks from both sides for about eight minutes, took the weapons from the first, and were gone into the cane before the 7th Division's battery at Cai Lay had been given a target. The 247th lost 31 dead and 18 wounded and about 40 weapons, most of them M4A1 carbines and two M249s [X-008]. The post itself lost one man, wounded by the mortar. The district reported 46 of the enemy killed; eight bodies were found at the culvert in the morning, and I believe the 514th lost between ten and fifteen killed [X-008].

Liem wrote new rules the following week, and they are the reason the night belongs in the history. A post's first call would be for fire, not men: every post in the district had its concentrations plotted for the 105mm battery at Cai Lay and the 7th Division's guns at Cai Be, and the call was answered by the guns within four minutes [X-008]. A relief would move by water or at first light, never by road at night on the one road the Front could predict. And a post that could hold behind its wire would be expected to. The rules spread through IV Corps in the spring, carried by the province advisers and by the 7th Division's artillery officers (EV006-1). In the months that followed, attacks on posts within range of the guns in Dinh Tuong fell by about a third, and the Front's district companies turned to the posts beyond the guns' 11 kilometres and to the posts whose batteries were run down at the end of the exchange week (F008-6, F008-16) [X-008].

The rules had a cost that the returns did not show and Liem's adviser did. A platoon that could call down the guns had less reason to leave its wire after dark, and in 1967 the night patrols of the PF platoons in Cai Lay fell by about a third; the post held itself, and the hamlet outside it was walked at night, more often than before, by the Front's tax collectors (F008-12) [X-008]. Ap My Loi held every attack made on it for the rest of the war. Whether the hamlet that bore its name was the government's after dark is a question the post's radio log cannot answer.

Sergeant Muoi had passed the leader's course at Nha Trang in 1961 and the platoon leader's course at the Dinh Tuong centre in 1964, and he kept a listening post of two men on the canal bank 300 metres south of the post every night (EC010-2). At 2241 they reported men moving south in the cane on the far side of the canal, toward the road, and Muoi added it to his report: the mortar, an assault forming to the west, and movement south toward the culvert [X-010]. The district operations room logged it as the third of three lines. Liem read the first two and sent the trucks, because the rule said the reaction within the hour and the post had called; the sergeant had seen the ambush and the decision was not his (F010-8, F010-9) [X-010]. The ambush went as told. The third of Liem's new rules came from Muoi's line in the log: a post's report of movement on the relief route stops the relief until the route is fired on or cleared at first light, and the district's posts were taught, through their platoon leaders, to report the road as well as the wire.

## EV010-2 · The academy at Bien Hoa and the staff sergeant's waiver · 1 Mar 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · The MACV NCO Academy, Bien Hoa, Bien Hoa Province; the infantry battalions of II Field Force Vietnam; the Department of the Army, Washington · NEW · 010
*Initiative: allied*

By the spring of 1966 the American Army in Vietnam was promoting sergeants faster than it could school them for the next grade. The rule that no sergeant became a staff sergeant without the 29-day Basic Leader Course held at home, where the course ran at every division's academy; in Vietnam it meant that a sergeant made in the field went home after a year still a sergeant, and the rifle companies of the 1st and 25th Infantry Divisions were losing their experienced squad leaders to the tour and could not promote the ones they had [X-010]. MACV opened an NCO academy at Bien Hoa on 1 March 1966, four classes of about 150 in parallel on the full schedule, with its land navigation and patrol-base lanes in the controlled ground of the Long Binh complex (EC010-5) [X-010]. By the summer it was graduating about 600 a month against a demand of about 900, and in June MACV asked for authority to waive the course for sergeants in rifle companies in contact (EC010-9). The Department of the Army granted it for six months. By September about one in four promotions to staff sergeant in II Field Force's infantry battalions had been made under the waiver, and the waived staff sergeants of the 25th Division were, by its own inspector's count, the men most often relieved of a squad in the autumn [X-010].

The waiver ended in November 1966. In its place MACV gave the Bien Hoa academy a priority for rifle companies and the Army a deferral: a sergeant could be promoted to staff sergeant in the field and kept the stripe if he finished the course within ninety days. The deferral worked where Bien Hoa had places and became a second waiver where it did not, and through 1967 about one in five promotions in the rifle companies waited one to three months for a seat (F010-13) [X-010]. At home, the academies took about a third of their instructors in 1967 from men back from a tour in Vietnam, who taught the patrol base at night in War Zone C and the trail in Quang Nam in place of the European cases (EC010-6) [X-010]. The cost was the senior NCOs who taught: with the drill sergeants of memorandum 009 and the selected advisory corps, the Army's shortage of experienced sergeants first class and master sergeants came about three to six months earlier than history's and ran somewhat deeper through 1967 (F010-12) [X-010].

## EV010-3 · Operation STEADY HAND · 14–19 Nov 1966 · Cai Be District, Dinh Tuong Province, between Route 4 and the Plain of Reeds; the hamlets of Hau My village · NEW · 010
*Initiative: both*

The rice was the reason for both sides. The Mekong flood of September and October 1966 had drowned much of the Plain of Reeds and the main-season crop in the districts north of Route 4, and the Front's Dinh Tuong provincial committee, its own stocks lost in the water, ordered the 263rd Main Force Battalion in late October to escort its rice levy in the hamlets of Cai Be as the harvest came in [X-010]. The 7th Division, with the province chief, mounted STEADY HAND to cover the government's procurement in the same hamlets: the 2nd and 3rd Battalions of the 11th Regiment, with an M113 troop of the division's armored cavalry, swept north from Route 4 into Hau My village on 14 November to clear the canal lines along which the farmers would move the paddy to the Cai Be market [X-010].

On the afternoon of 16 November the 3rd Battalion's lead company, moving north along a canal dike in file, walked into the 263rd's ambush: two companies in an L along a tree line and a second canal, with an RPD on each arm and a 57mm recoilless rifle at the corner, at 40 to 60 metres [X-010]. The company commander, Captain Nguyen Huu Tam, and both of his forward platoon leaders were hit in the first two minutes. On the historical course of such ambushes, a company that lost its officers on a dike in the open lost about half its men before the rest of the battalion reached it [E: the 7th Division's ambushes of 1963–65]. This one did not. Sergeant First Class Tran Van Loc, the senior platoon sergeant, had passed the senior course at Nha Trang the year before, and his squads were led by two sergeants and four acting squad leaders, corporals first class who had passed the leader's course, because the field commissions of September had taken two of the company's best sergeants to Thu Duc (F010-15, EC010-3) [X-010]. The squads went off the dike into the paddy on the side away from the long arm of the L, each squad leader putting his M249 on the tree line while the riflemen crawled; Loc took the radio, reported the contact and the company's position on the AN/PRC-25 in a form the battalion could plot, and asked for the 105mm M101A1 battery at Cai Be on the tree line at 150 metres beyond his own men [X-010]. The first rounds came at 1534, eleven minutes after the ambush opened. The M113 troop came up the next dike at 1550 with its .50-calibre guns on the tree line, and the 263rd broke contact at about 1610 and went north into the flooded reeds, carrying its wounded [X-010].

The company lost 14 killed and 22 wounded, of about 120, and all its weapons came out with it; on the historical course I would have expected 40 to 60 killed and the company's squad guns and radio left on the dike [X-010]. Eleven of the 263rd's dead were found in the tree line; I believe it lost 20 to 30 killed, and I do not credit the district's report of 74 (A13). The sweep went on to 19 November without further contact. In the following three weeks the government's procurement in Hau My and the two villages beside it bought about 1,400 tonnes of paddy, about twice the province's figure for the same villages in 1965, and the Front's levy there took about a third less than its committee had ordered, by the committee's own figures captured in 1967 [X-010] (DIRMT01-11). The pay of the 3rd Battalion's soldiers, after the devaluation of June, bought about a third less rice than it had in the spring (EV009-3), and two of Loc's acting squad leaders were absent without leave for a week in December to bring their families' harvest in [X-010]. Loc was made a master sergeant in the board's spring list of 1967; Captain Tam survived and returned to the battalion in March.

## P2 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam [H40] and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year [H40]. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December [H9], and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December [H39]. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000 [H39], and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view" [H39]. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi [H]. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general (EV001-6, EV001-7) [X-001]. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside [X-001].

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest (EV002-4, EV002-5) [X-002]. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

The enemy's external supply is measured: the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count, the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what the South's forces need from the North (EV003-3, EV003-4) [X-003]. That knowledge is honest from 1965 rather than from 1970, and it has not changed the rules, the bombing or the strategy. Its main effect on Washington has been to deliver bad news early.

The RVNAF enters 1968 with the rifles it has carried for four years and a Regional Force that the corps commanders want for their own operations, bound since 1967 by the rule that keeps it in its provinces (EV004-2). The American army has had no rifle crisis and no inquiry, and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights; it is the same army in every other respect, fighting the same battles on the enemy's terms.

The bombers have learned two things in two years: that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot (EV005-2, EV005-3), and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle (EV000-36). The second lesson is the one the headquarters remembers going into 1968.

Phung Hoang has a centre in every district and an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, about one in five of them members of district or village committees, and the Front's district administration in the held belt now governs from the contested edge by courier (EV006-2, EV002-4, F006-4) [X-006]. The word Phoenix has been a press story since March 1967 (EV006-4). About 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps; the American battalions have changed over once or twice each as units, have fought better in the contacts they were granted, and have learned at Dien Ban to keep their reliefs off the dark of the moon (EV006-3) [X-006]. None of it has changed who chooses the battle.

The ARVN ends 1967 with about four in five of its battalions commanded by officers the board chose, on eighteen-month commands, with the captain-led battalion nearly gone and the thirty-day review feared and read; about a third fewer of its battalions have been rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, which no report shows because the enemy declined the fights that would have shown it (EV007-2, EV007-3) [X-007]. The 25th Division has a commander from the list and the corps have the commanders they had (EV007-4). The Military Security Service has its first file on the board's officers as a political factor, and COSVN has had every list the board published.

The factories end 1967 producing what the domestic share buys, with the steelworks short of power and the arsenal short of its gunners' trust after Phu Cat (EV008-3, EV008-5) [X-008]. Every post of the held belt has a radio, the Front attacks those that can call about a third less often than those that cannot, and where the guns can reach the posts the first call is for fire (EV008-4) [X-008]. The posts hold themselves better than the hamlets around them.

The ARVN ends 1967 trained to the man and as short of squad leaders as history's; its battalions have lost about a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still lays, beyond what the rifles and the officers saved, and about one in eight fewer battalions has been broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms alone would have left (EV007-3) [X-009]. The drill-sergeant corps has begun to return about 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year to the battalions (F009-4) [X-009]. In the lowlands of Quang Nam and Quang Ngai the mine has replaced the firefight as the enemy's way of killing Americans (EV009-2), and in the districts around Saigon the Front's guerrilla squads are drilled by men the Republic trained (EV009-3).

The ARVN ends 1967 with its squads and platoons led by schooled men: about two in three of the slots the field commissions emptied are held by corporals first class who passed the leader's course, and the squads of the 7th Division held their base of fire at Hau My when their officers fell (EV010-3, EC010-3) [X-010]. I judge that about one in six to one in five fewer ARVN battalions were broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms and the basic course alone would have left, and that the posts of the Delta lost about a tenth to a fifth fewer to night attack than the radio and the trained platoon had already saved (F010-25) [X-010]. None of it shows in a return: the enemy attacked less what kept its routine (F010-19). American sergeants pass the Basic Leader Course at Bien Hoa or wait for it, and the waiver of 1966 has become a ninety-day deferral (EV010-2).

## P3 · Overview

1968 is the year the war turns, and it turns on a battle the enemy lost. In the first hours of 31 January PAVN and VC forces attack Saigon, Hue and most of the provincial capitals of the South at once, breaking the Tet truce and every assumption of the American progress campaign. By the measures of the battlefield the General Offensive fails: there is no general uprising, the cities are retaken, Hue after a month, and the Viet Cong lose the larger part of the cadres and local forces they had built over a decade. By the measures of politics it succeeds beyond Hanoi's hopes. Within two months the President has refused Westmoreland's request for 206,000 more men, halted most of the bombing of the North, offered negotiations and withdrawn from the presidential race. US deaths by hostile action reach 14,592 in the year, the highest of the war [H40], and US strength in Vietnam stands at about 537,000 at its end [H40].

The phase has three offensives, in January–March, May–June and August–September, and three American responses. The first is military: Khe Sanh is held under the heaviest bombardment in the history of air warfare, the cities are cleared, and a new commander, General Creighton W. Abrams, takes over in June. The second is political: the decision of 31 March caps the American commitment and opens talks in Paris. The third is the countryside: with the VC's main and local forces weakened and its infrastructure exposed in the offensive, the government and CORDS move into the vacuum, with a general mobilization in June, the Phoenix programme and an Accelerated Pacification Campaign from November. The year ends with the bombing of the North stopped, a new president elected on a promise to end the war, and the war's centre of gravity shifting from the American divisions to the territorial forces and the hamlets.

The human cost in the South is the heaviest of the war to date. The offensive and the counter-attacks create about 630,000 new refugees, on top of about 800,000 displaced before; more than 70,000 homes are destroyed; by the end of the year about one South Vietnamese in twelve lives in a refugee camp [H39]. The government counts 14,300 civilian dead and 24,000 wounded in the first phase alone [H39].

In this war the year runs on the same dates and turns the same way; Tet is still decided in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington (EV000-45) [X-001]. The difference is in the Delta and in the hamlets. The Front enters the offensive with a smaller base in the titled districts of the Bassac and the coastal Centre, recruits fewer men there in 1968 (EV000-52), and loses the towns of the western Delta faster because the hamlets around them warn the garrisons and do not hide the attackers (EV000-42). When the government and CORDS move into the countryside after the offensives, they move into hamlets where about 900,000 hectares are already titled, and the Commission audits the new Phoenix programme against the land register (EV000-54) [X-001]. The American political calendar is not changed by any of it.

The police changes Tet in the same places and in the same way: in the held belt of the Delta the Front's urban cadres are known and its guides do not arrive (EV000-42), and in Hue its lists are better because the Republic's own census fed them (EV000-44). Phoenix, here, is only the name Washington learned for a programme whose district centres had run since 1964 without quotas and under an audit (EV000-54).

Above all this the radar watches the border, and at Khe Sanh it gives the headquarters and the White House the most complete picture of an enemy concentration either has ever held, while the forces attacking the cities move among the Tet crowds where no radar can tell them from anyone else (EV000-41). After the halt it measures exactly how the North uses it (EV003-5), and Commando Hunt opens with a second ledger beside the Air Force's claims (EV000-55).

The ARVN and the territorials go into Tet with the weapons the historical ARVN received only after it. In the district towns, which the first wave attacked with local forces, about a third of the attacks never get inside the compound walls; in the cities, where the PAVN and the main force fight block by block, the rifles lower the cost and do not shorten the battles (EV000-42, EV000-44). The mobilization of June arms its new territorials from stock within the month (EV000-51).

In the hills of Quang Tri the siege of Khe Sanh is measured every night and its trenches destroyed before they reach the wire; the PAVN pulls back about two weeks early, at a much heavier price, and the garrison loses about a third fewer men (EV000-41). It is the most complete American victory of the war on the ground and changes nothing in Washington; the brigade pulled from Thua Thien's hamlets to win it is missing when the Front returns to them. Abrams refuses to make the victory a doctrine of border bases and closes Khe Sanh on the historical date (EV000-51), and Commando Hunt opens against a panhandle net built since 1966 to be cut (EV000-55).

The district centres change Tet in the held belt in one more way: the leaders who came into the Delta towns to direct the uprising had been charted for three years, and a third of them were taken in February (EV000-42). In Hue the province centre's informant cards burn in the first hour and the district centres' do not (EV000-44). The American battalions that fight the offensive have been together for months, and the advisers beside the ARVN have been in their posts for a year; the battles are shorter at the edges and the same at their centres.

The officer reforms meet Tet at the battalions and the companies, which is where they had worked. The ARVN units that fought in the towns had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and in IV and III Corps they recalled their men from leave faster and fought the first day closer to their strength (EV000-42) [X-007]. The mobilization of June finds its platoon leaders without the historical crash course, because the field commissions of 1966–68 had already made about 3,000 lieutenants from the army's sergeants, and the new territorials pay for it in sergeants (EV000-51). COSVN, reading its failure in the towns, opens a proselytizing campaign aimed at the officers the board had passed over (EV000-52). At Hue and Khe Sanh the battles are the historical ones, and the decision of 31 March is unchanged.

The private soldier in Tet is trained, and the mobilization of June finds a training base that will not send men to units untrained. In the district towns the RF companies that hold their compound walls are firing as the territorial course taught them, and in the towns and the cities the wounded of both allied armies are alive in larger numbers in the morning (EV000-42). In June the Joint General Staff wants the ten-week course cut to six to put the mobilization's men into the field by August; the training command holds it at ten by running the centres in two shifts, and the new battalions and territorial companies reach the field about a month later than history's and trained, with a man who has held the rank of sergeant in about four new PF platoons in five rather than two in three (EV000-51). The Front's third offensive goes, as its earlier offensives had, for what the course did not reach: the People's Self-Defense Force (EV009-4).

The sergeants of both armies meet Tet schooled. In the Delta the posts of Cai Lay that kept their listening posts out saw the 514th Battalion moving on My Tho before it arrived and were left alone (EV010-4); in the mobilization of June the empty squads and platoons are given to corporals who have passed the leader's course, and the share of new PF platoons without a schooled leader falls to about one in fifteen (EV000-51) [X-010]. The price is the academy's: its course is cut to four weeks for the duration, and its best instructors go to the new platoons.

## EV000-51 · Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned · 10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005,007,009
*Initiative: allied*

On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff [H50] [H]. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces [H50]. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy [H50].

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended [H62]. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969 [H62]. Historically the expansion outran the rifles: the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better [H]. In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment, about 260,000 carbines already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June against the year's expected growth, and were on the ranges within a month of induction [X-004]. The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but sergeants: the training centres could zero a carbine in an afternoon and could not make a squad leader in less than a year, and Venn's successor reported in September that about a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before (F004-3) [X-004]. The old weapons the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law [X-004].

On Khe Sanh the decision was harder than it had been historically, because in this war the base had been a victory rather than an ordeal. Westmoreland, in his last weeks, argued for keeping it as the anchor of a line of border bases under the siege box, and the III Marine Amphibious Force's staff drew up a plan for three more such bases along Route 9 and the Laotian border, to be held by battalions drawn from the coastal districts (F005-14) [X-005]. Abrams refused it on 15 June. His reason, in a message to Wheeler that his staff kept, was that a base the enemy would not attack was a base that held nothing, and that after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one [X-005]. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July [H46]. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ. Here it was read in Hanoi correctly, as a decision by an American commander that the siege the PAVN had lost was not worth inviting again, and in the American press, less correctly, as a retreat from a battle won [X-005].

The officers for the mobilization came differently. Historically the Thu Duc candidate course was cut from 37 weeks to 24 in June 1968 to commission the platoon leaders the expansion needed [M007·S2]. In this war the cut was to 32 weeks, and the gap was filled by the field commissions: about 3,000 sergeants had been commissioned since 1966 and about 2,500 more were commissioned in the second half of 1968, after the branch basic course that the decree did not allow to be waived (F007-12, F007-22) [X-007]. The cost was in the ranks they came from. The sergeant shortage that Venn's successor reported in September, a third of the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank, was in part the field commission's price: the army had made lieutenants of the sergeants who would have led those platoons [X-007]. The board's lists governed the new RF companies from their first month, and the new PF platoons not at all.

The mobilization met a training base that would not send men to units untrained, and the cost was a month. Historically the RVNAF in 1968 sent some draftees straight to units without basic training, and the ARVN basic course was cut from twelve weeks to nine to put men through faster [M009·S9]. In this war, on 21 June, the Joint General Staff ordered the course cut to six weeks for the duration of the mobilization; the Central Training Command, with Coyle's successor and the Muster Service's chief behind it, answered that six weeks would leave out the Forge and the field week, the part the new soldier most needed, and proposed instead to run the 33 national and division centres in two daily shifts, which raised their capacity from about 65,000 to about 105,000 in cycle (EC009-7) [M009·S9] [X-009]. The Joint General Staff accepted on 28 June. About 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended; about 40,000 of them waited three to six weeks at the centres for a cycle to open, and the new battalions and territorial companies reached the field about a month later than they did historically, after the third offensive had begun rather than before it [X-009]. They reached it trained. The new RF companies and PF platoons of the summer went through the six-week territorial course as platoons, on the M4A1 they had drawn from the depots, and came back to their districts in September and October rather than August [X-009].

The sergeants came differently too, and the result was not the one memorandum 007 predicted. The field commissions had made lieutenants of about 5,500 of the army's best sergeants by the end of 1968 (C007-3), and on the historical training base that would have left the new PF platoons with no one who had held the rank. In this war the drill-sergeant corps had been returning sergeants to the battalions since 1966, about 650–1,800 a year, and in July 1968 the training command released about 1,100 more from the centres' second shift to the new territorial platoons, each a man who had trained a platoon of recruits for two years (F009-4) [X-009]. Venn's successor reported in September that about a fifth of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank of sergeant, rather than about a third [X-009]. The People's Self-Defense Force, which the law raised in the same summer and which the course did not reach, was given its Garands and carbines and a few days of instruction by the district chiefs, as historically (F009-6, EV009-4).

The schooled corporal filled what was left. The leader's course had been taught to corporals first class since 1957, regular and territorial, and in 1968 about two in three of the corporals first class in the regular battalions and about half of those in the RF companies had passed it [X-010]. The training command gave the new PF platoons their leaders from that pool: where no sergeant could be found, the platoon was given a corporal first class who had passed the course and had served a year in an RF company, as acting platoon leader (EC010-3). Of the fifth of new platoons with no one who had held the rank of sergeant, about two in three got such a man, and Venn's successor reported in September that about one PF platoon in fifteen had no leader who had passed the course [X-010]. The academy paid. The Joint General Staff ordered the leader's course cut to three weeks for the mobilization; the training command, on the precedent of the basic course, kept the field week and cut the classroom, and the course ran at four weeks from July 1968 to June 1969, with the academy's second shift taught by instructors who had been platoon sergeants in 1967 [X-010]. In the regular battalions, about two in three of the squads the commissions had emptied were held by acting leaders who had passed the course (F010-25).

## EV009-4 · The self-defence hamlets · 17 Aug – 31 Oct 1968 · Duc Hue and Trang Bang Districts, Hau Nghia Province; Can Duoc and Rach Kien, Long An Province; Cai Be and Cai Lay Districts, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 009
*Initiative: enemy*

The third offensive went for what the course did not reach, and in the provinces around Saigon that was the People's Self-Defense Force. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee, planning the offensive of August after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, ordered its subregions to avoid the RF and PF posts that had held in February and May and to strike the new self-defence groups of the mobilization law, whose members were townsmen and farmers with a Garand and three days' instruction (F009-6) [X-009]. The Front's subregion commands around Saigon had a second reason that the Military Affairs Committee did not need to state: every self-defence group destroyed told the hamlet that the government's new law armed it without protecting it [X-009].

The attacks began on the night of 17–18 August and ran through September. In Duc Hue and Trang Bang the Front's local-force companies attacked about 30 self-defence groups in their hamlets, usually at night and from two sides, and overran about two-thirds of them; in Long An, around Can Duoc and Rach Kien, where NIGHT LANTERN had left the posts strong and the infrastructure weak, it attacked fewer and lost more, because the PF platoons beside the hamlets came out to the self-defence groups' calls (EV002-4) [X-009]. In Cai Be and Cai Lay in Dinh Tuong it went for the self-defence groups in the hamlets between the posts, where no platoon was near enough to come [X-009]. Across the three provinces, by the end of October, I estimate that about 600–900 members of the self-defence groups were killed and about 1,500–2,000 of their weapons taken, almost all of them Garands and carbines of 1950 that the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 (C004-4) [X-009]. In the same weeks the RF and PF posts of the three provinces were attacked about a third less often than on the historical course, and held about as often as they had in May [X-009].

The trained PF platoon could not give the self-defence group its own training, but at Rach Kien it showed what it could do for it. On the night of 2–3 September a company of the Front's 506th Battalion attacked the self-defence group of a hamlet a kilometre from the PF post at the Rach Kien canal bridge; the group's leader, a schoolteacher, fired a flare as the PF corporal had shown him, and the platoon came across the paddies in sixteen minutes, in pairs, firing and moving as the territorial course had taught it, and broke the attack at the hamlet gate [X-009]. Two self-defence members died and four were wounded, all four with tourniquets the PF soldiers put on. In the next month the district chief of Can Duoc sent his PF platoon sergeants, the men the drill-sergeant corps had released in July, to give the self-defence groups of the district three evenings each of the first ten minutes, which no order required [X-009]. It was the only place in the three provinces where anyone did, and the only one where the groups held through the autumn.

The PF platoon sergeants whom the Can Duoc district chief sent to the self-defence groups were men who had passed the platoon leader's course, which taught a sergeant to train his own platoon and anyone the district put under him (EC010-2). On 24 October Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, the province chief, made Can Duoc's practice the province's: every PF platoon leader in Long An gave the self-defence groups within two kilometres of his post three evenings of the first ten minutes and the flare signal, and was rated on it [X-010]. In November and December the Front's local companies attacked eleven self-defence groups in Long An and overran four, against about two in three in Hau Nghia in the autumn [X-010]. Hau Nghia's province chief did not follow until February 1969. The difference was a province chief's order and a platoon leader who had been taught to teach.

## EV010-4 · The posts of Cai Lay at Tet · 30 Jan – 2 Feb 1968 · Cai Lay and Cai Be Districts, Dinh Tuong Province; the post at Ap My Loi, My Phuoc Tay; the posts along the canal and Route 4 east to My Tho · NEW · 010
*Initiative: enemy*

The Front's battalions came through Cai Lay to attack My Tho, and most of the posts on the way were left alone. The 514th Local Force Battalion and the 261st and 263rd Main Force Battalions moved east toward the province capital on the nights of 29–31 January, along the canals north of Route 4, and the Dinh Tuong command's order was to isolate the posts on the route rather than take them, because the battalions needed to reach My Tho intact [H] [X-010]. Of about 34 PF posts in Cai Lay District, three were attacked and nine more were mortared to keep their platoons inside their wire [X-010]. The post at Ap My Loi was not attacked at all. Its two-man listening post on the canal bank, kept out every night since 1964, reported at 2310 on 30 January a column of about 200 men with heavy weapons moving east on the far bank, and Sergeant Le Van Muoi sent the report up with the count and the time; it reached the 7th Division at Cai Lay at 0140, and with two other posts' reports it told the division that a battalion was going for My Tho, not for the district town (F010-2) [X-010]. It changed nothing at My Tho, which the 261st, 263rd and 514th attacked at 0310 on 31 January and which was retaken in three days by the ARVN 7th Division and the US 9th Division's riverine battalions, as historically [H]. It did keep the 247th RF Company off Route 4 that night, under Liem's rule of 1967 (EV008-4) [X-010].

The post that fell was the one whose leader had held the platoon for three months. At Ap Hau Thanh, on the canal four kilometres east of Ap My Loi, the platoon's sergeant had been commissioned in the autumn of 1967 and the platoon was led by an acting leader, a corporal first class who had passed the leader's course and had not yet been given the platoon leader's course (F010-15) [X-010]. He kept no listening post out on the night of 31 January, because the post had not been attacked in two years; a company of the 514th came over the wall at 0200 with satchel charges, and the post lost nine killed and its radio and weapons [X-010]. Across the two districts in the three nights, the posts that kept their listening posts out were mortared and left; the one that did not was taken. The enemy chose which, and he chose by watching the routine (F010-19) [X-010].

## P3 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1968 the war has changed shape without changing direction. The VC in the South have lost much of their main and local forces and many of their cadres in the three offensives, and their recruitment has collapsed; the main-force war is now predominantly a PAVN war, and the parallel administration in the hamlets, though intact in much of the country, is under more pressure than at any time since 1959. The government in Saigon has survived Tet, mobilized, and begun with CORDS to push into the countryside; ARVN units fought better in February than anyone expected, though they remain dependent on American firepower and logistics. Against these gains, the United States has decided not to escalate further and to seek a negotiated way out; the bombing of the North has ended; and the American public's tolerance for casualties, measured in a year in which 14,592 Americans died by hostile action, has been spent [H40].

In Hanoi, the leadership has paid heavily for the General Offensive and turned back to protracted war for the moment, but has won what it most needed: an American president who will not reinforce and a successor committed to withdrawal. The Paris talks exist; the shape of the table is being settled. Phase IV opens with a new administration in Washington, a war in the countryside that the South is better placed to fight than it has been for years, and an American army whose withdrawal is now a matter of timing.

In this war the countryside is better placed than that. About 900,000 hectares have been titled, the Front's Delta recruitment has fallen from a lower base, and its Delta battalions are filling with northerners earlier [X-001]. The American decision of 1968 is unchanged, and so is its timing.

The National Police numbers about 95,000 and the Special Branch has buried its first constables of the Tet battles; the infrastructure in the held belt is weaker than anywhere since 1959, and every file it keeps is now held under a destruction plan [X-002].

The enemy's rear enters Phase IV counted: the surge after the halt is measured, the first dry season of Commando Hunt is scored against the radar as well as the claims (EV003-5, EV000-55) [X-003], and the American decision of 1968 is unchanged by any of it.

The RVNAF ends 1968 larger and armed throughout with weapons the historical one was still waiting for; its new territorials have rifles and too few sergeants (EV000-51) [X-004]. The gap the rearmament opened over history is now closing from the historical side, as the M16 reaches the ARVN in 1968 and the territorials in 1969.

The PAVN leaves 1968 having learned that it does not lay siege to a position the Americans can measure; that is the one way of choosing its battles it has lost [X-005]. It chooses all the others. The panhandle is harder to cut than history's, because the bombers taught its engineers how (EV000-55).

Phung Hoang enters 1969 with five years of files, an audited count the press has learned to quote, and no quotas; the Front's Delta committees have lost a third of the leaders they sent into the towns (EV000-42, EV000-54) [X-006]. The Hue district files have shown what the national file would be worth to the enemy (EV000-44). The selected advisers are in every field post, the first of them on their second tours, and the American battalions have fought the year as units.

The ARVN's battalions come out of 1968 with commanders who have held them through Tet and a board that has not stopped working; the mobilization's platoons have lieutenants who were sergeants and squads that have no sergeants (EV000-51) [X-007]. COSVN has opened a campaign among the officers the board passed over, and the Military Security Service has a second file on the board's officers, this time as targets (EV000-52).

The RVNAF ends 1968 with the mobilization's soldiers trained to the same standard as the regulars of 1957, and about a month late in the field for it; the People's Self-Defense Force, which no course reached, has paid for the difference in Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong (EV000-51, EV009-4) [X-009]. About 2,150 rather than 2,300 ARVN soldiers died in the first wave, and the hospitals of both armies are full of men who would have died on the historical course and are alive without a leg (F009-16) [X-009].

The RVNAF ends 1968 with its new platoons led, in about fourteen cases in fifteen, by a man who has passed the leader's course, against about four in five on the course of memorandum 009 and about two in three historically (EV000-51) [X-010]. The academy's course is four weeks for the duration and its field week intact. The posts of Cai Lay held Tet by not being attacked (EV010-4).

## P4 · Overview

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971 [H40]. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 [D] to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971 [H40]. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest [H62]. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The revelation of My Lai, the Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the trial of Calley and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win [H]. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington [X-001]. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001]. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle (EV001-8) [X-001].

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses (EV002-6). The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective (EV000-66); Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season (EV000-68); and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically (EV000-71).

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open (EV000-58). Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy (EV000-68). In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home (EV000-71).

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless (EV000-60). The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election (EV002-6). And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute (EV000-72). The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

The officer reforms reach their limit in this phase, and it is the limit their memorandum named: the American lever. As the aid conditions are dropped with Vietnamization, the palace makes the board's general-officer list advisory in January 1970, keeps the board for everything below colonel, and in December 1970 answers a petition of colonels of the classes of 1957–59 by moving about forty of the army's best regimental and battalion commanders to staff and schools (EV007-5). Lam Son 719 goes into Laos weeks later with new commanders in two of the 1st Division's regiments and an Airborne brigade, and fails for the historical reasons, which were decided at corps and in Saigon (EV000-71). Thieu's election of 1971 is run against the background of the colonels' petition, read into the record of the Lower House (EV000-73). The battalions themselves are the best-led the Republic ever had.

Under the withdrawal, the Republic's own plants close the loop that Vietnamization would otherwise have left open: the American equipment handed over in 1969–71 goes into rebuild works and a signal depot that have run since 1966, and by the end of 1971 the RVNAF makes about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, most of its batteries and all of its wire, filled and powered from abroad (EV008-6). In the Delta the subsector advisers hand the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets before they leave (EV008-7). And the Directorate of Defence Industry goes to the palace with the Board, the list, the file and the Commission, keeping its output and losing its honesty (EV002-6).

The schooled sergeant meets the drawdown in both armies. In the American units that stay longest, the sergeant who enforces standards is the man the worst soldiers resent and some of them try to kill, and the schooled sergeant holds more squads together than history's without stopping the decay (EV010-5). In the Republic the gate on NCO promotion is the first of the army's merit rules that the palace breaks, by the National Day decree of 1 November 1970, in the same months it takes the board and the Directorate (EV010-6) [X-010]. The academy survives the advisers, because it was Vietnamese from the start, and the territorial platoon leaders who learned to call fire on a grid call Vietnamese guns when the Americans go (EV008-7).

## EV008-7 · The handover of the call at Long My · 1 Feb 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · Long My District, Chuong Thien Province; the Vi Thanh sector operations centre; the district's territorial artillery platoon · NEW · 008
*Initiative: allied*

When the Americans left the districts of the Delta, the call for help stayed behind, and Long My is the place to see it. Major Huynh Cong Tri, a list chief who had held the district since 1966, had forty-one PF posts and two Regional Force companies along the Cai Lon and the roads to Vi Thanh, every post with a set on the district net and every set made at Go Vap [X-008]. Since 1967, under the rules that came out of Cai Lay (EV008-4), each post's first call had gone to a gun: until 1970 most often to an American battery or helicopter fire team through the subsector adviser's radio, and from February 1970 to the district's own two-gun 105mm territorial artillery platoon, formed that month on howitzers rebuilt at Bien Hoa and fed with Bien Hoa's rounds (EC008-10) [X-008].

Major Daniel R. Kearsley, the selected adviser who held the subsector from 1969, spent his last year handing the call over (P006c). Every post's concentrations were re-plotted for the district platoon and the 21st Division's guns at Vi Thanh; the post commanders called them in Vietnamese, on Vietnamese sets, to Vietnamese gunners, with Kearsley listening and not speaking; and from September 1970 the American fire teams were called only through the district operations room, at Tri's request [X-008]. On the night of 23–24 February 1971, two months before Kearsley left, a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion came against the post at Xeo Mat at 0110; the post called at 0112, the district platoon fired its first round at 0117, and by 0140 it had put about sixty rounds on the post's three concentrations and the company had withdrawn, leaving four dead in the canal [X-008]. Nobody in the chain was American.

The handover worked because what it handed over was Vietnamese at both ends: the set, the battery, the gun and the round. It did not work for anything that had been American at one end. The B-52Js and the coordinate cell that had broken the sieges of the larger war were not handed to anyone, and no district could call them (POS006-3, POS005-2). When the advisers left Long My in June 1971, the district kept every post it had and the Front kept the canal at night between them, as it had before (F008-12) [X-008]. Tri's returns, which kept all forty-one posts through the offensive of 1972, began in that handover (EVMT01-2).

The post commanders who called the district platoon in Vietnamese had all passed the platoon leader's course at the Chuong Thien and Phong Dinh centres, which taught a sergeant to call fire on a grid fixed by map and compass, and on the night at Xeo Mat Sergeant Danh Son, a Khmer Krom platoon leader of the post, gave the district platoon its adjustment in two corrections (EC010-2) [X-010]. The course was the American one, and it taught the American habit of fixing the enemy and calling the guns (F010-24). At Long My the habit found a Vietnamese gun at the other end, which is the only place in this war where the dependence the course carried down to the squad was answered by the Republic's own fire [X-010].

## EV010-5 · The sergeants of the drawdown · 1 Jan 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · US Army and Marine Corps units in I, II and III Corps, especially the 23rd (Americal) Infantry Division at Chu Lai and LZ Baldy, Quang Tin and Quang Nam Provinces; the NCO academies in the United States · NEW · 010
*Initiative: neither*

The American NCO corps that fought the drawdown was schooled for its grades to the man and too thin to hold the army it led. The sergeants first class and master sergeants of 1969 were on their second or third tours; the staff sergeants and sergeants of the rifle companies were men of two or three years' service, many of them graduates of the NCO Candidate Course, who had taken the Basic Leader Course's lanes as its last module or at Bien Hoa (EV010-2) [X-010]. In the units that stayed longest, the war they were asked to lead was one their soldiers had stopped believing in. Drug use, racial violence and refusals rose through 1969–71 on their historical course, and the Army and the Marine Corps recorded about 600–800 fraggings and attempted fraggings in those three years, most of them against officers and NCOs [E: commonly cited service figures for 1969–71].

The schooled sergeant was both the man who held a squad together and the man its worst soldiers resented most. On 14 March 1970 Staff Sergeant Raymond L. Ochoa, a platoon sergeant of a battalion of the 196th Infantry Brigade at LZ Baldy, who had passed the Advanced Leader Course and had used its counselling and its standards to take four men of his platoon off the line for heroin, was wounded by a fragmentation grenade rolled under his bunk; he lived, and two of the four were convicted [X-010]. The 23rd Division's inspector general counted in 1970 that the platoons whose sergeants held the course and had led them for six months or more had about a third fewer drug cases and refusals than those whose sergeants had been waived or deferred (F010-18) [X-010]. That is the provision at its true weight: it held some squads together, and it did not stop the decay, which came from the war's politics and the draft rather than from the sergeant (F010-10). The MACV academy at Bien Hoa graduated its last class on 30 June 1971 and was handed to the ARVN as a division school [X-010].

## EV010-6 · The National Day decree and the gate · 1 Nov 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · The Presidential Palace and the Joint General Staff, Saigon; the National NCO Academy at Dong De, Nha Trang; the corps and division headquarters · NEW · 010
*Initiative: allied*

The gate on NCO promotion was the first of the army's merit rules that the palace broke by decree, and it broke it for the soldiers' gratitude. On 1 November 1970, National Day, President Thieu promoted every enlisted man of the RVNAF one grade, regular and territorial, without exception for the courses, as the government had done on national occasions before (M010·S11) [X-010]. The decree came in the same months as the general-officer list's demotion to advice and the Directorate's capture (EV007-5, EV002-6), and it was judged in Saigon as they were, as a matter of the President's gift. It made about 40,000–60,000 men sergeants or senior sergeants without the course for the grade they now held [X-010].

The training command, under Brigadier General Phan Van Huong and with Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc's academy behind it, did not contest the stripes. It contested the next promotion: on 20 November it ruled, under the 1957 regulation the decree had not repealed, that no man promoted by the decree could be promoted again until he had passed the course for the grade the decree had given him, and it opened the academy's second shift to them [X-010]. The Joint General Staff let the ruling stand because the corps commanders, who wanted the stripes for their men, did not care about the next board. By June 1971 about half the men the decree had made sergeants had passed their course, and the other half held a rank they had not been schooled for and could not rise beyond [X-010]. The gate survived the palace as a Vietnamese rule, enforced by the training command and the boards below colonel, after the American lever behind the board, the Directorate and the Commission had lapsed (F010-5, F010-16). Of the four institutions that fell to the palace in 1970–71, it was the one whose damage was repaired within a year, because nobody in the palace wanted it and nobody in the army needed it gone (DIRMT01-5) [X-010]. The academy's last American advisers left Dong De in March 1971; Rourke's successor reported that the course was taught as he had found it.

## P4 · How the phase ends

At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving [H40]. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before [H62]. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed (EV000-65) [X-001]. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it (EV001-8) [X-001]. These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal [H56] [H]. The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent (EV002-6) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

The radar's ledger has made the interdiction debate honest and changed nothing it was about: the Trail is larger at the end of 1971 than ever, the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia is mapped and open, and the aircraft that count both will leave with the Americans (EV000-68) [X-003].

The ARVN's I Corps ends the phase believing, on the evidence of Lolo and Sophia, that a besieged base under American bombers holds (EV000-71). It is true, and it is the belief that will cost it most when the bombers go [X-005].

Phung Hoang ends 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon, serving the President as well as the war (EV000-72, EV002-6) [X-006]. About 3,500 selected advisers remain, with the territorials and the province teams more than with the divisions; the American battalions have gone home whole and their discipline has decayed on its historical course (EV000-60) [X-006].

The board ends 1971 as the machinery of the army below colonel and nothing above it: battalions and regiments are commanded by officers it chose, for terms it sets, and the divisions and corps by the President's choices (EV007-5) [X-007]. The colonels who asked for the list back are in the schools and the staffs. The battalions that went into Laos came out of it better than the operation did (EV000-71).

The RVNAF enters 1972 with a closed rebuild loop and a domestic share that covers about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its batteries, under a Directorate the palace now runs (EV008-6, EV002-6) [X-008]. The districts of the Delta call Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets (EV008-7). Nobody can see the base's weight in a year of free American supply, and it has not yet been tested by a year without it.

The NCO academy at Nha Trang ends 1971 Vietnamese, as it began, with its American advisers gone and its course whole; the gate on promotion has been broken once by decree and half restored by the training command, and about half the sergeants the decree made have taken their course since (EV010-6) [X-010]. The American NCO corps leaves Vietnam schooled and spent, its best sergeants among the dead of the fraggings as well as the fighting (EV010-5).
