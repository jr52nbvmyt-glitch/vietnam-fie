## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I took over on 1 January 1964 was an advisory war being lost, and it is still the war I took over. The coup of November 1963 had emptied the provinces of their chiefs, the Front held most of the countryside after dark, and the ARVN fought the enemy's main force on ground the enemy chose. What has changed, ten provisions in, is the Republic's interior. On the first day it decreed land to the tiller, set up an independent commission against corruption, gave itself a rural police and chiefs chosen by examination, and put a Phung Hoang committee over the new Special Branch. Above the war, two U-2S and four Global Hawks gave this headquarters the first honest picture of the enemy's rear, and B-52Js with guided bombs waited on Guam for targets Washington would let them strike. In the ranks, every soldier carried a modern carbine, every regular squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman, and every post of the held belt a radio made in Saigon.

Three things reached back past the first day, and this memorandum adds the third. Since 1957 every officer commissioned had been educated for his grade, and every private trained to the standard of 2026. Since 1957 every sergeant had been schooled for his grade too, in both armies, regular and territorial, at the academy at Dong De above Nha Trang and the division and territorial schools, or at his own service's schools in the United States, and none had been promoted without the course. The academy began 1964 in its eighth year (EV010-1). It did not make one more sergeant than history made. It meant that the squad and the platoon, where the trained private met the educated lieutenant, were led by men who had been taught the job, and that when a squad had no sergeant it could be given a corporal who had.

The first year showed both the gain and the price. The Republic's governments fell and rose four times before the summer of 1965, and the land and police reforms survived each coup because each new junta found them cheaper to keep. The Front answered the posts by killing the best district chiefs and the best constables, and in Can Giuoc it killed the families who had taken titles. In the field the general reserve was still being broken. At Binh Gia on the last day of 1964 the 271st Regiment ambushed the 4th Marine Battalion in the rubber and killed its commander in the first minutes, as it had historically, and the battalion came out because its first sergeants took the companies when their captains fell and closed them into perimeters that held (EV000-9). At Dong Xoai in June 1965 the 7th Airborne's survivors were brought to Thuan Loi by first sergeants and acting squad leaders after their captains were hit (EV000-18). Neither battle went the other way. The enemy chose both fields, and no sergeant chose a landing zone. The price was paid at the academy: in the year the army could least spare them, about 1,300–2,500 of its best sergeants were teaching, on top of the drill sergeants at Quang Trung (F010-11).

By the summer of 1965 the advisory war was over. The Americans came in strength, the Marines to Da Nang and the 173rd Airborne Brigade to Bien Hoa, then the 1st Cavalry Division to An Khe, and the war became the main-force war of 1965–67. The Americans won their battles and did not change the war, at the Ia Drang, in the Bong Son plain and along the DMZ, and the enemy declined every fight he did not want. The measured siege became his worst battle, from Con Thien on, because the B-52J and the coordinate cell could find the trenches he dug around a fixed position; nothing found a regiment that moved at night under canopy. In the countryside the provisions of 1964 did their slow work. Phung Hoang broke the Front's district committees in the held belt before Tet, three years ahead of history, and the land programme titled most of the tenanted rice land of the Delta.

The sergeants worked under all of it. The American Army promoted sergeants faster than it could school them for the next grade and opened an academy at Bien Hoa in March 1966 to do it in theater; when the academy filled, it waived the course for a season, found the waived staff sergeants the men most often relieved, and replaced the waiver with a ninety-day deferral (EV010-2). In the ARVN the officer reforms were filling the battalions with board-chosen commanders, and the field commissions were taking the best sergeants to Thu Duc to make them lieutenants. The squads they left were given to corporals first class who had passed the leader's course. On 16 November 1966, in the rice fields of Hau My in Cai Be, where the flood had drowned the Front's stocks and both sides had come for the harvest, the 263rd Main Force Battalion ambushed a company of the 7th Division's 11th Regiment on a canal dike and hit its captain and both forward platoon leaders in two minutes (EV010-3). Sergeant First Class Tran Van Loc took the company. Its squads, two of them under acting leaders, went off the dike into the paddy, put their squad guns on the tree line, and called the battery at Cai Be onto it eleven minutes after the ambush opened; the company lost 14 killed where history would have lost 40 to 60, and the government bought twice the paddy in those villages that winter. That is the provision's best effect, and it was invisible: no return showed a company not destroyed.

The posts of the Delta showed it the same way. At Ap My Loi, north of Cai Lay, Sergeant Le Van Muoi kept two men on the canal bank every night, and in March 1967 they saw the ambush being laid for his relief and he reported it; the district chief, Major Liem, read the first two lines of the report and sent the trucks, and the 247th RF Company lost 31 men at the culvert (EV008-4). The sergeant had seen it and the decision was not his. Liem's rules of the next week, that a post's first call is for fire and that a report of movement on the relief route stops the relief, were written partly from Muoi's third line. By the end of 1967 I judged that about one in six to one in five fewer ARVN battalions had been broken than the officer reforms and the basic course alone would have left, and that the Delta's posts had lost a tenth to a fifth fewer to night attack than the radio and the trained platoon had already saved (F010-25). The enemy read all of it from the academy's own manuals, which he had held since 1964, and fought what kept the routine less often (F010-20).

Tet came on its historical dates and turned on the historical battles: Saigon, Hue, Khe Sanh and Washington. Khe Sanh was measured every night and the PAVN pulled back early at a heavy price; the district towns of the Delta held better than history's because their posts had radios, their privates were trained and their sergeants schooled. At Cai Lay the battalions marching on My Tho left most posts alone, and the one that fell was the one whose new acting leader kept no listening post (EV010-4). Then the mobilization of June raised the army by a quarter of a million men and emptied it of sergeants, because the field commissions had taken the best of them. History left a third of the new Popular Forces platoons without a man who had held the rank; the basic course's returning drill sergeants cut that to a fifth; and in this war the corporals first class who had passed the leader's course took most of the rest, so that about one platoon in fifteen had no leader who had been taught the job (EV000-51). That is the plainest single number this memorandum adds to the war, and it is a judgment, not a count.

None of it changed the American decision of 1968. The President stopped the reinforcement and the bombing, a successor committed to withdrawal was elected, and the war became the war of the drawdown. In the American units that stayed longest, drugs, refusals and violence against their own leaders rose on their historical course, and the schooled sergeant was both the man who held some squads together and the man the worst soldiers resented most (EV010-5). In the Republic, the institutions built behind the American lever fell to the palace in 1970–71 as the lever lapsed: the general-officer list, the Selection Board, the Directorate of Defence Industry and the Commission. The NCO gate fell with them by the National Day decree of 1 November 1970, which made about 40,000–60,000 men sergeants without their course, and it was the only one repaired within a year, by the training command, because it was a Vietnamese rule that nobody in the palace wanted and nobody in the army needed gone (EV010-6). The academy's American advisers left in 1971 and the course went on as they had found it.

From here the line does not bend. In 1972 the Easter Offensive was stopped at An Loc and Kontum by American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts, and lost at Quang Tri by the orders of the corps and the division. The schooled sergeants on the perimeter at An Loc called fire the Americans supplied; at Quang Tri they retreated on orders no sergeant gave. The Paris Agreement took the Americans out in 1973 and left the PAVN in the South. The aid cut of 1974 fell on an army built for American fire, and the American course had carried that habit down to the squad (F010-24); at Long My, where the district had its own guns, the platoon leaders called Vietnamese fire, and nowhere else did the habit find a Vietnamese answer at the other end (EV008-7). In March 1975 the palace ordered the Highlands abandoned and I Corps collapsed on the road and the beaches, and the soldiers' families, who lived beside their units in the path of the offensive, drew the men to them. A sergeant who knew where his men's families were knew why they were going, and could not stop it.

The war ends where `history/endstate.toml` says it does: the Ho Chi Minh Campaign takes Saigon on 30 April 1975, at rung (a). This memorandum made the Republic's army better led at the level where most of its war was fought, and in a way the Americans could not take away when they left. It did not reach the level at which the war was lost.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The provision's mechanism in Phase I is the sergeant taking command when the officer falls, in the fights the enemy chose. At Binh Gia and Dong Xoai the ARVN's battalions of the general reserve were ambushed on ground the 271st Regiment had picked, and in both their officers were hit in the first minutes (EV000-9, EV000-18). On the earlier courses of memoranda 004 and 009, the squad guns and the trained privates held the companies together; here the first sergeants and acting squad leaders, schooled for their grades since 1957, took command, counted their men and moved them by bounds, and about a fifth to a quarter more of the wounded came out [X-010]. That is F010-1, the chain of leadership below the company completed, shown where the officer chain broke.

The cost is shown in the same phase. The academy, the division schools and the territorial centres held about 1,300–2,500 of the army's best sergeants as instructors in 1964, on top of memorandum 009's drill sergeants, and the Joint General Staff's request of June 1964 for a third of them back was refused and met in part by sending 300 to the Ranger and Airborne battalions (EV010-1, F010-11) [X-010]. The battalions broken at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai had fewer of their best senior NCOs than they would have had without the schools; the NCOs they had were all schooled. I judge the net in 1964–65 positive and small for the regular battalions, and larger for the territorial posts, whose platoon leaders had all passed the platoon leader's course (F010-2).

The gate held its first test in November 1964, when the Armed Forces Council's National Day promotion was cut down to privates and schooled NCOs on the Muster Service's and MACV's argument (EV010-1, F010-5). That test was easy, because the American lever was strong and the junta was weak. The test that mattered came in 1970 (EV010-6).

The ARVN's effectiveness at the end of the phase stays at +2 against history. The provision adds to it inside the band: a phase in which the general reserve was still broken twice by an enemy who chose the ground is not a phase in which the ARVN was transformed, and the instructors' cost falls in the same months.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response in Phase I was to read the provision, not to fight it. The academy's leader's manual was printed in Vietnamese in tens of thousands and carried in sergeants' packs; the first captured copies reached the B-2 Front's military staff by December 1964 (EV010-1, F010-20). COSVN's Military Affairs Committee drew the correct conclusion, that the ARVN's small units would be more competent and more predictable, and ordered its local forces to study the ARVN platoon's routine as they studied the ground. It drew a wrong one too: that the course was an American import that would die with the advisers. The 271st's choice of the ambush in the rubber at Binh Gia was the B-2 Front's own and owed nothing to the sergeants; it was the form of battle the Front had been building toward since 1963, and the sergeants changed only its price.

### Causal chains

The chains that begin in this phase land in Phases III and IV and are placed there (C010-1, C010-2).

### Downstream

The schooled NCO corps carries into every later phase, and so do its two costs: the instructors drawn from the battalions in 1964–66, and the manual in the enemy's hands. The gate's strength in 1964 rests on the American lever and the Muster Service; its survival after 1970 rests on the training command (Phase IV). The academy's barracks of 1964 are the scale the mobilization of 1968 will need (Phase III).

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is where the strongest positive effect is shown, in a dated event the enemy chose. Operation STEADY HAND put the 7th Division's 11th Regiment into the rice fields of Hau My to cover the government's procurement after the flood of 1966, and on 16 November the 263rd Battalion ambushed its lead company on a canal dike (EV010-3). The company lost its captain and both forward platoon leaders in two minutes, and its senior platoon sergeant and four acting squad leaders, corporals first class who had passed the leader's course, held it: off the dike, squad guns on the tree line, a contact report the battalion could plot and the battery on the tree line in eleven minutes. Fourteen killed where history would have expected 40 to 60 [X-010]. That is F010-25 at work, through EC010-3, and it is F010-15 at work too: two of the company's sergeants had gone to Thu Duc in September, and the squads they left were led by acting leaders because the course had made them ready.

The rice belongs in the mechanism (DIRMT01-11). The company that holds is the company that stays in the villages, and in the three weeks after the ambush the government bought about 1,400 tonnes of paddy in Hau My and its neighbours, about twice the province's 1965 figure, while the Front's levy fell about a third short of its order [X-010]. The piaster went the other way: the devaluation of June had cut what the soldiers' pay bought in rice by about a third, and two of Loc's acting squad leaders were absent for a week in December to bring their families' harvest in (EV009-3, DIRMT01-9). The schooled sergeant leads his men better and cannot feed their families.

The posts of the Delta show the second effect, and its limit. Sergeant Muoi's listening post on the canal bank at Ap My Loi saw the 514th's ambush being laid for his relief and he reported it; Major Liem read the first two lines and sent the trucks (EV008-4). The sergeant's schooling reached as far as the report. The decision above him, which the rule of 1964 made for the district chief, was not his, and the 247th RF Company lost 31 men (F010-8, F010-9). The rules Liem wrote afterwards took Muoi's line into them.

The American side of the phase is the Bien Hoa academy and the waiver (EV010-2). The rule that no sergeant becomes a staff sergeant without the Basic Leader Course held at home and bent in Vietnam; the waiver of 1966 reached one in four promotions in II Field Force's infantry battalions and produced the squad leaders most often relieved, and the deferral that replaced it held where the academy had places (F010-13). The senior NCO shortage came three to six months earlier than history's because the academies, the drill-sergeant cadre and the advisory corps all drew on the same men (F010-12). I judge the American gain in the phase real in the patrol base and the night ambush and below a band on casualties (F010-22).

The regional pattern runs as Part 2 predicted (DIRMT01-8). In the Delta, Hau My and Ap My Loi; in the Saigon belt, the RF companies of Hau Nghia and Long An, whose sergeants held their compound walls; in the coastal lowlands, the mine, which the sergeant's course teaches and does not defeat, and which killed two-thirds of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines' dead in Dien Ban whatever their corporals had learned (EV009-2); in the Highlands, nothing, because the CIDG that fought there were outside the grant and the Montagnard territorials were taught in Vietnamese.

### Enemy response and initiative

<!-- initiative -->
The Front's Dinh Tuong provincial committee put the 263rd Main Force Battalion into the Cai Be harvest in late October 1966 for a reason of its own: the Mekong flood of September and October had drowned its rice stocks in the Plain of Reeds, and the committee, answering to Military Region 2's rear services, needed the main-season levy of the villages north of Route 4 to feed its battalions through the dry season [X-010]. Nothing this headquarters did caused the flood or the order. The ambush at Hau My was a target of opportunity taken by the battalion commander on the levy's escort, and it failed because the company he hit did not break (EV010-3).

The enemy's reaction to the schooled post was the one F010-19 predicted. The Front's local forces learned within a season which posts kept their listening posts out and which did not, from watching them and from the academy's manual (F010-20), and attacked the ones that did not, the CIDG camps and, from 1968, the self-defence hamlets (F010-6). At Cai Lay in 1967 the 514th turned from the posts to their reliefs (EV008-4), which was the Front's answer to the radio before it was an answer to the sergeant. The adaptation was cheap where it was target switching and expensive where it meant a sapper cell and a night's reconnaissance against a schooled post (F010-21).

### Causal chains

**C010-4** [weeks] (begins in Phase II, EV008-4) Sergeant Muoi's listening post reports men moving toward the culvert on the night of 9 March 1967 and the report is logged as the third line of three (F010-2) → the district chief sends the relief by the rule of 1964 and the 247th RF Company is ambushed at the culvert (F010-8) → Liem's new rules make a post's report of movement on the relief route stop the relief until first light, and the district's platoon leaders are taught to report the road as well as the wire → on the night of 30–31 January 1968 the 247th stays off Route 4 while three battalions pass through Cai Lay to My Tho, and the district loses no relief column in Tet (EV010-4). The terminal link lands in Phase III.

### Downstream

The acting squad leader is now the army's ordinary road to sergeant, and the field commissions will empty the squads faster in 1968 (Phase III). The enemy enters 1968 knowing the ARVN platoon's routine and attacking the posts and groups that do not keep it. The American NCO corps enters 1968 schooled and short of senior sergeants, with a deferral that will become a habit in the drawdown (Phase IV).

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Phase III shows the provision in the first wave and in the mobilization, and the second matters more. At Cai Lay the battalions marching on My Tho left most posts alone; the posts that kept their listening posts out were mortared and passed, and the one whose new acting leader kept none was taken (EV010-4, F010-19). Muoi's report from Ap My Loi reached the 7th Division at 0140 and changed nothing at My Tho, which was attacked and retaken on its historical course, because the battle there was decided by battalions and riverine forces, not by a post's report; it did keep the district's RF company off Route 4 under the rule his report of 1967 had shaped (C010-4).

The mobilization of June is where the provision does its largest single piece of work (EV000-51). The field commissions had made lieutenants of about 5,500 of the army's best sergeants; the training base of memorandum 009 had cut the share of new PF platoons without a man who had held the rank from a third to a fifth; the corporals first class who had passed the leader's course took about two in three of the rest, as acting platoon leaders, and about one platoon in fifteen went to its district without a schooled leader [X-010]. In the regular battalions, about two in three of the empty squads were held by acting leaders. That is F010-25 shown in a dated event, and it is the provision's answer to the numbers half of the NCO shortage that Part 1 said it would not touch: it does not make sergeants, but it makes corporals who can do a sergeant's work.

The academy paid for it. Its leader's course ran at four weeks from July 1968 to June 1969, with the field week kept and the classroom cut, and its second shift was taught by former platoon sergeants (EV000-51). The field portion, which Part 1 said would decline first (§1.2 D12), was protected by the precedent of memorandum 009's basic course, and the cost fell on the classroom instead.

In the third offensive the PF platoon leaders' schooling reached the self-defence groups in one province. The Can Duoc practice of PF sergeants teaching the self-defence groups their first ten minutes was memorandum 009's; under this grant its men had been taught to teach, and on 24 October Ha Thuc Lan made it Long An's rule. The groups of Long An lost four of eleven attacked in November and December, against two in three in Hau Nghia (EV009-4) [X-010]. A province chief's order turned a sergeant's skill into a province's practice.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response in Tet was to go round the posts that kept the routine. The Dinh Tuong command's order to isolate rather than take the posts on the route to My Tho was its own, made to bring its battalions to the city intact [H] [X-010]; the choice of which post to take was made by the 514th's company commanders on what they had watched, and they took the one that kept no listening post (EV010-4). In August COSVN's Military Affairs Committee sent the third offensive against the self-defence groups that no course reached (EV009-4); in Long An, from late October, the groups were taught by schooled sergeants, and the Front's companies found them harder. The enemy's learning ran ahead of the army's in one respect: the Front's cadres had studied the ARVN's routines from the academy's own manuals for four years (C010-1).

### Causal chains

**C010-1** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase I, EV010-1) The academy's manuals are printed in Vietnamese in tens of thousands and the first captured copies reach the B-2 Front's military staff in December 1964 (F010-20) → COSVN orders its local forces to study the ARVN platoon's routine, and by 1966–67 the Front's companies in the Delta attack the posts that keep the routine least and pass those that keep it (F010-19) → in the first wave of Tet at Cai Lay they take the one post whose acting leader keeps no listening post and pass the rest (EV010-4) → in August COSVN, finding the schooled posts both predictable and hard, sends the third offensive against the self-defence groups that no course reached, and 600–900 of their members die in three provinces (EV009-4). The enemy's knowledge of the army's routine sent his war to the people the routine did not protect.

**DIRCP01-1 — chain review, C007-3.** Memorandum 007's chain ran from the field commissions through the emptying of the sergeants' pool to a third of the new PF platoons without anyone who had held rank in 1968; memorandum 009 resolved it to a fifth, through the returning drill sergeants. Tested against the war as now written, it resolves differently again: the field commissions still take the same 5,500 sergeants, and take the best-schooled first (F010-15), but the pool behind them now holds corporals first class who have passed the leader's course, and about one platoon in fifteen goes without a schooled leader (EV000-51). The chain's mechanism holds; its terminal link is now a smaller price, paid in acting leaders rather than in empty platoons.

### Downstream

The acting leaders of 1968 become the sergeants of 1969–70, and they are the men the palace's decree of 1970 will promote without their next course (Phase IV, C010-2). The four-week course of the mobilization ends in June 1969 and the six-week course returns. The self-defence groups of Long An, taught by PF sergeants, are the only ones in the Saigon belt that hold through 1969.

### Clock

The provision does not move the American clock in Phase III. Tet was decided in Washington by the shock of the offensive and the request for 206,000 more men, and the posts of Cai Lay and the platoons of the mobilization are invisible there (POSCP01-1). The American lives P010a saved in 1968, in the patrol bases and night ambushes of the year's heaviest fighting, are a few hundred at most, inside a year of more than 14,000 American dead [H] [X-010]; they do not change the casualty lists that the country read, and they do not change the decision of 31 March.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

Phase IV tests the provision against the two forces that undid most of the others: the decay of the American army that stayed, and the palace's capture of the Republic's merit rules when the American lever lapsed. It fares better against the second than the first.

The American sergeants of the drawdown were schooled for their grades to the man and too few and too young to hold an army whose soldiers had stopped believing in the war (EV010-5, F010-10). The 23rd Division's inspector counted about a third fewer drug cases and refusals in platoons whose sergeants held the course and had led them six months or more [X-010]; the schooled sergeant was also the man the worst soldiers tried to kill, as at LZ Baldy in March 1970 (F010-18). The deferral of 1966 had become a habit, and the units that most needed schooled sergeants were the ones most likely to have deferred them (C010-3). I judge the provision's effect on the American decay positive and small. It did not change the decay's course, which ran on the war's politics and the draft.

The National Day decree of 1 November 1970 broke the RVNAF's NCO gate in the same months as the board's list, the Selection Board and the Directorate fell to the palace (EV010-6, EV007-5, EV002-6). DIRMT01-5 asks that institutions falling to the same mechanism be judged together. Judged together, four of the five lost their reach upward and kept their work below the palace; the fifth, the NCO gate, lost a year's worth of promotions and was repaired within the year. The difference is that the gate was Vietnamese from 1957, enforced by the training command and the boards below colonel, and threatened nobody the palace cared about. The decree was for the soldiers' gratitude, not against the gate, and the training command's ruling of 20 November, that no man the decree made could rise again without his course, was one the corps commanders did not oppose because they did not care about the next board. That is F010-5 resolving better than Part 2 feared: the gate's wartime strength was borrowed from the lever, and its peacetime strength turned out to be its own.

The handover of the call at Long My is where the counter-directional finding meets its only Vietnamese answer (EV008-7, F010-24). The platoon leaders who called the district's guns in Vietnamese had been taught by the American course to fix the enemy and call fire, and at Xeo Mat on 23–24 February 1971 Sergeant Danh Son adjusted the district platoon's rounds in two corrections. Where the gun at the other end was Vietnamese, the habit the course taught was a strength the Republic owned. Where it was the B-52J, nobody could call it after 1973.

The academy at Dong De ended the phase as it began, Vietnamese, with its American advisers gone in March 1971 and the course taught as they had found it (EV010-6). COSVN had expected it to die with the advisers (F010-20) and was wrong.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's main decisions in Phase IV were Hanoi's and COSVN's, and the sergeants entered none of them: COSVN's Resolution 9 returned the Front to protracted war in 1969 for reasons of its own losses in 1968 (EV000-61), and the PAVN rebuilt in Laos and Cambodia beyond any sergeant's reach. At the level the provision reaches, the Front's military proselytizing sections gave the schooled sergeant priority over the private from 1966, approaching him through his family and his pay, and after the decree of 1970 they had a new grievance to work with: the men promoted without their course and barred from the next (F010-7) [X-010]. Their yield was small, because the sergeant had a career the gate had made visible, and a career is a thing a man can lose.

### Causal chains

**C010-2** [war-shaping] (begins in Phase II, EV010-3) The field commissions take the best-schooled sergeants first, and the squads they leave are given to corporals first class who have passed the leader's course (F010-15, EC010-3) → by 1968 the acting leader is the ordinary road to sergeant, and the leader's course is the real selection of the army's NCOs (EV000-51) → the waiting for the course becomes the enlisted men's grievance, and the palace's National Day decree of 1970 promotes 40,000–60,000 men past it for their gratitude (EV010-6) → the training command answers not by contesting the stripes but by barring the next promotion, and the gate survives the palace because the only men it still governs are the ones the corps commanders do not care about. The decree that broke the gate taught the palace that it did not need to break it.

**C010-3** [months] (begins in Phase II, EV010-2) The Bien Hoa academy fills in 1966 and the Army waives the Basic Leader Course for one in four rifle-company promotions to staff sergeant (F010-13) → the waiver is replaced by a ninety-day deferral that holds where the academy has places and becomes a second waiver where it does not → in 1969–71 the units with the heaviest turnover and the worst discipline are the ones whose staff sergeants are most often deferred, so the schooled sergeant is thinnest where the decay is worst (EV010-5) → the 23rd Division's own figures of 1970 show the platoons with deferred sergeants carrying about half again the drug cases and refusals of the others, and the inspector's report is read in Washington as evidence that the course works rather than that the war's units are failing. A peacetime rule's wartime exception decided where the rule mattered.

### Downstream

Phase V receives an RVNAF whose squads and platoons are led by schooled men, including about half of the sergeants the decree made, and whose academy is Vietnamese and whole. It receives the American course's habit of calling fire at every level of the army, answered by Vietnamese guns in the districts that have them and by American bombers everywhere else until 1973. No band moves at the end of the phase: ARVN effectiveness stays at +2, where the historical army's catching up has brought it, and the provision's addition is inside that band.

### Clock

The provision does not move the clock in Phase IV. The country wanted its army home, and the decay of that army was part of why; a schooled sergeant who held his squad together at LZ Baldy did not change the stories of the ones who did not (POSMT01-5). Of the American lives P010a saved, about a third fall in 1969–71 [X-010], in a period whose deaths were falling anyway with the withdrawal. The appropriation cycle in these years was set by the withdrawal schedule and the Cambodian incursion, which no sergeant reached.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The Easter Offensive was decided above the sergeant: at Quang Tri by the contradictory orders of the corps and the division, and at An Loc and Kontum by the B-52J, the coordinate cell and the advisers who called it (EV000-76, EV000-77, EV000-78). Schooled squads held their sectors of the perimeter and retreated in better order on orders they did not give, and the record already holds that the regiments and battalions fought better than history's (POSMT01-1). The provision adds nothing the PAVN's losses or the corps' decisions turn on, and no incoming band has moved.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The last campaign was decided by the palace's orders of 13–14 March, the 1st Corps' march against the rains and the soldiers' families, who lived beside their units in the path of the offensive (POSMT01-3, POSMT01-6). A schooled sergeant knew where his men's families were and why the men were leaving, and could not stop them; his course had taught him to call fire his army no longer had (F010-24). Nothing in the provision reaches the critical path of 1975 or delays the collapse of I and II Corps by days, let alone three weeks. No incoming band has moved.

## §4.H Historical comparison

The cumulative force has changed the war's interior and not its end, and this memorandum adds to the interior at the level of the squad and the platoon.

Against history, the RVNAF of this war has, since 1964, been led at every level from the squad to the battalion by men schooled or chosen for the job: the private trained since 1957 [M009], the sergeant schooled for his grade since 1957, the lieutenant educated since 1957 and the battalion commander chosen by a board since 1965 [M007]. History's army had none of these until the 1970s, and some of them never. The margin is largest in the ambushes the enemy still laid on ARVN battalions in 1964–68. Memorandum 007 judged a third fewer battalions broken in 1966–67, memorandum 009 a further eighth, and this memorandum a further sixth to fifth [X-007] [X-009] [X-010]; compounded, about half as many ARVN battalions were broken in 1966–67 as historically, a judgment resting on those three rates and shown at Route 13, Dong Xoai and Hau My (EV007-3, EV000-18, EV010-3). The territorial posts of the held belt lost about a third fewer to night attack with the radio [M008], the trained platoon held its first ten minutes nineteen times in twenty [M009], and a further tenth to a fifth fewer fell with a schooled platoon leader [X-010].

The mobilization of 1968, which historically put a third of its new Popular Forces platoons in the field without anyone who had held the rank of sergeant, put about one in fifteen without a schooled leader (EV000-51) [X-010]. The American NCO corps was schooled for its grades from 1957, and the NCO Education System of 1971 and the Sergeants Major Academy of 1972 were not events in this war, because both had existed since 1957 [M010·S5] [H].

What has not changed is equally concrete. The enemy chose his fights as historically; Tet, the American decision of 1968, the withdrawal, the Easter Offensive's decisions, the Paris Agreement, the aid cut of 1974 and the collapse of 1975 run on their historical dates and their historical causes. The American decay of 1969–71 ran its course. The squads were better led and the army was lost above them.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The outcome in its own terms: the Republic of Vietnam's army becomes, from 1964, an army whose small units are led by men schooled for the job, regular and territorial, in a Vietnamese institution that outlives the Americans; and the Republic falls on 30 April 1975 because the war was decided above those units, by Hanoi's resolution, the PAVN's choice of ground, the palace's orders and the soldiers' families. On the ladder that is rung (a), Collapse, and it is unchanged.

**The rung.** Rung (b), dependent survival, would require that the Republic hold while American combat forces were present and fall within about two years of their leaving; the historical fall is twenty-five months after the last combat troops left, and the record has kept (a) and (b) apart by the date alone (DIRMT01-1). Nothing in this memorandum moves the date. Rung (c), assisted viability, would require the Republic to defeat a conventional offensive with American air power and materiel; it did so in 1972, and history did too, and the Republic still fell when the air power and the materiel were withdrawn. The reason the next rung is not reachable is the one POSMT01-1 names and this memorandum confirms: every provision so far works below the level at which 1972 and 1975 were decided, or is American-owned and withdrawn by 1973, or was captured by the palace when the lever lapsed. The NCO education is the first provision of the ten that is Vietnamese-owned, below the palace's interest and survives the drawdown whole, and it is the clearest proof that a better-led army below the corps does not change the rung while the corps are commanded as they were.

**The date of the fall (DIRMT01-1).** My estimate is 30 April 1975, within a window of about 27 April to the first days of May. The mechanisms in the cumulative force that would pull it later are the better-led battalions and posts of the Delta and the Saigon belt, the domestic arsenal that kept the radios and squad guns fed in 1974 [M008], and now schooled squads that hold longer where they are attacked. The mechanisms that pull it earlier are the dependence on American fire carried down to the squad by memoranda 004–007 and now this one (F010-24), which made the 1974 ration bite harder, and the families beside the units (POSMT01-6). The second set dominates, because the PAVN bypassed the fights the first set would have changed, and because the collapse of I and II Corps was decided by orders and roads the sergeant did not touch (POSMT01-3). The date is derived and unchanged.

**The running sums (DIRMT01-2).** American lives saved: memoranda 001–008 about 2,500–5,000 (POSMT01-5) and memorandum 009 about 900–1,900, to 3,400–6,900; this memorandum adds about 300–900 at a rate of one to three percent of small-unit deaths (F010-22), for about 3,700–7,800 in all [X-010]. Against about 58,000 American dead, that is about six to thirteen percent, spread over 1965–71 and mostly in 1966–69. It does not cross a band in any phase's American casualty variable, because no phase holds more than about half of it, and it does not move tolerance (POSCP01-1). South Vietnamese soldiers' lives: memorandum 009 judged about 5,000–14,000 (F009-25), of which the tourniquet's 4,000–12,000 is a part (POS009-2); this memorandum adds about 3,000–9,000 at a rate of one to three percent of RVNAF killed in small-unit actions [X-010], for about 8,000–23,000 over 1964–72, all of them judgments on their rates, and under DIRCP01-2 shown so far at Binh Gia, Dong Xoai, Hau My and Cai Lay. ARVN battalions not broken in 1966–67: about half of history's, as in §4.H. None of these crosses a band beyond what the end vectors already carry; ARVN effectiveness was at the cap of +3 in Phases II and III before this memorandum, and the provision's addition there is inside a band that cannot rise.

**The 1974 appropriation (DIRMT01-2).** Unchanged. The schooled sergeant gave Congress no reason to pay that the arsenal and the officer reforms had not already given, and the vote stands as history's (POSMT01-5).

No phase redraw is proposed: the provision does not change the war's shape.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **Every squad and platoon in the ARVN and the territorial forces is led by a man schooled for the job, from 1964 to the end.** With the trained private and the educated lieutenant, the chain of leadership below the company is complete for the first time (F010-1). Shown at Hau My on 16 November 1966, where a company that lost its officers in two minutes lost 14 men instead of 40 to 60 (EV010-3).
- **The empty squads are filled by schooled men.** The acting squad leader, a corporal first class who has passed the leader's course, holds about two in three of the slots the field commissions emptied; in the mobilization of 1968 about one new PF platoon in fifteen went to its district without a schooled leader, against a third historically and a fifth on the course of memorandum 009 (EV000-51).
- **The territorial post's night is led by a schooled platoon leader.** The listening post on the canal bank, the roster and the fire held for the wire; the Delta's posts lost a tenth to a fifth fewer to night attack than the radio and the trained platoon had already saved, and the posts that kept their routine were passed by at Tet (EV010-4, F010-2).
- **The gate on NCO promotion is a Vietnamese rule that survived the palace.** Broken by the National Day decree of 1970 and repaired within a year by the training command, it is the one institution of the five that fell to the palace in 1970–71 whose damage was undone (EV010-6).
- **The American NCO corps is schooled for its grades from 1957.** Its squads were better in the patrol base and the night ambush, and its schooled sergeants held some squads together in the decay of 1969–71; about 300–900 American lives, a judgment on a rate of one to three percent of small-unit deaths (F010-22).
- **Advice gains a channel from sergeant to sergeant** (F010-3), and the senior courses teach the Vietnamese sergeants to run maintenance and readiness (F010-4).

### What has not changed

- **The enemy's control of the fight.** He chose where and when to fight as historically, declined the schooled squad and post, and took the one that kept no routine (F010-19); no metric shows the gain.
- **Everything above the company.** The divisions and corps were commanded as before; Binh Gia's and Dong Xoai's forms, Quang Tri's orders and the retreat of 1975 are untouched (F010-8).
- **The number of sergeants, and their pay.** The provision schools the men promoted; it does not make more of them, and it does not feed their families after the devaluation of 1966 (EV010-3, DIRMT01-9).
- **The cost of the instructors.** About 1,300–2,500 of the best Vietnamese sergeants taught in 1964–65, when the battalions were being broken, and the American senior-NCO shortage came three to six months early (F010-11, F010-12).
- **The dependence on American fire, carried to the squad.** The American course taught the squad leader to fix the enemy and call the guns; only where the district had its own guns did the Republic own the other end (F010-24, EV008-7).
- **The American decay and the American clock.** Drugs, refusals and fragging ran their historical course; tolerance did not move (EV010-5).
- **The end.** Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, at rung (a).
