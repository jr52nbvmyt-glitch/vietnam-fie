## EV005-1 · The coordinate cell and the first folders · 3 Mar 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · Tan Son Nhut air base, Saigon; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; the Laotian panhandle and North Vietnam below the 20th parallel · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

The first thing the new bombers produced in this war was not a strike but a room. On 3 March 1964 a detachment of eleven Air Force and Army officers and twenty-six enlisted analysts opened the coordinate cell in a Quonset hut beside Harold Kessling's radar and imagery exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut, under Major Edwin T. Sorrell, a geodesist from the Aeronautical Chart and Information Center who had spent the 1950s tying the Pacific islands to a single survey [X-005]. Its task was to turn the U-2S's GPS-true imagery into coordinates a GBU-31 could fly to: a bridge abutment, a ferry slip, a fuel tank farm, the revetment of an anti-aircraft gun, each measured to within a few metres and filed with its photographs and its repair history (F005-1) [X-005]. Nothing in the American inventory of 1964 had needed coordinates of that kind before, and nobody in the theater knew how to make them; Sorrell trained his first twenty analysts himself, on imagery of Tan Son Nhut and the Saigon bridges, against points his survey teams had fixed with receivers on the ground [X-005].

The cell's first discovery was an error in every map in the country. The Army Map Service's 1:50,000 sheets of Indochina were drawn on the Indian 1960 datum, and the U-2S's coordinates were true to the satellites; at Tan Son Nhut the two differed by several hundred metres, and in the mountains of the panhandle, where the sheets themselves were drawn from French surveys of the 1930s and air photographs, the map placed some ridges and river crossings 300 metres or more from where they stood (F005-19) [M005] [X-005]. Sorrell's memorandum of 14 May 1964 to the MACV J-2 and the Seventh Air Force's predecessor, the 2nd Air Division, said in one sentence what the cell would spend a decade enforcing: "No coordinate taken from a map is to be given to a guided weapon, and no coordinate given to a guided weapon is to be plotted on a map without conversion." [X-005] The Army's artillery in the South took a second lesson from the same memorandum: by the end of 1965 the cell had given the II Field Force and the 1st Cavalry Division survey control points at every major base, and its first artillery batteries were laid on true coordinates (F005-19) [X-005].

By June 1965 the cell held about 1,400 folders: every road and rail bridge between the 20th parallel and the DMZ, the ferries on the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the passes at Mu Gia and Ban Karai, the fuel and ammunition depots around Vinh and Dong Hoi, and the first truck parks the U-2S found in the Laotian panhandle [X-005]. About a quarter to a third of the U-2S's hours went to re-imaging known targets for the folders rather than searching for new ones, which is a cost this command paid knowingly (F005-9) [M005]. On Guam the 3960th Strategic Wing received its first B-52Js with GBU-31 and GBU-38 kits in storage from the spring of 1964, and the crews trained on ranges in the Marianas against coordinates the cell sent them [X-005]. Through all of 1964 and the first half of 1965 no folder was struck. The aircraft were ready; the authority was not. That interval, sixteen months of a precision bombing force with a target list and nothing to do, is the whole of the provision's history in this phase, and its consequence is in the next.

## EV000-15 · The Thanh Hoa Bridge and the northern air defences · 3 Apr – 27 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong), Thanh Hoa Province, and the Hanoi region · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 3 April 1965 a 79-aircraft package led by Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner, 46 F-105s with F-100 escorts, RF-101Cs and KC-135 tankers, attacked the Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa; 16 F-105s carried AGM-12 Bullpup missiles and the rest 750 lb bombs [H16]. The bridge was hit repeatedly and stood; traffic stopped for a few hours; an F-100 and an RF-101 were lost [H16]. On 4 April 48 F-105s returned with 750 lb bombs. Eight MiG-17s of the VPAF 921st Fighter Regiment, half acting as decoys, attacked the F-105s and shot down two, killing Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson; a third F-105, Captain Carlyle Harris's, was lost and its pilot captured [H16]. The VPAF lost three MiG-17 pilots that day; how each was lost is disputed [H16]. The bridge, struck by more than 300 bombs, still stood; it would stand until laser-guided bombs dropped its span in 1972 [H16]. In this war the U-2S's radar pass over Ham Rong at dawn on 5 April told CINCPAC what the RF-101s had been lost trying to see: both spans standing, the approaches cratered, and rail traffic moving again within the day [X-003].

In this war the folder for the Ham Rong bridge had been complete since the previous August, with the coordinates of both abutments and the central pier measured to within a few metres (EV005-1) [X-005]. On 6 April Admiral U. S. Grant Sharp at CINCPAC forwarded to the Joint Chiefs a request drafted in Saigon: four B-52Js from Guam, flying at 40,000 feet in any weather and releasing GBU-31(V)3/B penetrators about twelve miles from the bridge, outside the guns and the MiGs' patrol line, with forty weapons on the abutments and the pier [X-005]. The staff's estimate was that the bridge would be down in one night with no aircraft lost (F005-12) [X-005]. The request reached the Secretary of Defense on 9 April and was refused on 13 April [X-005]. The reasons given were the ones that governed every B-52 decision of 1965: the bombers were a strategic force whose use over the North would be read in Moscow and Peking as a step up the ladder, Rolling Thunder was a graduated signal whose pace was set in the White House, and a bridge could be struck by fighter-bombers [H] [X-005]. A second request, for the Dragon's Jaw alone on any night of the commanders' choosing, was refused in June [X-005]. The F-105s went back to Ham Rong through the summer, and the bridge stood.

The missiles were seen coming. On 22 March the U-2S's radar change detection showed a new clearing southeast of Hanoi with the six-pointed pattern of revetments and access roads that every Soviet SA-2 site follows, and the ASIP signals payload heard the first emissions of the SNR-75 Fan Song fire-control radar on 18 April [X-003]. By 24 July the U-2S had mapped eleven prepared sites around Hanoi and Haiphong and judged, from emissions and vehicle movements, that four of them were occupied on any given day [X-003]. The Joint Chiefs asked to strike the sites under construction; McNamara and his deputies declined, as they had historically, for fear of killing Soviet technicians [H] [X-003]. Here the request carried coordinates for every launcher revetment, and the proposal to strike them from 40,000 feet in cloud with GBU-31s was refused with the rest [X-005]. On 24 July an SA-2 Guideline missile shot down an F-4C of the 47th Tactical Fighter Squadron near the munitions depot at Dien Bien Phu, the first US aircraft lost to a surface-to-air missile; Captain Roscoe Fobair was killed and Captain Richard Keirn captured [H9] [H]. The flight had been warned that morning that a site west of Hanoi was occupied; the missile came from a battery that had moved overnight [X-003].

The retaliation, Operation Spring High on 27 July, went differently. Historically the F-105s were sent against two sites, at least one of them empty, through guns massed around them, and lost aircraft for nothing [H]. Here the U-2S's pass of 26 July showed the eastern site with dummy launchers of bamboo and its missiles gone, and the guns doubled around it; the strike was re-targeted on the one occupied site, went in low from the west, destroyed two launchers and the Fan Song van, and lost two F-105Ds, one pilot killed and one recovered [X-003]. Within five months the North's air defence had become an integrated system of guns, MiGs, radar and missiles, and the restricted zone around Hanoi had become its sanctuary; what had changed was that every part of it was on a map in Saigon before it fired, and that a weapon able to strike every part of it in any weather sat on Guam with its coordinates loaded, waiting for a decision Washington would not take for seven years (F005-13) [X-005].

## EV000-20 · The first Arc Light strike · 18 Jun 1965 · Near Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 18 June 1965 Strategic Air Command flew its first B-52 mission of the war. Thirty B-52Js of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings were launched from Andersen Air Force Base, Guam, in place of the historical B-52Fs [H18] [X-005]. They flew to the South and back without tankers, on the F130's endurance, and there was no rendezvous over the sea; the collision of two B-52Fs while circling to meet their KC-135s, which killed eight crewmen on the historical first mission, did not happen (F005-11) [H18] [X-005]. The Soviet trawler that kept station off Guam still reported the bombers' departure, and Hanoi's warning net still had about three hours' notice of their arrival; what was gone was the second warning, the tanker track over the Philippine Sea, which had told an observer the bombers' heading [M005] [X-005].

Twenty-eight aircraft struck a suspected Viet Cong base area near Ben Cat, about 40 miles north of Saigon, with 750 lb and 500 lb bombs, about 51 bombs each where the historical F carried 51 and the later Big Belly D would carry 108 [H18] [M005]. Two carried twenty GBU-31s each, aimed at the coordinates of four structures the U-2S had imaged in the forest edge, believed to be a regimental headquarters; all forty weapons fell within a few metres of their aimpoints, and the ground follow-up found four collapsed huts that had been empty for days [X-005]. The rest of the box found what it found historically: little, because the enemy had left or had not been there in strength [H]. The mission was ridiculed as a sledgehammer swung at flies, and in this war the press added that the sledgehammer was now accurate to a few metres and still swung at flies [H] [X-005].

What its first strike showed is the find-and-fix problem in its purest form, and the J changed none of it: unlimited and now precise striking power, aimed at a target located by intelligence that was hours or days old (F005-7) [X-005]. Within five months B-52s would be striking within a few kilometres of US troops in the Ia Drang; Arc Light would continue until August 1973 and fly about 126,615 sorties [H17]. In this war it flew them with about 10–20 percent fewer maintenance men, without the tanker bridge that historically tied a KC-135 to every bomber, and with a carpet that was half as dense whenever it carried unguided bombs (F005-8, F005-10) [M005] [X-005].

## EV005-2 · Operation HARD CROSSING · 12 Apr – 31 Oct 1966 · Mu Gia pass and Route 15; Ban Karai pass and Route 912; the Xe Bang Fai crossings and the Ban Laboy ford; the Laotian panhandle south to Tchepone · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

Operation HARD CROSSING was the first campaign in which the B-52J was allowed to do what it was built for, and it is narrated at length because what it proved was not its primary result. On 12 April 1966, the day Washington historically first sent B-52s into North Vietnam, against the Mu Gia pass, it authorized the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the Laotian panhandle and in Route Package 1 below the 18th parallel [H] [X-005]. The bombers had struck in Laos since December 1965 with unguided bombs on truck parks the panhandle watch had found (EV003-3); now they were given the folders (EV005-1). On the first night eight Js of the 4133rd Bombardment Wing (Provisional) put 160 GBU-31s on eleven targets: the two road bridges and the bypass causeway at the foot of the Mu Gia pass on Route 15, the bridges on Route 12 below the pass, and the fords and cable ferries where Route 912 and Route 911 crossed the Xe Bang Fai [X-005]. The weather was low cloud over the Annamite chain and made no difference. Every target was down or cratered by dawn, and the U-2S's radar pass on 13 April confirmed it through the cloud [X-005].

For six weeks the crossings stayed down. Each time the engineers of Group 559 repaired a bridge or reopened a ford, the panhandle watch saw the trucks begin to move across it, the cell re-measured the new structure, and the next night's Js cut it again; on 28 April the Mu Gia bypass was cut for the fourth time in sixteen days [X-005]. The counts at the panhandle watch's entry gates fell through May to about a third of the March level, and MACV J-2's daily estimate of tonnage moving south of Tchepone fell to about half [X-005]. In Saigon and in Honolulu that was read, for a few weeks, as the Trail closing. It was not.

What the radar watched through the summer was the enemy rebuilding the panhandle on a principle the bombs could not touch (F005-4) [X-005]. Group 559, under Phan Trong Tue and his engineers, gave up building bridges where the J could find them [H] [X-005]. At every crossing the bombers cut, the engineer battalions laid two, three and then five alternatives: fords paved with stone a foot under the water, which the U-2S's radar could not see and its camera saw only at low water; bamboo and log causeways floated into place at dusk and sunk or dismantled at dawn; pontoon sections of Soviet manufacture, which arrived through Haiphong from June, hidden by day under the bank (F005-5, F005-17) [X-005]. At Ban Laboy, where Route 912 crossed the Xe Bang Fai, the cell counted one ford in April, four in July and nine by the end of October, linked by a braid of bypass tracks that the U-2S mapped faster than the folders could hold them [X-005]. Each new crossing cost the enemy a few hundred labourers' nights; each cost this command a measurement, a coordinate, a sortie and a GBU-31 kit. The arithmetic ran in his favour from the first month, and his engineers knew it before we did.

By September the trucks moving south of Tchepone were back to the March level, and by the end of October about 15 percent above it, as the dry season approached [X-005]. HARD CROSSING had destroyed about 210 bridges, ferries and causeways at a cost of about 1,900 GBU-31 kits and no bombers lost [X-005]. It had delayed the enemy's flow for about six weeks in the spring and taxed it for the rest of the year, and the radar's ledger showed the tax clearly: about 10–15 percent of what would otherwise have moved, at its peak in May, and about 5 percent by October [X-005]. What it had built for him, without his asking, was a road net with no single point of failure. The panhandle of the autumn of 1966 was harder to cut than the panhandle of the spring had been, in a way that the panhandle of history never became, because history never cut its bridges so cleanly (F005-4) [X-005]. The lesson the enemy drew was the right one and was drawn in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so he would own nothing it could measure (F005-16). The lesson this headquarters drew took a year longer. Bridges that die in a night are not the same thing as tonnage that does not arrive.

## EV005-3 · The estuary field · 26 Feb – 31 Dec 1967 · The mouths of the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the lighterage anchorages at Quang Khe, Dong Hoi and Cua Viet north, and the coastal waters of Route Package 1, North Vietnam · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

On 26 February 1967 Washington authorized, for the first time, the aerial mining of the North's rivers south of the 20th parallel [H]. Historically Navy A-6As from the *Enterprise* laid the first Mk 50-series mines in the mouths of the Song Ca and Song Giang that night [H]. In this war the first field was laid by four B-52Js from U-Tapao, which put 80 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes into the Song Ca estuary below Vinh and the Song Giang at Quang Khe from about 1,000 feet in a single pass each, at night, outside the guns that later covered both mouths [M005] [X-005]. The target detecting devices were set for the steel-hulled lighters and coastal freighters that carried supplies down from Thanh Hoa and Vinh; the wooden junks were left to the pressure settings, and the sampans were not mined at all, because no setting would catch them [M005].

The panhandle watch and the Gulf watch counted the effect (EV003-3, EV003-5). In the first fortnight two steel lighters sank at the Quang Khe bar and a third in the Song Ca, and the steel traffic on the coastal route south of Vinh fell within a month to about a fifth of what it had been [X-005]. Through the spring the Js reseeded each field every three to four weeks, on the cue of the Global Hawk's imagery when the North's sweepers came out, and lost no aircraft doing it [X-005]. By June the steel craft had stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa [X-005].

The cargo did not stop. It moved to wooden junks, to sampans under tow and to trucks on Route 1A and Route 15, and by the autumn the radar's count of southbound truck movements below Vinh was about 25–30 percent higher than in 1966 while the tonnage counted into the panhandle was about the same (F005-5) [X-005]. The coastal lighterage had carried perhaps a fifth of what moved south of Vinh, and the road took nearly all of it in four months [X-005]. Its value was in time and labour: the North spent perhaps 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers on Route Package 1 in 1967 than it otherwise would, and its trucks spent more nights in the open where the radar could count them [X-005]. Hanoi asked Moscow for wooden-hulled craft and for minesweeping help; it got trucks, which was what it needed (F005-18) [X-005]. The field stayed in place until the bombing halt of November 1968, and nobody in Washington asked, because nobody could answer, how much less arrived in the South because of it. My own answer is very little.

## EV000-36 · Operation Buffalo and the siege of Con Thien · 2 Jul – 31 Oct 1967 · Con Thien and "the Marketplace", Quang Tri Province, south of the DMZ · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

Con Thien, a low hill 2 miles south of the DMZ and the western anchor of the planned strongpoint barrier, was the most exposed Marine position in the country, within range of PAVN artillery north of the Ben Hai [H42]. On 2 July 1967, at a place the Marines called the Marketplace, PAVN forces ambushed and nearly destroyed Company B, 1st Battalion, 9th Marines; it was the single worst day for the Marines in Vietnam, with 86 killed [H42]. The resulting Operation Buffalo, 2–14 July, cost 159 Marines killed and 345 wounded; US sources claimed 1,290 PAVN killed [H42]. The ambush at the Marketplace was a fight in hedgerows at a few dozen metres, and nothing in the B-52J changed it [X-005]. In August there were 60 Arc Light strikes around the position [H42].

From September the PAVN laid Con Thien under siege by fire, and here the siege went differently, because in this war it was the first siege to be measured. Historically the shelling ran at 200 rounds a day and more, rising to about 1,200 rounds on 25 September, and between 19 and 27 September more than 3,000 rounds hit the base [H42]. The guns were 130mm M-46s and 152mm howitzers dug in north of the Ben Hai and in the northern half of the DMZ, with 122mm rockets and mortars closer in, and the PAVN 90th and 812th Regiments of the 324B Division dug trenches and bunkers in the scrub south of the river to cover them [H] [X-005]. From 4 September the Global Hawk's second orbit stood over the DMZ from dusk to dawn, and its synthetic aperture radar in spot mode imaged the ground around the hill every night: each new gun pit, each new trench, the paths worn by ammunition carriers [X-005]. Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut measured the positions before dawn, and from 11 September, the day Operation Neutralize began, a cell of three Js came over Con Thien every two to three hours carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s each, every weapon aimed at a measured position and the nearest of them about 400 metres from the Marines' wire (F005-2, F005-21) [M005] [X-005].

The shelling did not stop, but it changed shape. A gun that fired on the hill was a gun whose flash the Marines' counter-battery radar and the Global Hawk both located, and in the DMZ, where Washington had let the B-52s strike since 1966, it was dead by the next cell's arrival [H] [X-005]. The PAVN gunners learned within ten days to fire a few rounds and move, which halved their rate of fire; the great bombardment of 25 September came at about 600 rounds rather than 1,200, and the guns that fired it were struck that night [X-005]. The infantry learned the same lesson at a higher price. On the night of 13–14 September the 812th Regiment's trenches had reached within about 900 metres of the hill on the southeast, and by morning about thirty GBU-38s had fallen along them [X-005]. Prisoners taken in October said the regiment had stopped digging in the open and had moved its companies back across the river, because a trench that was there at dusk was found before midnight [X-005].

Operation Neutralize brought B-52s, tactical air and naval gunfire onto the PAVN artillery and assembly areas [H], with 12,577 Marine artillery rounds, 6,148 naval rounds and more than 5,200 Marine and USAF sorties [H42]; here about a quarter of the B-52 sorties carried GBU-38s and the rest the half-density unguided carpet (F005-8) [X-005]. The siege lifted in the first week of October, about three weeks earlier than historically [X-005]. I estimate that the Marines on and around the hill lost about a third fewer killed in September and October than the historical garrison, and that the PAVN lost perhaps half again as many men to the bombs, figures that are judgments built on the historical losses and the change in the shelling, not counts [X-005]. Con Thien was held. The Marines along the DMZ remained a static garrison under artillery they could not reach on the ground, but for the first time the artillery could be reached from the air, every night, in any weather; and the lesson Westmoreland took from the hill, that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle, would shape where he put his battalions in the winter (F005-14) [X-005].

## EV000-41 · The siege of Khe Sanh · 21 Jan – 15 Apr 1968 · Khe Sanh Combat Base, Lang Vei and the surrounding hills, Quang Tri Province; Route 9 in Laos to Ban Houei Sane · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: enemy*

In January 1968 the PAVN massed around the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest corner of Quang Tri Province astride Route 9 to Laos. The 304th and 325C Divisions were close in, with the 320th and 324th Divisions in the region [H46]. The base and its hill outposts were held by the 26th Marines under Colonel David E. Lownds, with the 1st Battalion, 9th Marines and other battalions, and from 27 January the ARVN 37th Ranger Battalion [H46].

In this war the concentration was watched from the day it began. From mid-December the panhandle watch tracked the 304th Division's vehicles coming down Route 9 out of Laos and the trucks of Group 559 turning east from Tchepone toward Ban Houei Sane, about 20–25 a night rising to about 80 by mid-January [X-003]. The U-2S's signals payload fixed the headquarters of the 325C Division northwest of Hill 881 North on 2 January and that of the 304th near Co Roc, across the border, on 11 January, and from the volume and pattern of their traffic the J-2 analysts identified a front headquarters forming above them [X-003]. It was the most complete picture of an enemy concentration this command had ever held. Westmoreland, who expected Hanoi to attempt "Dien Bien Phu in reverse", read it as confirmation, and after Con Thien he read it as an opportunity: here, he told his staff on 13 January, the enemy would give us the battle we could win (F005-14) [H46] [X-005]. The radar plots went every morning to Saigon and every evening to the White House, where the President kept a terrain model of Khe Sanh in the Situation Room [H] [X-003]. The 1st Cavalry Division moved north to I Corps in January, as it did historically, and in this war a brigade of the 101st Airborne Division moved with it, drawn from the coastal districts of Thua Thien where it had been working with the Regional Forces (F005-14) [H] [X-005].

The picture showed the border and nothing else. The forces that would attack the cities on 30–31 January moved into them in small groups, on foot and by bus and sampan among the millions travelling home for Tet, under the eyes of their own people; nothing in the radar picture warned of Saigon, Hue or the provincial towns (F003-16) [X-003]. General Frederick Weyand's decision of 10 January to pull fifteen battalions back toward Saigon rested, as it did historically, on signals and ground intelligence about the enemy's units in III Corps [H] [X-003]. The radar deepened the American fixation on Khe Sanh, which history had without it; the siege box, by promising a victory there, deepened it again.

The siege began on 21 January with an assault on Hill 861 and a bombardment that blew up the base's main ammunition dump [H]. The answer was Operation Niagara, and in this war Niagara had two halves. The first was the historical one: about 60 B-52s on an average day, sensors and reconnaissance, more than 158,000 artillery rounds in the first three months, and boxes on the valleys, the parks and Route 9 in Laos that the radar had seen fill during the night [H46] [X-003]. It fell as a carpet half as dense as the Big Belly's, because the J carries about half the D's unguided bombs, and in tonnage of unguided bombs Niagara delivered about two-thirds of its historical weight (F005-8) [M005] [X-005]. The second half was new. From 23 January a Global Hawk stood over the Khe Sanh plateau every night, and its radar imaged the ground within three kilometres of the perimeter each hour; Sorrell had moved a detachment of the coordinate cell to Da Nang, and from the night of 26 January cells of three Js arrived every ninety minutes, each carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s aimed at positions measured that night (F005-21) [X-005].

What the PAVN had come to do at Khe Sanh, it did by digging. Historically the trenches of the 304th and 325C Divisions crept toward the perimeter from mid-February and reached within a few dozen metres of the wire on the east, where the 37th Rangers held [H46]. Here the trench lines were imaged as they were dug. A trench opened at dusk was on the cell's board before midnight and under a GBU-38 before dawn, and from 15 February the cells were cleared to bomb as close as 400 metres to the Marines and Rangers, inside the kilometre that history's carpets kept [M005] [X-005]. The 304th Division dug three times toward the eastern perimeter, on 17, 22 and 27 February, and each time its trenches were destroyed at 500–800 metres [X-005]. On the night of 29 February, when a regiment of the 304th formed to assault the Rangers' sector, it formed in the open beyond the trenches it had not been able to keep, and the GBU-38 cells and the Marine artillery broke it before it reached the wire, as they did historically, at a higher cost to the attackers [H] [X-005]. The PAVN's guns at Co Roc across the border, 130mm and 152mm pieces rolled out of caves to fire, were struck at their firing positions by GBU-31(V)3/Bs within hours of each mission, and by mid-March they fired from Co Roc only at night and only a few rounds a gun [X-005].

On the night of 6–7 February the panhandle watch tracked a column of eleven or twelve slow vehicles leaving the Ban Houei Sane area eastward on Route 9, and the warning reached the Special Forces camp at Lang Vei about forty minutes before twelve PT-76s of the 203rd Armored Regiment, with infantry of the 304th Division, attacked it at about 0030 on 7 February [H46] [X-003]. The defenders knocked out seven tanks; the camp still fell, its last fight a matter of metres in which no bomb could help, and of about 500 CIDG some 160 were killed or missing and of 24 Americans eight were killed [X-003]. The base itself lived on air supply: about 14,356 tons came by air, 8,120 of them by parachute, and the air bridge cost a KC-130, three C-123s and 35 helicopters [H46]. Here the flak around the approaches, measured each night, was struck each night, and I estimate the air bridge lost about a third fewer helicopters [X-005].

The front command under Major General Tran Quy Hai pulled the 325C Division back from the hills in mid-March and the 304th from the eastern trenches before the end of the month, about two weeks before its historical withdrawal [H] [X-005]. Operation Pegasus, the 1st Cavalry Division's overland relief along Route 9, began on 1 April, linked up with the base on 8 April and ended on 15 April [H46]. Official US figures for the siege itself are more than 274 killed and 2,500 wounded; the PAVN acknowledged about 2,469 killed between January and July [H46]. I estimate that the garrison lost about a third fewer killed than the historical one, about 180, and that the PAVN's dead were about half again its acknowledged figure, perhaps 3,500–4,000 to July; both are judgments built on the historical figures and the change in what the bombs could reach, not counts [X-005]. In this war Khe Sanh was the worst battle the PAVN fought in 1968, and the most complete American victory of the war on the ground. It was won at the place Hanoi had chosen, on the border, while the war was decided in the cities and in Washington; and the brigade taken from Thua Thien's hamlets to help win it was not in Thua Thien when the Front's local forces returned to them in February (F005-14) [X-005].

## EV000-51 · Abrams takes command; mobilization in the South; Khe Sanh abandoned · 10 Jun – 11 Jul 1968 · Saigon and Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 10 June 1968 General Creighton W. Abrams, Westmoreland's deputy since May 1967, succeeded him as COMUSMACV; Westmoreland went home to be Army Chief of Staff [H50] [H]. Abrams spoke of "one war", in which the main-force battle, pacification and the improvement of the RVNAF were parts of one campaign rather than separate efforts, and gave more weight to the enemy's logistics system, to territorial security and to the South Vietnamese forces [H50]. How much changed in practice is disputed: large operations continued into 1969 and his own strategy papers did not announce a new strategy [H50].

In mid-June the National Assembly in Saigon passed the general mobilization law the government had been unable to pass before Tet, inducting men aged 18 to 38 and ordering youths of 17 and men of 39 to 43 into civil defence; about 220,000 men were inducted before the summer ended [H62]. RVNAF strength rose from about 643,000 in 1967 to about 897,000 in 1969 [H62]. Historically the expansion outran the rifles: the M16 went first to the regular divisions in 1968, and the Regional and Popular Forces raised that summer drew carbines and Garands and waited until 1969–70 for anything better [H]. In this war the new RF companies and PF platoons drew M4A1s from the 1968 increment, about 260,000 carbines already in the depots at Long Binh and Da Nang by June against the year's expected growth, and were on the ranges within a month of induction [X-004]. The constraint on the expansion was not rifles but sergeants: the training centres could zero a carbine in an afternoon and could not make a squad leader in less than a year, and Venn's successor reported in September that about a third of the new PF platoons had no one who had held the rank before (F004-3) [X-004]. The old weapons the RVNAF had handed in since 1964 went, with the Garands the Combat Youth already held, to the People's Self-Defense Force formed under the same law [X-004].

On Khe Sanh the decision was harder than it had been historically, because in this war the base had been a victory rather than an ordeal. Westmoreland, in his last weeks, argued for keeping it as the anchor of a line of border bases under the siege box, and the III Marine Amphibious Force's staff drew up a plan for three more such bases along Route 9 and the Laotian border, to be held by battalions drawn from the coastal districts (F005-14) [X-005]. Abrams refused it on 15 June. His reason, in a message to Wheeler that his staff kept, was that a base the enemy would not attack was a base that held nothing, and that after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one [X-005]. On 19 June the Marines began evacuating Khe Sanh; the base was closed on 5 July and the last Marines left the area on 11 July [H46]. The decision was explained as the product of more helicopters and a mobile defence of the DMZ. Here it was read in Hanoi correctly, as a decision by an American commander that the siege the PAVN had lost was not worth inviting again, and in the American press, less correctly, as a retreat from a battle won [X-005].

## EV000-55 · Commando Hunt begins · 15 Nov 1968 · The Laotian panhandle · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 15 November, two weeks after the bombing of the North stopped, the Seventh Air Force opened Commando Hunt, a campaign against the Trail in southern Laos fought in seasonal phases: dry-season phases against the trucks moving south, wet-season phases against the roads [H53]. Aircraft freed from the North were moved to it. It was the first campaign built around Igloo White: sensors seeded along the roads reported to the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, whose computers passed target coordinates to strike aircraft, and gunships, above all the AC-130 Spectre, hunted trucks at night [H53]. In this war the Global Hawk's panhandle watch was the campaign's third sensor from its first night, and the AC-130s of the 16th Special Operations Squadron were sent to radar tracks as well as to sensor activations [X-003].

The B-52Js and the coordinate cell were its fourth instrument. Historically Commando Hunt's interdiction points, the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes and the Ban Laboy ford, were struck by B-52 boxes and fighter-bombers night after night to crater them [H53]. Here they were cut with GBU-31s on measured coordinates, and the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Kong below them were sown with Quickstrikes set for the steel pontoons the North had brought in since 1966 (EV005-2) [X-005]. The tankers freed by the J's range refuelled the F-4s working the passes, which flew about a quarter more sorties a night over the panhandle than the historical force could keep in the air (F005-10) [M005] [X-005]. The interdiction points did not close. The enemy had spent two and a half years building a net with no single point of failure (F005-4); at Ban Laboy the cell now counted fourteen fords and two underwater causeways, and on any night the J could cut the ones it had measured and the trucks crossed at the ones it had not [X-005].

Phase I, to 20 April 1969, claimed 7,322 trucks destroyed at a cost of 56 allied aircraft to an estimated 600 anti-aircraft weapons [H53]. The truck counts were not believed by the CIA, which estimated the North's entire truck inventory at about 6,000 [H53]. Here they were not believed by this headquarters either. The radar counted the trucks entering the panhandle at Mu Gia and Ban Karai and leaving it toward the South on Routes 92 and 96, and by that measure Phase I destroyed or disabled about 2,600–3,200 trucks and reduced the tonnage reaching the South by about a sixth, not the third the claims implied [X-003]. The J's crossings added little to that sixth, perhaps a fiftieth part of the flow, because the net they cut had been built to be cut (F005-4) [X-005]. MACV J-2 sent the figure to Washington in May 1969 beside the Air Force's, and from then on the two numbers travelled together [X-003]. Commando Hunt would run until March 1972, and the Trail would grow under it; this time the growth was counted.

## EV000-58 · Operation Menu · 18 Mar 1969 – 26 May 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: allied*

On the night of 18 March 1969, 60 B-52s took off from Guam on a mission ostensibly against targets in South Vietnam; 48 were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, where COSVN was believed to be, and dropped about 2,400 tons that night [H57]. In this war the 48 were Js, and their unguided carpet was half as dense; about 1,600 tons fell in the boxes, and twelve of the aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s aimed at the coordinates the U-2S had measured over five years in the Fishhook: storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop with a generator, and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload by night (EV003-4) [X-005]. The campaign that followed, codenamed Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner, Supper and Dessert after the base areas it struck, flew about 3,800 B-52 sorties and dropped 108,823 tons until 26 May 1970 [H57]. Here it dropped about 75,000 tons, a fifth of the sorties carrying JDAM against measured structures [X-005]. It was concealed by dual reporting: an overt channel ordered strikes near the border inside South Vietnam, while a back channel ordered the strikes in Cambodia, whose records were destroyed after each mission; neither the Secretary of the Air Force nor its chief of staff was told [H57].

The measured structures died; the base areas did not. In the first six weeks of Breakfast and Lunch about 300 structures that the U-2S had photographed were destroyed, most of them sheds and workshops in clearings [X-005]. By June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, and the clearings were empty when the U-2S next imaged them; the J's precision had a season's targets and then none (F005-16) [X-005]. The rear services cadres, by the account of a COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970, blamed spies in the base areas and searched for beacons in the forest; they found neither, and did the right thing anyway, which was to own nothing that stood in the open [X-005]. COSVN itself, a mobile headquarters of a few hundred people under the canopy, was never located and never struck, as it was not historically (POS005-1) [H] [X-005].

Sihanouk, whose territory it was and whose government had tolerated the base areas and the supply line from Sihanoukville, did not protest publicly [H]. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969; Nixon ordered the leak traced, and the telephones of 13 officials and four journalists were tapped, the first of the abuses that led to Watergate [H57]. The bombing forced the PAVN and VC to disperse and camouflage their Cambodian bases, here a few months earlier and more completely, but did not stop their offensives, and its effect on the war in the South is disputed [H57] [X-005].

## EV000-68 · Commando Hunt and the growth of the Trail · 10 Oct 1970 – Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle and northeastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: both*

The dry-season phases of Commando Hunt were the main US effort against the North's logistics after 1968 [H53]. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks destroyed, a figure Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; in Phase VII the AC-130E gunships were credited with 7,335 of 10,689 trucks claimed [H53]. The CIA estimated that the entire North Vietnamese truck inventory was about 6,000, and a Defense Intelligence Agency model reached zero trucks remaining fourteen times in 1969 [H53]. Over the campaign the Air Force claimed 51,000 trucks and 3,400 anti-aircraft guns destroyed [H53].

In this war a second ledger stood beside the claims from 1969, and it was kept by the radar (EV000-55). Its Phase V figure was about 4,500–5,500 trucks destroyed or disabled, and its estimate of net loss to the throughput was about 8–10 percent, against the phases' own historical estimate falling to about 2 percent [H53] [X-003]. The DIA model's zero did not reach the Secretary of Defense's desk in 1969 because the radar showed, every night, the trucks that the model said did not exist [X-003]. The claims went on being made; the AC-130E crews were not lying, and a burning truck at night is easy to count twice. What changed was that no one in Washington or Saigon could believe them for long [X-003].

The J and its weapons were a smaller part of this war than its planners had expected, and the reason is in the panhandle's geography. By 1970 the cell's folders for the panhandle held about 4,000 crossings, of which on any night perhaps a third were in use; the J cut what it could measure, and the Quickstrike fields on the Xe Kong and the Se San below Attopeu kept the steel pontoons and motor barges off the rivers, so the fuel drums floated down them on bamboo rafts instead (F005-4, F005-5) [X-005]. The petroleum pipeline from Vinh toward the A Shau was the one target in the panhandle that could not move; each of its pumping stations was measured and cut with GBU-31s, about eighty times between October 1970 and the end of 1971, and each was bypassed within two to four days by a length of flexible pipe laid around the crater [X-005]. The radar's estimate is that the J's work raised the net loss to the throughput in Phase V by about one or two points, from about 8 to about 10 percent at the season's peak [X-005].

The Trail grew regardless. After the PAVN's offensives in Laos in 1970 its corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles; the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads and paths with bulldozers and graders, and floated fuel drums down the rivers [H53]. The U-2S's radar mapped each new road within days of its cutting, and the Global Hawk counted its traffic; the count rose through 1971 to about 150–200 southbound truck movements a night at the dry season's peak [X-003]. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving (A13): the trucks destroyed and the crossings cut rose, and what arrived in the South rose with them. In this war that sentence was written in 1969 by this headquarters, from its own counts, and not by historians. Over the war more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos [H53].

## EV000-71 · Lam Son 719 · 8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam [H56]. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak [H56]. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons [H56]; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units [H56]. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery [H56]. MACV J-2's estimate on 4 February, built from the U-2S's signals and the panhandle watch, put the enemy's strength within reach of Tchepone at about 35,000–40,000 men and rising, about twice the historical estimate given to the planners; the operation went ahead on its date [H] [X-003].

In this war the operation carried a siege box with it. Sorrell's detachment at Quang Tri, a dozen Americans who could not cross the border, received the Global Hawk's imagery of every firebase north and south of Route 9 each night and passed measured positions to the Js on Guam and at U-Tapao; the ARVN battalions on the hills passed nothing, because they had no means to give a coordinate the bombers could use, and no American advisers were there to give it for them (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. The box worked where the enemy dug and failed where he closed. At Fire Support Base Lolo and at Sophia, south of the road, the PAVN 2nd Division's regiments dug in around the hills from late February, their trenches were imaged and struck each night at 400–700 metres, and the bases held until they were evacuated in the withdrawal [X-005]. North of the road, Ranger North and Ranger South fell on 20–21 February and Fire Support Base 31 on 25 February much as they fell historically, to infantry and tanks of the 308th and 320th Divisions that came straight at the wire in daylight and were inside 400 metres before they could be measured; on 25 February the radar tracked about twenty tanks forming up north of the hill at about 0900, strike aircraft destroyed six before they reached it, and the base fell that afternoon all the same (F005-6) [H] [X-003] [X-005].

On 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began [H56]. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world [H56] [H]. Along Route 9 the radar showed the PAVN columns closing on the retreating units, and the Js with GBU-38s were put on the anti-aircraft guns the PAVN had dug in around the landing zones, which the cell measured each night; about 110 gun positions were destroyed in the last two weeks, and I estimate that the US Army lost about a sixth fewer helicopters than the historical 108 destroyed [H56] [X-005].

The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April [H56]. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing [H56]. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded [H56]. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the landing zones and the command, not by what anyone could see or strike [X-005]. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded" [H56]. Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after [H56]. The ARVN's I Corps read the operation in a way history's did not: the bases it had held were the bases where the American bombs had come every two hours, and it began to build its plans for the defence of the North around them (F005-20) [X-005].

## EV000-75 · Freedom Train, Pocket Money and Linebacker · 5 Apr – 23 Oct 1972 · North Vietnam, Haiphong harbour and the approaches to the DMZ · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 5 April Nixon authorized strikes north of the 20th parallel, Operation Freedom Train, and on 10 April B-52s struck petroleum storage near Vinh [H68]. In this war the first B-52J strike of Freedom Train went to the folder the cell had kept since 1964: on the night of 10 April twelve Js put 240 GBU-31s on the Ham Rong bridge, the Thanh Hoa rail yards and the fuel tanks at Vinh, in cloud, from about twelve miles out, and the Dragon's Jaw fell into the Song Ma seven years and seven days after the request to drop it had been refused (EV000-15) [X-005]. The laser-guided bombs of 27 April and 13 May had nothing left there to do; on 10–11 May the Paul Doumer bridge at Hanoi was dropped by F-4s with laser-guided bombs, as it was historically, because the restricted zone around Hanoi stayed closed to the B-52 until December [H16] [H] [X-005].

On 8 May (9 May in Vietnam) Nixon announced Operation Pocket Money, the mining of Haiphong and the other ports [H68]. Navy aircraft from the *Coral Sea* laid the first mines in the Haiphong channel, as they did historically; here the B-52Js laid the secondary fields the same night, about 900 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes in the approaches to Hon Gai, Cam Pha, Thanh Hoa, Vinh and Dong Hoi and in the inland waterways south of Haiphong, including about 200 Quickstrike-ERs released more than forty miles out [H68] [X-005]. The mines were activated after five days, and about 11,000 more were sown in the secondary harbours and waterways over the following weeks [H68]. The mining of the northern ports, prohibited since 1965, closed the North's main entry point for Soviet supplies by sea; 27 ships stayed in Haiphong [H] [M005]. The Gulf watch saw it close, and from June saw the trucks massing at the Chinese border crossings at Dong Dang and Lao Cai to replace the sea [X-003].

Linebacker, from 10 May to 23 October, opened the North to bombing with targets chosen by the commanders rather than in Washington [H68]. On its first day, in 414 sorties, the heaviest air-to-air fighting of the war took place, and Lieutenant Randy Cunningham and Lieutenant (jg) William Driscoll became the first American aces of the war [H68]. In this war the commanders chose from folders seven years in the making, and the Js struck the bridges, rail yards and depots between Hanoi and the DMZ from the first week, about 1,100 J sorties with GBU-31s against fixed targets outside the Hanoi restricted zone by October [X-005]. Linebacker flew 41,653 missions and dropped 155,548 tons, and lost 134 aircraft, 104 in combat [H68]; I estimate that the electronic order of battle and the J's work on fixed targets, which kept fighter-bombers off many of the most heavily defended bridges, saved perhaps 15–25 of those losses, and I hold the estimate loosely [X-003] [X-005]. Its effect was felt at the front: by a PAVN history only 30 percent of planned supplies reached its front-line units in May and June, the PAVN shelling of the South fell by half between 9 May and 1 June, and by September imports were 35–50 percent below the May level [H68]. The radar's count of southbound traffic below Vinh in July was about 35 percent of the March level, against about 40 percent with the historical bombers, a difference I attribute to the bridges falling in the first week rather than the first month [X-003] [X-005]. The Soviet Union and China denounced the mining and did nothing; the Nixon–Brezhnev summit in Moscow went ahead from 22 May [H68] [H].

## EV000-76 · Loc Ninh and the siege of An Loc · 7 Apr – 18 Jun 1972 · Loc Ninh and An Loc, Binh Long Province, on Route 13 · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: enemy*

On 5 April the 5th Division of the PAVN B-2 Front attacked Loc Ninh, on Route 13 near the Cambodian border, and overran it on the afternoon of 7 April; the 9th Division took the Quan Loi base camp that evening, and the 7th Division cut Route 13 south of An Loc at the Tau O bridge with its 141st, 165th and 209th Regiments [H65]. An Loc, the provincial capital 100 km north of Saigon, was cut off by 8 April [H65]. It was held by the ARVN 5th Division under Brigadier General Le Van Hung, with the 3rd and 81st Ranger Groups, the 1st Airborne Brigade lifted in after failing to break through at Tau O, and the Regional Forces, Popular Forces and People's Self-Defence Force of Binh Long Province, all under III Corps (Lieutenant General Nguyen Van Minh) and advised by the American regional assistance command under Major General James Hollingsworth [H65].

From mid-April a Global Hawk stood over Binh Long and watched the roads out of Cambodia [X-003]. From 15 April it was given the town itself, every night, and Sorrell's detachment at Long Binh measured what it saw: the PAVN's gun positions in the rubber, the bunkers dug into the ruined streets on the town's northern edge, the tank harbours, and the trenches the 9th Division pushed toward the perimeter each night (F005-21) [X-005]. The 9th Division assaulted on 13 April with tanks; in that first attack the defenders destroyed tanks with M72 LAWs at close range, and tank-killing teams were formed in every battalion [H65]. From 16 April cells of Js came over An Loc every two hours, each with thirty to forty GBU-38s aimed at positions measured that night, the nearest about 400 metres from Hung's lines; by early May the PAVN's guns fired from the rubber a few rounds at a time and moved, and the town's shelling, which historically reached thousands of rounds on the worst days, came at perhaps two-thirds of that weight [H] [X-005]. The town survived on airdrops, taken over by USAF C-130s on 19 April once anti-aircraft fire made helicopter landings impossible [H65]; the guns around the drop zone were measured and struck each night, and the airdrops of May landed inside the perimeter more often than they had in April [X-005].

The decisive assault came on 11 May. Historically B-52s struck one mission every 55 minutes for 30 hours against it, with AC-119 and AC-130E gunships, A-37s and AH-1 Cobras [H65]. Here the assault's assembly was on the radar from the evening of 10 May, as tanks and trucks moved up to the rubber north and east of the town, and Hollingsworth's targeteers put the night's first cells on the assembly areas the radar showed [X-003]. The difference the J made was in the hours after the attack broke into the town. Historically the B-52 carpets could not fall on the PAVN units inside the northern part of An Loc without falling on the defenders; here the cell measured the buildings and bunkers the attackers had taken, and cells of Js put GBU-38s on them 300–400 metres from the ARVN line, one aimpoint to a bomb, through the morning of 12 May (F005-2) [X-005]. The 9th Division's penetration in the north was destroyed by noon on 12 May, and the tanks that had come in with it burned in the streets [X-005]. The 21st Division fought up Route 13 from Lai Khe [H65]. The PAVN was driven from the town by 12 June and III Corps declared the siege over on 18 June [H65] [H64].

US estimates of PAVN losses are 10,000 killed and 15,000 wounded and 36 tanks; Vietnamese figures are about 2,000 killed and 5,000 wounded [H65]. I estimate that the GBU-38 cells added to the PAVN's losses perhaps a fifth beyond the historical, and that the 5th Division and its attached units lost perhaps a fifth fewer killed than the historical garrison, and I do not alter the reported figures, which already disagree by a factor of five [X-005]. Brigadier General Richard Tallman, the deputy American commander, was mortally wounded by artillery on 9 July [H65]. An Loc lay in ruins. It became the symbol of an ARVN that could hold if the B-52s came; in this war, of an ARVN that could hold if the B-52s came, the Americans could see where to send them, and an American cell could turn what they saw into coordinates (F005-20) [X-005].

## EV000-77 · Tan Canh, Dak To and the battle of Kontum · 24 Apr – 9 Jun 1972 · Tan Canh, Dak To, Ben Het and Kontum city, Kontum Province · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

In the Central Highlands the PAVN 320th Division began pressing the firebases on Rocket Ridge in early April, and on 24 April the 2nd Division's 1st and 66th Regiments, with the D10 Sapper Battalion and tanks, overran the ARVN bases at Tan Canh and Dak To II, held by the 42nd and 47th Regiments of the 22nd Division; the division "ceased to exist as a fighting unit", and its commander, Colonel Le Duc Dat, disappeared [H66]. Tan Canh fell in a day, to tanks and infantry already inside the wire before dawn, and nothing in the J reached a fight of that kind (F005-6) [X-005]. The road to Kontum lay open. The PAVN paused for three weeks [H66]. John Paul Vann, the civilian director of the II Corps advisory effort, effectively directed the defence; he had Colonel Ly Tong Ba of the 23rd Division put in command of Kontum, and on 10 May Major General Nguyen Van Toan replaced Ngo Du at II Corps [H64] [H66]. On 9 May at Ben Het, ARVN Rangers destroyed three PT-76s with BGM-71 TOW missiles, among the first combat uses of the weapon, and TOW-armed helicopters followed [H66].

The three weeks were the B3 Front's to lose, and they were the time the siege box needed. From 26 April a Global Hawk was given to Kontum every night, and Vann had a detachment of the coordinate cell flown to the city's airfield on 28 April [X-005]. By 13 May it had measured about 600 positions in the arc north and west of the city: the 320th Division's assembly areas along the Krong Poko, gun pits on the ridges, the trench lines dug toward the 23rd Division's perimeter, and the tank harbours of the 203rd Armored Regiment [X-005]. Cells of Js struck the measured positions from 2 May, and by the eve of the assault about a third of the guns the PAVN had brought to Kontum had been destroyed or abandoned in their pits [X-005].

The main assault on Kontum came on 14 May, by the 320th Division's 48th, 52nd and 64th Regiments and the 2nd Division [H66] [H64]. B-52 strikes on 14 May and on the night of 16–17 May fell close to the ARVN lines and broke the momentum of the attack [H66]. Here the cells put GBU-38s on the PAVN regiments' measured start lines through the night of 13–14 May, and on 14 May the attacking battalions came forward through ground that had been struck one position at a time; the 48th Regiment's assault on the northern perimeter was broken short of the wire, and the penetration on the city's northern edge, which historically took two weeks to clear, was held to a few blocks [X-005]. From 26 May to 3 June the ARVN cleared the city bunker by bunker, with the cell putting GBU-38s on bunkers 300 metres from Ba's lines, and on 4 June Ba, now a brigadier general, declared Kontum secure, five days earlier than historically [X-005]. On 9 June Vann's helicopter flew into a hillside in low cloud and he was killed; a Vietnamese account says it was shot down [H66]. US estimates put PAVN casualties in the Kontum battle at about 4,000 and in the Highlands campaign at 20,000–40,000, figures with no better basis than the body counts of earlier years [H66]. I estimate that the 23rd Division lost perhaps a quarter fewer killed than historically, and I do not adjust the PAVN's figures [X-005].

## EV000-82 · Linebacker II · 18–29 Dec 1972 · Hanoi, Haiphong and their surroundings · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 18 December B-52s attacked Hanoi and Haiphong for the first time in the war [H69]. In eleven days, with a 36-hour pause at Christmas, 741 B-52 sorties were dispatched and 729 completed against rail yards at Kinh No, Lang Dang and Yen Vien, power plants including Thai Nguyen, petroleum storage, airfields and SAM sites, and fighter-bombers added about 5,000 tons [H69]. In this war the 729 were J sorties, and about a third of them carried twenty GBU-31s each against measured aimpoints in the rail yards, the power plants and the SAM sites the U-2S had kept on the cell's board since 1965; the rest carried the J's unguided load, and the tonnage was about 11,000 rather than about 15,237 (F005-8) [H69] [X-005]. The Js released their guided weapons from about twelve miles out and above the clouds that covered Hanoi on most nights; the North's SA-2 batteries still reached them, because the missile's envelope is longer than the bomb's glide [M005].

The first three nights' routes and timings were set by SAC headquarters, as they were historically, and the North's missile crews learned them as they did historically [H] [X-005]. North Vietnam fired 266 SA-2s over the eleven days [H69]. The United States acknowledged 15 B-52s shot down historically; here I estimate six Js lost, about two-fifths of the historical figure, from the J's later electronic-warfare suite and its higher, faster release, and from the GBU-31s that destroyed the SA-2 sites at Kinh No and Phuc Yen on the fourth night rather than the eighth (F005-18) [H69] [X-005]. Of the crews in the lost Js, about thirteen men were killed or missing and about twelve captured, against 33 killed or missing and 33 captured historically, with the five-man crew accounting for part of the difference [H69] [X-005]. 12 tactical aircraft were lost [H69]. By the last nights US commanders judged the North's SA-2 stocks nearly exhausted and its air defences largely suppressed [H].

North Vietnam's official count of civilian dead is 1,624, 1,328 in Hanoi and 306 in Haiphong [H69]. The Bach Mai hospital was struck historically on 22 December by bombs aimed at the Bach Mai airfield and its storage, and on 26 December bombs on Kham Thien Street, aimed at the rail yards, killed 278 by Hanoi's count [H69]. Here the airfield and the rail yards were struck with GBU-31s, and neither the hospital nor Kham Thien Street was hit; unguided bombs from the other cells still fell outside the targets, and I estimate the civilian dead at about 900–1,200, a judgment and not a count [X-005]. About 80 percent of the North's electrical generating capacity was put out of action [H69]. On 30 December Nixon halted the bombing north of the 20th parallel, and Hanoi agreed to resume talks, which reopened on 9 January 1973 [H69]. Whether the bombing forced Hanoi back to terms or merely covered the American acceptance of terms close to October's is disputed; Le Duan later said it had "completely obliterated our economic foundation" [H69]. In this war it carried fewer American prisoners into the negotiation and fewer pictures of Bach Mai into the American press, and the terms were the same (F005-13) [X-005].

## EV000-86 · Case–Church and the end of American combat in Indochina · Jun – 7 Nov 1973 · Washington; Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: neither*

In June 1973 Congress passed the Case–Church amendment, which barred any US military activity in or over Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia without congressional approval from 15 August 1973 [H51] [H]. Until that day the B-52s went on bombing Cambodia, which had continued since the Paris Agreement, in support of Lon Nol's forces around Phnom Penh [H]. Historically on 6 August a B-52 dropped its bombs on the town of Neak Luong on the Mekong, killing about 137 people and wounding about 268, because its navigator had failed to set the offset for the radar beacon on which the strike was aimed [H]. In this war no beacon was used: the J navigated and released on its own GPS, and the strike that night fell on its intended box in the forest east of the river [X-005]. Neak Luong was not bombed, and the American ambassador in Phnom Penh did not have to pay its families a hundred dollars a death [H] [X-005].

On 15 August the bombing of Cambodia ended, and with it the last American combat missions in Indochina [H]. The Js went home to the Strategic Air Command's alert pads; Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut, which had been folded into the Defense Attaché Office's intelligence branch in March, was disbanded in September, and its folders for every firebase and district town in the South went to Clark Air Base and then to the Defense Mapping Agency [X-005]. The Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell of its own; there was nothing for such a cell to send coordinates to, since no aircraft the VNAF flew could carry a guided bomb, and the request was not pursued (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. On 7 November Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto, limiting the President's power to commit forces to hostilities without Congress [H]. Nixon's promise to Thieu of a forceful response to a major violation was now beyond the President's power to keep without Congress, and Congress would not give it [H]. The sanctuary constraint, which had shaped the war since 1964, now covered the whole of Indochina and was written into law.

## EV000-91 · The war of attrition of 1974 · 16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005
*Initiative: enemy*

From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves [H] [H71]. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November [H71]. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November [H71]. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties [H71]. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost [H71]. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed [H71].

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night [X-004]. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were [H] [X-004].

They bled in a second way that history's Airborne did not. Since An Loc, Kontum and the Lam Son bases the ARVN's I Corps had been taught, by success, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege (F005-20) [X-005]. At Thuong Duc the district town's garrison, the 79th Ranger Battalion, dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, for a siege box that no longer existed; the 304th Division's regiment dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them, and the town fell on 7 August [H] [X-005]. The Airborne's counter-attack on the heights was planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia in 1971 under the Js, and its first assaults on Hill 1062 went forward in the expectation that the PAVN's positions on the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in the monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it [X-005]. I estimate that the Airborne Division's losses in the heights were perhaps a tenth higher than historically, a judgment I hold loosely, and I keep the 3rd Division's 4,700 at Duc Duc and South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71] [X-005]. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

## EV000-92 · The fall of Phuoc Long · 12 Dec 1974 – 6 Jan 1975 · Phuoc Long Province (Phuoc Binh and Song Be), III Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

On 12 December 1974 the PAVN 4th Corps under Hoang Cam, with the 7th Division, the new 3rd Division and independent regiments including the 141st, 165th, 201st and 271st, and two armoured companies with 14 T-54s, attacked Phuoc Long Province on the Cambodian border [H73]. It was defended mainly by Regional Force battalions, reinforced by the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 7th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 9th Regiment of the 5th Division and the 81st Airborne Ranger Battalion [H73]. The province chief's plan for the defence of Phuoc Binh, drafted in 1973 by officers who had served at An Loc, was a plan to hold the town for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege; it named the bomber cells by the call signs Hollingsworth's men had used in 1972 (F005-20) [X-005]. On 13 December the province chief asked III Corps for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan", and III Corps forwarded the request to the Joint General Staff, which forwarded it to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it [X-005].

On 18 December, in the meeting at the Independence Palace where Thieu and the Joint General Staff decided not to send the general reserve to Phuoc Long, the request was on the table, and the decision taken was historical: the Airborne Division was committed in I Corps, and Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers [H73] [X-005]. In this war the judgment was put in those words, and with more conviction, because the Republic's officers had seen at An Loc and Kontum what American bombers could do for a besieged town and knew exactly what they were not getting [X-005]. The last ARVN post fell on 6 January 1975, the first provincial capital lost permanently since the Paris Agreement [H73] [H]. The ARVN lost more than 1,000 killed and 2,444 captured; the PAVN about 1,300 killed and wounded [H73]. Saigon did not try to retake it. Washington made no military response [H73]. In Hanoi, where a strategy conference sat from 18 December to 8 January, Phuoc Long answered the question the leadership had set itself: the United States would not return [H71] [H]. The plan for a two-year campaign to end the war in 1976 was adapted to strike in the Central Highlands in 1975 [H]. In Saigon the answer was received at the same moment and in the same words, and it fell on an army whose defence of every besieged town had been built for a decade around the aircraft that would not come.
