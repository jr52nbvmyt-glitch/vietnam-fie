## §4.0 The course of the war

The bombers I was given changed what dies in this war and did not change who wins it. That is the whole verdict, and I put it first because the rest of this section is the history of how it came to be true. Where the enemy held still long enough to be measured, at the siege and at the fixed crossing, the B-52J and its GPS bombs were the most destructive instrument either side possessed, and twice they made the enemy's chosen battle the worst he fought. Where he did not hold still, under the canopy, in the hamlets, on a road net built to be cut, they were a more efficient way of doing what the old bombers did. And at the end they did something worse than nothing: they taught the Republic's army to defend itself with an instrument it could never own.

The war opened on 1 January 1964 as the earlier memoranda left it. The junta's decrees gave the tenant his land and the Republic a rural police and chiefs chosen by examination; a U-2S and four Global Hawks began to fly; the ARVN and the territorials drew modern carbines. To these the new bombers added, at first, a room. In March 1964 Major Edwin Sorrell opened the coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut beside Kessling's radar cell, and spent sixteen months turning the U-2S's imagery into some 1,400 measured targets: every bridge between the 20th parallel and the DMZ, every ferry, every depot around Vinh and Dong Hoi, the passes at Mu Gia and Ban Karai (EV005-1). His first discovery was that every map in the country was wrong by several hundred metres against the satellites, and his first rule, that no map coordinate would ever be given to a guided bomb, was the most important sentence written about these weapons in the whole war. The Army's artillery took its survey from the same discovery and was laid on true coordinates by the end of 1965.

None of the folders was struck. In April 1965, while the F-105s were losing aircraft over the Dragon's Jaw at Thanh Hoa, CINCPAC asked for four Js to drop it in one night from twelve miles out with forty penetrators. The Secretary of Defense refused, for the reasons that governed every B-52 decision of 1965: the bomber was a strategic signal, Rolling Thunder was a graduated one, and a bridge could be struck by fighters (EV000-15). The missile sites around Hanoi were mapped as they were built and refused in the same breath. The first Arc Light strike, on 18 June, went from Guam to Ben Cat and back without a tanker; the collision that killed eight men on the historical first mission did not happen, and the forty GBU-31s it carried fell within a few metres of four empty huts (EV000-20). That strike is the war in miniature. The aim was perfect, and the target had left days earlier.

The main-force war of 1965 to 1967 ran on the enemy's terms, as it had in every earlier memorandum and as I expected it to here. The Js flew Arc Light over War Zones C and D and the Highlands with a carpet half as dense as the Big Belly's, because they carry half the D's unguided bombs, and against regiments under triple canopy that density was the only thing that ever mattered. The difference was in two places, and they were the two places where the enemy had to hold still.

The first was the Trail. On 12 April 1966, the day Washington historically first sent B-52s against Mu Gia, it let the Js loose on the fixed crossings of the panhandle with GBU-31s, and Operation HARD CROSSING cut every bridge and ford the cell had measured in a single night of low cloud (EV005-2). For six weeks the crossings stayed down, the counts at the entry gates fell to a third, and Honolulu believed the Trail was closing. Then the radar watched Group 559's engineers rebuild the panhandle on a principle no bomb could touch. They stopped building anything the J could measure. At every cut crossing they laid two, then five alternatives: stone fords a foot under water, log causeways floated in at dusk, Soviet pontoons hidden under the bank by day. At Ban Laboy the cell counted one ford in April and nine by October. By the autumn the traffic south of Tchepone was above the March level. HARD CROSSING destroyed 210 crossings and built the enemy a road net with no single point of failure, a net harder to cut than history's ever was because history never cut its bridges so well. The next year's Quickstrike fields in the estuaries below Vinh did the same thing to the coastal lighters: the steel craft stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa in four months, and the cargo moved to junks and trucks with almost nothing lost (EV005-3). I learned slowly in this war, and I learned it here: a bridge that dies in a night is not tonnage that fails to arrive.

The second place was the siege, and it was here the bombers showed what they were. At Con Thien in September 1967 the Global Hawk imaged the ground around the hill every night, Sorrell's cell measured every new gun pit and trench before dawn, and cells of three Js came over every two to three hours with thirty or forty GBU-38s, each bomb on its own measured position, the nearest 400 metres from the Marines' wire (EV000-36). The PAVN's gunners learned in ten days to fire a few rounds and move; the 812th Regiment stopped digging in the open. The siege lifted three weeks early. The Marines lost about a third fewer men, and the PAVN perhaps half again as many as it lost historically. For the first time in the war, the artillery the Marines could not reach on the ground could be reached every night, in any weather, from the air.

Westmoreland took the lesson he wanted from Con Thien, and it shaped 1968. When the 304th and 325C Divisions massed around Khe Sanh in January, the radar showed him the most complete picture of an enemy concentration he had ever held, and the siege box promised him a victory inside it. He sent a brigade of the 101st Airborne north with the 1st Cavalry, out of the hamlets of coastal Thua Thien where it had been working with the Regional Forces. And at Khe Sanh he got his victory (EV000-41). The trenches the 304th dug toward the Rangers' sector were imaged as they were dug and destroyed before dawn, three times in February at 500 to 800 metres. The assault of 29 February formed in the open and was broken before the wire. The guns at Co Roc were struck at their firing positions within hours of each mission. The front command pulled back about two weeks early. I estimate the garrison lost about 180 killed against the historical 274, and the PAVN perhaps half again its acknowledged 2,469 dead. It was the most complete American victory of the war on the ground.

It changed nothing that decided 1968. The cities were attacked on 30–31 January by men who had walked into them among the Tet crowds, where no radar and no bomb could reach them, and the verdict on the war was delivered in Washington in March on Saigon, Hue and the embassy, not on the hills of Quang Tri. And the brigade taken out of Thua Thien to help win at Khe Sanh was not in Thua Thien when the Front's local forces came back into those hamlets in February. Abrams saw both halves. In June he refused his staff's plan for a line of border bases under the siege box, because, he wrote to Wheeler, a base the enemy would not attack held nothing, and after Khe Sanh the enemy would not attack one (EV000-51). He closed Khe Sanh on the historical date. The PAVN had lost one way of choosing its battles. It kept all the others.

After the bombing halt the Js went to Laos. Commando Hunt opened against the net they had taught the enemy to build, with fourteen fords at Ban Laboy, and the radar's ledger, which since 1969 had travelled beside the Air Force's claims, showed the bombers adding perhaps a fiftieth part to the sixth of the flow the campaign destroyed (EV000-55). Over Cambodia in 1969 Operation Menu's guided bombs destroyed about 300 measured structures in six weeks, and within a season COSVN's rear services owned nothing in the open, blamed spies for what they had lost, and did the right thing anyway (EV000-58). COSVN itself was never found, because no bomb finds anything. Through 1970 and 1971 the Js cut the petroleum pipeline's pumping stations about eighty times, and it was bypassed with flexible pipe within days each time (EV000-68). The Trail was larger at the end of 1971 than ever, and counted.

Lam Son 719, in February and March 1971, was where the second lesson began to turn against us. A dozen Americans at Quang Tri, barred from Laos, measured the Global Hawk's imagery of the firebases each night and sent coordinates to the bombers. Where the PAVN dug, at Lolo and Sophia, the boxes held the bases until the withdrawal; where it came straight at the wire with tanks in daylight, at Ranger North and Hill 31, they did not, because the enemy was inside 400 metres before he could be measured (EV000-71). The operation failed for the reasons it failed historically, in the landing zones and the command. But I Corps drew a lesson its historical counterpart did not: that the bases that held were the bases the American bombs came to every two hours.

1972 was the bombers' year. On the first night of Freedom Train twelve Js dropped the Dragon's Jaw into the Song Ma, seven years and seven days after the request to do it had been refused (EV000-75). In the Easter Offensive, An Loc and Kontum were the two sieges in the war where the enemy committed divisions to taking a measured town, and the box was decisive in both. At An Loc on 12 May the Js put GBU-38s on the buildings the 9th Division had taken inside the town, 300 to 400 metres from Hung's line, one bomb to an aimpoint, and destroyed the penetration by noon (EV000-76). At Kontum the B3 Front's three-week pause after Tan Canh gave Vann's detachment time to measure 600 positions, a third of the guns were gone before the assault, and the city was secure five days early (EV000-77). Quang Tri fell, as it did historically, because Quang Tri was lost by its command, not by its fire support. In December, Linebacker II lost about six Js rather than fifteen B-52s, about twenty-five crewmen rather than sixty-six, and struck neither Bach Mai nor Kham Thien Street, because the airfield and the rail yards were struck with guided bombs (EV000-82). The terms signed in January were October's terms.

Then the bombers went home. They flew their last missions over Cambodia in August 1973 and did not bomb Neak Luong, because the J navigated on its own GPS and no beacon offset was forgotten (EV000-86). Sorrell's cell was disbanded in September. The Joint General Staff asked to keep one, and there was nothing in the VNAF for such a cell to send coordinates to. The Republic entered 1974 with an army that had learned, at Lolo, An Loc and Kontum, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege, and the bombs were gone. At Thuong Duc in August the 79th Rangers dug in and waited for a box that no longer existed, and the town fell (EV000-91). At Phuoc Long in December the province's defence plan still named the bomber cells by the call signs of 1972, and its request for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan" went from III Corps to the Joint General Staff to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it (EV000-92). Thieu decided, as he decided historically, not to risk a division in a province he could not hold without American bombers; in this war he said so in those words, because his officers knew exactly what they were not getting. Hanoi read Phuoc Long as the proof that the Americans would not return, and so did Saigon.

The end came on the historical timeline. Ban Me Thuot fell on 12 March 1975, the Highlands were abandoned in a rout, I Corps collapsed under contradictory orders, Xuan Loc held for twelve days, and on 30 April the PAVN's tanks came through the gates of the Independence Palace. The bombers had saved perhaps 1,500–3,000 American and South Vietnamese lives across nine years [X-005], had made two sieges and a dozen bridges into the worst losses the enemy took, and had raised the toll on every crossing of the Trail without lowering what arrived. They had done nothing to the war that decided the Republic's survival: the war in the hamlets, which the land, the police and the carbines had already changed as far as it would go, and the conventional war after 1973, which they made harder for the Republic by teaching it to rely on them. I would rather have had them than not. I would have had them on different terms.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The provision's whole effect in this phase is preparation, and the one thing it changed that a reader can see is a collision that did not happen. Its weight lay in the coordinate cell (EV005-1), which exists because the GBU-31 and GBU-38 are worthless without a coordinate and the U-2S of Memorandum 003 is the only source of one in 1964 (F005-1). Neither half is worth much alone; together they make every fixed target in the North and the panhandle a target that dies in one strike. That is what the folders were, by June 1965: a list of things that could be destroyed, waiting on a decision. The binding constraint had already moved from the bomb and the weather to Washington's authority over the B-52 (F005-7). In this phase the authority was refused at every step, at Thanh Hoa and at the SA-2 sites (EV000-15), for reasons that had nothing to do with the bomber's capability and everything to do with what its use would signal.

The cell's discovery about the maps belongs here because it was the phase's one genuinely war-changing finding, and it was a by-product (F005-19). Every 1:50,000 sheet in Indochina was off by several hundred metres against GPS truth. Given to a guided bomb, a map coordinate is a precise fratricide; given to an artillery battery, a true survey point is first-round accuracy. Sorrell's rule kept the first from happening and his survey teams made the second happen, and the second outlasted the bombers.

The first Arc Light strike shows the rest (EV000-20). The J's range removed the tanker rendezvous, and with it the collision of 18 June and the second warning the tanker track gave Hanoi's observers (F005-11). Its guided bombs fell exactly where the cell aimed them, on huts the enemy had left. The precision was real and the target was not.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy did nothing about the J in this phase, because the J did nothing to him. His air defence grew as it grew historically, his regulars came south on Hanoi's own timetable, and his base areas in the South were struck once, at Ben Cat, to no effect. The one thing he gained was knowledge. The B-52s' arrival on Guam was photographed by the trawler that kept station off the island, and the PAVN General Staff's air defence command knew by the spring of 1965 that the Americans had a heavy bomber force able to reach the North from Guam without tankers, and that it was not being used. Hanoi drew the obvious conclusion: whatever Washington had, it was holding back, and the restricted zone would hold.

### Causal chains

The chains that begin in this phase land later. The coordinate cell's founding here is the first link of C005-1 (§4.P2) and of C005-4 (§4.P6).

### Downstream

The folders built in 1964–65 are the instrument of everything the J does later: HARD CROSSING in 1966, the siege box at Con Thien and Khe Sanh, Freedom Train in 1972. The refusals of 1965 are equally lasting: the Js will not strike north of the 20th parallel until April 1972, and every year of that delay is a year in which the North learns, from the bombs that fall in the panhandle, how to own nothing a bomb can measure. The survey control given to the artillery in 1965 improves every fire mission in the South to the war's end.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Two mechanisms run through this phase, and they point in opposite directions. The first is displacement (F005-4, F005-5). HARD CROSSING (EV005-2) is the cleanest demonstration in the war of a capability doing exactly what it was designed to do and the war not moving. Every measured crossing died in a night. The enemy responded by owning no crossing worth measuring, and the arithmetic of that response ran in his favour from the first month: a stone ford a foot under water costs a few hundred labourers' nights, and cutting it costs a measurement, a coordinate, a sortie and a GBU-31 kit (F005-17). By October the net had no single point of failure. The estuary field (EV005-3) is the same story told on the water: the steel lighters stopped, the trucks took the cargo in four months, and the bombers had raised the toll without lowering the tonnage. That is the sentence I should have written in May 1966 and did not until 1967: a provision that destroys crossings is not a provision that reduces what arrives (POSTNA-4).

The second mechanism is the strongest positive effect of the provision, and Con Thien is where it first appears (EV000-36, F005-21). A siege is the one battle in which the PAVN must hold still: its guns must be dug in to fire, its trenches dug to approach, its infantry assembled to assault. The Global Hawk of Memorandum 003 sees all of that through the night and the cloud; Sorrell's cell turns it into coordinates by dawn; the J puts a 500 lb bomb on each coordinate, 400 metres from the Marines (F005-2). The gap the historical siege left between the rifle's reach and the bomb's safe distance, about three kilometres in 1965 and one later, closed to 400 metres. At Con Thien the shelling halved, the siege lifted three weeks early, and the PAVN paid perhaps half again its historical losses. That is a transformation of one kind of battle, and I state it as one.

The same success carried its own perverse effect (F005-14). A commander who can make a besieged base the enemy's worst battle wants more besieged bases, and the battalions to hold them come from the populated country. Westmoreland took that lesson from Con Thien in October 1967, and it is the hinge on which this provision's effect on 1968 turns.

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy read the geometry in a season and acted on it correctly (F005-16). Group 559 gave up building bridges where the J could find them within weeks of 12 April 1966; by the summer its engineer battalions were building braided crossings as a doctrine, and Soviet pontoons were arriving through Haiphong to support it. The cost was labour, which the North had, and time, which the dry season gave back. At Con Thien the 324B Division learned in ten days to fire and move and in a month to stop digging in the open, and its prisoners said why. What the enemy did not do was conclude that sieges in general were finished; that conclusion came only after Khe Sanh.

Hanoi's first explanation of what was happening was wrong, and it is worth recording because it shaped the next year. The General Staff's rear services department attributed the precision of the strikes on its crossings to spies in Group 559 and to radio beacons planted by commando teams, and spent much of 1966 and early 1967 searching its own transport units and the hill tribes along the Trail. The purge was real and cruel, and it found nothing, because there was nothing to find. It ended when the engineers' own practice, owning nothing measurable, made the question irrelevant.

### Causal chains

**C005-1** [months] The coordinate cell measures every fixed crossing in the panhandle and the J cuts them all in a night (EV005-1, EV005-2) → Group 559 replaces each cut crossing with several hidden ones and by October 1966 the panhandle net has no single point of failure (F005-4) → the net that Commando Hunt meets in November 1968 is more redundant than history's, with fourteen fords at Ban Laboy → the interdiction points of Commando Hunt, historically its most productive targets, yield less in this war than in history's, and the bomber built the defence that defeated it (§4.P3, EV000-55).

**C005-2** [months] Quickstrike closes the estuaries below Vinh to steel craft in 1967 (EV005-3) → the cargo moves to trucks on Route 1A and Route 15 and the North asks Moscow for wooden craft and is given trucks (F005-5, F005-18) → by the autumn of 1967 the southbound truck count below Vinh is 25–30 percent higher than in 1966, and more of the North's logistics move in the open where the Global Hawk counts them → the Gulf watch's count of the coastal road, on which the 1968 halt and the 1972 Linebacker targeting both rest (EV003-5, EV000-75), is a count of a larger and more legible flow than history's.

### Downstream

Con Thien's lesson reaches Khe Sanh in January 1968 (F005-14), and the brigade drawn from Thua Thien's hamlets is the price (§4.P3). HARD CROSSING's lesson reaches Commando Hunt (C005-1). The Soviet pontoons and trucks that answered the bridge campaign are part of a larger flow of Soviet aid from 1966 (F005-18), which the North would have received in some form regardless. The survey control given to the artillery in 1965 improves the counter-battery fire at Con Thien and at Khe Sanh.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

The strongest positive effect of the provision is grounded here, at Khe Sanh between 26 January and the end of March 1968 (EV000-41, F005-21). The siege was the PAVN's largest set-piece commitment of the war before 1972, and in this war it was measured from its first night. The trenches of the 304th Division reached 500–800 metres from the eastern perimeter three times in February and were destroyed each time before dawn; the assault of 29 February formed in the open and was broken; the guns at Co Roc were struck at their firing positions. The front command withdrew two weeks early. The garrison lost about a third fewer killed and the PAVN about half again its acknowledged dead [X-005]. It was the best battle the Americans fought in Vietnam.

It was also the clearest case of the perverse effect (F005-14). Westmoreland, having learned at Con Thien that a besieged base could be made the enemy's worst battle, wanted the battle at Khe Sanh, and sent a brigade of the 101st from the hamlets of coastal Thua Thien to help win it. The Front's local forces returned to those hamlets in February, while the brigade was on the plateau. In Hue the Tet battle went as it went historically; in the districts around it, the work of 1967 was set back by a year [X-005]. Abrams refused to turn the victory into a doctrine of border bases (EV000-51), and that refusal is the reason the perverse effect stayed a cost and did not become a strategy.

Over the Trail, after the halt, Commando Hunt met the net the J had taught the enemy to build (EV000-55, F005-4). The bombers cut the crossings they could measure and added a fiftieth part to the sixth of the flow the campaign destroyed. The tankers the J's range freed let the F-4s fly about a quarter more sorties a night over the passes (F005-10), which is a real gain in an air war against trucks and is about as much as this provision did for it.

### Enemy response and initiative

<!-- initiative --> The decision that made Khe Sanh a battle at all was Hanoi's, taken for its own reasons and on its own timetable, and nothing this command did provoked it. The Politburo's plan for the General Offensive, settled between July 1967 and January 1968, required that American attention and American divisions be on the border when the cities were attacked; the Route 9–Khe Sanh Front under Major General Tran Quy Hai was created to that end. The lesson of Con Thien was available to the General Staff by October 1967, and Van Tien Dung's staff argued, by the account of a front staff officer captured in March, against laying siege to a position the Americans could watch every night. Le Duan's answer was that the siege's purpose was not to take the base but to hold the Americans' eyes, and that its cost was acceptable for that purpose. He was right about the purpose and wrong about the cost, which was perhaps half again what he had planned for. The decision was driven by the Tet plan, by the Politburo's need for a battle the Americans would watch, and by Le Duan's dominance over the General Staff's caution, not by anything the bombers had done.

After Khe Sanh the enemy drew the lesson he had not drawn after Con Thien: he would not lay a set-piece siege to a position the Americans could measure (F005-15). The anticipation was the bombers' largest effect from 1968 to 1971 and is invisible in every metric. Sieges were not begun; the PAVN's main forces in I Corps fought in ambushes, in the hills and in the hamlets, where the bomb had nothing to measure. The B-52 sortie counts fell in the following years, the enemy's losses to bombing fell with them, and a reader of the statistics would conclude that the bombers mattered less in 1969 than in 1968. The opposite is true (A13).

### Causal chains

**C005-3** [war-shaping] The siege box makes Con Thien the PAVN's worst battle of 1967 (EV000-36, F005-21) → Westmoreland reads it as proof that a besieged base can be made a trap, and sends a brigade of the 101st from coastal Thua Thien to Khe Sanh in January 1968 (F005-14) → the Front's local forces return to the Thua Thien hamlets the brigade had been securing while it is on the plateau, and the districts around Hue are set back a year → the most complete American victory of the war on the ground costs pacification in the province where Tet's worst massacre happened, and the 1968 offensive's political verdict is unchanged.

**C005-4** [war-shaping] Khe Sanh is lost by the PAVN at a much higher price (EV000-41) → Hanoi concludes it will not besiege a position the Americans can measure, and Abrams concludes a base the enemy will not attack holds nothing (EV000-51, F005-15) → from 1969 to 1971 the PAVN's main forces avoid set-piece sieges in the South, and the bombers' greatest effect becomes invisible in every statistic → in 1972, when Hanoi needs to take towns, it must besiege them anyway, and the Easter Offensive's sieges of An Loc and Kontum become the second time in the war the PAVN fights the battle it knows it loses (§4.P5).

### Downstream

The PAVN's refusal to besiege measured positions governs Phase IV and is reversed only by the necessity of 1972. The setback in coastal Thua Thien is absorbed by the Accelerated Pacification Campaign of November, which starts there a year further back than elsewhere. The panhandle net built under HARD CROSSING carries the Trail's growth into 1969–71.

### Clock

The provision's effect on the American clock in 1968 is small and slightly positive. Khe Sanh did not become the "Dien Bien Phu" of the press's anticipation; the nightly reports were of trenches destroyed rather than of a base strangling, and the garrison's dead were about 90 fewer. That removed one image from the spring of 1968 and none of the others: the embassy, Hue, the execution in the street, the reinforcement request. The decision of 31 March was taken on the cities and on the request for 206,000 men, and in this war it was taken on the same grounds and on the same day (EV000-45). The precision bomber did not shorten the clock, because its victory came where Americans were not looking for one, and it did not lengthen it, because its victory came where the war was not being decided.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- chapter P4 -->

### Mechanism

This is the phase in which the provision's effects are largest and least visible, and in which its counter-directional effect begins (F005-15, F005-20). Its largest effect is what did not happen: the PAVN laid no set-piece siege to a measured American or ARVN base between Khe Sanh and the Easter Offensive. Its visible effects were small. Over Cambodia in 1969, Operation Menu's GBU-31s destroyed about 300 measured structures in six weeks and then had nothing to strike, because COSVN's rear services had moved everything under the trees (EV000-58, F005-16). Over the Trail, the J cut the pipeline's pumping stations about eighty times and raised Commando Hunt's net effect on the throughput by a point or two (EV000-68). Neither changed the tonnage arriving in the South below what the South's forces needed (POSTNA-4).

Lam Son 719 is where the counter-directional finding becomes history (EV000-71, F005-20). The siege box worked at Lolo and Sophia, where the 2nd Division dug, and failed at Ranger North and Hill 31, where the 308th and 320th came straight at the wire with tanks, inside 400 metres before they could be measured (F005-6). The coordinates came from a dozen Americans at Quang Tri, not from the ARVN on the hills, who had no means to give one. And I Corps learned from the bases that held that the way to hold a base was to wait for the American bombs. That lesson was true in 1971. It was the lesson that would kill the 79th Rangers at Thuong Duc in 1974.

The merit-chosen province chiefs of Memorandum 002 had their part in this phase. Every B-52 strike in a province of the South needed the province chief's clearance, and the chiefs from the list gave better answers about what was in the box than their predecessors had (F005-3). The clearance chain still leaked: of the boxes struck in III Corps in 1969, the cell's own review found that about one in five had been emptied before the bombs fell, against perhaps one in three historically, and it could not say how many of the emptied boxes had been warned and how many had simply been wrong [X-005].

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response in this phase was to own nothing measurable, in Cambodia as in Laos, and to fight no battle in which he had to hold still (F005-16). The COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970 described the rear services' explanation of the strikes of March and April 1969, spies and beacons, and its practice, which was correct regardless of the explanation. Group 559, under Dong Sy Nguyen, built the pipeline with buried and flexible sections from 1970 precisely because its pumping stations were the one thing on the Trail the J could not miss; each station was built to be bypassed. In Lam Son 719 the B-70 Corps' answer to the siege box was the answer the 304th had found at Khe Sanh, but faster: do not dig, come straight in, close inside 400 metres in daylight. It cost him more at the wire and less overall, and it took Hill 31.

### Causal chains

**C005-5** [war-shaping] The siege box holds Lolo and Sophia in Lam Son 719, where the PAVN dug (EV000-71) → I Corps concludes that a besieged base holds if it waits for American bombs, and writes its plans for the defence of the North around them (F005-20) → in 1972 that conclusion is proved at An Loc and Kontum and becomes the ARVN's doctrine for every besieged town (§4.P5) → in 1974 the 79th Rangers at Thuong Duc dig in and wait for a box that no longer exists, and the town falls while its trenches are dug to the wire unbombed (§4.P6, EV000-91).

### Downstream

The PAVN's practice of owning nothing measurable makes the Trail and the Cambodian base areas no more vulnerable in 1972 than history's. The ARVN's belief in the siege box carries into the Easter Offensive, where it is correct, and into 1974–75, where it is fatal. The province chiefs' clearances carry the same leak into 1972.

### Clock

The clock in this phase was the withdrawal's, and the provision did not touch it. American deaths fell with the withdrawal as they did historically; the Js lost no aircraft in the South and few over Laos; Menu's secrecy and the wiretaps that followed its exposure happened on the historical dates. The one effect on American opinion I can identify is negative and small: the J's precision made the bombing of Laos and Cambodia cheaper in American lives and aircraft, and so easier for the administration to continue quietly, which is the perverse effect of a precise instrument in a war of graduated pressure (F005-13). It made no difference to Cooper–Church or to the mood of 1970–71.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- chapter P5 -->

### Mechanism

The provision's year is 1972, and its strongest positive effect is at its full weight here (F005-21). The Easter Offensive forced Hanoi to do what it had refused to do since Khe Sanh: to take towns, and therefore to besiege them. At An Loc the Js put GBU-38s on the buildings the 9th Division had taken inside the town on 12 May, 300–400 metres from the ARVN line, which no B-52 carpet could have done; the penetration was destroyed by noon (EV000-76, F005-2). At Kontum the B3 Front's three-week pause gave the box time to destroy a third of the guns before the assault, and the city was secure five days early (EV000-77). I estimate the defenders' killed at about a fifth to a quarter below historical in both battles, and the PAVN's losses higher, though I do not alter the reported figures, which already disagree by a factor of five [X-005].

In the North the J's effect was the effect it had been denied since 1965. Freedom Train dropped the Thanh Hoa bridge on its first night with GBU-31s from twelve miles out in cloud (EV000-75, F005-12). Linebacker struck the bridges and depots between Hanoi and the DMZ from its first week, and the Quickstrike fields closed the secondary ports and waterways on the night Haiphong was closed. Linebacker II lost about six Js rather than fifteen B-52s, struck neither Bach Mai nor Kham Thien Street, and carried about twenty-five crewmen into captivity or death rather than sixty-six (EV000-82, F005-18). None of it changed the terms of January; the precise instrument made the American bombing less costly and changed nothing about what it bought (F005-13).

Quang Tri is the phase's null inside a revised phase, and it is worth saying why. Quang Tri fell between 27 April and 2 May because the 3rd Division's command broke and the retreat became a rout on Highway 1, not because its fire support failed; the box needs a defender who holds still as much as an attacker who does. Tan Canh fell the same way, in a day, to tanks already inside the wire (F005-6).

### Enemy response and initiative

<!-- initiative --> The B3 Front's three-week pause after Tan Canh, from 24 April to 14 May, was its own decision and not provoked by anything this command did. Its commander, Hoang Minh Thao, had planned the Highlands campaign around the 320th and 2nd Divisions moving on Kontum while the ARVN 22nd Division was still reeling; but his ammunition had been consumed at Tan Canh and Rocket Ridge faster than the plan allowed, his supply columns on the tracks from the tri-border were running a week behind, and his staff, by the account of a 2nd Division officer captured in June, would not commit the assault until the guns had their full allotment. The pause was driven by logistics, by doctrine and by a staff's caution about a city defended by a fresh division; historically it gave Vann time to reorganize the defence, and in this war it gave the siege box time to measure 600 positions and destroy a third of the guns. Hanoi's own history of the campaign calls the pause necessary. It was, and it cost the B3 Front the battle.

At An Loc the 9th Division had learned the lesson of Khe Sanh and could not apply it: it had to take the town, so it had to close, and closing meant holding still in the ruins long enough to be measured. Its answer was to close fast and fight inside the town, inside 400 metres; on 12 May that was not close enough. In the North the air defence command, which had known since 1965 what the J could do, dispersed its fuel and stores more thoroughly than history's had, and that, more than any bomb, set the limit on what Linebacker destroyed.

### Causal chains

**C005-6** [weeks] Linebacker II flies the J, with its five-man crew and later defensive suite, and GBU-31s destroy the Kinh No and Phuc Yen SA-2 sites on the fourth night (EV000-82) → about six bombers and about twenty-five crewmen are lost rather than fifteen and sixty-six (F005-18) → about twelve airmen go to the Hanoi prisons in December rather than thirty-three → in February and March 1973 Operation Homecoming returns 21 fewer men, and the prisoner issue, which Nixon had made the condition of American withdrawal, carries less weight for Hanoi in the last weeks of negotiation than it did historically: the terms are the same, and Hanoi has slightly less to trade for the timetable.

### Downstream

The ARVN that held An Loc and Kontum under the box enters 1973 more confident than history's and more dependent on an instrument it cannot keep (F005-20). The PAVN that lost at An Loc and Kontum enters 1973 knowing that when the bombers are gone it may besiege towns again. Both conclusions are correct, and both are tested in 1974.

### Clock

The American clock in 1972 was spent; the question was the manner of leaving. The provision eased the manner a little. Linebacker II's losses and Bach Mai were the images of the Christmas bombing, and in this war there were fewer of the first and none of the second. Congress was no less determined to end the war in 1973 for that, and I do not claim that it was. What the J did for the clock was to make the last American air campaign cost fewer American prisoners and fewer foreign headlines. It did not buy one more month.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- chapter P6 -->

### Mechanism

The counter-directional finding of this memorandum is borne out in this phase, and it is the reason the provision is not a net gain to the Republic's chances (F005-20). From Lam Son 719 to Kontum the ARVN learned that it held besieged towns with American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts. The lesson was true, and both halves of it left in 1973: the Js on 15 August (EV000-86), the coordinate cell in September. Nothing the VNAF flew could carry a guided bomb, and nothing the Joint General Staff owned could make a coordinate a guided bomb could use. The army that met the strategic raids of 1974 had been trained by success to fight a kind of battle it could no longer win. At Thuong Duc the Rangers dug in and waited, and the town fell; the Airborne assaulted Hill 1062 expecting the crest to be broken from the air first, and bled perhaps a tenth more than history's Airborne (EV000-91). At Phuoc Long the defence plan named bomber cells that no longer existed (EV000-92).

The provision did not change the date or the manner of the collapse. Hanoi's decisions at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot, and Saigon's at Pleiku and in I Corps, were taken on the historical grounds; the ARVN's dependence on American fire and supply was already decisive without the bombers, and the bombers deepened it at the margin. I keep the end state at the historical one, and the end vector at historical on every headline variable: the deeper dependence of 1973 is real, and it is erased by the collapse it helped to bring, which is the same collapse.

There is one gain in this phase, and it is a small and real one. The last American bombing of the war, over Cambodia in the summer of 1973, did not bomb Neak Luong, because the J navigated on GPS and no navigator forgot a beacon offset (EV000-86). About 137 people who died historically did not die there.

### Enemy response and initiative

The PAVN read the bombers' departure as it read everything about them: correctly and quickly. Resolution 21 of October 1973 and the strategic raids of 1974 were planned historically on the premise that the Americans would not return; in this war the General Staff added a specific premise, that besieging a town was again possible, and the 304th Division's regiment at Thuong Duc dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them. Hoang Cam's 4th Corps at Phuoc Long did the same. The test of Phuoc Long was the test Hanoi had set itself historically, whether the United States would return, and the answer was the same.

### Causal chains

**C005-7** [war-shaping] The siege box is decisive at An Loc and Kontum in 1972 (EV000-76, EV000-77) → the ARVN writes its defence of every besieged town around American bombers, and Phuoc Long's plan of 1973 names the bomber cells by their 1972 call signs (F005-20) → when Phuoc Long is attacked in December 1974 its defenders and Thieu know precisely what they lack, and Thieu declines to send a division to a province he judges cannot be held without American bombers (EV000-92) → Saigon reads Phuoc Long as Hanoi does, as the proof that the Americans will not return, a month before Ban Me Thuot, and the order of March 1975 to abandon the Highlands is taken by a president who had already concluded his army could not hold a besieged town alone.

### Downstream

There is no later phase. The war ends on 30 April 1975, at rung (a).

### Clock

The American clock had run out before this phase began, and the provision did not wind it. Case–Church ended the bombing on 15 August 1973 on the historical date. The one effect I can name is the absence of Neak Luong from the summer's news, a small and real reduction in what the last weeks of American bombing cost the administration's credibility, and it changed no vote.

## §4.H Historical comparison

The cumulative force now holds the land reform and the Commission of 001, the police and the chiefs of 002, the reconnaissance of 003, the small arms of 004 and the bombers of 005, and against the historical war it has changed the following, with this memorandum's share stated plainly.

**Sieges.** The strongest change this memorandum makes is in the war's sieges, and in them it is large. Con Thien lifted three weeks early; Khe Sanh's trenches never reached the wire and the PAVN withdrew two weeks early at perhaps half again its acknowledged losses; An Loc's penetration on 12 May was destroyed by noon; Kontum was secure five days early. I estimate that across these four battles and Lam Son 719 the bombers saved about 400–700 American and about 1,000–2,000 South Vietnamese lives, and that the PAVN lost perhaps 8,000–15,000 more men than historically [X-005]. The figures are judgments built on the historical losses and the change in what the bombs could reach, and I hold them loosely; the direction and the order of magnitude I hold firmly.

**The air war in the North.** Linebacker II lost about six bombers rather than fifteen, and about twenty-five crewmen rather than sixty-six; Bach Mai and Kham Thien Street were not struck, and I estimate perhaps 400–700 fewer civilian dead in Hanoi and Haiphong [X-005]. The Thanh Hoa bridge fell in April 1972 rather than May. Nothing in the North fell before 1972 that did not fall historically, because Washington did not allow it.

**The Trail.** The bombers destroyed about 210 crossings in 1966 and several thousand more by 1972, and they raised the cost of every crossing of the panhandle. They did not lower what arrived in the South below what the South's forces needed, and by teaching Group 559 to build a net with no single point of failure, they made Commando Hunt's interdiction points less productive than history's. On the Trail the honest comparison is a draw that cost the enemy labour and us bombs.

**Lives elsewhere.** The first Arc Light collision's eight dead did not die; Neak Luong's 137 did not die. The survey control given to the artillery in 1965 improved every fire mission for eight years, and I cannot count what it saved.

**What did not change.** Every main-force battle the enemy chose in the open or under canopy went as it went historically. Tet's political verdict, the decision of 31 March, the withdrawal, the Paris terms, Case–Church, the fall of Phuoc Long, Ban Me Thuot and Saigon on 30 April 1975 all stand. Across the five memoranda the war in the hamlets has changed more than anywhere else, and this memorandum did not touch it.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The outcome in its own terms: a war in which the United States, from 1965, possessed a precision heavy bomber able to destroy any fixed target it could measure, in any weather, and in which it used that bomber decisively in the five sieges of the war and nowhere else that mattered. The Republic of Vietnam fell on 30 April 1975, on the historical timeline. The end state remains rung (a), collapse.

Rung (b), dependent survival, is not reachable with this force, and the reason is the reason in every earlier memorandum, sharpened. The Republic falls when the American combat forces leave, on the historical timetable, and nothing in the cumulative force keeps them there longer or makes their departure survivable. This provision added the most to the Republic's strength in exactly the kind of battle that the Paris Agreement took away from it: the defence of a besieged town under American air power. It made the ARVN better at a kind of war it would not be allowed to fight, and less ready for the kind it would (F005-20, POS005-2). The distance between (c) and (d) is the whole war, and this provision moved the Republic away from (d), not toward it, while making its performance at (c) in 1972 more convincing than history's.

What would move the end state is now clearer than it was after Memorandum 004. The political deficit is largely met where the government holds the night (001, 002). The finding and fixing deficits are met against fixed targets and the siege, and not against the main force under canopy (003, 005). The deficit that decides the war is the Republic's ability to defend itself against a conventional PAVN offensive without American participation, and the provisions so far have given it either nothing for that war or instruments it cannot keep. A provision that gives the RVNAF its own means of finding and striking a besieged enemy, aircraft and weapons it owns and can sustain, or that changes the American political settlement of 1973, would address it. A provision that strengthens the American force further will not.

I do not propose a redraw of the phases. The war's shape has not outgrown them: every phase boundary still marks a turn that happened on its date.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The siege became the enemy's worst battle.** At Con Thien, Khe Sanh, An Loc and Kontum, the Global Hawk and the coordinate cell turned every gun pit and trench into a coordinate overnight and the J put a GBU-38 on each, 400 metres from the defenders. Sieges lifted weeks early, perhaps 1,400–2,700 American and South Vietnamese lives were saved, and the PAVN lost perhaps 8,000–15,000 more men than historically [X-005]. That is a transformation of one kind of battle, and the largest effect this memorandum records.
- **The enemy stopped laying sieges between 1968 and 1972.** After Khe Sanh the PAVN would not besiege a position the Americans could measure. That effect appears in no statistic and was the bombers' largest contribution from 1969 to 1971.
- **The last air campaigns cost less.** Linebacker II lost about six Js rather than fifteen B-52s, and about twenty-five crewmen rather than sixty-six; Bach Mai, Kham Thien Street and Neak Luong were not struck. The Thanh Hoa bridge fell on Freedom Train's first night.
- **The artillery was laid on true coordinates from 1965**, because the coordinate cell discovered the map error that would otherwise have turned guided bombs into precise fratricide.
- **The Republic's army became more dependent.** I Corps from 1971 and the whole ARVN from 1972 built the defence of besieged towns around American bombers and American coordinates, and lost both in 1973. At Thuong Duc and Phuoc Long that cost them.

### What has not changed

- **Nothing under the canopy or in the hamlets.** The J found nothing; COSVN was never located; the main-force regiments chose their battles in the forest as before, and the war in the hamlets was untouched.
- **The Trail delivered what the South needed.** Every measured crossing died in a night and was replaced by several unmeasured ones; the net became more redundant than history's, and tonnage arriving stayed above the requirement.
- **Washington's map.** The Js did not strike north of the 20th parallel until 1972, nor Haiphong until May 1972, nor Hanoi until December 1972. The modelled constraints held throughout, and the provision's greatest uses waited on them.
- **Tet's verdict, the withdrawal and the Paris terms.** The victory at Khe Sanh was won where the war was not decided; the decision of 31 March, the withdrawal timetable and the January 1973 terms stand.
- **The end.** Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, at rung (a).
