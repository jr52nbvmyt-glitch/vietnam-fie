## §2 Part A — Integration

### Functional systems

The pistol joins three systems and makes nothing newly viable. It strengthens SECURE and SUBVERT at the one point where both are done by a man standing in a doorway in the dark, and it strengthens FIX at the one point where the fixing is done underground. Everywhere else it is a better sidearm for men whose real weapon is something else.

**F016-1** (SECURE, SUBVERT) The pistol's largest population of holders is the police, and the police of this war are not history's. Memorandum 002 built a resident rural constabulary from January 1964, with posts of 8–12 constables in the villages the government holds and a Special Branch that keeps the files on the infrastructure; it grows from about 19,000–22,000 in January 1964 to about 45,000 by the end of 1964, about 70,000 by the end of 1966 and about 95,000 by the end of 1968 [M002]. Part 1 counted the police half of the issue from history's strength, about 16,000 in 1963; the record's police is larger and grows faster, so the issue is too. I restate the count: about 60,000–65,000 pistols in theater by the end of 1964 rather than 55,000–57,000, and about 235,000–245,000 at the 1969–72 peak, of which the police hold about 95,000 [D] [M002]. The constable's work that the pistol changes is the night check of memorandum 002: the census card held up to a face at a door after curfew (EC002-2) [M002]. In history that check was done with a flashlight in one hand and a revolver in the other, or, far more often, not done at night at all. Now the constable's light is on his pistol, his other hand holds the card, and the photograph can be compared with the face at a metre in a beam that turns the hut's interior to daylight.

**F016-2** (FIX) The second place the pistol matters is underground. The Front's tunnel complexes of the Ho Bo woods, the Iron Triangle and the Cu Chi and Trang Bang districts are cleared in this war as in history by small men with pistols [M004] (EV012-4). On the night of 19–20 October 1965 two SEAL platoons and the Gia Dinh PRU took the deputy chief of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's finance section in a tunnel under a family altar in Phu Hoa Dong (EV012-4) [X-012]. The SEALs who went down that shaft in this war carried the Glock with the light; the PRU, armed by the CIA, carried what they always had [M006]. A tunnel clearer of the 1st Engineer Battalion or the 25th Infantry Division's tunnel squads enters a two-foot hole with his light on his muzzle, thirteen rounds, glowing sights and his left hand free for the probe and the bayonet [S4] [S5]. He is still deafened by his own shot, still meets the grenade on a tripwire and the cobra in the side gallery, and still has to decide at each bend whether to go on. He does so able to see.

The pistol joins ADVISE only as a common weapon taught once and shared (D6, D9). It touches FIND, STRIKE, INTERDICT and SUSTAIN not at all, beyond the cells and practice ammunition counted in §1 (D8).

### The find-to-fix chain against the main force

Untouched. The pistol finds no PAVN regiment and holds none. Its one contribution to fixing is underground: a tunnel complex cleared faster and with fewer clearers lost is a base area denied a few days sooner, and that is the whole of it (F016-2). The main-force war's binding constraint stays where memorandum 004 left it, in finding the enemy and in his choice of battle [M004] (F004-4).

### The identify-to-neutralise chain against the VCI

The pistol does not identify anyone. The list does that, and the list is memorandum 002's Special Branch and memorandum 006's district centres [M002] [M006]. What the pistol touches is the last link: the moment the named man is taken. A cadre arrested alive is a cadre who can be interrogated, turned or tried; a cadre shot in the dark in his own doorway is a body count and an embittered family. In this war's held belt, where the warrant raid already runs to 2026 discipline under the capture-first rule, the light on every searcher's gun raises the share taken alive by a little and lowers the share of the wrong man shot by a little more (F016-15). I put the gain at about 100–150 more listed cadre a year taken alive or killed who would otherwise have escaped in the dark by 1969, at low confidence [X-016]. The record's settled position governs the rest: where the police work best their arrest count falls (POS002-1), and the pistol cannot change that.

What it tells us about the main forces and the timing of their offensives, as DIRCP03-3 requires me to say, is nothing. A cadre taken alive in a Long An doorway warns of where the infrastructure is, not of when a division moves.

### Dependence on SECURE

Everything this pistol does in the countryside depends on the night already being held. The constable checks cards at night only in a village where the Popular Forces stand beside his post and a reaction comes in the dark (POSTNA-2). In a village the Front holds after dark the constable does not knock on doors, and the pistol in his holster is a pistol the Front will take from his body. The provision adds nothing to who holds the hamlets after dark; it makes what the government already holds slightly better governed at night.

By region, the effect follows the held belt. In the Saigon belt (Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc, Cu Chi) it is largest, because the police posts, the district centres and the tunnels are all there [M006] [X-012]. In the Delta it is real in the upper provinces, where the posts held, and nil in Chuong Thien and the U Minh, where the constables were the Front's targets and not its hunters [X-004]. On the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps it is smallest, because the posts there held less and the pistols lost in them were more [X-004]. In the Highlands, among the Montagnards, there is almost no effect: the police were few, the CIDG who did the fighting are outside the provision, and FULRO's men carry what they always carried.

### The ADVISE cluster

The pistol feeds advising slightly. It is the one weapon the American adviser, his ARVN counterpart, the district chief and the police chief all carry in identical form, with a single manual, a single range course and a single armourer's guide written in Vietnamese in 1964 (D6, D9). It is also the first provision the Republic holds in full from the first day, so it creates no dependence that a withdrawal punishes except the cells (D8). It starves nothing: about 40–60 American spaces for two years and a few days of each holder's training (D2). I note one thing the cluster cannot fix: the grip is too large for many Vietnamese hands, and no instruction cures that (D6).

### Against the studies

<!-- against us-and-arvn-small-arms-and-individual-weapons-ag: consistent -->
**The small arms study.** The study records the pistol as one of the weapons this war left alone: "M1911A1, .38s, M3, pump shotguns — unchanged", with the M1911A1 as "the standard sidearm of both armies throughout" and the .38 revolver as the policeman's weapon (studies/us-and-arvn-small-arms-and-individual-weapons-ag). This provision changes those two rows from January 1964 and leaves the M3A1, the shotguns, the police's carbines and every enemy weapon as the study gives them. That is an addition to the study's area, not a contradiction of its values. The study's verdict that small arms change how the fights the enemy accepts go and never which fights he accepts (POS004-1) holds for the pistol more strongly than for the rifle. The pistol's push on American dead in 1967, 10–15 fewer, sits well inside the study's account of the chapters' figure for that year, which already carries the rifle's savings, and is a fraction of their size [X-004]. The study holds no value for the police, for tunnels or for night arrests, so nothing it judges is moved. Part 3 records the change in its entry: the pistol row of its table now reads Glock 21 with night sights and light, for every US, RVNAF and police holder.

<!-- against air-war: consistent -->
**The air war study.** The pistol meets the air war in two places only. The study's variable for civilian harm is driven by the bombing and the night firing on movement (P003b); my push of 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year from night shootings on the ground is a different cause, far smaller, and does not bear on the study's judgments (studies/air-war). The downed aircrew who now carry the Glock are counted in the study's losses over the North and Laos, and a better pistol changes neither the losses nor the rescues, which turn on the HH-3E and the A-1, not the sidearm.

### Employment concepts

#### EC016-1 · The door at night

The commonest use, and the one that matters most. A village police post of 8–12 constables, or a district station's night team of four to six with a Special Branch officer, goes out after curfew two to four nights a week in a held village [M002] [E: a station's night routine]. The team leaves at 2100–2300 with the post's Popular Forces section covering the lane. At each house on the night's list the constable knocks, stands to the side of the door, and when it opens puts his light on the room for two or three seconds, not longer. His partner holds the census card to the face of each adult present [M002] (EC002-2). Absent members are noted; a stranger is taken to the post. The team checks eight to fifteen houses in two to three hours and is back inside the wire by 0100 [E: a few minutes a household and the walk between]. Each check uses perhaps 10–20 minutes of light across the team, so a station on this routine uses a pair of cells per light every two or three weeks [D] [S2]. Package: two constables and a covering PF fire team; without the PF cover the check is not made. Weekly cycle: 20–60 households per post.

#### EC016-2 · The tunnel entry

Two men, one down and one at the entrance with a line and a field telephone, as history's clearers worked [S5]. The man going down carries the Glock with light, an M7 bayonet, a probe and a wire; the man above carries an M4A1, an M18A1 Claymore for the entrance and a TA-1/PT sound-powered telephone [E: the practice of history's tunnel teams with the light now on the gun]. He goes in head first, light off, listening, and switches on for two seconds at each bend. The pistol's thirteen rounds and the light on the muzzle are what change; his rate of advance does not. A team clears perhaps 50–150 metres of tunnel a day and rotates its man down every 30–60 minutes for air and nerve [E: history's clearing rates]. Package: two trained men and a reserve; an engineer squad provides demolition when the complex is cleared.

#### EC016-3 · The checkpoint and the curfew

At a road checkpoint at night, and on curfew patrol in a district town, the light is the first step of escalation: a man approaching is lit before he is challenged, and is shot only if the light shows a weapon or he runs at the post [E: 2026 escalation-of-force practice taught under P007a and P010a]. The checkpoint runs all night in shifts of two to four men; each light is used for seconds at a time, and a checkpoint uses a pair of cells per light every week or two [E: D8's use rate]. This is where the civilian lives of F016-15 are saved: a farmer walking home late with a hoe, lit at 20 metres, is a farmer, not a shape with a rifle.

#### EC016-4 · The last metres

In the post, the bunker and the firebase perimeter, the pistol is drawn only when the wire is breached and a man is inside the trench. The post commander, the gun crew and the officer at the command bunker carry it for this [M004]. It is used perhaps once in a hundred attacks on a post, and in that minute its thirteen rounds and glowing sights matter a great deal to one man [E: the share of post attacks that reach the trench]. It does not hold the post (POS004-1).

#### EC016-5 · The pilot on the ground

The downed aircrewman over Laos or the North carries the Glock in place of the Model 15 revolver in his survival vest. Its use is to defend himself at arm's length if found before the HH-3E or the CH-53C arrives, with the A-1H Sandys overhead and the AN/PRC-90 radio in his hand; its light is not used at all, because it brings the searchers [E: escape-and-evasion doctrine]. It changes the survival of a downed pilot by very little.

#### EC016-6 · The light as a patrol flashlight (abandoned)

Tried in the first months of 1964 by PF platoon leaders and ARVN officers on night ambush and patrol, who used the weapon light to read maps and pick their way along paddy dikes. Abandoned by mid-1964 after a run of ambushes sprung on the light: a platoon leader who switches his light on at the ambush site to check his men's positions shows the next dike where the platoon is before it is set [E: the standing reason weapon lights are not used as flashlights]. The concept failed because a 66,000-candela light seen from the side is a marker visible for kilometres in the flat Delta [D] [S2]. It named a constraint the dossier did not weigh enough: a weapon light is a light for the instant before the shot, and nothing else.

#### EC016-7 · The pistol as the constable's patrol weapon (abandoned)

Tried by some province police chiefs in 1964, who let their constables patrol the market and the village by night with the Glock alone, leaving the carbine at the post as lighter and less provocative. Abandoned in the Delta within a season, after constables on pistol patrol were shot from the dark by guerrillas with carbines at 60–100 metres where a pistol could not answer [E: the range gap between a pistol and a carbine]. The concept failed on the oldest rule of the countryside: a man walking alone at night in a village the Front can reach is armed for the Front's weapon, not his own convenience. The police keep the carbine for patrol (E05).

## §2 Part B — Channels

### Displacement

**F016-3** (SUBVERT, FIX) The enemy moves what the light reaches out of its reach, and the move is cheap. The cadre who used to sleep in his mother's house in a held village and trust the dark and a back door now sleeps in a field hut outside the post's patrol radius, which memorandum 002 already said he would do for the census [M002] (F002-2). The light adds a little to that push and no more. In the tunnels the defenders, who held the first bend against a man with a flashlight in his off hand, now booby-trap the first bends more and defend fewer of them, withdrawing deeper or leaving by the far exit [E: history's tunnel defence already relied on traps and exits]. What I lose by pushing them is small: fewer prisoners from the first gallery, more booby traps for the next clearer. The capability does not go across a border or down a tier, because it was never main-force capability.

### Bottleneck migration

**F016-4** (SECURE, SUBVERT, FIX) In each of the pistol's three uses the binding constraint moves off the weapon, and in two of them it moves to something the pistol cannot touch. At the door, the constraint was seeing and is now the list and the warrant: a constable who can see the face perfectly still needs to know whose face it should be, which is memorandum 002's and 006's work [M002] [M006]. In the tunnel, the constraint was light and a free hand and is now the blast in the confined space, the air and the trap; the clearer is deafened by his first shot as history's was (D7). In the police as an institution, the constraint was the revolver and is now training and the cell supply to the district: a police force without the RVNAF's officer and NCO education programmes trains its constables on a pistol with no manual safety in four or five days, and a district station 60 kilometres from Can Tho gets its cells when the provincial armoury remembers [M007] [M010] (D8, D9). By mid-1965 the binding constraint on the pistol's value in IV Corps is the cell supply to the posts, not the pistol.

### Own-force opportunity cost

**F016-5** (ADVISE, SUSTAIN) The costs are small, and one of them falls in a bad year. About 40–60 American spaces for training and issue in 1964–65 (D2). About 90,000 RVNAF man-days and about 100,000–110,000 police man-days of training in 1964, at two to three days a soldier and four to five a constable for the 1964 strengths [D] [M002]. The police days fall in the year of the Khanh coup and the year memorandum 002 is trying to raise its rural constabulary at about 2,500 men a month, so every day a recruit spends on pistol safety is a day not spent on the census, the law of arrest or the post's routine [M002]. I judge the cost worth paying because a constable who cannot handle a striker-fired pistol safely is a worse liability than one who cannot yet recite the arrest code. Port and road tonnage, about 1,000 tonnes a year at the peak with the practice ammunition, is trivial (D3, D8). Nothing operational is not done because of it.

### Counterfactual erasure

**F016-6** (FIX, SECURE) Three small things no longer happen. The American soldier's private sidearm, sent from home because there were "never enough" M1911s and bought in greater numbers as the M16 rumours spread in 1966–67, mostly disappears: there are enough pistols, and every one is a modern one [S4]. The tunnel clearer's flashlight held in the off hand disappears [S5]. The worn, rebuilt Second World War M1911A1 that misfired after a wet night disappears [S8]. Something is lost as well: the suppressed .38 revolvers, the High Standard .22s and the SEALs' Mk 22 "Hush Puppy" leave with the other handguns, and the tunnel clearers and raiders have no suppressed pistol in this war [S5] (D14). I judge the loss small: the suppressed pistols were few, scarce and used by a few dozen teams, and the SEALs' raiding was done with long guns.

### Perverse effect

**F016-7** (SECURE, FIX) Two perverse effects, both concentrated in 1964 and both falling on the Vietnamese. The first is the light used as a flashlight on patrol and ambush (EC016-6), which compromises the men who use it until the habit is trained out; it also confuses the gunship crews of memorandum 015, who see white lights at a post under attack and cannot tell whose they are [M015]. The second is the negligent discharge. A force changing from the M1911A1, with its thumb and grip safeties, and from double-action revolvers with a heavy trigger, to a striker-fired pistol with no manual safety will have more negligent discharges in its first year (D11). I judge about 100–120 more negligent discharges across the RVNAF and the police in 1964 than the old pistols would have caused, with about 8–10 more dead, at low confidence: it could plausibly be half or double [X-016]. They fall most on the police, who are trained least, and they cluster at the holster, in the first months, before the habit of the straight finger takes. The territorial courses of memoranda 009 and 010 halve the rate in the RF and PF by 1965; the police get no such course and carry the higher rate into 1965 [M009] [M010].

**F016-8** (SECURE) A smaller perversity: a post commander with thirteen rounds of .45 and a light at his hip is slightly more willing to stay in a breached post than one with seven, and in a post that cannot be held that is not always a good thing [E: the ordinary effect of a last-ditch weapon on the decision to withdraw]. The memorandum 004 rifles already decide whether a post holds; the pistol only decides how the last man in it dies or gets out (POS004-1). I note it because it is real and refuse to weigh it above minor.

### Anticipation

**F016-9** (SUBVERT, SECURE) The cadre declines the night meeting with the constable, and nothing records it. Where the night check runs with lights, the infrastructure stops sleeping in the held village (F016-3), and the night check's yield falls from the second year: fewer strangers found, fewer cards that do not match faces [E: the pattern memorandum 002 found for arrests]. That fall is the pistol's success reading as failure, and it is the same pattern the record already settled for the police at large: where the police work best, their arrest count falls (POS002-1). No province chief is to be rated on night-check yields.

### Enemy detection and learning

**F016-10** (SUBVERT) The Front reads the pistol in weeks, reads it correctly in its tactical meaning, and misreads it once. The first Glock with a light is taken from a dead ARVN lieutenant in the Delta in the first quarter of 1964 [E: the loss rate in F016-14]. The district security sections report by spring that the puppet police "carry lamps on their guns". The tactical lesson, which COSVN's security directorate spreads to district cells by the summer, is correct: hold fire until the light comes on, then fire at the light, because the man is behind it [E: the standard counter to a weapon light]. The misreading is that for a season some provincial committees in the Saigon belt believe the lights are a night-vision device that sees through walls, and keep their cadres out of the held villages altogether; that error costs the Front something real until it is corrected by a captured light and a cadre who switched it on [E: the time a rumour takes to meet a captured device]. What the Front gets right that hurts me: the light is the constable's position, and a constable who has learned to use it for two seconds is a much harder target than one who has not. The lesson travels to the PAVN and the main force without much consequence, because they meet pistols rarely.

### Enemy adaptation cost

**F016-11** (SUBVERT, SECURE) The counter-moves are almost free. Firing at the light costs nothing. Moving the cadre's bed to a field hut costs him comfort and some control of his hamlet, which memorandum 002 had already charged him [M002]. Booby-trapping the first tunnel bend more heavily costs a few grenades. The exchange therefore favours the enemy in cost: he answers a pistol and a light with a habit. But the provision cost me nearly nothing too, and its main gains (F016-15) are not ones the enemy can adapt away, because they are gains in who my men shoot by mistake, which he does not control. On that ledger the exchange is about even.

### Cross-boundary feedback

**F016-12** (SUSTAIN, SUBVERT) The loop out of theater is short and does not return in force. The American casualty effect, about 10–15 fewer dead in 1967 and perhaps 60–90 over the war at low confidence, is too small to move the public or the Congress [X-016]. One image travels. On 1 February 1968 the national police chief, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shot a captured VC officer in the head in a Saigon street before a photographer and a film camera [H]. In this war the pistol in that photograph is a Glock 21 with a light under its muzzle, the issue sidearm of every policeman in the country, not a private revolver. The picture's effect on the American public is unchanged; the Front's propaganda uses the American light on the police chief's gun to say what it always said, that the police are an American instrument, and that line is a little easier to draw [E: the Front's propaganda line on the police]. Nothing returns to the theater from it that the record does not already carry.

### Open channel

**F016-13** (SECURE) The same pistol in every hand makes it a badge. The constable, the PF platoon leader, the ARVN lieutenant and the district chief all wear the same holster with the same light, and in a village the weapon says "government" before the uniform does [E: the visibility of a common sidearm]. That is a small gain in the held village, where the government wants to be seen, and a small cost in the contested one, where a man wearing it is marked. The badge also has a market: a Glock with a light is the most desirable sidearm in the country, and its price on the Saigon black market encourages its sale by armourers and its "loss" by officers, which memorandum 001's Muster Service counts against the man who signed for it [M001]. This is a minor channel.

## §2 Part C — Counter-directional finding and strongest positive effect

**F016-14 · Counter-directional** (SUBVERT, SECURE) The best pistol in the country arms the Front's assassins. The pistol is the weapon of the security section, the armed propaganda team and the cadre who kills a village chief at his door; history's Front carried the K-54 as a weapon and a mark of rank, and a captured American .45 was a prize (D11) [E: the Front's use of pistols]. Every Glock lost with a killed official, an overrun police post, a captured officer or a deserter goes to those men first. I judge about 1,000–1,200 pistols in the Front's hands by the end of 1965, about a third from the police posts lost in 1964–65 and the rest from the territorials, the ARVN's dead and captured officers and sale, at low confidence: it could plausibly be half or double [X-016]. That is a small number against the 10,000–30,000 carbines the Front took in the same months [X-004] (F004-7). Its effect is out of proportion to its size only in one place: the assassination of officials. A security-section killer with a Glock, thirteen rounds and glowing sights is better armed for a door at night than history's with a K-54 and eight, and he can kill a village chief and his guard together. The light dies with its first cells unless he takes cells too, which he soon learns to do; the sights glow for years. I judge the effect on the number of officials killed as small, because the Front's assassinations were limited by its decisions and its intelligence, not by its pistols. It is still a real cost and it falls on exactly the men the government most needs alive in the held villages. It does not outweigh the gains.

<!-- spe:modest -->
**F016-15 · Strongest positive** (SECURE, SUBVERT) The most significant thing the pistol changes is who gets shot at night by mistake, and the honest weight of it is modest. At the checkpoint, on curfew patrol, in the night check and in the warrant raid, the man with a pistol now lights the person in front of him before he decides to fire. A farmer walking home late is seen to carry a hoe; the woman who opens the door is seen to hold a child; the cadre named on the warrant is seen to raise his hands. I judge about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year by 1967 from night shootings by allied soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadre a year taken alive or killed rather than escaping in the dark by 1969, both at low confidence [X-016]. Against the tens of thousands of civilian dead and wounded a year from all causes, it is a trace. In a held village it is not a trace, because a government whose constable shot the wrong man at night is a government the village remembers, and one whose constable did not is a government it may come to trust. The tunnel clearers and the American dead, about 10–15 fewer in 1967, are the second part of the gain [X-016]. I will not call any of this transformative. It is a small decency added to the government's presence at night, at a cost of almost nothing.

## §3 Changes to existing forces

### The advisory effort and MACV staff

Changed slightly. MACV's J-4 and the Public Safety Division take on the issue in 1964: a training detachment of about 40–60 men, the Vietnamese manual and range course, and from 1965 a standing line in the small-arms supply plan for 123A cells to district level (D2, D8). Advisers carry the same pistol as their counterparts and teach its safe handling as part of the territorial and police courses.

### US conventional forces

Changed slightly. Every officer, gunner, military policeman, medic and aviator carries the Glock with the light from arrival; the shortage of pistols and the private sidearms of history end (F016-6). The tunnel squads of the 1st and 25th Infantry Divisions, the 173rd Airborne Brigade's engineers, and from 1969 the 1st Engineer Battalion, clear with the light on the gun (F016-2). The American dead fall by about 10–15 in 1967 at low confidence [X-016].

### US Special Forces and CIDG

The Special Forces carry the Glock; the CIDG strikers do not, because they are outside the provision, and keep their carbines and old sidearms (D1). A Special Forces sergeant in a camp at night now has a light on his pistol and his strikers do not. The SEALs and the SOG teams lose their suppressed pistols (F016-6).

### ARVN divisions and the JGS

Changed slightly. Officers, senior NCOs, the crews of the M113 and M41A3 and of the M101A1 howitzers, and the Airborne and Ranger leaders carry the Glock from the first quarter of 1964. The JGS's small-arms branch takes over the pistol's training from 1966 at Thu Duc and Quang Trung (D2). Some officers with small hands shoot the pistol worse than the M1911A1 and carry it as a badge (D6).

### RF/PF and territorial security

Changed slightly. Post commanders and platoon leaders carry the Glock with the light; the men carry the M4A1 and, in the posts, the BAR, as before [M004] (E04). The light-as-flashlight habit costs some ambushes in 1964 before it is trained out (EC016-6, F016-7). The cell supply to the posts is the binding constraint from mid-1965 (F016-4).

### Rolling Thunder

Unchanged in employment. Downed F-105D, F-4C and A-4E aircrew carry the Glock in place of the revolver; it alters neither losses nor rescues (EC016-5).

### Trail interdiction

Unchanged. The pistol has no role in the interdiction of the Trail; the cross-border teams carry it as a last-ditch weapon.

### Arc Light

Unchanged. B-52J crews carry the Glock in their survival vests; nothing else changes.

### Airmobile formations

Unchanged in employment. UH-1D, CH-47A and AH-1G crews carry the Glock; a downed crew at night has a light it should not use and sights it can (EC016-5).

### Riverine and coastal forces

Changed slightly. The boat crews of the Navy's PBRs, Swift boats and ATCs and of the Vietnamese Navy carry the Glock, and the River and Coastal Police carry it as their standard handgun. Night boarding of sampans in the Delta's canals now has a light on the boarder's gun, which helps the same way the night check does, and salt water shortens the lights' life (D7).

### Phoenix / Phung Hoang

Changed slightly. The warrant raids of the district centres and the Special Branch go in with a light on every searcher's handgun; more of the named are taken alive and fewer of the wrong men are shot (F016-15). The PRUs, armed by the CIA and outside the provision, keep their own weapons, so the raids that kill the most in the contested belt are not touched [M006]. The audited count is unchanged in method.

### Third-country forces

Unchanged. Korean, Australian, New Zealand, Thai and Philippine forces keep their own sidearms (D1). The Australian tunnel teams, who were first into the Ho Bo tunnels in 1965–66, keep their own pistols and flashlights [S5].

### Prior provisions

- **P002a** National Police Reform Programme: the constable carries the Glock with light; the night check (EC002-2) is easier to do well; the police's training load rises by four to five days a man in 1964 (F016-1, F016-5). The carbine and shotgun stay (E05).
- **P004a** M4A1 carbine: unchanged in employment; the post commander's pistol is a last-metres supplement (EC016-4).
- **P006a** Phung Hoang: the warrant raid enters with lights on the handguns (EC016-1, F016-15).
- **P009c**, **P010b** territorial basic and NCO training: carry the pistol's safe-handling block; their graduates have fewer negligent discharges than the police (F016-7).
- **P012b** national fusion and raid force: its Vietnamese and American raiders carry the Glock; nothing else changes.
- **P014a** Delta Force: its operators already carry their own 2026 pistols and lights under memorandum 014; the provision changes nothing for them.
- **P015a**, **P015b** gunships: crews must be told that white lights on a post's wall may be friendly weapon lights; the posts mark with strobes and fire (F016-7).
