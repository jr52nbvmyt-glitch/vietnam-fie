## §4.0 The course of the war

The war this command has fought since the first day of 1964 is a war in which the Republic was given, in a single session of decrees, most of the institutions history let it build too late, and lost anyway, a year later than history's Republic and in the same way. The pistol of this memorandum does not change that sentence. It changes something at the bottom of the war, where a man stands at a door at night, and I will say where.

On 1 January 1964 Saigon made land to the tiller law, gave the Commission Against Corruption an American power to suspend aid behind it, created a resident rural police and a Special Branch with the files on the infrastructure, and put its province and district chiefs on a ranked list chosen by examination (EVR05-1). The same week the first ship of IRON GATE berthed at Khanh Hoi with carbines for the whole army and the territorials, and the gunships of the 14th Air Commando Wing flew their first night over the posts (EV004-1, EV015-1). The pistols came through the same gate: every officer, gunner and post commander of the RVNAF, every American in the country and, by August, every constable of the new police drew a Glock with glowing sights and a light, in exchange for a worn .45 or a six-shot revolver (EV004-1). Khanh's coup came on 30 January and used the combined command's network rather than breaking it (EV000-1). The Republic's governments went on falling for a year and a half, as history's did, but its new institutions did not fall with them.

The first year in the countryside was the year of the posts. About 600 village police posts were settled by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by July 1965, each with ten constables and a Popular Forces platoon beside it, and the constable and the PF corporal walked the hamlet every night with the census booklets (EV002-2). From the summer the constable lit the face at the door with the light on his pistol and held the booklet in his other hand, and the night check became a thing a ten-man post could do in two hours and believe. The Front read the posts within weeks and set out to kill the constables before they learned the village. It overran Long Dinh on Route 4 on 3 October and killed seven of its nine constables, and by July 1965 it had taken about 85 posts and killed about 410 constables, almost all where the posts had been settled ahead of the night security that should have stood beside them. Where the platoon was there, the post held, as Long Huu held on 17 November 1964, and from December the Front stopped assaulting such posts and went for whatever had been left out: the constables on the road, the camps, the militia with the handed-down Garands.

The pistols had their own first season, and it was not a clean one (EV016-1). The PF leaders took the lights on ambush and used them as torches, and four platoons in Long An and Dinh Tuong were fired on from the next dike before the province chiefs banned it in June. The police, who drew last and were trained least, shot their own feet and colleagues with a pistol that has no safety lever, until the Directorate doubled its course in October. A gunship over a Dinh Tuong post held its fire for six minutes in November because its crew could not tell the post's lights from the enemy's. And the Front took its first Glock at Rach Kien on 22 March and by June had told its security sections to hold fire until the lamp came on and then fire at it. By the end of 1964 the light was used for two seconds at a door or a bend and for nothing else, and both sides knew it.

The American war arrived in 1965 on the historical timetable and with a different air campaign. Washington approved an opening strike on the whole target list, and between 2 and 9 March the B-52Js destroyed the North's power, its fuel, its great bridges, its jet base and Haiphong's cranes in a week (EV013-1). The North dispersed, Moscow and Beijing rebuilt its sky, and the flow south never noticed. In the South the summer offensive broke battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, the Marines came ashore, Thieu and Ky took power in June, and in July the President sent the Army. The advisory war was over.

The main-force war of 1965–67 was history's war fought by a better-armed, better-led, better-supported American and Vietnamese force against an enemy who chose when to fight and usually chose not to. The Ia Drang cost about 165–170 American dead rather than 237 (EV000-23). Behind the big-unit war the allied campaign against the Front's political apparatus drove its district committees out of the villages of the Saigon belt and the upper Delta, and they went on governing them from further away: from the Ho Bo woods, from the contested edge, and after COSVN's circular of April 1967 from across the Cambodian border (EVR05-4). Under the woods the American tunnel teams went down with the light on the muzzle and their left hand free, and at Phu Hoa Dong on 19 April 1966 a specialist of the 65th Engineers saw the man in the gallery four metres off and fired first (EV016-2). They lost about a third fewer men underground than history's teams in two years, and the Front answered by leaving the first galleries empty and wired.

The Front's war on the list-appointed district chiefs in 1965–66 killed nineteen of them and two province chiefs, and four of those were shot in their own houses at night with pistols taken from the Republic's dead (EV002-3). At Cai Be on 2–3 March 1966 a three-man team of the Dinh Tuong security section used a Glock from the Long Dinh post, its light still working on stolen cells, to pick the district chief from his wife in the doorway. The Republic's best pistol in its enemy's hands changed the manner of those deaths and not their number, which COSVN's decision and the sections' intelligence set; the chiefs' deputies acted at once and the Board filled each vacancy within about three weeks. And at Tan Thoi Nhi in Hoc Mon on 14 August 1967 a police sergeant lit an old man with a sickle at a door, saw what he was holding and did not fire, and at the tenth house lit a courier of the district finance section against a wall with his hands empty and took him alive (EV016-3). That night is the measure of the pistol: about 150–200 fewer civilians shot at night a year by soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadres taken alive at their doors rather than lost in the dark.

The year ended with 486,000 Americans in the country and, on the record's rates, about 8,435–9,060 American dead in 1967 against history's 9,378, of which the pistol accounts for about 10–15. The air war over the North had destroyed everything that could be destroyed. The Trail carried about as much as history's, and the Politburo had set Tet for the General Offensive and General Uprising.

Tet in this war was history's Tet fought against a Republic that held its district towns more often. About two-thirds of the 64–72 district towns attacked were penetrated rather than nearly all, and the infrastructure's files told the allies where the political half of the offensive would come and not when, so the surprise stayed national. The national police chief, Loan, shot a captured officer in a Saigon street before a camera on 1 February, and the photograph's effect on the American public was history's, whatever pistol was in his hand. The Americans lost 14,450–14,560 dead that year, close to history's 14,592, and the public's tolerance was spent on the historical schedule. The Paris talks opened in November with no partial halt of the bombing to trade.

Vietnamization in this war handed over a better army and a countryside held more firmly than history's. The land programme of 1964 had finished its work with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, the territorials were more present in the hamlets than ever, and from 1969 the Vietnamese Air Force's radar gunships held the posts' nights through the monsoon cloud in their own language. The Commission survived below the rank of minister and general and lost its reach above it when the American lever lapsed. In 1972 the Easter Offensive met an ARVN that held An Loc and Kontum with American air power and retook Quang Tri City, as history's did, and lost the north of Quang Tri, the western Highlands and the border of III Corps, as history's did.

The end came differently, and for reasons a long way above the level of anything this memorandum touches. Nixon did not fall. The fiscal 1975 appropriation came near the level of 1974, and Thieu, believing on that evidence that he need not give anything up, refused the withdrawal of 14 March 1975. The Highlands went in a five-week siege rather than a ten-day rout, Hue in an ordered withdrawal on 18 April rather than a collapse on 25 March, and with no double collapse to resolve on, the Politburo put the offensive on Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, under Thieu, with no American air power, and its fall is expected between February and May 1976. In the spring of 1974 the Vietnamese gunships had beaten Military Region 9's rice campaign in Chuong Thien; in 1975 they could not fly over a PAVN division, and Thuong Duc and Pleiku went as before.

The pistol is in that war everywhere a man in a government uniform carries a sidearm, and it decided nothing at the level where the war was decided. It made the government's presence at night a little more decent and a little more effective in the villages the government already held, it saved a few dozen American lives and a few hundred Vietnamese civilians a year, and it put several hundred of the country's best pistols into the hands of the men who killed its officials. It did not move a post, a battalion, a battle or the date.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The pistol enters the war in IRON GATE and works through the posts. Three events carry it. IRON GATE now issues about 55,000–60,000 Glocks with the carbines and through the summer, about 40,000 old pistols coming in and about 9,000 of them going, over this headquarters' objection, to village and hamlet chiefs and the Combat Youth's leaders (EV004-1). The first posts carry them from the summer, and the night check of memorandum 002 becomes a check done with a light on the face and the booklet in the other hand (EV002-2, F016-1). The first season, EV016-1, is the pistol's own lesson: the light as a torch on ambush, the negligent discharges at the holster, the gunship that could not tell the post's lights from the enemy's (F016-7).

The constraint at the post did not move. The record's settled position is that small arms change how the fights the enemy accepts go and never which fights he accepts (POS004-1), and at a police post the pistol is weaker still: the posts fell to company assaults and the constables died on the roads to rifle fire at 50–150 metres, where it does not reach. The 85 posts lost and the 410 constables killed by July 1965 stand as written, and what absorbs the difference is plain: the pistol only decided how the last man in a breached post died (F016-8, DIRMT01-4). What moved was the Front's armoury: about 280–320 of the constables' Glocks by July 1965 and about 1,000–1,200 pistols of all sources by the end of the year, at low confidence (F016-14).

On the ground the effect is not the same everywhere (DIRMT01-8). In the Saigon belt, at Can Duoc and Rach Kien, the pistols reached the posts with the platoons and the night check worked from August; it was also where the light-as-torch ambushes happened. In the Delta the upper provinces got the night check and Rach Gia got the discharges; Chuong Thien, where the posts were the Front's, got nothing. On the coast of I and II Corps the posts held less often, so the pistols there were more often lost than used. In the Highlands there was no effect: the CIDG camps, which did the fighting, are outside the provision.

The soldier's family (DIRMT01-9) is untouched: the pistol does not change where a PF soldier's family lives or what his pay buys. Rice and the piaster (DIRMT01-11) are untouched too: the pistol costs the Republic nothing, imported with the carbines, and moves no rice. The Saigon black-market price of a Glock with a light, about a month of a captain's pay by 1965, is the one place it touches money, and it pays a few armourers and officers to sell (F016-13) [X-016].

### Enemy response and initiative

The COSVN security directorate read the pistol from the first captured example, taken at Rach Kien on 22 March, and its circular of 4 June gave the right answer: hold fire until the lamp comes on, then fire at it (EV016-1, F016-10). That cost the Front nothing (F016-11). Two committees of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region misread the lights for four months as night-vision that saw through walls, and kept their cadres out of the held villages; the error cost them some control of those villages until a captured light was switched on in Hau Nghia. The Dinh Tuong security section's decision in October to take the Long Dinh post's Glocks from the local-force company that had captured them, so that the pistols went to the men who killed officials rather than the men who fought posts, was the Front putting the weapon where it would matter most (EV002-2, F016-14).

### Causal chains

**C016-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV016-1) The posts' platoon leaders and post commanders put their pistols' lights on the walls during night attacks in 1964 → on the night of 18–19 November an AC-130J over a Dinh Tuong post holds its fire for six minutes because its crew cannot tell the post's lights from the attackers' torches → the 14th Air Commando Wing and the IV Corps province chiefs agree in December that posts mark with strobes and fire and keep white light off their walls → from 1965 the Front's mortar crews, which had ranged the posts at night from 300 metres on the lights the posts showed, lose their aiming points, and the posts that held by mortar in 1965 are hit less accurately than the posts that had not changed their habits (EV002-2).

### Downstream

The Glocks taken from the posts in 1964–65 go to the security sections and turn up in the killing of district chiefs in 1966 (EV002-3). The police's doubled course of October 1964 is the one they keep. The light discipline learned in 1964 is the habit the posts carry through the war. Nothing in Phase I's end vector moves: the pistol's effect on the American dead of 1965 is a handful of men, inside the range the record already carries, and its effect on the posts and the night is a trace inside ranges set by the carbine, the radio, the gunship and the courses.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II shows the pistol's two real uses and its one real cost, each in a dated event. The tunnel teams of Cu Chi and the Ho Bo woods go down with the light on the muzzle and the left hand free, and Specialist Ortiz at Phu Hoa Dong on 19 April 1966 fires first at four metres because he can see (EV016-2, F016-2). They lose about a third fewer men underground in two years, about 6–9 lives, at low confidence, and they take more prisoners alive from the first galleries in 1966 and fewer in 1967, when the Front stops defending them (F016-3).

The strongest positive effect is shown at Tan Thoi Nhi on 14–15 August 1967, where a police sergeant lights an old man with a sickle and does not fire, and lights a courier against a wall and takes him alive (EV016-3, F016-15). Across the held belt that is about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year by 1967 from night shootings by soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadres a year taken alive or killed rather than escaping in the dark [X-016]. The end vector carries the second figure inside the range for identifying the infrastructure, now about 1,650–3,300 more listed cadres a year than history's, and the American dead of 1967 at about 8,435–9,060 against history's 9,378, the pistol's part being about 10–15 (P2 end vector).

The cost is shown at Cai Be on 2–3 March 1966: a Glock from the Long Dinh post, its light working on stolen cells, picks the district chief from his wife in his bedroom doorway (EV002-3, F016-14). Four of the twenty-one chiefs killed in 1965–66 die this way. The number does not change, and what absorbs the difference is named in the event: COSVN's decision and the sections' knowledge of a chief's house set the rate, not their pistols.

The Mo Duc raid of 11 February 1967, where a PRU team shot a schoolteacher's family after a man in the house fired a pistol, stands as written (EV006-4): the PRUs are armed by the CIA and are outside the provision, which is why the raids that killed the most in the contested belt are not touched (DIRCP04-3). The removed M1911A1 and revolvers are named in no other event of any phase. In the Delta, Phase II's effect is in Dinh Tuong (Cai Be) and the upper provinces; in the Saigon belt it is at Hoc Mon and Cu Chi; on the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps there is none worth stating, because the night check there ran in few villages; in the Highlands, none (DIRMT01-8).

### Enemy response and initiative

The Front answered the tunnel teams by leaving the first galleries empty and wired and the held villages by sleeping outside them, both cheap habits (F016-3, F016-11). The night checks' yield of strangers fell by about half from 1966 to 1967 in Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc and Cai Lay, the check's success reading as decline (F016-9).

<!-- initiative -->
One decision in Phase II was the enemy's own. In May 1966 the Party committee of Military Region 2, which ran the upper Delta for COSVN, ordered its province security sections to cut their killing of officials by about half for the rest of the year and to put their men into tax collection [X-016]. Nothing this command did drove it. The region's finances had collapsed with the price of rice after the devaluation of June 1966, and the committee's own reports said its local forces were going unfed (EV009-3) [X-016]. The order was obeyed slowly and with argument: the Dinh Tuong section, flush with captured pistols and proud of Cai Be, killed two more village chiefs in June before it complied. The effect was that the second half of 1966 in the upper Delta saw fewer officials killed than COSVN's August directive of 1965 had demanded, for a reason that was all rice and no war.

### Causal chains

**C016-2** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV002-2) The Long Dinh post is overrun without its platoon on 3 October 1964 and its constables' Glocks are taken → the Dinh Tuong province security section takes them from the local-force company within the month and keeps them for its killers → a Glock from Long Dinh kills the Cai Be district chief in his bedroom on 2–3 March 1966 → the Special Branch traces the killing in May through the serial of a Glock taken from a courier, the first case in which a weapon's own number on the muster roll led to the men who used it, and from 1967 the province chiefs log every pistol lost by serial (EV002-3, F004-14).

**C016-3** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV016-1) The constable's light makes the night check believable from August 1964 → the cadres of Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc and Cai Lay stop sleeping in the held villages in 1966 → the night checks' yield of strangers falls by about half from 1966 to 1967 → the province chiefs, warned against quotas by the record's settled position, read the fall as the cadres' flight rather than the constables' failure and keep the checks running, so the courier taken at Tan Thoi Nhi on 14 August 1967 is caught on a route the district committee thought safe precisely because the checks had found nobody for months (EV016-3, F016-9, POS002-1).

**Chain review (DIRCP02-2).** C004-1, written by memorandum 004, ran: the PF platoons beside the police posts draw modern carbines → posts with their platoon hold and the Front stops assaulting them → the Front turns to the constables on the road, the CIDG camps and the militia with the handed-down Garands. Tested against the war as now written, it resolves as its author predicted, with one addition. The constables on the road now carry Glocks, which do not help them against an ambush at 50–150 metres; they die as often, and each one killed now gives the Front a pistol that ends in a security section (EV002-2, EV002-3). The chain's terminal link stands; what it delivers to the Front is slightly worse for the Republic than memorandum 004 wrote.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits a police and a territorial force that use their lights for two seconds, a Front that holds about a thousand of the Republic's pistols and has learned to fire at lamps, and tunnel complexes whose first galleries are empty. None of that changes Tet. Phase IV's handover passes the pistols to the RVNAF and the police with nothing to Vietnamise but the 123A cells, which come from America and keep coming until 1975. The values Phase II moves are small enough to be absorbed in each later phase's ranges. Phase II's chapter now stands at about 16,000 words, above the 15,000-word limit, and is flagged for the consolidation run at the next checkpoint (DIRCP03-1).

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- null P5 -->
The Easter Offensive was a conventional war of tanks, artillery and sieges at An Loc, Kontum and Quang Tri. Nothing in it turned on a sidearm. The territorial war behind the front, where the pistol works, was quieter in these months because the Front's local forces were spent supporting the offensive, so the pistol had even less to do.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

The end was decided in Washington, in the palace and by the PAVN's corps, not at a door. The pistol's only exposure here is the cell supply: the 1974 appropriation keeps the 123A cells coming at a reduced rate, the lights go dark in the posts of the north first, and the pistol stays a .45 with glowing sights. ## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, this provision has changed four things, each small, and none of them the war's course.

First, the sidearm. In history the American and Vietnamese armies carried worn M1911A1s from the Second World War, never enough of them, and the police carried six-shot revolvers; Americans bought their own pistols. Here every holder carries a Glock 21 with night sights and a light from 1964, about 60,000–65,000 by the end of 1964 and about 235,000–245,000 at the peak.

Second, lives. About 10–15 fewer American dead in 1967 and perhaps 60–90 over the war, most of them tunnel clearers and men in the last metres of overrun positions, at low confidence [X-016]. About 150–200 fewer South Vietnamese civilians shot at night by their own soldiers and police each year from 1967 [X-016]. Against these, about 8–10 more men killed by negligent discharges in 1964 [X-016].

Third, the infrastructure. About 100–150 more listed cadres a year taken alive or killed at their doors rather than escaping in the dark, by 1967 [X-016]. The Front's apparatus was driven out of the held villages by the police, the centres and the raids of memoranda 002, 006, 012 and 014; the pistol helped at the door and nowhere else.

Fourth, the enemy's armoury. About 1,000–1,200 of the Republic's pistols in the Front's hands by the end of 1965, at low confidence, and four of the district chiefs killed in 1965–66 shot with them [X-016].

The margin is modest and stated as such. No battle, post, offensive or month has changed.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The cumulative force makes rung (b) reachable and no higher, and this memorandum does not move it. The Republic survives while American combat forces are present, and in this war it survives past 30 April 1975 because of decisions taken in Washington and Saigon, not because of anything that holds a door. Rung (c) is not reached because the insurgency is not contained in every corps region and Saigon does not stand with American air power available at the end, and the distance between (c) and (d) is the whole war, as it has been in every memorandum.

**The date of the fall (DIRMT01-1).** The Republic's fall is expected between February and May 1976, as the record has it. The mechanisms pulling it earlier are the PAVN's five corps assembled north of Saigon and the Republic's loss of half its Highland force in 1975; those pulling it later are the appropriation near the 1974 level, the Vietnamese gunships over the Delta's posts, and III and IV Corps held intact. The appropriation dominates, because it decided whether Thieu stood in March 1975. The pistol pulls in neither direction: it cannot touch a corps, a vote or the rains.

**The running sum of American lives (DIRMT01-2, DIRCP04-1).** The latest entry, POSCP04-1, puts the American lives saved by the provisions at about 4,100–9,500, a judgment on the record's rates. This memorandum adds about 60–90 over the war, on a rate of about 10–15 a year in 1966–69 and fewer before and after [X-016]. The sum is about 4,150–9,600, about 7–16 percent of the war's American dead, and it still does not move tolerance.

**The countryside (DIRMT01-8, DIRMT01-11).** By region: the Saigon belt (Hoc Mon, Cu Chi) and the upper Delta (Dinh Tuong) carry the whole effect; the coast of I and II Corps and the Highlands none worth stating. The pistol moves no rice and no piaster beyond a black-market price; the one dated event in this memorandum that turns on rice is the enemy's own, Military Region 2's order of May 1966 to put its security sections into tax collection.

**Unjudged dependencies (DIRR03-3).** The memorandum's evidence does not bear on ARVN night operations as a share of ambushes and patrols, on outpost defence or on the RVNAF's maintenance at a scale that would justify a judgment in units; the pistol changes how a night check or a breached post ends, not how often a unit goes out after dark. They stay as they are.

No phase redraw is proposed.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- Every American, Vietnamese soldier and policeman who carries a sidearm carries a better one, with twice the rounds, sights that glow and a light on the gun; for the constable, who had six rounds and a torch, it is a large improvement in his own safety at a door at night.
- Fewer people are shot by mistake at night: about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year from 1967, and about 100–150 more listed cadres a year taken alive at their doors rather than lost in the dark (EV016-3). In a held village that is a government that can be trusted after dark a little more.
- The tunnel teams of Cu Chi and the Ho Bo woods go down able to see, and lose about a third fewer men underground in 1966–67 (EV016-2); the American dead fall by about 10–15 in 1967 and perhaps 60–90 over the war.
- The Front holds about 1,000–1,200 of the Republic's best pistols by the end of 1965, and its killers use some of them on district chiefs and village officials (EV002-3).
- About 100–120 more negligent discharges and 8–10 more dead in 1964, mostly police, and four platoons ambushed on their own lights before the habit was trained out (EV016-1).

### What has not changed

- Who holds the hamlets after dark: the pistol adds nothing to night security and works only where it already exists (POSTNA-2).
- How many posts fell and how many constables died in 1964–65, and how many district chiefs the Front killed in 1965–66: the rates were set by assaults at rifle range and by COSVN's decisions, not by sidearms.
- The main-force war, Tet, the Easter Offensive and the end of the war, none of which turned on a pistol; the date of the fall stays between February and May 1976.
- The police's long arms (E05), and the weapons of the CIDG, the PRUs, the militia and the People's Self-Defense Force, which the provision does not reach.
- The end state, rung (b).
