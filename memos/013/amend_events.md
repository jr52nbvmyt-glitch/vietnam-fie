## EVCP01-2 · The American escalation: Flaming Dart, the opening strike, the Marines and the July decision · 7 Feb – 28 Jul 1965 · Pleiku and Qui Nhon; North Vietnam; Da Nang; Washington · HIST-ALT · CP01,CP02,013
*Initiative: both*

The six months in which the United States went from an advisory war to a war of its own are told here as one event, because each step made the next one. At 01:50 on 7 February 1965, while McGeorge Bundy was in Saigon and Kosygin in Hanoi, about 300 men of the 30th Company of the VC 409th Sapper Battalion attacked Camp Holloway and the advisers' compound at Pleiku, killing seven Americans and wounding 104 and destroying 10 aircraft [H5]. About twelve hours later 49 aircraft from the *Coral Sea* and the *Hancock* struck the barracks at Dong Hoi, the first strike of Flaming Dart; after the VC blew up the enlisted billet at Qui Nhon on 10 February and killed 23 Americans, about 160 aircraft struck Chanh Hoa and Chap Le [H5]. Bundy's report of 7 February argued for "sustained reprisal", and the administration accepted it [H].

What the President approved on 13 February was not history's "measured and limited air action" south of the 19th parallel [H]. In this war the theater's plan had been on his desk since the autumn: the Joint Chiefs' list of 94 targets of August 1964, extended by the MACV Combined Targeting Cell with the North's command centres, radars, power plants and bulk fuel, and a request that the campaign be run from Saigon, without zones around Hanoi and Haiphong and without pauses, with a buffer of 15 miles along the Chinese border and one strike on Haiphong's port (P013a, P013b) [X-013]. Johnson kept two things: the decision whether the bombing would happen at all, and its date. He gave the rest. Rolling Thunder opened on 2 March, history's date, with the opening strike of Operation HAMMER FALL on the whole list (EV013-1) in place of history's 104 USAF aircraft at Xom Bang; the 19 VNAF A-1s still struck the naval base at Quang Khe that day, as they had historically [H9] [X-013]. The route packages divided the North between the services from the spring, as historically, but the targets in every package came from Tan Son Nhut, not from the President's Tuesday lunch (F013-2) [X-013].

On 8 March Battalion Landing Team 3/9 came ashore at Da Nang and BLT 1/3 flew in from Okinawa, the nearly 5,000 men of the 9th Marine Expeditionary Brigade under Brigadier General Frederick J. Karch, to guard the air base that Rolling Thunder needed and the Bien Hoa attack had shown to be vulnerable [H15] [H14]. Their orders, that they "will not, repeat will not, engage in day-to-day actions against the Viet Cong", lasted three weeks: on 1 April the President approved their use in active combat and 18,000–20,000 more support troops, and on 6 May the brigade became the III Marine Amphibious Force [H15] [H14].

On 7 June Westmoreland asked for a major reinforcement and by 22 July the President had authorized 44 battalions [H11]. On 28 July, at a midday press conference rather than before Congress, Johnson announced that US strength would rise at once from 75,000 to 125,000, with more as requested, that monthly draft calls would rise from 17,000 to 35,000, and that the Airmobile Division would deploy; the reserves were not called and no new taxes were sought [H21] [H]. Westmoreland's concept behind it, to halt the losing trend in 1965, take the offensive in 1966 and then destroy the enemy's main forces and base areas, committed the United States to attrition against an enemy who controlled his losses by deciding when to fight.

The ground war's steps came on their historical dates for their historical reasons. The air war over the North did not. Its opening came on history's day and its shape was the theater's, and by the July decision the North had already lost what history spent two and a half years taking from it (F013-9) [X-013]. Nothing in that loss changed the decision of 28 July, which was driven by the ARVN's defeats of May and June at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai and not by anything in the North (EV000-17, EV000-18) [X-013].

## EV013-1 · Operation HAMMER FALL: the opening strike on the North · 2–9 Mar 1965 · Phuc Yen airfield, Hanoi (Bach Mai, the Citadel, Yen Phu, Duc Giang and the Paul Doumer bridge), Haiphong port and Thuong Ly, Nam Dinh, the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa, Vinh; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; Yankee Station; Korat and Takhli, Thailand · NEW · 013
*Initiative: allied*

The plan began on 5 August 1964, the morning after the false alarm in the Gulf that this war never answered with Pierce Arrow (EV000-3). Major Edwin T. Sorrell's coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut, which had measured the panhandle since March, was given the North above the 20th parallel, and Brigadier General Walter E. Pruitt of the 2nd Air Division was sent to build a MACV Combined Targeting Cell around it, with Captain Harlan J. Reese of CINCPAC's staff as his deputy for the Navy (F013-2) [X-013]. The Joint Chiefs' study of 24 August gave them 94 targets in five categories, airfields, lines of communication, military installations, industry and the routes of armed reconnaissance [H]. The cell added what the list held thinly: the Air Defence–Air Force Command and its operations centre at Bach Mai, the Ministry of Defence and General Staff in the Citadel, the national radio transmitters at Me Tri, the long-haul exchanges, the early-warning radars, the Yen Phu and Haiphong power plants and the transformer stations between them, and the bulk fuel at Duc Giang, Thuong Ly, Nam Dinh and Vinh. By February 1965 the U-2S had imaged every one of them under the standing condition's satellite link, and the cell held about 760 desired points of impact, each measured to a few metres (EV005-1) [X-013].

The President's order of 13 February set the day as 2 March. The crachin set the hour. On the night of 2–3 March the cloud over the Red River delta sat at about 800–1,500 feet with drizzle, and nothing but the bombers could strike (§1.1 D4 of memorandum 013) [X-013]. Twenty-four B-52Js of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings left Andersen in cells of three from 15:00 local time, flew about 2,200 nautical miles to entry points over the Gulf of Tonkin, and released from 36,000–40,000 feet between 23:40 and 01:15, from up to fifteen miles out [X-013]. The order of the night was fixed by what could fight back. The first two cells put 70 GBU-38s on Phuc Yen's runway intersections and its 38 revetments, and destroyed or wrecked 22–26 of the 921st Fighter Regiment's 36 MiG-17Fs on the ground; eight that were on alert in the northern dispersal survived, and two of them flew to Kep at first light [X-013]. The third cell struck the early-warning radars at Bach Mai, Phu Ly and Kep and the four fire-control radars the cell had located, all with GBU-38 [X-013]. Then the penetrators: twelve GBU-31(V)3/Bs on the Bach Mai operations centre, sixteen on the Ministry of Defence buildings in the Citadel, eight on the Me Tri transmitters [X-013]. Then the fixed economy: the Yen Phu power station and Haiphong's, the transformer yards at Dong Anh and Thai Binh, the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly, two spans of the Paul Doumer bridge over the Red River, the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa with twenty penetrators on its abutments and central pier, and the rail and road bridges at Nam Dinh, Ninh Binh and Phu Ly [X-013].

The last target of the night was Haiphong's port, its one strike (EC013-5). At dusk the Gulf watch had counted fourteen ships in the port, among them three Soviet, two Polish and one British freighter alongside the main quay [X-013]. Six Js put 120 GBU-31(V)1/Bs on the gantry and portal cranes, the transit sheds and warehouses behind the coal quay, the port's substation and the lighter basin, and none on the berths with ships alongside [X-013]. The British *Dartford* lost her gangway and a lifeboat to blast; no seaman of any flag died [X-013]. The port's cranes, about forty of them, were destroyed or toppled; the North's own figures, given to the Polish member of the International Control Commission, were 214 port workers killed [X-013].

No J was lost or hit. The guns of the delta, about 1,500–2,100 of them, 37mm and 57mm, fired blind into cloud at aircraft five miles above their reach [H] [X-013]. The second wave flew on the first days the cloud lifted, 4 and 7 March: alpha strikes of 30–40 aircraft from the *Hancock* and the *Ranger* on Kep, Gia Lam and the guns around the struck bridges, and F-105D packages from Korat and Takhli with F-100D flak suppression on the Hanoi and Haiphong rail yards, Thai Nguyen's blast furnace under construction and the Viet Tri chemical works [X-013]. The MiG-17s that had reached Kep rose on 7 March and lost two to F-8Es; the guns, now firing at aircraft under the cloud, shot down four F-105Ds, two A-4Cs and an F-8E in the week, with three pilots killed, two captured and two recovered at sea [X-013]. Two more cells of Js restruck the Doumer bridge and the Haiphong power plant on 6 March on the U-2S's imagery of the 5th, the first orders of the restrike watch (EC013-3) [X-013].

By 9 March the North had lost, on this command's count checked against the U-2S, about 70–80 percent of its generating capacity, most of its bulk fuel in storage, the Doumer, Ham Rong and Nam Dinh bridges, its only jet base and most of its jet fighters, its warning net, and the cranes of its only deepwater port [X-013]. Hanoi radio was off the air for nine hours and its air defence command was in the provincial switchboards for three weeks [X-013]. No member of the Politburo or the Central Military Commission was killed or, as far as anyone in Saigon could tell, inconvenienced; the Ministry's buildings had been empty but for duty officers, and General Van Tien Dung's staff had worked from Hoa Lac since February [X-013]. The North's civilian dead of the week, by Hanoi's count given in Geneva in April, were about 1,700; this command's own estimate was 800–1,500, most in Hanoi and Haiphong [X-013]. The trucks of Group 559, the porters in the panhandle and the Front's regiments in the South noticed nothing at all (F013-7) [X-013].

## EV013-2 · Moscow, Beijing and the rebuilt sky · 10 Mar – 31 Jul 1965 · Moscow; Beijing; Hanoi; the frontier crossing at Pingxiang and Dong Dang; the airfields of Mengzi and Ningming in Yunnan and Guangxi · NEW · 013
*Initiative: enemy*

The answer to HAMMER FALL was decided in three capitals, and each decided for its own reasons. In Hanoi the Politburo under Le Duan, at its meeting of 25–27 March, ordered the North onto a war footing, the dispersal of every factory, depot and fuel store that could be moved, and a militia of the whole people for air defence and repair; and, reading the strike as the preparation for a landing, held the 308th and 312th Divisions and two regiments of the 320th in the delta and on the coast through the summer instead of preparing them for the South (F013-13) [X-013]. In Moscow, Brezhnev and Kosygin, whose premier had left Hanoi on 10 February promising help, accelerated the air-defence package: SA-2 equipment for four battalions instead of two by July, about 1,000 advisers and technicians, MiG-21F-13s for the autumn, and diesel generators, pumps and trucks in place of the heavy plant the North had asked for in 1964 [X-013]. In Beijing, Mao and Zhou Enlai sent Chinese railway, engineer and anti-aircraft troops into the North from June, earlier and in larger numbers than they had historically, and placed three anti-aircraft divisions along the northeast and northwest railways, some inside the 15-mile buffer the Americans had left [X-013].

<!-- initiative -->Beijing's own decision, taken for its own reasons, slowed Moscow's. In April Moscow asked for an air corridor across China for Antonov transports carrying SA-2 equipment and its crews, and for the use of two airfields in Yunnan; the Chinese leadership refused both, as it did historically, and required every Soviet shipment to cross China by rail under Chinese escort and at Chinese pace [H] [X-013]. The refusal had nothing to do with the opening strike. It came from Beijing's quarrel with Moscow over the leadership of the communist world, and from Mao's suspicion that a Soviet presence in Yunnan was a Soviet presence on China's frontier. Its effect was that the first Soviet battalions of SA-2 reached Hanoi in late April and May by train through Pingxiang, three to five weeks later than Moscow had promised and with about a fifth of their equipment held at the border for inspection [X-013]. The VPAF was given what Moscow could not use: its surviving MiG-17s and their pilots were moved to Mengzi and Ningming in China to train and re-form, and from the autumn its regiments flew into the North from Chinese fields to fight and back to rest (F013-6) [X-013].

By the end of July the North had about 2,500–3,000 guns rather than history's rising count, four SA-2 battalions in the Hanoi area rotating among about twenty prepared sites, and a fighter force that lived in China (EVCP02-2) [X-013]. Its fuel was in drums under the trees, its power in about 700 village diesels from the Soviet shipments, and its bridges were pontoons by night [X-013]. It had begun to pay for the war in labour: perhaps 300,000–400,000 people were repairing, carrying and manning guns full time by July, a year before history's figure reached that size (F013-14) [X-013]. Its rice imports from China were promised for the autumn, about 200,000 tons for 1966 [X-013].

## EVCP02-2 · The air war of 1965: the Thanh Hoa Bridge, the missiles and the first Arc Light · 3 Apr – 27 Jul 1965 · Thanh Hoa (Ham Rong) and the Hanoi region, North Vietnam; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; Ben Cat, Binh Duong Province · HIST-ALT · CP02,013
*Initiative: allied*

The four months after HAMMER FALL were the months in which the North rebuilt and the cell learned how fast. The Ham Rong bridge over the Song Ma at Thanh Hoa, which history's 79-aircraft package under Lieutenant Colonel Robinson Risner hit repeatedly and failed to drop on 3 April, had been in the river since the first night of March (EV013-1) [H16] [X-013]. On 3 April Risner's wing flew instead against the ferries and the pontoon bridge the North had built a kilometre upstream, and against the guns that now ringed it; the MiG-17s of the 921st Fighter Regiment that history sent against the F-105s on 4 April had been destroyed at Phuc Yen or were in China, and Major Frank Bennett and Captain J. A. Magnusson came home that week [H16] [X-013]. The folder for Ham Rong, complete since the previous August (EV005-1), became the model for every bridge in the North: the U-2S's dawn pass found each pontoon, and a cell of Js or a package of F-105Ds struck it within a day or two (EC013-3) [X-013]. The requests of April and June for Js against the Dragon's Jaw, refused historically and in the record (F005-12), were never needed [X-013].

The missiles were seen coming and struck as they came. The U-2S's change detection showed the six-pointed pattern of an SA-2 site southeast of Hanoi on 22 March, its signals payload heard the first Fan Song emissions on 18 April, and by 24 July it had mapped about twenty prepared sites [X-003] [X-013]. Under the provision there was no Soviet-technician rule: each site was struck by a cell of Js with GBU-38s on its revetments and van pads within 24–48 hours of its first image, and from May each occupied site the signals payload located was struck the same night (EC013-4) [X-013]. The North answered by preparing three or four sites for every battalion and moving the battalions by night, which was history's answer brought forward a year [X-013]. The first SA-2 to bring down an American aircraft in this war was fired on 22 August, not 24 July, and Captain Roscoe Fobair's F-4C of the 47th Tactical Fighter Squadron was not lost; Spring High was never flown, because there was nothing in July for it to answer [H9] [X-013]. Over the five months to the end of July about 60–75 American aircraft were lost over the North, against perhaps 85–100 in history's same months [E: history's 180 aircraft lost to 24 December 1965 over ten months, weighted to the spring's campaign], nearly all of them to guns at low level over the restruck bridges and yards [X-013].

The first Arc Light, on 18 June, was flown by thirty B-52Js of the 7th and 320th Bombardment Wings from Andersen in place of the historical B-52Fs, to the South and back without tankers, so the collision over the sea that killed eight crewmen historically did not happen (F005-11) [H18] [X-005]. The Soviet trawler off Guam still gave Hanoi about three hours' notice; the tanker track that had betrayed the bombers' heading was gone [M005] [X-005]. Twenty-eight aircraft struck a suspected base area near Ben Cat, about 40 miles north of Saigon, with about 51 bombs each [H18] [M005]; two carried twenty GBU-31s each onto four structures the U-2S had imaged, and found four huts empty for days [X-005]. The rest found what history found, little, and the press called it a sledgehammer swung at flies, now accurate to a few metres [H] [X-005]. It was the find-and-fix problem in its purest form, and the J changed none of it (F005-7) [X-005]. Arc Light would run to August 1973 and fly about 126,615 sorties [H17]; here with 10–20 percent fewer maintenance men, no tanker bridge, and a carpet half as dense when it carried unguided bombs (F005-8, F005-10) [M005] [X-005]. By the end of July the B-52's war had two halves: over the North a bomber that had destroyed in a week what history's fighter-bombers spent years on, and over the South a bomber that found nothing under the canopy (F013-1) [X-013].

## EV013-3 · The restrike watch on the Red River · 1 Aug 1965 – 31 Dec 1966 · The Paul Doumer, Long Bien and Chuong Duong crossings at Hanoi; the Canal des Rapides and Bac Giang bridges on the northeast railway; the ferries at Phu Ly and Ninh Binh; the MACV Combined Targeting Cell, Tan Son Nhut · NEW · 013
*Initiative: both*

This event is told at length because it shows what the provision did once the opening strike was over, and why its measure of success fell as its success grew (F013-5, F013-12). From August 1965 the Combined Targeting Cell ran the northern campaign on a single list of about 300 targets and a daily repair list, and the Red River crossings at Hanoi were its pattern [X-013]. The Paul Doumer bridge, 1,680 metres on 19 spans, had lost two spans on 2–3 March and a third on 6 March (EV013-1) [H] [X-013]. The North did not try to rebuild it while the restrike watch stood over it. By June the Hanoi Bridge and Road Company and the Chinese railway engineers of the 1st Railway Engineer Division had built a pontoon bridge 3 km downstream, floated by night and broken into sections along the bank by day, and a rail ferry at Chuong Duong [X-013].

Pruitt's cell answered with the rhythm that became its law. The U-2S's dawn pass, read at Bien Hoa by 07:30, found each crossing in use and each repair; the cell's midday list ordered a J cell for the night or an F-105D package for the next morning; the Gulf watch and the U-2S's evening pass showed whether the trains moved [X-013]. Between August 1965 and December 1966 the Red River crossings at Hanoi were struck 61 times, 28 of them by Js with GBU-31s, and were impassable to rail on about 290 of the 517 days [X-013]. The pontoons were the problem the J could not solve: a 200-metre bridge of steel sections is a target only for the hours it is assembled, and the U-2S saw it at dawn after it had been taken apart [X-013]. The cell learned to strike the pontoon parks on the banks with GBU-38s by day and the approaches by night, and Captain Reese's carriers took the ferries; the North learned to keep three pontoon sets for every one in the river [X-013].

What the watch measured was the North spending labour to keep moving. By the autumn of 1966 about 2,000 men of the Hanoi crossings' repair and pontoon units were working the river at night, beside a Chinese railway engineer regiment, to carry about 40–60 percent of the trains the bridge had carried in February 1965 [X-013]. The cell's own monthly count of targets destroyed rose through the summer of 1965 to about 140 a month, and fell month by month thereafter to about 35 by December 1966, as the North stopped owning anything else worth a GBU-31 [X-013]. In October 1966 J-2 sent Washington, beside the cell's figures, the Gulf watch's count of the coastal traffic south of Thanh Hoa and the CIA's estimate of the North's imports: neither had fallen since the spring of 1965 [X-013]. General Westmoreland signed the covering note: the campaign was destroying everything in the North that could be destroyed, and the North's war in the South needed none of it (F013-7, F013-16) [X-013].

## EV013-4 · Christmas without a pause · 24 Dec 1965 – 31 Jan 1966 · Hanoi and the panhandle of North Vietnam; Washington; Warsaw, Rangoon and Moscow · NEW · 013
*Initiative: allied*

The 37 days in which Rolling Thunder stood down historically, from 24 December 1965 to 30 January 1966, while the President's envoys carried a peace offensive to forty capitals, did not happen (F013-9) [H] [X-013]. Nor had the six-day halt of May 1965 and the MAYFLOWER message that went with it [H] [X-013]. Johnson sent the envoys anyway: Harriman to Warsaw, Belgrade and New Delhi, Goldberg to the Vatican, McGeorge Bundy's messages through the embassy in Rangoon, with the "fourteen points" of 29 December 1965 [H] [X-013]. Without a pause to point to, they carried words alone. Hanoi's answer of 28 January 1966 was its four points and the demand for an end to the bombing, as historically [H]; the North had lost nothing it valued by the absence of a pause, and saw no reason to pay for one.

The North lost its season of free movement. Historically the pause let it move supplies south by day and repair the panhandle's roads and bridges in the open [H]. In this war the restrike watch and the armed reconnaissance of Route Package 1 went on through Christmas and Tet, with about 2,800 sorties over the North in the 37 days, a third of them in the panhandle [X-013]. The Gulf watch counted the trucks south of Vinh at about the level of November, not the doubling it counted in the pause of history's measure [X-013]. What that cost the B-3 Front and the regiments on the DMZ in the spring of 1966 was a few hundred tons, perhaps two to three weeks of the 1966 flow, which the panhandle's labour had made up by April (F013-7) [X-013]. The President's critics in the Senate, J. William Fulbright first among them, said in the February hearings that the peace offensive had been a pretence because the bombs had not stopped; the charge cost the administration more in the press than in votes (F013-15) [H] [X-013].

## EV013-5 · The buffer's edge: Dong Dang and the Chinese guns · 12 May – 30 Sep 1966 · The northeast railway from Kep through Lang Son to Dong Dang; the frontier at Pingxiang, Guangxi; Washington and Warsaw · NEW · 013
*Initiative: enemy*

The northeast railway carried about a third of the North's imports from China, and the provision let the cell strike it to within 15 miles of the frontier (EC013-6) [H] [X-013]. From the autumn of 1965 that meant the yards at Kep and Bac Giang and the bridges north of Lang Son, struck weekly by F-105Ds from Takhli and monthly by Js [X-013]. Beijing answered on the railway itself. The Chinese 61st and 63rd Anti-Aircraft Artillery Divisions took over the defence of the line north of Kep from the summer of 1965, and the Chinese railway engineers repaired it [X-013].

On 12 May 1966 an F-105D of the 388th Tactical Fighter Wing, struck by 57mm fire over the Lang Son bridge, ran north and its pilot, Captain James R. Ainsley, ejected about 3 miles inside the buffer and 12 miles from the frontier [X-013]. He was taken by a Chinese gun crew and held at Pingxiang for eleven days before being handed to the North; Beijing published his photograph and the claim that he had been shot down over China [X-013]. On 19 May a flight of F-4Cs pursuing two MiG-17s from Ningming crossed about 4 miles into Guangxi before turning back [X-013]. China protested at Warsaw and in the press; the President, who held the decision whether to bomb, considered for three days whether to widen the buffer to history's 25–30 miles, and did not, because the provision allowed him no pause and Rusk judged that China would not fight for a railway in Vietnam [X-013]. He ordered instead that no aircraft cross the buffer's line in pursuit, and the cell kept its strikes on the line at least 18 miles from the frontier for the rest of the year [X-013]. The Chinese gun divisions claimed about 40 American aircraft in 1966; the cell's own records showed about 14 lost on the northeast line in that year [X-013].

## EVCP02-4 · The air war over the North and the barrier, 1966–67: the fuel that was gone, Bolo and Muscle Shoals · 29 Jun 1966 – Dec 1967 · Hanoi and Haiphong; the DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand · HIST-ALT · CP02,013
*Initiative: allied*

Three steps of the air war against the North and its roads are told here as one event, because each was measured and none changed the war in the South. The petroleum strikes that history flew on 29 June 1966, after McNamara's request of the day before, were not flown, because there was nothing in bulk to strike: the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly had burned on the first night of March 1965 (EV013-1), and the U-2S had watched the North's fuel go into drums under the trees, buried tanks at the crossings and barges in the delta creeks through the summer of that year [H44] [X-013]. MACV J-2 told CINCPAC in September 1965 that the North's fuel was no longer a target system, and the Gulf watch, which had counted no fall in the trucks south of Thanh Hoa since March, said the same of the bombing as a whole (EV013-3) [X-003] [X-013]. McNamara had the result a year before history gave it to him, and his doubts hardened in the winter of 1965–66 instead of the summer of 1966 [X-013]. The bombing went on, because the President had decided it would and because nothing in the South told him to stop. The Stennis subcommittee's hearings of August 1967, in which the Chiefs blamed history's restrictions for the campaign's failure, were never held: there were no restrictions to blame, and the campaign's failure was this command's report, not the Pentagon's secret (F013-16) [H] [X-013].

The MiG war was smaller and more dispersed than history's (F013-6). The MiG-21s that reached the VPAF in 1966 were based at Mengzi and Ningming in China and flew into the North for a sortie at a time, landing at Noi Bai or Kep only to refuel; Phuc Yen was restruck on every repair and Kep, Kien An and Gia Lam monthly (EV013-2) [X-013]. On 2 January 1967, in **Operation Bolo**, Colonel Robin Olds's 8th Tactical Fighter Wing flew F-4Cs on an F-105 strike's routes and call signs [H]. In history seven MiG-21s rose to them from Phuc Yen and were shot down in twelve minutes without loss [H] [H9]; here four rose from Noi Bai, refuelled from China that morning, and three were shot down [X-013]. The VPAF grew more slowly than history's, to about 120 MiGs by the end of 1967 against history's 180 by 1968, and fought from China rather than over its own fields [H9] [X-013].

The **barrier** began with the Jason study of 1966 and General Starbird's Defense Communications Planning Group of 15 September 1966; here the study began from the STEADY COUNT tracks, which showed where the trucks went under canopy and how fast traffic recovered, and from the restrike watch's proof that the North's own roads and bridges, struck without pause, did not limit what went south (EV003-3, EV013-3) [H43] [X-003] [X-013]. It reached the historical conclusion that bombing alone could not stop the flow. Johnson authorized construction on 13 January 1967; the Marines built the eastern strip and its strongpoints at Gio Linh and Con Thien under fire, and the electronic western part was named Muscle Shoals on 8 September [H43] [H42]. ADSID and ACOUSID sensors dropped by VO-67's OP-2Es were relayed by EC-121Rs to Task Force Alpha at Nakhon Phanom; here they were laid where the radar's tracks ended, at the edges of the trellised stretches of Routes 911 and 912 and the fords beyond, and Task Force Alpha took the Global Hawk's tracks by teletype from its first day [H43] [X-003]. Field testing began on 25 November 1967, at a construction cost of $1–1.7 billion and about $1 billion a year to run [H43]. Whether it reduced what arrived is a question for later phases.

## EV000-45 · The reinforcement request and the decision of 31 March · 20 Feb – 31 Mar 1968 · Saigon and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,013
*Initiative: allied*

On 12 February Westmoreland asked for 10,500 more troops; on 13 February a brigade of the 82nd Airborne Division and a Marine regimental landing team, already authorized, were sent [H39] [H]. On 20 February the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Earle Wheeler, went to Saigon, and returned with a request for 206,000 more men, framed by the Chiefs to force a mobilization of the reserves [H]. On 28 February McNamara left office and on 1 March Clark Clifford succeeded him, charged with reviewing the request [H39] [H]. On 10 March the *New York Times* reported it [H]. On 12 March Senator Eugene McCarthy, running against the war, took 42 percent of the vote in the New Hampshire primary; on 16 March Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race [H]. On 25–26 March the senior outside advisers, the "Wise Men", told the President that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost [H].

On 31 March Johnson announced in a televised address that he would send only about 13,500 more troops, that the United States was ready to talk "at any place, at any time", and that he would not seek or accept his party's nomination for another term [H]. In this war he did not stop the bombing north of the 19th parallel, because the campaign he had authorized allowed no partial stand-down, and the choice in front of him, set out by Clifford and Rusk on 28 March, was the whole campaign or nothing (F013-10) [X-013]. Rusk proposed ending all the bombing in exchange for talks; Clifford, Wheeler and Westmoreland's cable opposed it while the 304th and 325C Divisions were still around Khe Sanh and the North's resupply of Hue's attackers was being measured on Route 1; the President kept the campaign and made the offer in words [X-013]. The speech capped the American commitment as it did historically. Westmoreland's strategy of attrition by American forces had been judged by the President's own advisers to have no end that the country would accept, and from this point the questions were how to get out, and how to leave the South able to stand. Hanoi's answer was silence. The Politburo, which had answered history's partial halt on 3 April with an offer to meet, said on 4 April through its mission in Paris that it would talk when the bombing stopped, and not before [H] [X-013].

## EV000-48 · The Paris talks open · May 1968 · Paris · CANCELLED · 000,013
*Initiative: enemy*

The talks did not open in May. Historically they opened in Paris on 13 May, with W. Averell Harriman leading for the United States and Xuan Thuy for Hanoi, after the North answered the partial halt of 31 March [H51]. In this war there was no partial halt (EV000-45), and the Politburo held to the position it had taken since 1965: no talks while the bombs fell (F013-10) [X-013]. Harriman was named and waited in Washington; the North's representatives in Paris met French officials and journalists, and said the same thing every week [X-013]. Through the summer the war had no forum for statements, and the May and August offensives were fought for position on the ground with no table to bring the position to. The talks opened in November, after the halt (EV000-53).

## EV013-6 · The summer north of the 19th parallel · 1 Apr – 31 Oct 1968 · Hanoi, Haiphong and the Red River delta; the northeast railway; Route Package 1 and the coastal road to Vinh; the MACV Combined Targeting Cell, Tan Son Nhut · NEW · 013
*Initiative: allied*

Historically, from 1 April to 31 October 1968 the bombing of the North was confined below the 19th parallel and then the 20th, and its whole weight fell on the panhandle's roads [H]. In this war it went on over the whole North for seven months more (EV000-45). The cell's list was by then about 180 targets the North could not move, the Red River crossings, the rail yards at Yen Vien, Kep and Thai Nguyen, the power plants rebuilt as small stations, and the SAM sites, and it struck them on the repair list as it had since 1965 (EV013-3) [X-013]. The air defence over the delta was by now about history's density, about 25–30 SA-2 battalions rotating among some 200 sites and several thousand guns, its Soviet missiles and Chinese gun divisions in place since 1966 (EV013-2, F013-17) [X-013]. Between April and October about 70 American aircraft were lost over the North, about 40 of them over the Hanoi and Haiphong gun belts, where history's campaign of those months sent no aircraft [X-013]. The Seventh Air Force flew fewer sorties against the panhandle's roads than history's summer, because the North above the 19th parallel took about two-fifths of them, and the panhandle's trucks moved with a little less interference than they had historically [X-013]. The Gulf watch counted no difference in what passed Vinh southbound that could be told from the season's weather [X-013]. The North's own leadership used the summer to finish its dispersal: by October it held almost nothing above the 19th parallel that a GBU-31 could kill and that it could not rebuild in a night (F013-12) [X-013].

## EV000-53 · The bombing halt, the talks and the American election · 31 Oct – 13 Nov 1968 · Washington, Paris and Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,013
*Initiative: both*

In October Hanoi signalled that it would accept the Saigon government at the table if the bombing stopped [H]. On 31 October Johnson announced the end of all bombing of North Vietnam from 1 November, ending Rolling Thunder [H9] [H51]. In this war the same announcement opened the talks themselves, which history had opened in May: Harriman and Xuan Thuy met for the first time at the Hotel Majestic on 13 November, and the four-party conference with Saigon and the Front was agreed in principle for the new year (EV000-48) [X-013]. Thieu refused to join the talks on the terms agreed [H51]. Through Anna Chennault the Nixon campaign had encouraged him to wait for a better deal, and on 2 November she told the South Vietnamese ambassador to "hold on a while longer"; Johnson learned of it through intercepts and ordered the campaign watched, but did not make it public [H51]. Historians disagree on whether the channel changed anything, since Thieu had his own reasons to refuse [H51]. On 5 November Richard M. Nixon was elected president by a narrow popular margin [H]. The four-party talks did not meet in substance until the new year, after a dispute over the shape of the table was settled with a round table for the governments, on history's date [H51]. The six months lost in Paris cost the settlement nothing that anyone could later find: history's talks had spent them on statements (F013-10) [X-013].

Over three years and eight months Rolling Thunder had flown about the same number of attack sorties as history's 153,784 USAF and 152,399 Navy and Marine sorties, but about a third of them against fixed targets on the restrike list rather than one in a hundred, and far fewer in daylight armed reconnaissance of the roads [H9] [X-013]. It had lost, by this command's count, about 810–880 aircraft over or near the North rather than history's 922: about 30–60 fewer in 1965, when the North's air defence was broken before it was built, about 40–90 fewer in 1966–67, and about 30–40 more in the summer of 1968 over Hanoi (F013-17, F013-18) [H9] [X-013]. It ended, as history's did, without having broken Hanoi's will or stopped the flow of men and supplies south, and it ended with nothing left in the North for it to destroy that the North could not rebuild in a night.
