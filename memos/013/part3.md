## §4.0 The course of the war

The war opened on 1 January 1964 with the Republic of Vietnam given, by provision, most of what its critics said it lacked. It had land for the tiller and a commission against corruption from the first day. It had a rural police with a post in every village and a Special Branch with sole charge of the infrastructure, province chiefs chosen on merit, and an officer corps, an NCO corps and a soldiery trained since 1957 to the standards of 2026. It had a domestic arsenal, an electronics plant, and a combined command under an American commander with a task force against the Front's hidden government (EV001-1, EV002-1, EV007-1, EV008-1, EV012-1). Over the South two U-2S Dragon Ladies and four Global Hawks watched from January 1964 (EV003-1). On Guam every B-52 was a B-52J with GPS weapons, fed from March by a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut (EV005-1). The war these gave the Republic was better than history's in almost every hamlet it held, and the same as history's in almost everything that decided how it ended.

The first year was the police's and the Special Branch's. The Front's district government in the held belt began to leave it in 1964, broken from the edge at Can Giuoc and in Hoc Mon (EV012-2, EV006-2). The sea was watched and closed. The trawlers that history's Vung Ro would have found were seen, turned and abandoned within a year, and Hanoi's southern cargo moved to Sihanoukville, eighteen months early, where it could be counted and not stopped (EV003-2). On the night of 4 August 1964 a Global Hawk over the Gulf of Tonkin showed the President that there had been no second attack. Pierce Arrow was never flown, and the Southeast Asia Resolution passed on the real attack of 2 August (EV000-3).

That night also began this memorandum's war. The morning after the false alarm, the coordinate cell was given the North above the 20th parallel, and a combined targeting cell under Brigadier General Walter E. Pruitt was built around it to plan a campaign the theater would run itself (EV013-1). When Pleiku and Qui Nhon brought the reprisals of February 1965, the President approved more than history's "measured and limited air action". He approved the Joint Chiefs' list of 94 targets, extended by the cell with the North's command centres, radars, power and fuel, to be struck in a single opening. He let the campaign be run from Saigon without zones around Hanoi and Haiphong and without pauses, with a buffer of 15 miles along China and one strike on Haiphong's port. He kept only the decision whether to bomb and its date (EVCP01-2).

On the night of 2–3 March 1965, in the crachin, twenty-four B-52Js from Andersen did in four hours what history's fighter-bombers spent two and a half years trying to do. Phuc Yen's runway and most of the 921st Regiment's MiG-17s died in the first cells. Then the warning and fire-control radars, the air-defence centre at Bach Mai, the Ministry in the Citadel, the Yen Phu and Haiphong power plants, the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly, the Paul Doumer and Ham Rong bridges, and finally the cranes of Haiphong, struck once and never again (EV013-1). No bomber was lost; the guns fired blind into cloud. By 9 March the North had lost most of its power, its bulk fuel, its great bridges, its jet base and the cranes of its only deepwater port, for seven American aircraft lost to guns in the daylight wave. The Politburo was untouched. The trucks of Group 559, the porters in the panhandle and the Front's regiments in the South noticed nothing at all.

What followed was the North's answer, and it was history's answer brought forward a year and taken by three capitals for their own reasons. Le Duan's Politburo put the North on a war footing in March and dispersed everything it could move. It held three divisions at home against a landing that never came (EV013-2). Moscow sent missiles, technicians, diesels and trucks faster than it had historically. Beijing refused Moscow an air corridor, for reasons that had nothing to do with the bombing and everything to do with its quarrel with Moscow, so the Soviet missiles came late by rail. China sent its own railway troops and gun divisions instead, and gave the surviving VPAF fields in Yunnan and Guangxi (EV013-2). The SA-2 sites were struck as they were built, the first American aircraft lost to a missile fell in August rather than July, and Spring High was never flown (EVCP02-2). The North's war in the air moved to China, its fuel into drums and its power into village diesels. Its labour, 300,000–400,000 people repairing and carrying by July, was a year early.

The ground war took no notice. The ARVN's defeats at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, not anything in the North, drove the July decision. On 28 July Johnson sent the divisions on history's date, for history's reasons (EVCP01-2). The war of 1965–67 was fought as the record had it: the Ia Drang, the frontier battles, the measured sieges that the Js made the enemy's worst battles, HARD CROSSING's six weeks of cut crossings in the panhandle, and the posts of the held belt calling for fire (EV000-23, EVCP02-3, EV005-2, EV008-4). The combined task force broke the Front's district layer in the Saigon belt and the upper Delta (EV012-4). Nothing in the North changed any of it.

Over the North the campaign became what the theater had always said it should be, and showed what the theater had not said. Pruitt's cell ran a single list and a daily repair list. The Red River crossings at Hanoi were struck 61 times and closed to rail on about 290 of 517 days, and the pontoons came back each night under 2,000 men and a Chinese engineer regiment (EV013-3). The cell's count of targets destroyed rose to about 140 a month in the summer of 1965 and fell to about 35 by December 1966. The campaign was not failing. The North had stopped owning anything else worth a GBU-31. There was no Christmas pause in 1965 and no POL strike in June 1966, because there was no pause to offer and no bulk fuel left to burn (EV013-4, EVCP02-4). In October 1966 this command reported, over Westmoreland's signature, that the northern campaign was destroying everything in the North that could be destroyed and that the North's war in the South needed none of it (EV013-3).

That report was the provision's largest consequence in Washington. The argument that air power would win if only it were allowed was settled inside the government a year early, and by the theater that had made it. McNamara had the verdict a year before history gave it to him. The barrier began from it. The Stennis hearings of 1967, in which the Chiefs blamed history's restrictions, were never held, because there were no restrictions to blame (EVCP02-4). The MiG war was smaller and was fought from China. Bolo killed three MiG-21s rather than seven, because the airfields that history protected had been struck from the start. The Chinese guns on the northeast railway took an American pilot inside the buffer in May 1966, and the President kept the buffer where the provision had put it (EV013-5).

Tet came on its historical night and was decided where it was decided historically, in Saigon, Hue and at Khe Sanh, and in Washington (EV000-42, EV000-44, EV000-41). The President's decision of 31 March capped the commitment and ended his candidacy. In this war it carried no partial halt, because the campaign he had authorized in 1965 allowed none, and Hanoi would not talk while the bombs fell (EV000-45). The May and August offensives were fought with no table in Paris, and the bombing of the whole North went on through the summer. That cost about 40 aircraft over the gun belts of Hanoi and Haiphong, where history's summer campaign never went, and it did nothing to what passed Vinh southbound (EV013-6). On 1 November the President stopped the bombing on history's date, in exchange for the same Hanoi signal. The talks history had opened in May opened in November, and the four-party conference met in January on history's date. The six months lost in Paris had been months of statements (EV000-53).

The campaign ended with about 810–880 American aircraft lost over the North rather than 922 (EV000-53). The saving came in 1965, when the North's air defence was broken before it was built, and in 1966–67, when its MiGs fought from China. The summer of 1968 over Hanoi took part of it back. The campaign ended as history's did, having broken neither Hanoi's will nor the flow south. The North it left held almost nothing a GBU-31 could kill that it could not rebuild in a night.

From 1969 the war runs on the record's earlier course, because the halt of November 1968 left the North where history's halt left it. Commando Hunt met a Trail more redundant than history's, and the radar's second ledger kept it honest (EV000-55, EV000-68). Vietnamization took the Americans home. The palace captured the institutions the provisions had built once the American lever lapsed in 1970–71. In 1972 the Easter Offensive was broken at An Loc and Kontum by American air, with the Js' siege box decisive, and Linebacker chose from folders seven years old (EV000-76, EV000-77, EV000-75). This memorandum's campaign had already used Haiphong's one strike, and the mining of May 1972 closed a port whose cranes had never been rebuilt for the bombers to find. Linebacker II cost about six bombers rather than fifteen (EV000-82).

The Paris Agreement, Case–Church and the end of American air power in 1973 came on their historical dates (EV000-86). Nixon served out his term, and the fiscal 1975 appropriation was larger than history's (EV011-2). Phuoc Long fell. In March 1975 Thieu gave no order to withdraw from the Highlands, so the collapse of I and II Corps came three to five weeks later than history's. Hanoi put the assault on Saigon back to the dry season of 1975–76 (EVMT01-4, EV011-3).

The war therefore ends where memorandum 011 left it, and this memorandum does not move it. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, under Thieu, without American air power, with five PAVN corps assembled for an offensive on the capital in the first months of 1976. The air campaign the theater asked for throughout the war was given to it in 1965, and it did everything the theater had promised against the North. It did not touch the one thing the war turned on: an enemy whose war in the South needed nothing that could be bombed in the North.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

Phase I carries the opening strike, and its mechanism is the compound of F013-1. The authority of P013b, given by the President on 13 February 1965 (EVCP01-2), turned the record's folders (EV005-1) and the Js (P005a, P005b, P005c) into a campaign. The crachin of March decided that the campaign's first blow would be the bombers' alone (EV013-1). The opening strike destroyed what the North owned in measurable places in a week, and it is the ground of the strongest positive effect, F013-18. The North's air defence was struck before it was built. The MiG-17s of the 921st died on Phuc Yen's revetments, the fire-control radars in the first cells, and the SA-2 sites as they were prepared (EVCP02-2). About 15–40 fewer aircraft were lost over the North in March–July 1965 than in history's months, and the first missile kill came a month late.

The phase also shows the bottleneck that did not move (F013-7). Nothing on the list was a ton of the North's freight to the South. The porters and trucks of Group 559, the Front's regiments and the ARVN's defeats at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai took no notice of Hanoi's dark power stations. The decision of 28 July came on history's date for history's reasons (EVCP01-2). The displacement of F013-5 began at once: fuel to drums, power to village diesels, fighters to China (EV013-2).

### Enemy response and initiative

The North detected the provision on its first night and answered it in three capitals (F013-13, EV013-2). The Politburo under Le Duan, meeting on 25–27 March, ordered the war footing and the dispersal. Reading the strike as preparation for a landing, it held the 308th and 312th Divisions and two regiments of the 320th at home through the summer, the one wrong conclusion the North drew, and a costly one in time.

<!-- initiative -->The enemy coalition's decision of the phase that nothing in this memorandum provoked was Beijing's refusal, in April 1965, of Moscow's request for an air corridor across China and for two airfields in Yunnan (EV013-2). It was taken by Mao and Zhou Enlai for reasons of their own: the quarrel with Moscow over the leadership of the communist world, and Mao's suspicion that a Soviet presence in Yunnan was a Soviet presence on China's frontier. They took the same decision historically, against a campaign that had barely begun. Its effect in this war was that the first SA-2 battalions reached Hanoi by train three to five weeks late, with a fifth of their equipment held at Pingxiang. The cell's strikes on the prepared sites therefore fell on a North with fewer launchers than it would otherwise have had. Beijing gave instead what it controlled: its railway troops, three anti-aircraft divisions on the railways, and fields for the VPAF. That adaptation cost the North dependence, not labour.

The North's adaptation cost was labour, rice and dependence, paid by its people and its allies (F013-14). By July perhaps 300,000–400,000 people were repairing, carrying and manning guns full time, and about 200,000 tons of Chinese rice were promised for 1966 [X-013].

### Causal chains

**C013-1** [months] The opening strike burns the North's bulk fuel and darkens its power stations in a week (EV013-1) → the North asks Moscow for diesel generators, pumps and trucks in place of the heavy plant it had asked for in 1964, and receives them from the spring of 1965 (EV013-2) → trucks that would have hauled for the North's own industrialisation are released to the General Department of Rear Services in 1965–66, and Group 559's truck strength grows faster than history's (F013-14) → the panhandle that HARD CROSSING attacks in April 1966 already has more trucks than history's, and the record's judgment that the Trail's capacity stands one to three points above history's from 1966 (POS005-4) has a second cause the record did not know: a bombing campaign that destroyed the North's industry turned its imports into transport. Terminal link in Phase II.

### Downstream

Every later phase inherits a North without a fixed economy and a VPAF based in China. Phase II inherits the restrike watch, the North's dispersal and Moscow's faster missiles. Phase III inherits a campaign with no partial halt to offer. Phases IV to VI inherit nothing from this phase that the halt of November 1968 did not return to history (null P4–P6).

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is the campaign the theater asked for, and its mechanism is the anticipation of F013-12 and the displacement of F013-5, shown at length in the restrike watch on the Red River (EV013-3). The cell kept the crossings at Hanoi closed to rail on more than half the days of 1965–66. The North answered with pontoons, ferries, 2,000 men a night and a Chinese engineer regiment. The cell's measure of success, targets destroyed, fell from about 140 a month to about 35, because the North stopped owning anything else worth a GBU-31. The memorandum's most useful statement, F013-7, is shown in the same event. J-2's report of October 1966 set the cell's figures beside the Gulf watch's count of the coastal traffic and the CIA's estimate of the North's imports, and neither had fallen.

The rest of the phase follows from that. There was no Christmas pause, so the B-3 Front lost two or three weeks of the 1966 flow, made up by April, and the President's critics gained a charge (EV013-4, F013-9, F013-15). There was no POL strike in June 1966, because the fuel had burned in March 1965, and McNamara's doubts hardened a year early (EVCP02-4). The Chinese guns on the northeast railway took Captain Ainsley inside the buffer in May 1966, and the President kept the buffer at 15 miles (EV013-5, F013-15). The MiG war was smaller and fought from China (F013-6). Over 1966–67 about 40–90 fewer American aircraft were lost over the North than historically [X-013], the strongest positive effect (F013-18) continuing at a falling rate as Moscow's missiles and Beijing's guns rebuilt the sky.

Part 2 judged that the kit rationing would leave HARD CROSSING and the siege box a quarter to a third short (F013-8). The war went otherwise, and I record the change. HARD CROSSING used about 1,900 kits over six months (EV005-2), a small share of the year's supply. The cell put its own shortage on the northern restrike, which the Js flew with fewer kits a target and the F-105Ds with unguided bombs, and not on the panhandle or the sieges. Con Thien and Khe Sanh were fought with the kits the record gave them (EVCP02-3, EV000-41).

### Enemy response and initiative

The North's response was dispersal, labour and dependence, as Part 2 judged (F013-5, F013-14). The authors were distinct. The Hanoi Bridge and Road Company and the Chinese 1st Railway Engineer Division kept the Red River crossed (EV013-3). The Chinese 61st and 63rd Anti-Aircraft Artillery Divisions defended the northeast railway and took the pilot inside the buffer (EV013-5). The VPAF fought from Mengzi and Ningming (EVCP02-4). The Politburo's plan for the South was its own and unchanged: Resolution 13 and the decision of July 1967 for a general offensive owed nothing to the bombing (EV000-37). The Soviet supply was a variable with its own politics (DIRMT01-10). Brezhnev armed the North and kept his ships out of the line of fire. Haiphong unloaded his freight by lighter after its one strike, and his ships were never hit.

### Causal chains

**C013-2** [months] The opening strike destroys most of the 921st Regiment's MiG-17s on Phuc Yen's revetments and the cell restrikes the North's jet fields on every repair (EV013-1, EVCP02-4) → the VPAF re-forms in Yunnan and Guangxi and flies its MiG-21s into the North from China a sortie at a time, landing at Noi Bai or Kep only to refuel (EV013-2, F013-6) → Olds's trap of 2 January 1967 meets four MiG-21s instead of the regiment's full strength, and kills three rather than seven (EVCP02-4) → the war's most famous American air victory over the North is smaller in this war because the airfields that history protected were open, and the VPAF that fought from China grew more slowly but could not be destroyed on the ground.

**C013-3** [war-shaping] P013b makes the northern campaign the theater's own list and tempo (F013-16) → the campaign's failure to reduce the flow south is reported by this command itself, in October 1966, with the radar's counts beside its claims (EV013-3) → the Stennis subcommittee's hearings of August 1967, in which the Chiefs blamed history's restrictions for the campaign's failure, are never held, because there are no restrictions to blame (EVCP02-4) → the argument that air power was shackled, which history's airmen carried into 1972 and for twenty years after, has no hearing in this war; the case against more bombing is made by the theater that asked for it.

C013-1 terminates in this phase (§4.P1).

### Downstream

Phase III inherits a North that has outlasted three years of unrestricted bombing, a campaign with no pause to offer, and an air defence about as dense as history's. Phases IV to VI inherit nothing from this phase that the halt of November 1968 does not return to history (null P4–P6).

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

### Mechanism

Phase III carries the counterfactual erasure of F013-10. On 31 March the President had no partial halt to offer, because the campaign he authorized in 1965 allowed none. Rusk's proposal to end all the bombing was refused while the PAVN divisions stood around Khe Sanh, and the offer was made in words (EV000-45). Hanoi would not talk while the bombs fell. The talks history opened on 13 May did not open (EV000-48), and the May and August offensives were fought with no table in Paris. The bombing of the whole North went on through the summer (EV013-6). It cost about 70 aircraft, about 40 of them over the Hanoi and Haiphong gun belts where history's summer campaign never went, and that is the counter-directional finding (F013-17) at work: the later years over Hanoi gave back part of the early saving. The panhandle's trucks moved with a little less interference than in history's summer, because the North above the 19th parallel took two-fifths of the sorties. The halt came on 1 November on history's date, in exchange for the same Hanoi signal, and opened the talks themselves on 13 November. The four-party conference met in January as it did historically (EV000-53).

The campaign's last account is in EV000-53, which this memorandum rewrote, and it answers the air-war study's question Q-1. Rolling Thunder in this war lost about 810–880 aircraft over or near the North rather than 922: about 30–60 fewer in 1965, about 40–90 fewer in 1966–67 and about 30–40 more in the summer of 1968 [X-013]. That is the strongest positive effect (F013-18) at its net weight, a little below Part 2's judgment of 50–150, because the summer of 1968 was costlier than Part 2 allowed.

### Enemy response and initiative

The North's decision of the phase was the Politburo's, and it was the decision it had held since 1965: no talks while the bombs fell (EV000-45, EV000-48). Le Duan's leadership had no reason to pay for a halt it believed it would get, and it got the halt on history's date for history's price, the Saigon government at the table (EV000-53). The North used the summer to finish its dispersal. By October it held almost nothing above the 19th parallel that a GBU-31 could kill and that it could not rebuild in a night (EV013-6, F013-12).

### Clock

- **Against tolerance.** Hanoi's civilian dead were on television from March 1965, Fulbright's charge of February 1966 found a peace offensive without a pause, and the summer of 1968 sent aircraft over Hanoi after the President had offered to talk (EV013-1, EV013-4, EV013-6). The public facet's range at the end of 1968 widens to −1..+2 from memorandum 011's 0..+2.

- **Neither.** The appropriation cycle was untouched: the campaign cost what history's cost, in about the same sorties. The escalation was more visible in 1965 and no less visible thereafter.

The clock was spent by the ground war's casualties and by Tet, which nothing in this memorandum touched, and the decision of 31 March stands (POSCP01-1, EV000-45).

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the cumulative force now differs as follows.

**Over the North the margin is substantial, and it is stated plainly.**
- **The North's fixed economy** was destroyed in a week, on 2–9 March 1965, rather than by increments over two and a half years. That covered its power, bulk fuel, great bridges, jet base and fighters, warning net and Haiphong's cranes (EV013-1).
- **The air war** was fought from above and ahead. The first missile kill came a month late, Spring High was never needed, the MiG war was fought from China, and Bolo was smaller (EVCP02-2, EVCP02-4).
- **Losses.** About 810–880 American aircraft were lost over the North rather than 922, and perhaps 50–150 fewer American aircrew were killed or captured [X-013] (EV000-53).
- **The verdict.** The theater itself reported the campaign's limits in October 1966, a year before history's government agreed on them, and the Stennis hearings were never held (EV013-3, EVCP02-4).

**In Washington and Paris the changes are small.**
- No Christmas pause, and a peace offensive carried in words (EV013-4).
- No partial halt on 31 March 1968, and the talks six months late, which cost the settlement nothing (EV000-45, EV000-48, EV000-53).

**In the South there is no change attributable to this memorandum** beyond two or three weeks of the B-3 Front's 1966 flow, made up by April (EV013-4), and a Trail better supplied with trucks than history's (C013-1).

The cumulative force's other margins stand as the record holds them, and this memorandum moves none of them. The Front's district government is broken in the held belt. The sea route closed in 1964. The measured sieges were the enemy's worst battles. Hue was one battle. Every soldier was trained, and the officers and NCOs were schooled. The arsenal stretched the 1975 aid. The Republic survives 1975.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

**The end state does not change.** The Republic of Vietnam survives 1975 in III and IV Corps and on the coast south of Nha Trang, under Thieu, without American air power, facing five PAVN corps. Its fall is expected between February and May 1976 (EV011-3). The rung is (b), dependent survival, by the test below, because Saigon stands past 30 April 1975.

**Why this memorandum does not move it.** The provision acted on the North in 1965–68, and the war was decided in the South and in Congress. The North's war in the South needed nothing that could be bombed in the North (F013-7, POSTNA-4). Its leaders were not in the buildings (EV013-1), and its allies replaced what was destroyed faster than they had historically (EV013-2). The bombing ended on 1 November 1968 on history's date, and the North rebuilt in its absence. That leaves G-14 where the record has always put it: nothing in the force reaches the Politburo's will. This memorandum is the record's most direct test of that gap, and the gap holds. An unrestricted air campaign broke everything the North owned in measurable places and did not move its commitment by a point.

**Why the next rung is not reachable.** Rung (c) requires the countryside held in every corps region without American ground forces and American air power available at the end. This memorandum touches neither. Its air power was over the North and ended in 1968, and Case–Church ended all of it in 1973.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The North's fixed economy was destroyed in a week, not in two and a half years.** On 2–9 March 1965 the Js and the daylight wave took the North's power, bulk fuel, great bridges, jet base and fighters, warning net and Haiphong's cranes, for seven American aircraft lost (EV013-1). This is the largest single blow the record has struck at the North, seven years before Linebacker.
- **The North's air defence was fought from ahead for three years.** The SA-2 sites were struck as they were built, the MiG bases were open from the first day, and the VPAF fought from China. The campaign lost about 810–880 aircraft rather than 922, and perhaps 50–150 fewer American aircrew were killed or captured (EVCP02-2, EVCP02-4, EV000-53) [X-013]. That is the strongest positive effect (F013-18) at its net weight.
- **The argument over air power was settled inside the government, by the theater, a year early.** This command reported in October 1966 that the campaign was destroying everything in the North that could be destroyed and that the war in the South needed none of it. The Stennis hearings were never held, and the case that air power had been shackled has no standing in this war (EV013-3, EVCP02-4).
- **The North paid for its survival in labour, rice and dependence**, a year earlier and on a larger scale than historically. About 300,000–400,000 people were working full time by July 1965, about 200,000 tons of Chinese rice came in 1966, and the VPAF fought from Chinese airfields (EV013-2, F013-14).
- **The pauses and the partial halt did not happen.** There was no Christmas pause and no MAYFLOWER, and the peace offensive was carried in words. On 31 March 1968 there was no halt to trade, so the talks opened in November rather than May, at no cost anyone could later find (EV013-4, EV000-45, EV000-48, EV000-53).

### What has not changed

- **The flow south.** The enemy's war in the South needed nothing that could be bombed in the North, and nothing on the list was a ton of its freight. Tonnage arriving stays where the record put it, and the Trail is better supplied with trucks than history's (F013-7, C013-1).
- **Hanoi's will.** The Politburo outlasted an unrestricted campaign and got the halt on history's date for history's price. G-14 holds, and this memorandum is its most direct test (EV000-53).
- **The ground war.** The July decision, the frontier battles, Tet, and the decision of 31 March to cap the commitment all came on history's dates for history's reasons (EVCP01-2, EV000-45).
- **The end of the war.** Linebacker, Pocket Money, the Paris Agreement, Case–Church and 1975 run as the record holds them. The Republic survives 1975 and is expected to fall in the first half of 1976 (null P5, null P6; EV011-3).
