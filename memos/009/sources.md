# Sources — memorandum 009

[S1] Basic Combat Training — U.S. Army (goarmy.com), https://www.goarmy.com/army-life/basic-training — 10 weeks in four phases: Yellow (weeks 1–2, values, physical and tactical training, teamwork), Red (weeks 3–4, weapon familiarisation, hand-to-hand combat, lifesaving, the Hammer field exercise), White (weeks 5–7, basic rifle marksmanship, target engagement, the Anvil, two days and two nights), Blue (weeks 8–10, machine guns and grenades, maneuver, the multi-day Forge); about 90% complete; day from 0430 to 2100.
[S2] "Soldiers exposed to new combat realities with expanded training" — Military Times, 16 Oct 2024, https://www.militarytimes.com/land/2024/10/16/soldiers-exposed-to-new-combat-realities-with-expanded-training/ — Forge began as a 2016 concept with a 96-hour week-seven field exercise, regular since 2018; Forge 2.5 from March 2024 with large-scale combat scenarios, drill sergeants as squad leaders, every event collective, casualty evaluation, reports, mission planning and orders; a four-day patrol-base endurance test; fielded at Fort Moore, Fort Jackson and Fort Leonard Wood; the pre-basic Future Soldier Prep Course from 2022.
[S3] "Back to basics: big changes to Army recruit training" — Joint Base San Antonio, 7 Jun 2018, https://www.jbsa.mil/News/News/Article/1541421/back-to-basics-big-changes-to-army-recruit-training — the Hammer, the Anvil and the Forge; an 81-hour Forge as a graduation requirement; time restored for Army values, drill and ceremony, Army history and barracks inspections after a survey found new soldiers undisciplined; first aid expanded to Army Medical Command guidelines (shock, open wounds, fractures); Level 1 combatives.
[S4] "Boot camp" — Today's Military (Department of Defense), https://www.todaysmilitary.com/training/boot-camp — entry-level training lengths: Army BCT 10 weeks, Marine Corps recruit training 13 weeks (Parris Island and San Diego), Navy recruit training 10 weeks (Great Lakes), Air Force BMT 7.5 weeks (JBSA-Lackland), Coast Guard 8 weeks; Army fitness events.
[S5] "Fit to Fight: How the Services Are Meeting the New Fitness Mandate" — MOAA, 2026, https://www.moaa.org/content/publications-and-media/news-articles/2026-news-articles/reads/fit-to-fight-how-the-services-are-meeting-the-new-fitness-mandate/ — the September 2025 department-wide fitness directive; the Army Combat Field Test from April 2026; waist-to-height standards.
[S6] "What troops need to know about Hegseth's new memos for the force" — Military Times, 3 Oct 2025, https://www.militarytimes.com/news/pentagon-congress/2025/10/03/what-troops-need-to-know-about-hegseths-new-memos-for-the-force/ — a 60-day review of training-school standards since 1990; review of hazing and bullying definitions; combat-arms fitness at the male standard.
[S7] "How long was basic training during the Vietnam War?" — HistoryNet, https://historynet.com/long-basic-training-vietnam-war/ — Vietnam-era Army basic training eight weeks, in some cases nine; infantry AIT eight weeks; about six months from enlistment to Vietnam.
[S8] Induction statistics — Selective Service System, https://sss.gov/history-and-records/induction-statistics — inductions by year: 1957 138,504; 1958 142,246; 1959 96,153; 1960 86,602; 1961 118,586; 1962 82,060; 1963 119,265; 1964 112,386; 1965 230,991; 1966 382,010; 1967 228,263; 1968 296,406; 1969 283,586; 1970 162,746; 1971 94,092; 1972 49,514.
[S9] RVNAF training — GlobalSecurity.org, https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/vietnam/rvn-af-training.htm — basic training for Vietnamese soldiers 12 weeks, cut to nine in the 1968 mobilization; 33 training centres in 1968–70 with normal capacity about 65,000 and emergency about 105,655; 26 service schools, 24,000–34,000; 1968 training centre load 168,335 Army and RF recruits, 19,174 OCS preparatory students and 22,483 PF recruits; 588 new and 656 old PF platoons trained in 1968; units filled with hastily drafted men without formal training; training-centre posts given to officers relieved of combat command; 1966 relaxation of advisory standards; leadership the greatest obstacle.
[S10] Civil Guard (Regional Forces) — GlobalSecurity.org, https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/vietnam/rvn-civil-guard.htm — Civil Guard cycle cut from 24 to 12 weeks; 1964 RF recruit course nine weeks matching the ARVN centres; RF refresher course four weeks; about 46,000 Civil Guard and 40,000 Self-Defense Corps in 1959; 1964 RF companies 473 rising to 533, of which 494 trained; 123-man RF companies; 1964 recruiting goal 14,000 with about 2,000 trained; 137,187 RF in 759 companies FY1965–66; pre-1964 units "armed bands of young men".
[S11] Self-Defense Corps (Popular Forces) — GlobalSecurity.org, https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/vietnam/rvn-self-defense-corps.htm — a six-week programme at training centres in 26 provinces, 1960–64; 1964 unit and leadership training up to 60% behind programme, leadership quotas never filled, "grave deficiencies in all aspects of training"; 1964 programme on an authorised structure of 110,000; 35-man PF platoons in 1969; 391,000 RF and PF in 1969.
[S12] South Vietnamese Popular Force — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Vietnamese_Popular_Force — about 50,000 Self-Defense Corps in 1957; 3,892 platoons in June 1965; about 135,000 against 185,000 authorised at end 1965; 4,121 rifle platoons and 242 intelligence squads in October 1967; desertions 11,957 (1962), 18,540 (1963), 36,608 (1964), 49,224 (1965); 27.0 per 1,000 a month in early 1966; training camps cut from 37 to 19 under the Central Training Command; Westmoreland's view that the PF needed little training; RF and PF credited with about 30% of enemy casualties; mostly carbines and BARs in 1967; Westmoreland's 1968 request for 268,000 M16s and 11,200 M79s.
[S13] Quang Trung National Training Center — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quang_Trung_National_Training_Center — founded 1953 at Hoc Mon, 16 km north-west of Saigon; mid-1950s capacity about 24,000 a year, 8,000 at a time; eight-week basic course in the mid-1950s; 1959 advisers judged instruction hours, especially marksmanship, insufficient; MACV's January 1964 revision of the basic and advanced individual programmes, completed by March; May 1964 recommendation to extend recruit training to four other national centres and the Ranger centre at Duc My; 1967 relief of the commandant for using trainee labour on an ice plant.
[S14] Butler and Blackbourne, "Battlefield trauma care then and now: a decade of Tactical Combat Casualty Care" — Journal of Trauma and Acute Care Surgery, 2012, https://www.east.org/content/documents/MilitaryResources/b/TCCC%20Butler%20A%20Decade%20of%20TCCC%20J%20Trauma%202012.pdf — Maughon (1970): of 2,600 killed in action in Vietnam, 193 died of isolated extremity hemorrhage, 7.9%, the leading cause of preventable death; Kelly et al.: 77 of 982 early Iraq and Afghanistan deaths (7.8%) from failure to use a tourniquet; 2001 doctrine still taught not to use tourniquets; an estimate of 1,000–2,000 lives saved by tourniquets in the recent wars; preventable death falling to 3% in the 75th Ranger Regiment, where every Ranger was trained.
