## §4.0 The course of the war

The war I now command is fought, from its first day to its last, by private soldiers who have been trained. That is the whole of this memorandum's change, and it is both smaller and larger than it sounds. It is smaller because no battle in this war was decided by a private's training: the enemy chose where and when to fight, the generals chose where to send the battalions, and Washington and Hanoi chose how long it would last. It is larger because the private is the man who dies, and in a war of ambushes, outposts and night attacks, how he fights for the first minute and whether his friend can stop his bleeding decide how many of him come home.

On 1 January 1964 the Republic's army already held seven years of trained men. Since 1957 every recruit to the ARVN, the Airborne, the Rangers and the Marines had passed ten weeks at Quang Trung or a division centre on the 2026 American programme, and every Civil Guard and Self-Defense Corps recruit, the men who became the Regional and Popular Forces, six weeks of it at his province's centre. About 120,000–160,000 regulars and 60,000–100,000 territorials were trained men when this headquarters opened, and every American who arrived in the theatre had passed his own service's 2026 course [M009]. The rule that nobody joined a unit untrained was put on the muster roll as the number of the cycle a man had passed, and the Muster Service checked it at dawn. In September 1964 it caught a division training centre in Binh Dinh that had been sending men to the battalions after four weeks and drawing six weeks' rations for them, and the Commission charged the commandant (EV009-1). The course had a price, and the army paid it in the two worst years: two to three thousand of its best sergeants were standing in front of recruits at Hoc Mon while the battalions were being broken in Phuoc Long and Quang Ngai (F009-11).

Phase I ran its historical course in Saigon and on the main-force battlefield. Khanh took power, the generals fought each other, Hanoi sent its regiments south, and the Americans came in the summer of 1965. In the hamlets of the held belt the trained Popular Forces platoon changed the first ten minutes of a night attack. At Long Huu in Kien Hoa on 17 November 1964 Corporal Pham Van Tho's platoon had kept a third of its ammunition for the second rush, as the territorial course taught, and its two wounded were alive with tourniquets on when the boats came from Ham Long at 0320 (EV002-2). The posts with their platoon held about nineteen attacks in twenty rather than nine in ten, and the Front did what it does against every better post: it went for the constables, the militia and the camps the course did not reach. At Dong Xoai in June 1965 the CIDG companies broke first, as they always had; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment was landed on its division's plan beside the rubber and lost half its men regardless of its privates' training; and the 7th Airborne brought about 50 of its 60 wounded alive into Thuan Loi because men who had never been taught first aid in history's army had been taught it here (EV000-18). The army's battalions finished the phase a band steadier than history's, which is the first time in this exercise that the ARVN of 1964–65 has moved by a band on anything other than its weapons [X-009].

Phase II was the American war, and the American soldier in it was a little better and a good deal more likely to live. At the Ia Drang the troopers of the 1st Cavalry Division who lay in the grass at Albany through the night of 17 November 1965 put tourniquets on each other in the dark while the PAVN searched the field, and about eight to twelve of them lived who had died there historically (EV000-23). That is the rate on which this memorandum rests its whole American saving, about two to four percent of the American killed in action, and Albany is where it can be seen at work. It is not a large rate. Over the war it is about 900–1,900 American lives, and I sign for every one of them gladly [X-009].

The ARVN of Phase II was a trained army under board-chosen battalion commanders, carrying the carbines and squad guns of 1964, and the enemy treated it as he had treated every better unit: he declined to fight it. The 272nd Regiment that would not spring its ambush on Major Trieu's flanking companies on Route 13 in May 1966 ambushed the Regional Force convoy at Bau Bang instead, and the RF soldiers came off their trucks into the ditch and fought back in pairs, as their course had taught them; they lost about 32 men rather than 40, and they were still on their trucks when the fire came, because their sector commander had put them there (EV007-3). That is this provision in one morning: the private fought better, and the order above him was unchanged. Across 1966–67 I judge that the ARVN's battalions lost a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still laid, beyond what the rifles and the officers had saved, and that about one in eight fewer battalions was broken; no report shows it, because the fights it would have shown were not fought (F009-19, F009-25) [X-009].

The enemy's answer is the clearest thing in this memorandum, and it cost the Americans as much as the course saved them in one district. At the end of 1965 the command of the Quang Da Special Zone concluded that its local companies could not win firefights against units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds, and ordered its village guerrillas to make mines and booby traps their principal work (EV009-2). The Marines of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines at Dien Ban, who did everything right in a firefight and were given none, lost about two-thirds of their dead to devices in the year from September 1966, and the farmers of Dien Ban and Dai Loc stepped on the same devices. What the course gave those Marines was the tourniquet on the shattered leg, and it kept a dozen of them alive without their legs. The enemy had moved his war to the weapon the course did not teach, as this headquarters had said he would.

In June 1966 the piaster was devalued and the private's rice cost half as much again, and here the war showed what training does not buy (EV009-3). The men who walked out of the 25th Division's battalions that autumn had all been through Quang Trung; most went home to their families' fields, some to the Regional Forces, and a few hundred to the guerrilla platoons of Cu Chi and Duc Hoa, where a captured notebook records a three-week course for the district's squad leaders taught by a deserter on firing in pairs and the tourniquet, with the remark that the puppets' course was good and their pay was not. Colonel Hoan cut his division's desertion by a quarter in 1967 by moving the families inside the perimeters and paying the ration in rice. That was not training. It was the family, the pay and the rice, and it is the reason this provision moves the desertion band by nothing.

Tet came on the historical night and turned on the historical decisions, in Saigon, at Hue, at Khe Sanh and in Washington. The basic course changed it where the rifles and the officers had already changed it, on the compound walls of the district towns, where the RF companies held their fire and their ammunition, and in one place they had not: the wounded. Through the night of 31 January and the weeks of Hue, men lying in the streets with B40 fragments in their legs were alive in the morning because the man beside them had put on a tourniquet. I put the ARVN's dead in the first wave at about 2,150 rather than 2,300, and the American dead at 30–60 fewer, the rate of Albany at work in a second battle (EV000-42). The decision of 31 March, which was made on the casualties of 1965–67 and on the shock of the offensive, was made as before.

In June 1968 the mobilization met a training base that would not send men to units untrained, and the cost was a month. The Joint General Staff ordered the course cut to six weeks; the training command ran its centres in two shifts and held it at ten, and the new battalions and territorial companies reached the field after the third offensive had begun rather than before it, trained (EV000-51). The drill sergeants the army had held at the centres in 1964–65 came back as platoon sergeants, and about four in five of the new Popular Forces platoons had a man who had held the rank, against two in three on the course memorandum 007 had left. The third offensive went, as every offensive had since 1964, for what the course had not reached: the People's Self-Defense Force, armed with the Garands of 1950 and three days' instruction. In Hau Nghia it overran two-thirds of the groups it attacked. At Rach Kien in Long An a PF platoon came across the paddies in sixteen minutes to a self-defence group's flare and broke the attack at the hamlet gate, and the district chief of Can Duoc sent his returned drill sergeants to teach the groups the first ten minutes on their own evenings; it was the only district in three provinces where the groups held through the autumn (EV009-4).

From 1969 the line holds regardless of this provision. Vietnamization, the Cambodian incursion and Lam Son 719 are decided by the American withdrawal, the palace and the corps, and the trained private changes none of their outcomes; Lam Son 719 still goes in on its corps commander's plan and comes out as it did. The Easter Offensive of 1972 is decided at Quang Tri by the orders of the corps and at An Loc and Kontum by the B-52J; the trained soldiers who hold at An Loc hold for longer with their wounded alive, and the city held anyway. In 1974 the RVNAF bleeds in the attrition the ration imposes, with tourniquets and with fewer rounds than its training assumed, and in March 1975 its corps dissolve toward their families on the palace's orders. The course did not teach a soldier to leave his family on the road at Cheo Reo, and nothing a basic course could teach would have kept him from going to them (POSMT01-6). The Ho Chi Minh Campaign takes Saigon on 30 April 1975, and the course's graduates, the best-trained private soldiers the Republic ever had, are among the men in the camps.

What this provision did, it did to men. About 5,000–14,000 American and South Vietnamese soldiers are alive at the end of the war who died on the historical course, most of them South Vietnamese, many of them without an arm or a leg; the Front's opening volley broke fewer units in the years the war was being lost; the territorial post held its first ten minutes; and the enemy moved his war to the mine and the self-defence hamlet, where the course did not go [X-009]. It did not change who chose the battle, who chose the generals, what the soldier's family ate, or what Congress voted in 1974. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, and the Republic that falls is the same Republic with better privates.

## §4.P1 Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

<!-- chapter P1 -->

### Mechanism

The phase is changed in three places, and each is a mechanism the second part of this memorandum named. The first is the post's first ten minutes. A PF platoon that has been through the territorial course together, on the M4A1 it carries and with the PRC-10 the Go Vap plant gave it, fights the opening of a night attack as a unit: it holds its fire for the light, fires in pairs, reports what it sees, and keeps ammunition for the second assault (F009-2). At Long Huu on 17 November 1964 that is the difference between a post the Front's 516th Battalion company withdraws from and one it overruns on its second try, and the advisers' count of posts held across the held belt moves from about nine in ten to about nineteen in twenty (EV002-2) [X-009]. That is a large change in a small number. Of perhaps 400 attacks on posts with their platoons in 1964–65, it is the difference between about 40 posts lost and about 20, with their platoons, their radios and their carbines [X-009].

The second is the wounded. In both armies the course teaches every soldier the tourniquet, and the first place this war shows it is the rubber at Thuan Loi on 12 June 1965, where the 7th Airborne brings out about 60 wounded and about 50 live (EV000-18). The ARVN's evacuation was slower than the American and its surgical beds farther away; the tourniquet buys the wounded man the hours the helicopter takes, and in the ARVN those hours were longer. This is why the provision saves more Vietnamese than American lives by a factor of four or more [X-009].

The third is the army itself, and it is the reason for the one band this memorandum moves. The ARVN of 1964–65 had been broken in battle after battle by an enemy who chose the ground, and the historical cause the record gives, beside its commanders, is the untrained draftee sent straight to his unit and the private who could not shoot or move under fire [M009·S9]. With the course, the carbine of memorandum 004 and the lieutenants of memorandum 007 forming one standard from private to captain, the ARVN's battalions in this phase are steadier than history's by enough to move its effectiveness from one band above history to two (F009-1) [X-009]. They still lose Ba Gia and Dong Xoai, because the enemy chose those fights and the generals planned the reliefs, and the band records the battalions that came back from them, not the battles.

The phase also shows the course's cost and its corruption. The 2,000–3,000 drill sergeants it held at the centres were the sergeants the battalions most needed in the two years they were being broken (F009-11), and the training command's compromise of November 1964, sending the 1957–59 drill sergeants to the battalions on promotion and replacing them with corporals, began the circle that would repay the debt from 1966 (F009-4). The Binh Dinh centre that sold six weeks of rations in September 1964 was one of eleven such cases; the course number on the muster roll was the only check on a figure the centres scored for themselves (EV009-1, F009-17, F009-3).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy did not detect the course, because it had always been there. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee wrote its doctrine of 1963–64 against an army whose privates had been trained since 1957, and the lessons it drew in 1964 were the ones it was already drawing from the rifles: attack what is not trained or rearmed, ambush the column the general sends, avoid the wall of a post that holds (F009-20) [X-009]. Its district companies in the Delta moved from the posts with platoons to the constables, the militia and the camps from December 1964, as the chapter records, and the course widened the gap they were exploiting (F009-6).

Two things in its response were new. The Front's military proselytizing sections read the conduct block within a season and called it American indoctrination of the puppet soldier; in the held belt it took a little of the force out of their propaganda among the soldiers' families, and outside it none (F009-5) [X-009]. And from 1965 the district companies made PF deserters who had taken the territorial course their squad leaders, so that the course began, in a few hundred men, to train its own enemy (F009-24). That is the counter-directional finding of this memorandum, and the first phase is where it starts.

### Causal chains

The phase's chains begin here and land later: C009-2 (the trained soldier's first seconds to the mines of Dien Ban) and C009-1 (the drill sergeants held in 1964 to the self-defence groups of Can Duoc) are placed in Phases II and III, where their last links fall.

### Downstream

Phase II inherits an ARVN one band steadier than it would otherwise have been, though it is already at the cap there, so the gain appears as battalions not lost rather than in the band (POS007-1). It inherits a drill-sergeant corps about to begin repaying the battalions, a Front that has learned to attack what the course did not reach, and the first trained deserters in the Front's district companies. Phase III inherits the posts' first ten minutes in the towns, and the mobilization of 1968 inherits a training command that has already refused once to shorten the course.

## §4.P2 Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

<!-- chapter P2 -->

### Mechanism

Phase II is where the course's two effects separate. Its effect on the fighting is real and invisible, for the reason that memorandum 007 found: the enemy declines the better unit (F009-19). Its effect on the dead is real and visible, because a man who lives through a wound appears in the returns whether or not the enemy chose the fight. Of the two, the second is the one this headquarters can show, and the Ia Drang is where it shows it: about eight to twelve men alive at Albany on the morning of 18 November 1965 who died in the grass historically, about 180 American dead in the campaign rather than 190 (EV000-23) [X-009]. That is the rate, two to four percent of the American killed in action, and it holds whatever the enemy does, because no adaptation of his takes the tourniquet out of a trained man's pocket (F009-21).

The fighting gain is shown at Bau Bang on 21 May 1966, where the RF companies did everything the course taught them and nothing the course could not teach them (EV007-3). They came off their trucks, found the ditch, fired in pairs and put their squad guns into the treeline in two minutes, and they lost a fifth fewer men than on the course before this one; they were on the trucks in the first place because their sector commander rode them there. That is the bottleneck this memorandum predicted, moved from the private to the squad leader, the platoon and the officer who writes the order (F009-8). Across the ARVN I judge the ambush losses of 1966–67 down a tenth to a sixth beyond the rifles and the officers, about one in eight fewer battalions broken, and the strongest positive effect of this memorandum, the first government army that the Front cannot reliably break with its opening volley, at work in every fight the enemy accepts (F009-25) [X-009].

The phase also shows the limit DIRMT01-9 and DIRMT01-11 ask about, and it is a hard one. The devaluation of 18 June 1966 cut the private's rice by about a third by December, and the families of the 25th Division in Hau Nghia and Long An could not eat on his pay (EV009-3). Desertion rose as it had historically. The course touches none of its causes, and it adds one cost: the deserter carries his training home, and a few hundred a year in the Saigon belt carry it to the Front (F009-24). Colonel Hoan's answer, families inside the perimeter and the ration paid in rice, cut his division's desertion by a quarter, which is more than this provision did for the whole army; that is the measure of where desertion lived.

By region, as the standing directive asks: in the Delta, the trained territorial post and the casualty drill carry on what Phase I began, and the posts of Chuong Thien and Dinh Tuong hold their first ten minutes as Long Huu did. In the coastal lowlands of I and II Corps, the effect is the mine: Dien Ban and Dai Loc in Quang Nam and Son Tinh and Binh Son in Quang Ngai become the places where the enemy's answer to the trained soldier is written (EV009-2). In the Highlands, the course reaches the Montagnard recruits of the RF companies of Darlac, Pleiku and Kontum and taught them in Vietnamese, which told them that the army was Kinh (F009-23), and it does not reach the CIDG camps, which stay what the enemy attacks; FULRO's grievances are untouched by it. In the Saigon belt, the effect runs both ways: the board's battalions are trained and steadier, and the Cu Chi guerrillas are taught the tourniquet by a deserter (EV009-3).

### Enemy response and initiative

The enemy's response is the mine, and it is a reasoned one. The Quang Da Special Zone's command and the Military Region 5 Party Committee concluded at the end of 1965 that firefights with trained units cost them more than they gained, and made the mine and the booby trap the village guerrilla's principal work (EV009-2, F009-20). It cost them almost nothing: the explosive came from American duds and the Republic's grenades, the batteries from Go Vap, and the labour from guerrillas who were in the hamlets anyway (F009-21). It cost the Marines of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines about two-thirds of their dead in a year, and it cost the farmers of Dien Ban a third more mine casualties. It cost the Marines' relations with the hamlets, which the conduct block could not protect through a twelfth month of mines.

<!-- initiative -->
The enemy's own decision in this phase was taken in Hanoi, for reasons that had nothing to do with anything this headquarters did. On 19 March 1967 the Ministry of Defence established the Sapper Command as a separate branch of the People's Army, under the General Staff of Senior General Van Tien Dung, gathering the sapper battalions that had grown up in the regions since 1965 into a branch with its own schools, doctrine and commanders [H]. It was driven by the PAVN's own doctrine and its institutional history: the sapper had been the revolutionary army's answer to fortified positions since the war against the French, the regions' sapper units had been formed piecemeal and the General Staff wanted them under one doctrine, and the branch's advocates in the General Staff argued that the war against American bases, airfields and depots needed specialists the infantry regiments could not provide [H] [X-009]. This headquarters did not provoke it, and nothing in this memorandum makes it more or less likely. What it does is meet the trained soldier from the other side: the sapper's method, to reach the wire unseen and attack the sleeping post rather than the alert one, is the method that a soldier trained for the first ten minutes is least able to answer, because it is designed to give him no first ten minutes. From 1968 the sapper battalions are the units that attack the posts and the district compounds that the trained RF and PF hold against infantry, and the course has nothing to say to them.

The B-2 Front and COSVN drew a narrower lesson from Bau Bang and Route 13. The 272nd's notebook of January 1967 says it: the puppet territorials now fight from the ditch, and the regiment's ambushes must kill in the first volley or not be laid (EV007-3). That is a command that lays fewer ambushes and makes them heavier, which is consistent with the fighting gain being real and with its appearing as fights not fought.

### Causal chains

**C009-2** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV002-2, EV000-18) The trained ARVN and territorial soldier returns aimed fire in the first seconds and does not bunch up when hit, so the Front's local companies in the coastal lowlands lose more than they gain in firefights (F009-1, F009-2) → the Quang Da Special Zone's directive for 1966 makes mines and booby traps the village guerrilla's principal work and sets each platoon a monthly quota (EV009-2, F009-20) → in Dien Ban and Dai Loc the mine becomes the principal killer of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, about two-thirds of its dead in the year from September 1966, and civilian mine casualties at the Hoi An hospital rise by about a third (F009-7) → the Marines, losing men to devices in hamlets whose people know where they are, come to treat the villagers as the enemy's accomplices, and the conduct the basic course taught and the relationship Major Haney built with the PF platoons since 1965 do not survive the year, so that a provision that made the soldier better in the firefight made the American presence worse in the hamlet.

**C009-3** [months] (begins in Phase II, EV009-3) The devaluation of 18 June 1966 cuts what an ARVN private's pay buys in rice by about a third by December, and the families of the 25th Division in Hau Nghia and Long An cannot eat on it → desertion rises on its historical course, and every deserter of the 25th Division's battalions has passed the ten-week course at Quang Trung (F009-18) → a few hundred of them reach the guerrilla platoons of Cu Chi and Duc Hoa, and one teaches the Cu Chi district's squad leaders a three-week course on firing in pairs, bounding and the tourniquet (F009-24) → the Front's village platoons around Saigon go into 1967 and Tet somewhat better at the first minute of a fight than history's, so that the Republic's training reached its enemy through the soldier's pay and not through the battlefield.

### Downstream

Phase III inherits an army trained to the man and short of squad leaders, a drill-sergeant corps repaying the battalions with 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year, a Front that has moved to mines and sappers in the lowlands and to the untrained target everywhere, and a Sapper Command that will reach the district compounds after Tet. It inherits about a dozen Marines from Dien Ban alive and without legs, and the first of the amputees in the RVNAF's hospitals who would have died on the historical course (F009-16). The 1968 mobilization inherits a training base that has the capacity, and the habit, to refuse a short course.

## §4.P3 Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

<!-- chapter P3 -->

### Mechanism

Tet turns on the decisions it turned on historically, and the basic course changes it in the margins that the rifles and the officers had already opened, and in one they had not. On the compound walls of the district towns the RF companies fire as the territorial course taught, in pairs and with ammunition kept; at Cai Lay the 311th RF Company still has a third of its basic load at 0600 (EV000-42). In the night after the first assaults, and through the month of Hue, the wounded of both allied armies are alive in the morning in larger numbers. That is the second dated battle in which this memorandum's rate is shown at work, as the standing directive requires: about 150 fewer ARVN dead in the first wave and 30–60 fewer American, against about eight to twelve at Albany, the same rate in a battle of a different kind (EV000-23, EV000-42, DIRCP01-2) [X-009]. The strongest positive effect is in those numbers (F009-25).

The mobilization is the phase's real change, and it has a cost this memorandum must state at its weight (EV000-51). On the historical course the RVNAF sent some of its 1968 draftees to units untrained and cut its course from twelve weeks to nine. In this war the Joint General Staff ordered six; the training command held ten by running two shifts, and about 40,000 men waited at the centres for a cycle. The new battalions and territorial companies reached the field about a month after history's, in September and October rather than August, after the third offensive had begun. For those weeks the districts around Saigon had fewer new territorials than history's and better ones afterwards. I judge the trade right, because an untrained PF platoon in a hamlet is a supply of rifles for the Front, but it was paid in the self-defence hamlets of Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong in August and September (EV009-4).

The sergeants are the other half of the mobilization, and they show this memorandum's integration finding at work (F009-4). The drill sergeants the army could not spare in 1964–65 had been coming back to it since 1966, and in July 1968 about 1,100 more went from the centres' second shift to the new territorial platoons. The gap memorandum 007 left, a third of the new PF platoons without a man who had held the rank, closed to a fifth [X-009].

### Enemy response and initiative

COSVN's Military Affairs Committee planned the August offensive after two waves that had broken on the district towns' walls, and it aimed it at the People's Self-Defense Force, the one armed body in the hamlets that no course had reached (EV009-4, F009-6). That was a reaction, and a correct one: in Duc Hue and Trang Bang it overran about two-thirds of the groups it attacked and took 1,500–2,000 old rifles across three provinces, while the RF and PF posts were attacked about a third less often than historically. Its subregion commands around Saigon read a second purpose into it, that every group destroyed told the hamlet the government's law armed it without protecting it. Where a trained PF platoon was near enough to answer the group's flare, as at Rach Kien on 2–3 September, the attack failed, and the Front's 506th Battalion learned to strike groups more than a kilometre from a post.

The sapper battalions of the new branch began to appear against the district compounds in the May and August offensives, attacking at the hours before dawn when the trained garrison was asleep, and the course had no answer to them; that is the cost of Hanoi's decision of March 1967, met on its own timeline.

### Causal chains

**C009-1** [months] (begins in Phase I, EV009-1) The training command holds 2,000–3,000 of the army's best sergeants at the centres in 1964–65 and refuses the Joint General Staff's request for a third of them back (F009-11) → from 1966 the drill-sergeant circle returns 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year to the battalions, each a man who has trained recruits for two years (F009-4) → in July 1968 the training command releases about 1,100 more to the mobilization's new PF platoons, and the share without a man who has held the rank falls from about a third to about a fifth (EV000-51) → the district chief of Can Duoc sends those returned drill sergeants to teach the People's Self-Defense Force of his district the first ten minutes on their own evenings, and his is the only district of three provinces where the self-defence groups hold through the third offensive (EV009-4).

**C009-4** [weeks] (begins in Phase III, EV000-51) The Joint General Staff orders the basic course cut to six weeks for the mobilization on 21 June 1968, and the training command holds ten by running the 33 centres in two shifts (EC009-7) → about 40,000 inductees wait three to six weeks at the centres and the new RF companies and PF platoons reach their districts in September and October rather than August → COSVN's August offensive, finding the new territorials not yet in the hamlets and the old posts holding, goes for the self-defence groups armed with the Garands of 1950 → about 600–900 self-defence members die in Hau Nghia, Long An and Dinh Tuong in two months, the price in the hamlets of a decision at Quang Trung to train the army's new men properly (EV009-4).

**Chain review (DIRCP01-1).** Memorandum 007's C007-3 predicted that the field commissions of 1966–68 would empty the pool of sergeants, so that a third of the mobilization's new PF platoons would have no one who had held the rank. In the war as now written it resolves differently. The commissions still take their 5,500 sergeants, but the drill-sergeant corps this memorandum adds has been refilling the pool from 1966, and the gap is a fifth rather than a third (EV000-51). The chain's first three links hold; its terminal link is changed by a provision it could not have foreseen, which is what a chain review should sometimes find.

### Downstream

Phase IV inherits an RVNAF of about 900,000 trained men, its new territorials trained and a month late, and a People's Self-Defense Force that the course never reached and the third offensive bled. It inherits, in the hospitals at Cong Hoa and in the provinces, tens of thousands of RVNAF soldiers who would have died on the historical course and live, a third to a half of them amputees, whose grievance will reach the streets of Saigon with the other veterans' grievances of 1970 (F009-16) [X-009]. It inherits a Front whose village platoons around Saigon have been taught some of the course by deserters, and a Sapper Command that the course cannot answer.

### Clock

The clock in 1968 is spent by Tet and by the 14,592 American dead of the year, and this memorandum moves it by a sum it cannot show. It saves perhaps 300–600 American lives in 1968, at its rate, and about 900–1,900 over the war [X-009]. That is fewer funerals in American towns, one family at a time, and it does not touch the shock of 31 January, the request for 206,000 men or the decision of 31 March, which were made on the scale of the casualties and the gap between the reports and the offensive (POSTNA-5). Against it, the 2026 course failed about 12–18 percent of the Project 100,000 intake, and the draft calls rose by perhaps 5,000–15,000 men a year to replace them, a small addition to the domestic engine of the clock (F009-22) [X-009]. The two roughly cancel in the politics of 1968. The two weeks the course added to the Army's pipeline slowed the build-up of 1965–66 by two to three months, which was invisible to the public and visible to Westmoreland (F009-12). The clock is unchanged by a band.

## §4.P4 Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

<!-- null P4 -->
The basic course does not bite on Phase IV because the phase was decided by the American withdrawal, the palace's capture of the institutions in 1970–71, and the corps commanders' plans for Cambodia and Laos, and no private's training reaches any of them. Lam Son 719 failed for reasons decided at corps and in Saigon (EV000-71). The course's effects run on in the casualty returns and the posts, within the band, and the historical army's training improved from 1969 under the Central Training Command, narrowing the gap. Phase III's end vector is unchanged by this memorandum, so nothing arrives to answer.

## §4.P5 Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

<!-- null P5 -->
The Easter Offensive was decided at Quang Tri by the contradictory orders of the corps and the division, and at An Loc and Kontum by the B-52J and the coordinate cell (EV000-76, EV000-77, EV000-78). Trained soldiers hold in both places with more of their wounded alive, and change neither outcome nor the PAVN's losses, which the measured sieges decided (DIRMT01-6). The course does not reach the commanders who lost Quang Tri, and the countryside of northern Binh Dinh fell to a PAVN division, not to the territorials' first ten minutes. No incoming band has moved.

## §4.P6 Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

<!-- null P6 -->
The collapse of 1975 was decided by the palace's orders of 13–14 March, the corps dissolving toward their families, and Hanoi's calendar against the rains; the basic course reaches none of the three (POSMT01-6, POSMT01-3). In 1974 trained soldiers with tourniquets lose fewer of their wounded in the attrition the ration imposes, perhaps 1,000–2,000 lives over the year at this memorandum's rate, and the battles go as written, because the PAVN chose them (EV000-91). The course teaches no soldier to leave his family on Route 7. No incoming band has moved.

## §4.H Historical comparison

Against history, the war as now written differs in men more than in events. About 5,000–14,000 American and South Vietnamese soldiers live who died on the historical course: about 900–1,900 Americans over 1965–72, shown at work at Albany and in the first wave of Tet, and about 4,000–12,000 South Vietnamese over 1964–75, shown at Thuan Loi and in the streets of the Delta towns [X-009]. They are a few percent of each army's dead, and that is honest; they are also several thousand men, which is equally honest. Many of the South Vietnamese live without a limb.

The ARVN of 1964–65 is a band steadier than history's on this memorandum and the two before it, and from 1966 its battalions lose a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still lays, with about one in eight fewer broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms alone left [X-009]. No battle of the war changes its outcome for it. The territorial posts of the held belt hold their first ten minutes about twice as often as they fell before (nineteen in twenty against nine in ten), which in the Delta and around Saigon means perhaps a few hundred posts and their platoons kept over the war, and in the coastal lowlands and the Highlands much less, because the enemy there fought with mines and with divisions rather than at the walls [X-009].

The enemy is not weaker for any of it. He moved to the mine in Quang Nam and Quang Ngai, to the self-defence hamlet around Saigon, and to the sapper everywhere, and a few hundred trained deserters a year gave his village platoons around Saigon some of the course. The mobilization of 1968 was a month later and better; the self-defence groups paid for the month. The Republic falls on the historical date.

By region: in the Delta the effect is the post (Long Huu, Cai Lay); in the coastal lowlands it is the mine and what it did to the Marines' relations with the hamlets (Dien Ban); in the Highlands it is small, the RF companies' Montagnards taught in a language that told them whose army it was, and the CIDG camps untouched; and in the Saigon belt it is the self-defence hamlets of 1968 and the deserters of Cu Chi.

## §4.N Net assessment and end state

The rung stays at (a), collapse, and this memorandum gives it no reason to move. The ladder's next rung needs a Republic whose state survives the withdrawal of American combat forces for longer than history's two years, and the basic course does not reach any of the mechanisms that decided that survival. It does not give the Republic an air arm it owns, it does not survive the 1974 appropriation any better than the force did, and it does not choose the corps commanders (POSMT01-1). A better private in an army with the historical generals and the historical aid vote is still in that army.

**The date of the fall, tested (DIRMT01-1).** I estimate the fall at 30 April 1975 within a window of about 27 April to the first days of May, as before. The mechanisms in the cumulative force that pull it later are the ones meta-checkpoint 01 named, the better battalions at Duc Duc, Xuan Loc, Phan Rang and Cu Chi, the PAVN's greener divisions, the stronger held belt and, from memorandum 008, the domestic ammunition that stretched the last aid dollar; this memorandum adds trained privates who live through their wounds and hold their first ten minutes, which act in exactly the fights the PAVN bypassed in 1975. The mechanisms that pull it earlier are a President more certain his army could not hold alone and an army more dependent on fire it no longer had; this memorandum adds nothing to either, except that the families the soldiers went to in March were no better fed (EV009-3). The palace's orders and Hanoi's calendar dominate, as before.

**The critical path (DIRMT01-7).** Tested against the palace's orders of 13–14 March, the 1st Corps' march against the rains and Minh's surrender, the basic course reaches none: the orders were taken in the palace, the march was the PAVN's, and the surrender was the President's. The collapse of I and II Corps came from families on the roads and contradictory orders, and a trained private goes to his family as surely as an untrained one (POSMT01-6). The delay it could buy is hours at Xuan Loc or Phan Rang, not the three weeks that would have put the assault into the rains.

**Running sums (DIRMT01-2).** American lives saved by all provisions: about 3,400–6,900, the meta-checkpoint's 2,500–5,000 plus this memorandum's 900–1,900, a judgment resting on rates (two to four percent of killed in action for this memorandum's tourniquet), shown now at Albany and in the first wave of Tet; it is six to twelve percent of the historical dead and does not cross a band, because tolerance was spent by casualties of a scale and by Tet (POSMT01-5) [X-009]. RVNAF lives saved by this memorandum: about 4,000–12,000, two to five percent of the RVNAF's historical dead, below a band, shown at Thuan Loi and in the Delta towns at Tet [X-009]. ARVN battalions not lost: memorandum 007's third fewer broken in 1966–67, and this memorandum's further one in eight, within a band that is already at its cap [X-007] [X-009]. The 1974 appropriation: nothing in this memorandum changes what the Republic asked Congress to pay for or what Congress voted; a better-trained army needs the same ammunition, and the vote stands as history's (POS008-3).

**State of the Republic at the end of 1974 (DIRMT01-3).** Phase VI is not revised, but for the record: at the end of 1974 the RVNAF is trained to the man, its posts in the held belt hold better and its wounded live more often, and it is short of ammunition, fuel and pay exactly as before; the course has made the attrition of 1974 somewhat cheaper in lives and has not changed one position the PAVN chose to take.

No phase redraw is proposed. The war's shape has not changed.

## §6 Changed / not changed

### What has changed

- **The wounded live.** About 5,000–14,000 American and South Vietnamese soldiers who died on the historical course are alive, because every soldier of both armies was taught the tourniquet in basic training; shown at Thuan Loi in June 1965, at Albany in November 1965 and in the first wave of Tet [X-009]. It is the largest single effect of the provision and no adaptation of the enemy's touches it.
- **The first minute.** The ARVN and the territorial forces are the first government army in this war that the Front cannot reliably break with its opening volley: the ARVN a band steadier in 1964–65, its ambush losses down a tenth to a sixth in 1966–67, and the territorial post holding its first ten minutes nineteen times in twenty (F009-25, EV002-2, EV007-3).
- **Nobody joins untrained.** The untrained draftee sent straight to his unit is gone from the war, enforced by a number on the muster roll, and the mobilization of 1968 is trained rather than rushed (EV009-1, EV000-51).
- **The sergeants come back.** The drill-sergeant corps the army could not spare in 1964–65 repays it from 1966, and the sergeant gap memorandum 007's field commissions opened in 1968 is a fifth rather than a third (EV000-51, C009-1).
- **The enemy's war moved.** In the coastal lowlands the mine became the principal killer of American Marines and of farmers; around Saigon the third offensive went for the self-defence groups; and a few hundred trained deserters a year taught the Front's village platoons some of the course (EV009-2, EV009-3, EV009-4).

### What has not changed

- **Who chooses the battle.** The enemy declined the trained unit and attacked the one beside it, as he had declined the rearmed and the well-led ones; the fighting gain appears as fights not fought (F009-19).
- **The order above the private.** The RF convoy at Bau Bang rode its trucks because its sector commander put it there; the 1st Battalion, 7th Regiment landed where its division told it; the corps commanders of 1972 and 1975 were chosen as before (EV007-3, EV000-18).
- **Desertion and the family.** Men deserted for pay, rice and family, which the course does not touch; the devaluation of 1966 and the roads of March 1975 show it (EV009-3, POSMT01-6).
- **Unit training and the squad leader.** The battalion's collective training, historically almost nonexistent, is untouched; the binding constraint has moved from the private to the squad leader and the platoon (F009-8).
- **The clock and the end.** American tolerance, the 1974 appropriation, the critical path of 1975 and the date of the fall are as before. Saigon falls on 30 April 1975, at rung (a).
