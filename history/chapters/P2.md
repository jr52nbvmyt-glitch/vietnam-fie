# Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US strength rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967 [H40]. The big operations win their battles and do not change the war: the Ia Drang, Masher/White Wing, Hastings, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City each kill men the reports exaggerate and are followed by the enemy's return, because he decides when to fight and withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. In Saigon the junta survives the Buddhist uprising and wins an election with a third of the vote; pacification is reorganised under CORDS in May 1967; and in Hanoi the militants around Le Duan plan a general offensive for Tet.

Beneath the main-force war runs the one the provisions changed most, the war for the held belt. The land programme leaves the phase with about 820,000 hectares titled to about 640,000 families (EV001-6) [X-001]. With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages, chiefs who stay, district centres everywhere and Delta's raids, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it, and the committees go to govern from the base areas and from across the border by courier (EVR05-4). The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its legal cadres into the towns (EV002-3), and where the method is pushed into villages the government does not hold at night it becomes the historical Phoenix, three years early (EV006-4). None of this alters the main-force war, fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what that war is fought over.

The enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch, the gunships and Delta's road teams count the Trail's trucks from December 1965, and the B-52J cuts every bridge it can measure from April 1966; the enemy answers with fords and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before (EVR05-5, EV005-2). Sihanoukville is measured by March 1967 as the source of most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4). The northern campaign is the theater's own: the restrike watch keeps the Red River crossings closed more days than not while the cell's monthly kills fall from about 140 to 35, there is no Christmas pause and no POL strike, and this command reports in October 1966 that the North's war in the South needs none of what is being destroyed (EV013-3, EVCP02-4) [X-013].

The armies are better in the fights the enemy grants and get no more of them. Every infantryman carries a modern carbine and every squad a belt-fed gun; the Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle (EV000-23, EVCP02-3). The ARVN's battalions are filled from the board's list, its sergeants schooled and its privates trained, and the enemy does what he does against every better unit: he declines to fight it and lays his ambush for the one beside it (EV007-3). He moves his war in the lowlands south of Da Nang to the mine, which no course teaches against (EV009-2), and the devaluation of 1966 sends trained privates home to families their pay no longer feeds (EV007-4). The radio puts every post on the district net, and the Front answers by ambushing the company that answers the call, until the districts learn to answer with guns and gunships rather than trucks (EV008-4).

## EV002-3 · The war on the district chiefs · Aug 1965 – Jun 1966 · The Delta, the provinces around Saigon and the coastal Centre · NEW · 016
*Initiative: enemy*

By the summer of 1965 the Front's provincial committees reported that the list-appointed chiefs had stayed through three governments, and COSVN concluded in August that the chiefs who kept their Popular Forces in the hamlets at night were its most dangerous enemies and were to be killed (F002-12) [X-002]. Between August 1965 and June 1966 nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs died, sixteen of them from the list. On 14 November Major Tran Van Sang of Mo Cay, whom the Kien Hoa committee called "the most harmful of the puppet district chiefs in the province", was killed by a command-detonated mine on the road to Ben Tre [X-002].

Four of the twenty-one were not mined but shot in their own houses at night, and the pistols that killed them were the Republic's. On the night of 2–3 March 1966 three men of the Dinh Tuong province security section crossed the canal behind the district chief's house at Cai Be, where Captain Le Quang Hien had slept since his appointment in 1964 with two PF guards on the gate and none at the back [X-016]. Two of the three carried Glocks taken from the Long Dinh post in October 1964, and one of the two still had a light that worked, on cells taken from a district supply point in January (F016-14) [X-016]. The leader switched it on in the bedroom doorway for the time it took to see which of the two shapes was the captain and which his wife, and fired four rounds; the second man killed the guard who came round the house at the shots, and the three were across the canal before the PF on the gate understood that the shooting was inside [X-016]. The three other chiefs killed this way, at Duc Hue, Can Duoc and Mo Duc, were shot with pistols too, two of them with Glocks [X-016].

The Republic's best pistol in its enemy's hands changed the manner of these four deaths and not their number. The rate was set by COSVN's decision and by what the security sections could learn of a chief's house and his habits, not by their weapons: a section that could reach Hien's bedroom door could have killed him with a K-54, and the Front never killed chiefs faster than it could find them (F016-14) [X-016]. The killings did not empty the districts, and that was the reform's quiet gain: a dead chief's deputy acted at once, the Board sent a ranked list for the vacancy within about three weeks, and Mo Cay had a new chief on 6 December and Cai Be on 24 March [X-002] [X-016]. The chiefs learned to travel with escorts and vary their roads, and after Cai Be the province chiefs put a guard inside every chief's house at night with orders to keep his own light off until he fired; the Special Branch traced five of the twenty-one attacks within the year, Cai Be among them through the serial of a Glock taken from a courier in May, and the Front never killed chiefs at that rate again (F002-8, F004-14) [X-002] [X-016].

## EV013-3 · The restrike watch on the Red River · 1 Aug 1965 – 31 Dec 1966 · The Paul Doumer, Long Bien and Chuong Duong crossings at Hanoi; the Canal des Rapides and Bac Giang bridges on the northeast railway; the ferries at Phu Ly and Ninh Binh; the MACV Combined Targeting Cell, Tan Son Nhut · NEW · 013,R05
*Initiative: both*

From August 1965 the Combined Targeting Cell ran the northern campaign on one list of about 300 targets and a daily repair list, and the Red River crossings at Hanoi were its pattern [X-013]. The North did not rebuild the Paul Doumer bridge under the watch; by June the Hanoi bridge company and the Chinese 1st Railway Engineer Division had a pontoon bridge 3 km downstream, floated by night and broken up by day, and a rail ferry at Chuong Duong. The U-2S's dawn pass found each crossing in use and each repair, the cell's midday list ordered a J cell for the night or an F-105D package for the morning, and between August 1965 and December 1966 the crossings were struck 61 times and impassable to rail on about 290 of 517 days [X-013]. The pontoons were what the J could not solve, because they were targets only for the hours they were assembled, and the North kept three sets for every one in the river.

What the watch measured was the North spending labour to keep moving: about 2,000 men a night and a Chinese engineer regiment to carry 40–60 percent of the trains of February 1965 [X-013]. The cell's monthly count of targets destroyed rose to about 140 in the summer of 1965 and fell to about 35 by December 1966, as the North stopped owning anything else worth a GBU-31. In October 1966 J-2 sent Washington, beside those figures, the Gulf watch's count of coastal traffic south of Thanh Hoa and the CIA's estimate of the North's imports; neither had fallen. Westmoreland's covering note said that the campaign was destroying everything in the North that could be destroyed, and that the North's war in the South needed none of it (F013-7, F013-16) [X-013].

## EVR05-4 · The committees driven out: FOLLOW THROUGH, NIGHT LANTERN and the empty beds · 2 Aug 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · Hoc Mon, Cu Chi and Trang Bang; Can Giuoc and Can Duoc, Long An; Cai Lay, Cai Be and Giao Duc, Dinh Tuong; Memot, Cambodia; the towns of the Delta · NEW · R05
*Initiative: both*

Three operations in the held belt, by the task force, the province and Delta, did the same thing in three places, and together they are the story of what happened to the Front's district government before Tet: it was driven out of the villages it governed and went on governing them from further away. The first followed the Hoc Mon committee to Cu Chi. On 2 August 1965 the task force's III Corps task group, now about 420 strong under Major Garvin and Lieutenant Colonel Phuoc, opened FOLLOW THROUGH against the committee's survivors and the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's finance section in the hamlets along the Saigon River (EC012-7) [X-012]. For two months it took only couriers, each of whom knew only the next courier's name. On 14 October a cashier of twenty-three was arrested at a Hoc Mon market stall with 41,000 piasters in a rice sack and rallied against a named offer that her mother would not be detained (EC012-10). On the night of 19–20 October two SEAL platoons and the Gia Dinh PRU took the section's deputy chief in a tunnel under a family altar in Phu Hoa Dong, with the ledger for nine months of 1965, while a 25th Division company tasked against its division's plan held the land side (F012-6) [X-012]. The ledger named the collectors of four districts: 64 arrests followed by March 1966, and 17 killed in raids, four of whom the Commission's audit could not show to be cadres (F012-16) [X-012]. The committee's secretary, Nguyen Van Tu, went to the Ho Bo woods in January as the US 25th Division arrived at Cu Chi, and the region's collections from the posted villages of Hoc Mon and Go Vap in 1966 were about an eighth of 1964's (F012-8, F012-27) [X-012].

The second was Long An's own. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings, about 400 families' titles were still held at the district office. Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, the province chief, and Major Le Cong Phuong, the district chief, both from the list, built NIGHT LANTERN in the 14 villages between Route 5 and the Vam Co around the settled post, the census and the folder, with eight RF companies, 26 PF platoons raised in the villages, two Police Field Force companies, 140 constables, a gunship on call at night, and Lan's rule that no post was settled until its platoon slept in the hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour (EC002-1, EC002-2) [X-002]. The posts went in from 15 November. On 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, where the killings had begun; the platoon and the constables held for three hours under the gunship until an RF company came down the canal, and the 506th left 38 dead [X-002]. On 4 February a night check found a young man whose card did not match his booklet, and within a week his courier route had led to the chief of the district committee's security section, whose notebook the district centre matched against the census, 17 of 22 courier stations to their households (EC006-4) [X-006]. On 24–25 February the PRU took the committee's deputy secretary and finance chief at the Rung Sat's edge, where the committee had moved believing itself beyond the posts; the deputy secretary rallied and drew the chart of the province committee above him (EC006-2) [X-006]. By 30 April all 14 posts held, 212 had been arrested and 71 released, 64 had rallied to the police posts, the committee's first-quarter collections were a third of 1965's, and the 400 titles were handed to their families in their hamlets [X-002]. Captain Whitcomb, at the end of his eighteen months, reported that the committee's arrest and not a battalion's sweep had broken the tax, and that the families had asked him, not the district chief, whether the arrests would last (F002-1, F006-26) [X-006].

Delta was the third. By 1966 it had found its rate: 38 raids a month in 1966 and 44 in 1967, 171 and then 126 listed cadre of district rank or above taken, 61 of them province or region committee members, and nine finance sections with their ledgers, four raids in five in III and IV Corps, because that is where the files were (F014-5) [X-014]. It paid in the currency it could not print: fourteen operators killed, four missing and fifty-one wounded by the end of 1967, and a quarter of its assault troops selected after the war began (F014-13) [X-014]. In September 1967 the National Fusion Centre gave the Can Tho squadron twenty-six addresses in upper Dinh Tuong, the best file in the Delta. Twenty-three raids between 4 September and 14 November took two people. The other twenty-one found a bed that had been slept in and a family that said, truthfully, that the man had not been there for months [X-014]. The courier taken on 19 September told why. The province committee had lived since March at Memot, across the Cambodian border, and came into the province for four or five nights every second month under a COSVN circular of April 1967 in three sentences: senior cadre do not sleep twice in a hamlet; committees meet in the base areas or across the border; couriers carry nothing written (F014-20) [X-014].

The raid yield fell by two-fifths, and so did the Special Branch's arrests in the held belt, to about 40 percent of their 1966 peak, with the district centres' neutralizations of district cadre falling from about 140 a quarter to about 50 (F006-18) [X-006]. Komer proposed monthly arrest targets in February 1967; this headquarters answered on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working, Khoa and Hieu opposed the targets on the record, and no centre was given one in 1967 (F002-11, EC002-9) [X-002]. The measures the enemy could not fake agreed. The committees of the held belt were running their districts by courier from the Ho Bo woods, the Rung Sat and Memot at two months' remove, with collections at a sixth to an eighth of 1964's, and the falling yield was the measure of that success (F014-19) [X-014]. The Front did not pay in people. It paid in walking time, which against the best three hundred operators of the American army was the cheapest answer any provision drew from it (F014-21). And the legal cadres who left the villages settled in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the edges of Saigon among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000 (F002-2) [X-002].

## EVCP01-4 · The big-unit war: Starlite, A Shau, Hastings, Long Tan, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City · 18 Aug 1965 – 14 May 1967 · The Van Tuong peninsula and the DMZ in I Corps; the A Shau valley; Phuoc Tuy; War Zone C and the Iron Triangle in III Corps · HIST-ALT · CP01,R05
*Initiative: both*

The big-unit battles of 1965–67 were each won on the ground, each followed by the enemy's return, and none changed the war. On 9–10 March 1966 the 95B Regiment overran the A Shau camp under cloud, and the valley became a PAVN base for the rest of the war [H29]. In Hastings in July the 3rd Marine Division and Ngo Quang Truong's 1st Division drove the 324B Division back across the DMZ, and drew the Marines north, away from the coastal hamlets [H31]. On 18 August D Company, 6th Royal Australian Regiment held the Long Tan rubber for three hours against the 275th Regiment with the guns of Nui Dat behind it, losing 18 killed [H32]. Attleboro grew from a brigade's shakedown into a corps battle against the 9th Division at a cost of 155 American dead [H33]. In 1967 Cedar Falls emptied Ben Suc and the Iron Triangle, which was a staging area again a year later, and Junction City sent two divisions and three brigades into War Zone C, where the enemy attacked American firebases five times at night or at dawn, at Prek Klok, Ap Bau Bang, Suoi Tre and Ap Gu, and lost heavily each time; the Americans lost 282 killed, COSVN moved, and the 9th Division withdrew into Cambodia [H34] [H35].

The American battalions here, and the ARVN 1st Division at Hastings, carried the M4A1 and the M249; the Australians did not. In Junction City the Ghostriders of the 4th Squadron were over the night attacks, as they were over every allied position attacked at night in III Corps from 1964: at Suoi Tre and Ap Gu they lit the treelines and fired on the assault waves forming beyond the wire, and at Prek Klok they found the mortar positions [X-R05]. I do not adjust the historical returns for the rifles or the gunships. In each of these battles the fight was decided at the wire and by who attacked whom, where and when, and the enemy's losses were never counted closely enough for the difference to show (POS004-1) [X-CP01]. What they proved here is what they proved historically: the enemy lost heavily when he attacked an American position, and he decided for himself whether to do so.

## EV007-2 · The command lists and the thirty-day reviews · 1 Sep 1965 – 30 Jun 1966 · The Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters; the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · NEW · 007,R05
*Initiative: allied*

The board's second season changed the army's battalions faster than anything since 1955. Its panels selected about 230 battalion and regimental commanders, and by June 1966 about three-quarters of the battalions were under officers the board had chosen, mostly majors of the classes of 1957–59, for eighteen-month commands against a historical median under a year; the captain-commanded battalion fell from a third of the army to about one in ten by the end of 1966 [X-007]. The board relieved 27 battalion and 3 regimental commanders for cause after thirty-day reviews, mostly for what the courses taught against: a convoy moved without route security, a night position without listening posts, a fire plan never made. Each finding went to every battalion commander in a monthly digest called Lessons of Command [X-007]. About 600 sergeants were commissioned in 1966, most of them farmers' sons who would never have passed the diploma bar (F007-22). Major Bui Quang Trieu took the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment from the September list.

## EV008-3 · The Da Nhim line · 3 Oct 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The 230 kV line from the Da Nhim station at Don Duong, Tuyen Duc Province, through Di Linh in Lam Dong and Long Khanh to the Thu Duc and Bien Hoa substations · NEW · 008,R05
*Initiative: enemy*

The Bien Hoa melt shop drew about half its power down 250 kilometres of line from the Da Nhim hydro station, and on the night of 3 October 1965 sappers of the Front's Region 6 dropped two of its towers east of Di Linh; the region had chosen the line because it was the one target whose loss was felt in Saigon and which could be reached without fighting anybody (F008-4) [X-008]. The Bien Hoa shops built replacement towers in sets of four a week, and a dropped tower was back in four to ten days rather than the six to eight weeks of one shipped from Japan. The power company paid the Koho villages along the line a wage to watch it and gave each a hamlet radio, the first government wage or radio those hamlets had had (EC008-9) [X-008]. The Front dropped about forty towers in 1966 and twenty-five in 1967, and the steelworks lost a fifth and then a tenth of its hours, rolling 180,000 and 220,000 tonnes against a rated 200,000–300,000; the arsenal, on the Thu Duc station, lost nothing [X-008]. The Republic got from the line, without meaning to, thirty Koho villages that took its wage, carried its radios and told the sector when the Front came through.

## EV000-23 · Plei Me and the Ia Drang · 19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,007,009,015,R05
*Initiative: enemy*

On 19 October 1965 Major General Chu Huy Man's B3 Front attacked the camp at Plei Me and ambushed the relief by day; the 33rd Regiment's night assaults on the camp were met by Ghostriders from Nha Trang within about forty minutes, and its third, on the southern wall, was broken in the open [X-015]. The PAVN withdrew toward the Chu Pong, and the newly arrived 1st Cavalry Division followed. On 14 November Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore's 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry landed at X-Ray, in the 66th Regiment's assembly area, and held for three days with massed artillery, tactical air and the first tactical B-52 strikes [H27]. The troopers carried M4A1s with two M249s in each squad; two gunships held the night of 14–15 November over the perimeter, and the 7th Battalion, 66th Regiment, found forming on the slope at 0400, came in thinner at dawn [X-015].

On 17 November Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade's 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, marching in column through tall grass to Albany, was ambushed by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd, who grabbed the column by the belt where artillery and air could not reach [H27]. The head of the column formed a perimeter in the trees within minutes and held all afternoon. Behind it the companies broke into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, except two whose captains, of the educated classes of 1959–62, had put flank security into the grass on their own authority when the column halted; their men were found in perimeters rather than knots (EC004-4) [X-007]. Through the night the troopers in the grass put tourniquets on each other and on themselves, as the basic course had taught them, while the gunship overhead lit the field and fired on the PAVN search parties wherever the strobes in American helmets showed them more than 100 metres from any trooper (F015-12) [X-015].

The campaign's American dead were about 165–170 against 237 historically. The squad guns account for about 30 of the difference, the companies that kept their security about 15, the tourniquets about 10–15 and the gunships about 10–20, most of them at Albany, which remained the worst American loss of a single day so far (F009-22) [X-015]. Moore's count of 634 PAVN dead and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record, with the same caution [H27]. Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success and drew the lesson that confirmed their strategy. The B3 Front's study added what the historical one did not need: the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and the aircraft "that sees in the dark" made the night assault as costly as the day, so the belt-grabbing attack must be made at closer range, under cloud or in the forest, or not at all (F004-10) [X-015]. McNamara advised the President at the end of November that even doubling US forces might produce only a stalemate at a higher level [H].

## EVR05-5 · The panhandle watch: STEADY COUNT, NIGHT LEDGER and the road teams · 6 Dec 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The Laotian panhandle from the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes to Tchepone and the Ban Laboy ford; Ubon, Thailand; the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut · NEW · R05
*Initiative: allied*

Three instruments watched the Trail from December 1965, and their counts told one story. On 6 December, the day Tiger Hound opened, the Global Hawk orbit moved to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the panhandle, out of reach of the guns, and its radar watched Route 23 from Mu Gia, Routes 911 and 912 from Ban Karai and the fords down to Tchepone through the morning cloud on the passes (EC003-5) [M003]. The same night the sixteen Ghostriders of the 17th Air Commando Squadron, based at Ubon on a Thai condition that nothing be said of it, flew their first armed reconnaissance on its tracks, in an operation named NIGHT LEDGER because every engagement was filmed and counted against the radar (F015-18) [X-015]. From October two to four Delta teams of five were on the ground, under Ambassador Sullivan's limits on depth and target, moving at night on goggles and measuring what the radar could not see under the trees (F014-20) [X-014].

The strike force reached about one track in six within forty minutes; the gunships, flying to the tracks themselves, engaged about one in three within twenty (F003-5) [X-015]. In the first dry season the pilots claimed about 560 trucks and the cell counted about 210, and the gunships' video counted 1,100–1,500 against crews' claims of 2,600 [X-003]. Southbound movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 at the March peak. On 12 April 1966 the B-52Js of HARD CROSSING cut the crossings at the foot of the pass, and about twenty hours later the orbit watched the first trucks cross again on a ford reopened beside the broken bridges (EV005-2) [X-CP04]. The drivers learned the orbit's changeover hours and trellised the open stretches, Group 559 moved two battalions of 37mm guns to the crossings a year early, the convoys spread to intervals of five to ten minutes, and by the dry season of 1966–67 a third of the movements ran under canopy (F003-4, F015-6) [X-015]. On 2 March 1967 Ghostrider 22 was brought down by an unplotted 57mm battery at the Ban Laboy ford, with six of its nine crewmen lost [X-015]. Delta's teams completed 71 of 94 missions uncompromised and measured 84 truck parks, 31 gun positions and 19 storage points to a few metres, a third of them under canopy (F014-11) [X-014]. On 11–14 July 1967 Captain Robert L. Ainsley's team of five was lost whole near the Ban Laboy ford to a security battalion that had learned to hunt the watchers (F014-22).

MACV J-2 estimated from the tracks that 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle at the dry season's peak, more than the forces in the South needed from the North [X-003]. In 1966–67 the gunships counted 2,000–2,600 trucks destroyed or disabled, and the net loss to throughput rose by two to four points; Delta's cueing added one to four points more, inside the same width; the trucks were replaced from Soviet and Chinese deliveries within the year (POSTNA-4) [X-015]. It was the first time any headquarters had counted the Trail rather than estimating it, and the count said that the bombing raised the toll on the transit without lowering what arrived.

## EVCP02-4 · The air war over the North, 1966–67: no pause, no fuel, the buffer's edge, Bolo and Muscle Shoals · 24 Dec 1965 – Dec 1967 · Hanoi and Haiphong; the northeast railway and the frontier at Pingxiang; the DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand; Washington · HIST-ALT · CP02,013,R05
*Initiative: both*

The northern campaign of 1966–67 was measured at every step and changed nothing in the South. The 37-day pause of Christmas 1965 did not happen, nor the May halt and MAYFLOWER before it; Johnson sent his envoys to forty capitals with the fourteen points and words alone, and Hanoi answered with its four points as historically (F013-9) [X-013]. About 2,800 sorties flew over the North in the 37 days, and the season of free movement history gave the North cost it two to three weeks of the 1966 flow, made up by April [X-013]. Fulbright said in the February hearings that a peace offensive under bombs was a pretence, and the charge cost more in the press than in votes (F013-15).

The petroleum strikes of 29 June 1966 were not flown, because the tank farms had burned in March 1965 and the North's fuel had gone into drums under the trees; J-2 told CINCPAC in September 1965 that fuel was no longer a target system, and McNamara's doubts hardened a year before history's [X-013]. The Stennis hearings of 1967 were never held, because there were no restrictions to blame and the campaign's failure was this command's report, not the Pentagon's secret (F013-16) [X-013].

The buffer was tested once. On 12 May 1966 Captain James R. Ainsley's F-105D, hit by 57mm fire over Lang Son, ran north and he ejected inside the buffer; the Chinese gun crews of the 61st and 63rd Anti-Aircraft Divisions held him at Pingxiang for eleven days, and Beijing claimed he had been shot down over China [X-013]. The President considered widening the buffer to history's 25–30 miles for three days and did not, because Rusk judged that China would not fight for a railway in Vietnam; no aircraft was to cross the line in pursuit, and the cell kept its strikes 18 miles from the frontier (EC013-6) [X-013].

The MiG war was smaller and more dispersed than history's. The MiG-21s of 1966 lived at Mengzi and Ningming and flew into the North a sortie at a time (F013-6). In Operation Bolo on 2 January 1967 four rose to Robin Olds's F-4Cs from Noi Bai and three were shot down; the VPAF reached about 120 MiGs by the end of 1967, fighting from China [X-013]. The barrier began from STEADY COUNT's tracks, and Muscle Shoals laid its sensors where the radar's tracks ended, from 25 November 1967 [H43] [X-003].

## EV016-2 · The tunnel teams of Cu Chi and the Ho Bo woods · 8 Jan 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Cu Chi District, Hau Nghia Province; the Ho Bo and Boi Loi woods, the Filhol rubber and the Iron Triangle, Binh Duong Province · NEW · 016
*Initiative: allied*

The tunnels of Cu Chi were cleared in this war by the same small men who cleared them in history, and they went down with a light on the muzzle. The American tunnel teams began, as historically, in Operation Crimp in the Ho Bo woods in January 1966, where the Australian 3 Field Troop had gone down before them [M016·S5]. From that month the 25th Infantry Division at Cu Chi and the 1st Infantry Division kept tunnel squads of volunteers, men about 165 centimetres or shorter, each going down with a Glock 21, an M7 bayonet, a probe and a wire, and a second man at the entrance with a field telephone and a line (EC016-2) [M016·S5] [X-016].

What changed underground was the left hand. On 19 April 1966 Specialist Four Ramon Ortiz of the 25th Division's 65th Engineer Battalion went into a shaft under a haystack in Phu Hoa Dong with his light off, listened at the first bend for a minute and a half, and switched on for two seconds [X-016]. The beam put the end of the gallery four metres off and a man in it with a rifle, and Ortiz fired three rounds before the man's rifle came up; he could do it because his free left hand was flat on the floor and not holding a torch [X-016]. Behind the man were a document box and a second gallery with three wounded fighters of the Cu Chi district unit, who surrendered to the light. Ortiz's sergeant counted the gallery's contents by the same light: 140 kilograms of documents and a printing press [X-016].

The pistol did not change the rest of the tunnel war. The blast of a .45 underground deafened its firer as it had deafened history's men, the grenade on a tripwire at the second bend killed in the same way, and the Front answered within a season by defending fewer first bends and trapping more of them, and by leaving through the far exit when a team went down (F016-3) [M016·S5] [X-016]. Through Cedar Falls in January 1967 and into the clearing of the Ho Bo woods that summer, the teams went down about as often as history's and cleared about as much tunnel a day (EVCP01-4) [X-016]. They lost about a third fewer men underground in the two years, about 6–9 lives against history's toll, and brought out more prisoners alive from the first galleries in 1966 and fewer in 1967, when the first galleries were empty and wired (F016-2, F016-3) [X-016]. That is the whole of the change, at low confidence: a few more men alive at the end of a rope, and a few more of the enemy taken rather than killed in the dark.

## EV001-6 · Operation GOLDEN SHEAF · 10 Jan – 30 Jun 1966 · Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien Provinces, along the Xa No canal from Can Tho to Vi Thanh · NEW · 001,015,R05
*Initiative: allied*

GOLDEN SHEAF was the first operation of the war planned around the land programme rather than an enemy unit: the 21st Division, two Ranger battalions, about 40 RF companies, 24 titling teams and 6 muster teams carried the titling line west along the Xa No canal into about 60 villages of Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien in the dry season, with gunships over the posts at night [X-001]. A regiment cleared a block of villages by day; RF companies stayed in the hamlets overnight; the titling teams followed ten days behind, posting parcel numbers rather than names; and the muster teams raised a PF platoon in each titled hamlet from the families who had just received their land (EC001-2, EC001-3, EC001-4) [X-001]. A regiment moved on only when the last block was rated able to hold at night.

The enemy declined battle and withdrew toward the U Minh, leaving mines on the dikes. On 22–23 March two companies attacked an RF company and a new PF platoon at kilometre 28; they held four hours under a gunship and left 31 bodies [X-001]. The units reported about 690 enemy dead; the muster teams' audit, the first ever made of a division's body count, accepted 268 by body and weapon (F001-11) [X-001]. By 30 June about 46,000 hectares were titled to about 38,000 families in 112 hamlets, 74 of them held at night by their own forces and 38 by day only, and the operation stopped about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh at the line the territorials could hold, not at the limit of the titling teams, who sat idle in June (F001-4, F001-8) [X-001]. In the held hamlets the Front's collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn villages that had never sold rice at the government market at Phung Hiep sold there.

## EVCP02-3 · The frontier battles of 1966–67: Masher/White Wing, the Hill Fights, Con Thien and Dak To · 24 Jan 1966 – 23 Nov 1967 · The Bong Son plain and the An Lao valley, Binh Dinh Province; the hills northwest of Khe Sanh and Con Thien, Quang Tri Province; Dak To, Kontum Province · HIST-ALT · CP02,R05
*Initiative: both*

Four frontier battles were each won on the ground, and none changed who chose the next. In **Masher/White Wing**, 24 January – 6 March 1966, the 1st Cavalry Division with the ARVN and the Koreans swept the Bong Son plain against the PAVN 3rd Division, lost 228 killed, and displaced more than 27,000 civilians; the division slipped away and was back within a week [H28]. Here the parcels of the families who fled were held for the absent tiller under an instruction of February 1966, and families who came back from the camps in 1967 found their land in their own names, which took from the Front its best sentence in Binh Dinh (F001-2, F001-7) [X-001]. In the **Hill Fights** at Khe Sanh, 24 April – 11 May 1967, the 3rd Marines took Hills 861 and 881 from the 325C Division; their M4A1s jammed at about the M14's rate, the marksmen killed machine gunners in their apertures at 400–500 metres, and I put Marine losses at about 125 killed against 155, with no inquiry into the rifle (EC004-5, F004-13) [X-004].

At **Con Thien** the Marketplace ambush of 2 July killed 86, and nothing in the B-52J changed a hedgerow fight at a few dozen metres [H42]. From September the PAVN besieged the hill by fire from north of the Ben Hai. Here a second Global Hawk orbit imaged the ground every night from 4 September, the cell measured the gun positions before dawn, and from 11 September cells of Js with GBU-38s came every two to three hours (F005-2, F005-21) [X-005]. The gunners halved their rate to survive, the heaviest day came at about 600 rounds rather than 1,200, the 812th's trenches were struck and abandoned, and the siege lifted about three weeks early with about a third fewer Marines killed in September and October and perhaps half again as many PAVN dead [X-005]. Westmoreland learned from it that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle (F005-14). At **Dak To**, 3–23 November, the 4th Division, the 173rd Airborne and the ARVN fought the PAVN 1st Division through three weeks of bunkers for 361 American dead; with Loc Ninh, Song Be and Con Thien it drew about half the American battalions to the frontiers by January 1968, which was what Hanoi wanted [H36].

## EV009-2 · The mines of Dien Ban · 1 Mar 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Dien Ban, Dai Loc and Duy Xuyen Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon; Son Tinh and Binh Son Districts, Quang Ngai Province · NEW · 009,R05

The enemy's answer to the trained soldier was to kill him without fighting him. ## EV000-26 · The Buddhist uprising · 10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · HIST-ALT · 000,002,007,R05
*Initiative: neither*

After Honolulu, Ky moved against Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps and dismissed him on 10 March 1966; the Struggle Movement of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers under Thich Tri Quang took Da Nang and Hue, and Ky's first landing at Da Nang was blocked [H30]. On 15 May he airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang, with III MAF caught between two factions of its ally; Thi went into exile, the US Information Service library in Hue was burned, and Hue was retaken by 19 June, with about 150 dead [H30]. The uprising ended the Buddhists as an organised force, confirmed Thieu and Ky, and bought elections for a Constituent Assembly in September [H].

Here the police that Ky used was the one the decree of 1964 had built. On 12 March the Directory removed Director General Cuong unlawfully and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, and the Mission protested and did not pull the lever, because the Directory was the government it had just embraced (F002-16) [X-002]. In Hue the Special Branch gave Loan the census of the pagoda quarter and the streets round the university, and about 1,300 were detained by July, about 1,000 of them later released by magistrates whose review the Mission made the price of its police aid [X-002]. The repression was more efficient than history's and cost more: the first American stories of "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, and several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders went to the Front with what they knew of the census (F002-14) [X-002]. In the 1st Division four battalions under the board's commanders stayed on operations west of Hue and refused orders from both sides to march on the cities, which thinned the Struggle's armed support without speeding Ky's recapture. The board's list for the division was then headed by Ngo Quang Truong, whom Ky took, and the Military Security Service opened its first file on "the board's officers as a political factor" in July (F007-14) [X-007].

## EV005-2 · Operation HARD CROSSING and the estuary field · 12 Apr 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Mu Gia pass and Route 15; Ban Karai pass and Route 912; the Xe Bang Fai crossings and the Ban Laboy ford; the estuaries of the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang; Route Package 1 · NEW · 005,R05
*Initiative: allied*

On 12 April 1966, the day history first sent B-52s against the Mu Gia pass, Washington authorized the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the panhandle and Route Package 1. On the first night eight Js put 160 GBU-31s on eleven bridges, causeways and ferries at the foot of Mu Gia and on the Xe Bang Fai, under cloud that made no difference, and every one was down by dawn [X-005]. For six weeks each repair was seen, re-measured and cut the next night; by May the counts at the watch's entry gates were a third of March's, and in Saigon and Honolulu it was read for a few weeks as the Trail closing [X-005]. It was not. Group 559's engineers gave up building anything the J could find, and at every cut crossing laid two, three and then five alternatives: fords paved a foot under the water, causeways floated at dusk and sunk at dawn, Soviet pontoons hidden under the bank (F005-4, F005-17) [X-005]. At Ban Laboy the cell counted one ford in April and nine by October. HARD CROSSING destroyed about 210 crossings for 1,900 GBU-31 kits and no bombers, taxed the flow by 10–15 percent at its peak and 5 percent by October, and by the end of October the trucks south of Tchepone were 15 percent above March [X-005]. It had built the enemy a road net with no single point of failure, and he had drawn the right lesson in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so he would own nothing it could measure (F005-16).

On 26 February 1967 Washington allowed the North's rivers to be mined, and four Js laid the first field of Quickstrikes in the Song Ca and Song Giang estuaries, set for steel hulls [M005] [X-005]. Steel traffic south of Vinh fell to a fifth in a month and stopped by June, reseeded every three to four weeks without loss. The cargo moved to junks, sampans and trucks, and by the autumn truck movements below Vinh were a quarter higher with the tonnage into the panhandle about the same; the North spent 15,000–25,000 more porters on it, and asked Moscow for minesweepers and got trucks, which was what it needed (F005-5, F005-18) [X-005]. How much less arrived in the South because of it, nobody in Washington asked. My own answer is very little.

## EV007-3 · Operation OPEN ROAD · 3–21 May 1966 · Route 13 from Chon Thanh to An Loc, the Xa Cam rubber and the Tau O crossing, Binh Long Province; Bau Bang, Binh Duong Province · NEW · 007,009,R04,R05
*Initiative: enemy*

The ambush that was never sprung on Route 13 is the clearest case in this war of what the reforms did and why no report showed it. In April 1966 the VC 9th Division ordered Lieutenant Colonel Dang Huu Thoi's 272nd Regiment, about 1,500 men, to lay an ambush in the Xa Cam rubber south of An Loc for the next ARVN convoy, on the ground every regiment on that road had chosen, an L of two battalions along the eastern side and the Tau O crossing to stop the head of the column [X-007]. The convoy of 62 trucks left Chon Thanh at 0600 on 4 May under Major Bui Quang Trieu's 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, given him from the board's list, and Trieu had written the order on the pattern the career course taught: two companies on foot in the rubber 300 to 400 metres each side of the road a kilometre ahead, PF guides from Tan Khai, mortars and a battery registered on the Tau O and four points in the rubber, and no truck moving until the flanks reported each kilometre clear [X-007]. It was slow, and the division commander told him so twice by radio.

At 0830 the 272nd's scouts saw the flanking companies coming through the rubber toward the eastern arm of the L, at a walk. The Front's rule was to fight only where victory was certain. Thoi's political officer argued that the men had lain nine days in position and expected a fight. At 0910 Thoi withdrew the regiment east without firing, and the convoy reached An Loc at 1700 [X-007]. The division recorded OPEN ROAD as completed without contact, and MACV's summary did not mention it.

On 21 May the 272nd ambushed instead an RF convoy near Bau Bang, two companies riding fourteen trucks with no one in the trees. The territorial course held where it could: the men left the trucks on the side away from the fire, fought from the ditch in pairs, reported on the sector net, and the rear company put its BARs, not squad guns the territorials never drew, into the treeline within two minutes [M004] [X-009]. The RF lost about 32 dead rather than 40. A private's training does not reach the order that puts him on a truck in the Xa Cam rubber (F009-8). A notebook of the 272nd's operations officer, taken near Lai Khe in January 1967, recorded both days: "the puppet battalion now walks beside the road and not on it", the regiment should "seek its targets among the units that still ride", and the territorials "now fight from the ditch", so an ambush must kill in the first volley or not be laid (F007-5, F007-18) [X-007]. The better battalion lost fewer men because it was attacked less, and an attack that is not made leaves nothing to count; the phase's single estimate of what that was worth is in its ending (F007-24).

## EVR05-6 · The Commission's cases of 1966–67: the lot numbers, the quartermaster and the Phu Cat lot · 12 Jul 1966 – 20 Dec 1967 · Cao Lanh and the Plain of Reeds; the 9th Division supply point at Sa Dec; Can Tho; a firing position south of Phu Cat; the Bien Hoa arsenal; Cholon; the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · R05

Three cases in eighteen months showed the Commission's reach, its limit and what the war's own paper could prove. On 12 July 1966 a 9th Division company overran a VC firing position near Cao Lanh, and its adviser, Captain Robert Halloran, noticed that the 900 spent 5.56mm cases behind it all bore one headstamp lot, which his own battalion had drawn in May [X-004]. Khoa told the Board it was the first case in which the war itself had supplied the evidence.

On 14 August 1967 a 22nd Division howitzer burst its tube south of Phu Cat on a Bien Hoa round, killing four of its crew. A Directorate inspector had accepted that lot and three others without the proof-firing the specification required, and had been paid for the time he saved the line; he was convicted in December, and about 40,000 rounds were recalled (F008-14) [X-008]. The gunners' verdict took longer. ## EV006-4 · The Mo Duc raid and the Phoenix story · 11 Feb – 30 Jun 1967 · Duc Thanh village, Mo Duc District, Quang Ngai Province; Saigon; Hanoi; Washington · NEW · 006,R05
*Initiative: both*

The raid at Duc Thanh was what the method became where the night was not held. The word Phoenix was in the American vocabulary, and it meant what the Front had said it meant.

## EV000-37 · Hanoi decides on a general offensive · 6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · HIST-ALT · 000,001,004,R05
*Initiative: enemy*

In the spring of 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive; Thanh died in Hanoi on 6 July, and the plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho, with Giap modifying it reluctantly [H39]. From 27 July the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair" arrested hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals, removing the opposition and much of Giap's staff [H39]. The premise was that the ARVN was finished and the Saigon government unpopular, and that an offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising; in October the Politburo chose Tet, and the border battles of the autumn drew the Americans away from the cities [H39].

It was taken on history's dates by history's men for history's reasons. The provisions added an argument and weakened the premise. COSVN's reports recorded that the Delta's tax was falling, recruitment in the titled districts harder each season and the "land trick" unexposed, and Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait; COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 said that the government was not unpopular there, and was overruled (F001-12) [X-001]. A study group of Giap's combat research department had concluded that spring that the puppet infantry and territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat; its three senior officers were arrested in December for whose staff they were on, and the study went into the files with them [X-004]. The offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than history's, against an ARVN that had been better armed than its attackers for four years.

## EV016-3 · The night check at Tan Thoi Nhi · 14–15 Aug 1967 · Tan Thoi Nhi village, Hoc Mon District, Gia Dinh Province; the Hoc Mon district station and its centre · NEW · 016
*Initiative: allied*

The night check embodied what the pistol was for, and this night is the one the Hoc Mon station kept in its file. At 2240 on 14 August 1967 Sergeant Truong Van Lam of the Tan Thoi Nhi post took two constables and a PF fire team down the lane behind the market with the census booklets for eleven households (EC016-1, EC002-2) [X-016]. At the seventh house the door opened on a man with something in his right hand. In history Lam would have had a revolver in one hand and a torch in the other, or no torch at all; the station's report of 1965 counted three farmers shot in Hoc Mon that year by constables who saw a shape and an object at a door at night (F016-15) [X-016]. Lam's light was on his pistol and his pistol was low. He lit the man for two seconds and saw a sickle, an old man and a frightened face, and the census booklet in the other constable's hand said the old man lived there [X-016].

At the tenth house a younger man whom the booklet did not list went for the back of the hut when the light came on, and stopped when it found him with his hands empty against the wall. He was a courier of the Hoc Mon district committee's finance section, which had governed from the Ho Bo woods since 1966; he was taken alive, talked at the district centre within the week, and gave the centre the route by which the section's collectors entered three villages of the district (EV012-4, EVR05-4) [X-012] [X-016]. A courier is not a district cadre, and the section was not broken by him. But the old man with the sickle was alive in the morning and the courier was in a cell rather than a ditch, and both of those things were known in Tan Thoi Nhi by noon.

Across the held belt that is the honest measure of the pistol: about 150–200 fewer civilian dead and wounded a year by 1967 from night shootings by soldiers and police, and about 100–150 more listed cadre a year taken alive or killed rather than escaping in the dark, both judgments on rates and at low confidence (F016-15) [X-016]. The station logs of Hoc Mon, Can Giuoc and Cai Lay show the night checks' yield of strangers falling by about half from 1966 to 1967 as the cadres stopped sleeping in the held villages, which is the check's success and reads as its decline (F016-9) [X-016].

## EV000-38 · The constitution and the presidential election · 3 Sep 1967 · Republic of Vietnam · HIST · 000
The Constituent Assembly elected in September 1966 produced a constitution, promulgated on 1 April 1967 [H38]. In the presidential election of 3 September 1967, held with the Senate election, Thieu ran for president with Ky as his running mate, after the generals forced Ky to stand aside [H38]. Eleven tickets ran. Thieu and Ky won 34.83 percent, 1,649,561 votes; the runner-up, Truong Dinh Dzu, a lawyer campaigning on negotiations with the NLF, won 17.26 percent, 817,120 votes [H38]. The reported turnout was 83.17 percent of 5,853,251 registered voters [H38]. There were credible reports of soldiers showing voters how to mark their ballots, and of a secret military committee that would govern behind the elected government; on 10 August 57 members of the US House had condemned the conduct of the campaign [H38]. Dzu was later imprisoned [H]. Thieu was inaugurated on 31 October and the lower house was elected on 22 October [H] [H38].

The election gave the Republic of Vietnam an elected government for the first time since 1963 and, under Thieu, the stability that the previous four years had lacked. It did not give it legitimacy in the countryside: two-thirds of the voters chose someone else, and a civilian peace candidate had come second.

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 men in Vietnam and has lost 9,378 killed in the year [H40]. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on the North [H9], the Trail carries more than ever, and MACV and the CIA dispute whether the enemy numbers 300,000 or 430,000, a dispute over whether the guerrillas and the infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all [H39]. Westmoreland tells the National Press Club on 21 November that "the end begins to come into view" [H39]. McNamara is leaving. Saigon has an elected government under Thieu, and in Hanoi the Politburo has set Tet for the General Offensive and General Uprising. Each side is certain the other is weaker than it is.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts, and its committees govern from the Ho Bo woods, the Rung Sat and Memot by courier, with collections at a sixth to an eighth of 1964's; its legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less (EVR05-4) [X-002]. About 820,000 hectares are titled, rent has ended wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general (EVR05-6) [X-001]. Phung Hoang has an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, a fifth of them committee members, and the word Phoenix has been a press story since March (EV006-4) [X-006]. The infrastructure is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, which is still most of the contested countryside.

The enemy's supply is measured, the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count and the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what his forces in the South need (EVR05-5, EV003-4) [X-003]. The bombers have learned that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot, and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle (EV005-2, EVCP02-3). Over the North the theater has had the campaign it asked for and the result it predicted, about 40–90 fewer American aircraft lost than historically, and no hearings on restrictions that were never drawn (F013-18) [X-013].

The armies enter 1968 better in the fights they are given. The American army has had no rifle crisis and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights and in the nights after them; its battalions rotate as units with staggered reliefs that cost 15,000–25,000 spaces in theater, and about 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps (F006-12) [X-006]. The ARVN has about four in five battalions under officers the board chose, its NCOs schooled and its privates trained, and the drill sergeants returning 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year (F009-4) [X-009]. **About two-fifths to a half of history's ARVN battalions broken in 1966–67 were not broken in this war. The judgment is low-confidence, and the true figure is more likely near two-fifths than a half**, because the officer reforms, the basic course and the NCO schooling each claimed a share from overlapping cases and none checked its cases against the others' (REVR01-1, POSCP04-1) [X-CP04]. None of it shows in a return. The enemy declined the fights that would have shown it, attacked less what kept its routine, and moved his war to the mine in the lowlands and to the unit that still rode (EV007-3, EV009-2). The corps and the generals are what they were, and the palace has a file on the board's officers while COSVN has had every list the board published (EV007-4).

The factories produce what the domestic share buys, short of power and, after Phu Cat, short of their gunners' trust. Every post in the held belt has a radio, and where the guns reach, the first call is for fire, and the post holds itself better than the hamlet around it (EV008-4) [X-008]. The gunships have taken the clear night over the posts of the held belt; the coast's monsoon nights are still the Front's, and the first Vietnamese gunship squadron is flying (EV015-4). The combined command has one corps under a Vietnamese commander and three under American deputies, moving at the speed of the generals the palace chooses (EV012-6) [X-012]. The population of the South, the object of the war, is still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.
