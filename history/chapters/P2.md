# Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967 [H40]. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed most. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 titled to about 640,000 families [X-001]. GOLDEN SHEAF, its first clearing operation in the western Delta, shows what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of what the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark (EV001-6, F001-4). The Commission survives the Buddhist crisis and the election behind the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 convicts its first general (EV001-7). With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case (EV002-4). The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its cadres into the towns (EV002-3, EV002-5), and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before (EV000-26). None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

The war of the enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch counts the Trail's trucks from December 1965 (EV003-3); the U-2S photographs the dispersed fuel drums before the POL strikes fly (EVCP02-4); and by March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree, from the radar's count of convoys out of Kompong Speu, that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4). The counts show the bombing raising the toll on the transit without lowering what arrives, and change no rule in Washington.

Every American and South Vietnamese infantryman carries a modern carbine, and every squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman. The Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle (EV000-23, EVCP02-3). The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by the corps, and the districts they leave pay for it (EV004-2). None of it changes who chooses the battle.

Above the Trail the B-52J is allowed against fixed crossings in the panhandle from April 1966 and cuts every bridge it can measure; the enemy answers with fords, causeways and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before (EV005-2). At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is measured every night and lifts three weeks early at a heavy price to the PAVN (EVCP02-3). It is the first siege the enemy loses badly, and Westmoreland draws a lesson from it that will cost the coastal hamlets.

Three changes of 1964 run through the phase without altering its battles. Phung Hoang's centres cover every district by the end of 1965 and break the Front's district administration in the held belt before Tet rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN takes the Can Giuoc committee itself, not only its security chief (EV002-4). The method spreads by its own success into the contested belt, where it becomes the historical Phoenix, and a PRU raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 makes "the American Phoenix" a press story three years early (EV006-4). The American battalions arrive and leave as units for twelve-month tours, trained together and commanded for the whole of them; they fight better in the contacts the enemy grants and get no more of them, and the enemy learns to aim at the week each one changes over, as it did at Dien Ban in August 1966 (EV006-3). And the field advisers, now chosen and trained and on eighteen-month tours, are the men who carry what the American battalions forget each year.

The officer reforms change the ARVN in this phase more than any provision before them. From the board's second season in 1965 the battalions are filled from its command list, their commanders serve eighteen months and are relieved when they fail, and by the end of 1966 about three in four of the army's battalion commanders are men a board chose, against about one in two competent commanders on the historical course (EV007-2) [X-007]. The enemy notices within a season and does what he does against every better unit: he declines to fight it, and lays his ambush for the battalion beside it, as the 272nd Regiment does on Route 13 in May 1966 (EV007-3, F007-18). The Buddhist uprising finds the board's commanders in I Corps on neither side (EV000-26), and in 1967 the ranked list gives the 25th Division its first commander chosen for competence, under the aid condition, while an agent in the board's secretariat gives COSVN every list it publishes (EV007-4). Above the battalions nothing moves: the corps are commanded by the same men, Westmoreland's strategy turns the ARVN to pacification as it did historically, and the generals still decide where the battalions go.

A fifth war runs under the fourth, in the factories. The steelworks at Bien Hoa rolls the pickets, wire and bridge spans of the countryside, at a fifth to a tenth below its rating because the Front has learned to drop the towers of the Da Nhim line (EV008-3). The radio plant puts a PRC-25 in every post of the held belt from 1966, and the Front answers the radio as it answered the carbine: it attacks the posts that cannot call, and at My Phuoc Tay in March 1967 it attacks a post in order to ambush the company that answers the call (EV008-4). The district chiefs answer with guns instead of trucks, and their platoons stay inside their wire. In Chuong Thien the posts call and nobody comes, because the corps has borrowed the companies (EV004-2). And at Phu Cat in August 1967 a bad lot of shell bodies teaches the army what an arsenal is worth without honest acceptance (EV008-5). None of it changes who chooses the main-force battle.

Under all of it, the private soldier of both armies is trained. Every ARVN recruit of the phase passes ten weeks at Quang Trung or a division centre before he joins his battalion, every territorial six weeks at his province's centre, and every American his service's 2026 course; from 1966 the drill-sergeant corps begins to return its sergeants to the battalions as platoon sergeants (EV009-1) [M009]. The effect is the one the officer reforms found: the better unit is attacked less, and the attack that is not made leaves nothing to count. The 272nd Regiment's ambush of the Bau Bang convoy breaks on trained Regional Force soldiers who cannot be trained out of riding their trucks (EV007-3). At the Ia Drang the troopers keep more of their wounded alive through the night at Albany (EV000-23). And the enemy answers the trained soldier as he answered the rifle, by moving his war to the weapon the course does not teach: in the lowlands south of Da Nang the mine and the booby trap become the principal killers of the Marines (EV009-2). The devaluation of June 1966 then shows what training does not buy: a trained private whose pay no longer feeds his family goes home with his training, and some of it reaches the Front (EV009-3).

Between the private and the officer, the sergeant is schooled too. Every ARVN and territorial NCO of the phase has passed the course for his grade at the academy at Nha Trang, a division school or his province's centre, and every empty squad in a regular battalion can be given to a corporal first class who has passed the leader's course; every American sergeant who wants a staff sergeant's rocker passes the Basic Leader Course first, at home or at MACV's academy at Bien Hoa (EV010-2). The squads hold their base of fire when their officers fall, as the 7th Division's did in the rice fields of Cai Be in November 1966 (EV010-3), and the posts of the held belt keep their listening posts out at night (F010-2) [X-010]. The enemy reads all of it from the academy's own manuals and fights the posts and the battalions that keep the routine less often (F010-19, F010-20).

## EV002-3 · The war on the district chiefs · Aug 1965 – Jun 1966 · The Delta, the provinces around Saigon and the coastal Centre · NEW · 002
For its first year the Front read the Provincial Administration Reform as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, and it took until the summer of 1965, when the list-appointed chiefs had stayed through the fall of Khanh, the Quat government and the arrival of Thieu and Ky, for the provincial committees to report that the new chiefs were not changing (F002-12) [X-002]. The conclusion COSVN drew in August 1965 was the one that hurt: the chiefs who kept their Popular Forces in the hamlets at night and directed the police were the enemy's most dangerous officials, and the district committees were to kill them [X-002]. Between August 1965 and June 1966 nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs were killed by mines, ambush and assassination, sixteen of them appointed from the list [X-002]. On 14 November 1965 Major Tran Van Sang, district chief of Mo Cay in Kien Hoa since June 1964, was killed with his driver and two bodyguards by a command-detonated mine on the road from Mo Cay to Ben Tre; the provincial committee's own report, captured in 1966, called him "the most harmful of the puppet district chiefs in the province" because the hamlets of his district no longer sheltered its cadres at night [X-002].

The killings did not empty the districts, and that was the reform's quiet gain. A dead chief's deputy for administration acted at once, and the Board sent a ranked list for the vacancy within an average of about three weeks; Mo Cay had a new chief, a captain who had served as the district's deputy for security, on 6 December [X-002]. Under the patronage system a vacant district had waited months for a patron's choice, and the Popular Forces had drifted back to the district town while it waited [M002]. Each killing cost the Front a security cell's best men, because the chiefs now travelled with Field Force escorts and varied their roads, and five of the twenty-one attacks were traced and their teams taken by the Special Branch within the year [X-002]. The Front went on killing chiefs for the rest of the war. It never again did so at the rate of 1965–66, because the chiefs it could kill easily had been killed and the rest had learned (F002-8).

## EV012-4 · Operation FOLLOW THROUGH · 2 Aug 1965 – 30 Jun 1966 · Cu Chi and Trang Bang Districts, Hau Nghia Province; Hoc Mon District, Gia Dinh Province; the Ho Bo and Boi Loi woods and the Filhol rubber, Binh Duong Province · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

FOLLOW THROUGH was the task force's answer to the district committee that STILL WATER had driven out of Hoc Mon. Five of the Hoc Mon committee's 14 members had escaped STILL WATER and governed the district's posted villages from Cu Chi by courier (EV006-2) [X-006]. With them in the Cu Chi hamlets along the Saigon River sat the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region's finance and economy section, which collected taxes from the market gardens of Hoc Mon and Go Vap and the rubber workers of the Filhol plantation [X-012]. On 2 August 1965 the III Corps task group opened the operation from Hoc Mon district town, with Major Garvin commanding and Lieutenant Colonel Phuoc of the Special Branch as his deputy. The task group was now about 420 strong, with a Special Forces detachment, two SEAL platoons, the Gia Dinh and Hau Nghia PRUs and four UH-1Ds and two UH-1B gunships of its own [X-012].

The operation ran on the cycle the task force had learned in Long An: an arrest or a rallier, exploitation the same morning, the follow-on that night (EC012-7). Its first two months took eleven of the region finance section's couriers and none of its members, because every courier carried only the next courier's name [X-012]. The break was a woman again, as it had been in STILL WATER. On 14 October a 23-year-old cashier of the finance section, arrested at a market stall in Hoc Mon with 41,000 piasters in a rice sack, rallied on the 16th against a named offer that her mother would not be detained (EC012-10). She described the section's three meeting houses in An Nhon Tay and Phu Hoa Dong [X-012].

The raid on Phu Hoa Dong on the night of 19–20 October was the task force's first contested-belt operation at full scale. Two SEAL platoons and the Gia Dinh PRU inserted by helicopter at 0230 into a paddy east of the hamlet. A Regional Forces company of Cu Chi district held a cordon on the river side, and a company of the ARVN 25th Division's 49th Regiment closed the land side. The III Corps combined centre had tasked that company at 1500 the previous afternoon on the task group's request, against the division's own plan for the night (F012-6) [X-012]. The team took the section's deputy chief and two cadres alive in a tunnel entrance under a family altar, with the section's ledger for the first nine months of 1965. One SEAL was killed by a grenade thrown from a second tunnel mouth. The PRU killed three men in the paddy who had fired on the cordon. One of them was the deputy chief's 16-year-old son, carrying a K-44 rifle [X-012].

The ledger was the most valuable document the task force had yet taken. It recorded the region's tax collections hamlet by hamlet across Hoc Mon, Go Vap, Cu Chi and Trang Bang, and it named the collectors. Between November 1965 and March 1966 the task group made 64 arrests on warrants drawn from it, of which the magistrates released 21. It killed 17 people in raids, of whom the Commission's audit of May 1966 could show 13 to be cadres and could not show 4 [X-012]. Of the Hoc Mon committee's five surviving members, three were taken and one rallied. The fifth, the committee's secretary, Nguyen Van Tu, left Cu Chi for the Ho Bo woods in January 1966 with the finance section's remaining members, as the American 25th Infantry Division arrived at Cu Chi and began clearing the plantation edge [X-012] [H]. The task group could not follow him there. The woods were the 165th VC Regiment's base area and a main-force problem (F012-8).

What FOLLOW THROUGH achieved shows in the Front's own accounts. The region finance section's collections from the posted villages of Hoc Mon and Go Vap in the first half of 1966 were about one-eighth of the first half of 1964's, against about one-fifth on the course of memorandum 006. Its collections from Cu Chi's contested hamlets fell by about two-fifths, where on that course they had grown [X-012]. Nobody governed Hoc Mon from Cu Chi after March 1966. The Front governed it from the Ho Bo woods, eighteen kilometres further out, by couriers who now took two nights rather than one, and it never re-established a tax collector who lived in a Hoc Mon hamlet before 1968 (F012-27) [X-012]. The operation cost the task force two SEALs, four PRU members and a Regional Forces soldier killed. It cost the Front the region's tax administration west of Saigon for about a year. It cost the families of the four men the audit could not clear a reason to hate the government, and the census told the Front which families they were (F012-16).

## EVCP01-4 · The big-unit war: Starlite, A Shau, Hastings, Long Tan, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City · 18 Aug 1965 – 14 May 1967 · The Van Tuong peninsula and the DMZ in I Corps; the A Shau valley; Phuoc Tuy; War Zone C and the Iron Triangle in III Corps · HIST-ALT · CP01
*Initiative: both*

The big-unit battles of 1965–67 are told here together, because together they are the shape of the phase: each won on the ground, each followed by the enemy's return, none changing the war. On 18–24 August 1965, on a deserter's information, the 7th Marines under Colonel Oscar F. Peatross attacked the VC 1st Regiment on the Van Tuong peninsula south of Chu Lai by sea and helicopter in Operation Starlite, the first regimental-sized American battle of the war [H26]. The Marines lost 45 killed and 203 wounded and reported 614 VC killed but only 109 weapons taken, the first clear instance of a count inflated by its counters; the regiment was back in the field within months [H26]. On 9–10 March 1966, under cloud that kept most air support away, four battalions of the PAVN 325th Division's 95B Regiment overran the Special Forces camp in the A Shau valley; Major Bernard F. Fisher landed his A-1E on the strip under fire to rescue a downed pilot and received the Medal of Honor, and the valley became a PAVN base area and a terminus of the Trail for the rest of the war [H29].

In July 1966 the PAVN 324B Division crossed the DMZ, and Task Force Delta of the 3rd Marine Division, with the ARVN 1st Division under Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong, drove it back in Operation Hastings for 126 American and 21 South Vietnamese killed against about 700 PAVN claimed [H31]. Its lasting effect was to draw the Marines north to the DMZ, away from the coastal hamlets III MAF had wanted to secure [H31]. On 18 August, in monsoon rain in the Long Tan rubber, D Company, 6th Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment, about 108 men under Major Harry Smith, held for three hours against the VC 275th Regiment and the D445 Battalion with the guns of Nui Dat behind it, losing 18 killed and counting 245 enemy dead on the field [H32]. From September to November Operation Attleboro grew from a brigade's shakedown northwest of Dau Tieng into a corps battle against the VC 9th Division and the PAVN 101st Regiment, costing 155 American dead against a claimed 1,016 enemy, of whom about 500 is the better estimate [H33].

In 1967 the sweeps reached their largest size. In Cedar Falls, from 8 January, the 1st and 25th Divisions with brigades and armoured cavalry sealed the Iron Triangle, moved about 6,000 people from Ben Suc to a camp at Phu Loi and destroyed the village; the VC were back within days, and a year later the Triangle was a staging area for Tet [H34]. In Junction City, from 22 February to 14 May, II Field Force sent two divisions and three brigades into War Zone C to destroy the 9th Division and find COSVN, with the war's only major American parachute assault at Katum [H35]. The enemy attacked American firebases five times at night or at dawn, at Prek Klok, Ap Bau Bang, Suoi Tre and Ap Gu, and lost heavily each time; the Americans lost 282 killed and claimed 2,728 enemy dead, COSVN moved, and the 9th Division withdrew into Cambodia [H35].

The figures above are the historical returns. In this war the American battalions in these fights, and the ARVN 1st Division at Hastings, carried the M4A1 and the M249 (EV004-1); the Australians at Long Tan did not, because the provision did not cover them. I do not adjust these returns. In each of these battles who attacked whom, where and when decided the losses far more than the squad weapons did, and the enemy's own losses were never counted closely enough for a difference in rifles to show in them (POS004-1) [X-CP01]. What the phase's big-unit battles proved here is what they proved historically: that the enemy lost heavily when he attacked an American position, and that he decided for himself whether to do so.

## EV007-2 · The command lists and the thirty-day reviews · 1 Sep 1965 – 30 Jun 1966 · The Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters; the Command and General Staff College at Dalat · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The board's second season was the first in which it had two years of evaluation reports to read, and it changed the army's battalions faster than anything since 1955. Between September 1965 and June 1966 the panels selected about 230 battalion and regimental commanders, most of the army's manoeuvre commands, and by the end of June 1966 about three-quarters of the army's battalions were commanded by an officer the board had chosen, about two-thirds of them majors and lieutenant colonels of the classes of 1957–59 who had passed the career course and the first Dalat staff year [X-007]. Every command was for eighteen months, against a median of under a year on the historical course [M007·S1]. The captain-commanded battalion, about a third of the army in 1965, fell to about one in ten by the end of 1966 [X-007].

The thirty-day reviews were the part the army felt. In the season the board relieved 27 battalion commanders and three regimental commanders for cause, each after a review that heard the officer, his raters and his American adviser's written statement, and each review's finding was sent to every battalion commander in the army in a monthly digest the Central Training Command called Lessons of Command [X-007]. Most of the reliefs were for what the courses taught against: a convoy moved without route security, a night defensive position without listening posts, a fire plan never made, a company sent forward without the battalion knowing where the others were [X-007]. Two were for the sale of rice rations, and the Commission Against Corruption prosecuted both (F007-4) [X-007]. The digest was read, as such things are read, mostly by officers who wished to know what the board punished; that was the point of it.

The board's own sale of commands ended and patronage moved to where the board did not reach. In the corps headquarters the price of a command fell to nothing, and the price of an assignment rose: the generals could no longer give a man a battalion, but they could still give him a division staff post, a province's sector headquarters, a seat on the career course ahead of his turn, or a battalion in the Delta rather than one on the Cambodian border (F007-4) [X-007]. Lindqvist estimated in June 1966 that about half of what had been sold in 1964 was no longer for sale, and that the other half had changed its name [X-007].

The field commissions began in the same season. About 600 sergeants with a combat record and a commander's recommendation were commissioned in 1966 after a twelve-week course at Thu Duc and the branch basic course, most of them sons of farmers from the Delta and the coastal Centre, men who would never have passed the diploma bar the decree had abolished (F007-22) [X-007]. They went to the platoons of their own regiments, and in the PF and RF training camps it was noticed within the year that the new lieutenants spoke to the recruits in the recruits' own accent. Major Bui Quang Trieu took command of the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, 5th Division from the September list.

## EV008-3 · The Da Nhim line · 3 Oct 1965 – 31 Dec 1967 · The 230 kV line from the Da Nhim station at Don Duong, Tuyen Duc Province, through Di Linh in Lam Dong and Long Khanh to the Thu Duc and Bien Hoa substations · NEW · 008
*Initiative: enemy*

The steelworks ran on electricity it did not make, and the Front found that out in its second year. The melt shop drew 70–100 MW at peak, about half from the line that brought the Da Nhim hydro station's power 250 kilometres down from the Lang Biang plateau to Thu Duc and the rest from the 66 MW thermal station at Thu Duc [M008]. On the night of 3 October 1965 a sapper team of the Front's Region 6 dropped two towers of the line in the hills east of Di Linh with charges of TNT in Bien Hoa-made ammunition boxes, and by the end of the year it had dropped eleven more (F008-4) [X-008]. The Region 6 committee, which commanded the Front's war in the southern Highlands and the provinces east of Saigon, had chosen the line for its own reasons: it was the one target in its region whose loss was felt in Saigon, and it could be reached from the forest without fighting anybody [X-008].

The line was harder to cut than to mend, and the Directorate made it so. The fabrication shops at Bien Hoa built replacement towers to the Japanese drawing, a set of four in a week, and the line crews had a dropped tower back in four to ten days, against the six to eight weeks that a tower shipped from Japan would have taken [X-008]. The power company and the Directorate paid the Koho villages along the line, between Don Duong and Di Linh, a monthly wage to watch it, and gave each line-watch village a hamlet radio on the Tuyen Duc sector's net (EC008-9) [X-008]. They were the first Montagnard hamlets in the province with either a government wage or a radio. The watch reported the sappers more often than it stopped them, because the villagers were not armed and were not asked to be.

The cost to the works was real and bounded. In 1966 the Front dropped about forty towers and the steelworks lost about a fifth of its hours; in 1967, with the watch, about twenty-five and a tenth [X-008]. Bien Hoa rolled about 180,000 tonnes in 1966 and 220,000 in 1967 against a rated 200,000–300,000, and the shortfall fell on construction steel, not on the arsenal, whose lines ran on the Thu Duc station [X-008]. The FULRO risings of these years were in Darlac, Quang Duc and Phu Bon, among the Rhade and Jarai, outside the country the line crossed; the base did nothing for them and they did nothing to it [H] [X-008]. The Front got from the line a cheap and lasting target, and the Republic got from it, without meaning to, thirty Koho villages that took its wage, carried its radios, and told the sector when the Front came through.

## EV000-23 · Plei Me and the Ia Drang · 19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004,007,009
*Initiative: enemy*

On 19 October 1965 the PAVN B3 Front under Major General Chu Huy Man attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, south of Pleiku, and the next day ambushed the ARVN relief column; the relief reached the camp at dusk on 25 October and the PAVN withdrew west towards the Chu Pong massif on the Cambodian border [H27]. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), newly arrived, was turned loose in pursuit. On 14 November the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray at the foot of the Chu Pong, into the assembly area of the 66th Regiment [H27]. For three days the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th, with elements of the 33rd, assaulted a perimeter held with the help of the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert Tully, massed tube artillery, tactical air and, from 15 November, B-52s striking about 7 km to the west, the first tactical use of the bombers, in five missions and 96 sorties over the campaign [H27]. The troopers carried M4A1s, with two M249s in each squad and an M110A2 marksman in most, where historically they carried the M16 and had the M60 at platoon; the perimeter held as it held historically, and I put American losses at X-Ray at about 70 killed and 115 wounded against 79 and 121 [X-004]. Moore's count of PAVN dead, 634 after his own reduction, and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record and the same caution applies to them [H27].

On 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade, marching overland to Landing Zone Albany, was ambushed in column in tall grass by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd Regiment [H27]. The PAVN, following Colonel Nguyen Huu An's order to "grab them by the belt", fought inside the column where American artillery and air could not reach them, for about sixteen hours [H27]. This is the kind of fight the squad gun was built for, and it helped less than it should have. Two belt-fed guns in every squad meant that the platoons which kept their cohesion, Alpha Company's and the battalion command group's at the head of the column, formed a perimeter in the clump of trees at Albany within minutes and held it all afternoon; but the companies strung out behind in the grass were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, and a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 125 killed, 130 wounded and 4 missing, against 155 killed, 124 wounded and 4 missing on the historical course, still the worst American loss of a single day so far [X-004]. Over the campaign the United States counted about 205 dead, against 237 historically, and claimed a kill ratio of about ten to one; a PAVN report of 1966 gives 559 killed and 669 wounded [X-004] [H27]. Both sides inflated the other's losses [H27].

Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success, and each drew the lesson that confirmed its strategy: Westmoreland that airmobile forces with heavy fire support could destroy PAVN regulars at a favourable rate; the PAVN that by closing to close quarters and choosing when to fight they could neutralize American firepower and withdraw across the Cambodian border to rebuild [H27]. The B3 Front's after-action study added one sentence that the historical one did not need: that the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and that the belt-grabbing assault must therefore be made from closer still, at night, or not at all (F004-10) [X-004]. McNamara, visiting after the battle, advised the President at the end of November that even a doubling of US forces in 1966 might produce only a military stalemate at a higher level [H].

The officer education changed Albany in the companies and not in the column's order. Moore and McDade had been commissioned long before 1957 and the decision to march overland in column through the grass was McDade's; but the company commanders of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry were captains of the classes of 1959–62, and they had been through the branch course and the career course, which taught the movement to contact in close country as a drill [X-007]. Two of them put flank security out into the grass on their own authority when the column halted at about midday, and their companies were the ones whose men were found in perimeters rather than in knots; two did not, because the column was moving and the battalion had given no order [X-007]. I put the battalion's loss at about 105–115 killed rather than about 125, a judgment on two companies' conduct that I hold loosely, and the campaign's American dead at about 190 [X-007]. It remains the worst American loss of a single day so far.

The basic course changed Albany in the night after the ambush, which is where the battalion lost men it need not have lost. The troopers of the 1st Cavalry Division had all passed the Army's ten-week course of the 2026 programme, and every one of them had been taught in its second week to put a tourniquet on an arm or a leg and to put it on himself if no one else could [M009]. Historically, many of the wounded of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry lay in the grass through the afternoon and night of 17 November, where the medics could not reach them, and a share of those found dead in the morning had died of wounds that a tourniquet would have stopped [H27] [E: about a quarter to a half of deaths from extremity bleeding are preventable by early tourniquet, against 7.9% of US killed in action dying of it, M009]. Here the men in the grass put them on each other and on themselves, in the dark, while the PAVN searched the field. I put the lives this saved at Albany at about eight to twelve, and at X-Ray at two or three, and the campaign's American dead at about 180 rather than about 190 [X-009]. That is the rate this headquarters uses for the whole war, about two to four percent of American killed in action, shown on one night in one battle; it is not larger, and on that night it was not smaller (F009-22). The PAVN wounded who reached the Chu Pong had no such drill, and the B3 Front's medical returns, captured in 1966, put its dead of wounds after the battle at about one in four of its wounded [X-009].

## EV002-4 · Operation NIGHT LANTERN · 15 Nov 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
NIGHT LANTERN went back to the districts where the land programme had learned its first lesson. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings (EV001-3), the titles of about 400 families were still held in the district office, because no one could protect the families who kept them. Long An had been given a province chief from the first list in June 1964, Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, an engineer officer who had placed third in the country in the examination, and Can Giuoc a district chief from the second, Major Le Cong Phuong, who had grown up in Can Duoc [X-002]. In September 1965 they proposed to the American province adviser an operation built not around a regiment but around the settled post (EC002-1), the census and night check (EC002-2) and the target folder (EC002-3), in the 14 villages of the two districts between Route 5 and the Vam Co [X-002].

The forces were Long An's own: eight Regional Forces companies, 26 Popular Forces platoons raised in the villages themselves, two Police Field Force companies of about 150 men each with M1 and M2 carbines, M1918A2 automatic rifles and 60mm mortars, 140 rural constables for the 14 posts, the province Special Branch section of about 45, and four census teams [X-002]. Fire support was two M101 105mm howitzers at Can Giuoc and an AC-47 of the USAF 4th Air Commando Squadron on call at night; lift was the RF's sampans, two LCVPs of the River Assault Group at Nha Be, and UH-1B helicopters when III Corps would release them [X-002]. The rule was Lan's: no post settled until its PF platoon slept in the same hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour, and no arrest without three sources [X-002].

The posts went in from 15 November, one village at a time, working out from Can Giuoc town along the canals. The turn came in two steps. On the night of 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th Local Force Battalion attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, the village where the killings of June 1964 had begun. The PF platoon and the ten constables held the post and the hamlet behind it for three hours; an RF company came down the canal in sampans and reached the post at 01:40, and the AC-47 kept the attackers off the paddy until it arrived [X-002]. The 506th left 38 dead and 14 weapons; the defenders lost 9 killed, 3 of them constables [X-002]. On 4 February a night check in Long Huu found a young man whose card had been made for someone two years older; his booklet did not match the house, and within a week the courier route he served had led the Special Branch to the chief of the Can Giuoc district committee's security section, arrested on 11 February with his files [X-002]. The Can Giuoc DIOCC, open since August 1964 in the Special Branch's office beside the district chief's, took the files apart in nine days: the section chief's notebook listed the committee's courier stations by the names of the families who kept them, and the census matched 17 of the 22 to households and three to households that had been registered under false names in 1965 [X-006]. On the night of 24–25 February the Long An PRU and a Police Field Force platoon, in sampans with an RF company holding the canal behind them, took the district committee's deputy secretary and its finance chief in a hamlet of Phuoc Vinh Dong at the edge of the Rung Sat, where the committee had moved in January believing itself beyond the posts (EC006-4) [X-006]. Neither man fired. The deputy secretary rallied in April and spent the rest of the year with the province centre, drawing the chart of the Long An province committee above him (EC006-2).

By 30 April all 14 posts were settled and held. The Special Branch had arrested 212 people on three-source evidence, of whom the magistrates released 71, and 64 cadres and guerrillas had rallied to the police posts, which they found easier to approach than an army camp (F002-2) [X-002]. What was left of the district committee, five of its eleven members, moved deeper into the Rung Sat and visited its villages instead of living in them, and the Can Giuoc tax ledger captured in June showed collections for the first quarter of 1966 at about a third of the same quarter of 1965 [X-002]. The about 400 titles held since 1964 were handed to their families in April, in their hamlets [X-002]. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 RF, PF and police dead. It was the first operation in the war in which the province chief, the police and the land programme acted as one district government, and it did in six months what no battalion sweep of Long An had done in six years (F002-1, F002-17).

The American who saw the whole of it was Captain Whitcomb, the Can Giuoc subsector adviser, whose eighteen months ran from October 1964 to April 1966 and so spanned the killings' aftermath, the settling of the posts and the operation itself; his successor arrived on 1 March and they worked the district together for six weeks (EC006-8) [X-006]. Whitcomb's report of 30 April said three things this headquarters quoted to Washington: that the committee's arrest, not the battalion's sweep, was what broke the tax; that the Commission's sample of NIGHT LANTERN's 212 arrests had found 9 unsupported, all made in the first fortnight before the centre's charts were complete; and that the families who received their titles in April had asked him, not the district chief, whether the arrests would last (F006-26) [X-006].

## EV012-5 · The II Corps list and its abandonment · 15 Nov 1965 – 2 Apr 1966 · Tuy An and Song Cau Districts, Phu Yen Province; Phu My and Phu Cat Districts, Binh Dinh Province; Qui Nhon · NEW · 012
*Initiative: both*

The II Corps task group was formed at Qui Nhon in November 1965, as the ROK Capital Division settled into its area around Qui Nhon and Phu Cat [M012·S4]. It worked on thinner ground than any other. The coastal districts of Binh Dinh and Phu Yen had been the Viet Minh's Interzone V for nine years and the Front's since 1960, and the Special Branch held files on hamlets it had never entered at night [X-012]. The district centres opened there in 1965 had little but ralliers' accounts. The task group's commander, an American lieutenant colonel, Harold J. Beck, adopted in December what the 2026 task forces had called a high-value target list: 64 named members of the Phu Yen and Binh Dinh province committees, to be captured or, if they resisted, killed [X-012]. Its raid elements were a Special Forces detachment, a Korean special-forces team of the 1st Airborne Special Forces Group attached to the Capital Division, and the two provinces' PRUs.

Military Region 5 had its own plans for the coast, made for its own reasons. Its Party Committee had decided in October 1965 to hold the 2nd VC Regiment and the 3rd PAVN Division's regiments in the An Lao, Kim Son and Suoi Ca valleys through the dry season of 1966. One reason was to protect the rice harvest of the coastal plain, on which its own forces lived. The other was that it refused the B3 Front's request to send a regiment to the Highlands. The region's leaders held that the coast, not the plateau, was the decisive ground of their war [X-012]. That rivalry between two of Hanoi's commands kept a division on the Binh Dinh coast through the winter, and it kept the province committees close to the regiments that protected them.

<!-- initiative -->
The list was worked from January to March 1966. It produced 11 operations, 9 detainees and 14 dead [X-012]. Three of the dead were on the list. On the night of 19–20 January the Korean team and the Phu Yen PRU raided a house in Hoa Thanh village, Tuy An District, on a rallier's word that the Phu Yen committee's security chief slept there. They killed four men who ran from the house and found no papers. The Commission's inspectors, who reached Tuy An on 3 February, found that the four were a family who had sheltered a cadre once, two years before, and that the rallier had a land dispute with them [X-012]. Under the 1964 police statute a killing without a warrant was a matter for the magistrate. On 16 February the Phu Yen magistrate referred two members of the Korean team for homicide, and the Korean commander refused to surrender them [X-012]. Chae Myung-shin's position was that his soldiers answered to Korean military law and nobody else's. The Phu Yen province committee's position was a leaflet, in every market from Tuy Hoa to Song Cau within the week, naming the four men and the Korean unit.

On 2 April the combined commander and the task force commander ended the list in II Corps. The Korean team was withdrawn from the task force at Seoul's request, the American team was kept, and the II Corps task group returned to warrant arrests in the held towns of the coast and named offers in the contested districts (EC012-11) [X-012]. The episode cost the task force its credibility in Phu Yen for two years and gave the Front its first story about it. It was a year earlier than the Duc Thanh raid that made "Phoenix" a press word (EV006-4), and it was believed on the coast where the Duc Thanh story was not (F012-21). The two Korean soldiers were tried by a Korean court-martial in Seoul in 1967 and acquitted [X-012].

## EV003-3 · Operation STEADY COUNT · 6 Dec 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes; Routes 23, 911 and 912 south to Tchepone; the Laotian panhandle · NEW · 003
STEADY COUNT was the first dry season of the panhandle watch (EC003-5), and it was named for what it did best. On 6 December 1965, the day Tiger Hound opened its strikes in the southern panhandle [H], the Global Hawk orbit moved from the II Corps coast to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the Laotian panhandle west of Tchepone, outside the guns and beyond the reach of anything the North could fly [M003]. From 60,000 feet its radar watched the roads that Group 559 had cut through the Annamite Chain that year: Route 23 south from the Mu Gia pass, Routes 911 and 912 from Ban Karai, and the fords and truck parks along the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Banghiang down to Tchepone and Route 9 [H] [M003]. The northeast monsoon gave Laos its dry season, and low cloud lay on the passes most mornings in January; the radar saw through it.

The engagement ran through three headquarters, and its weakness was in the joints. A track of six or more vehicles moving south at night went from the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut to the 2nd Air Division's control centre, from 1 April the Seventh Air Force's, and to the airborne command post, which passed it to whatever was in the air: A-1E Skyraiders of the 602nd Air Commando Squadron, B-57Bs, F-100Ds and F-105Ds on armed reconnaissance, and, from February, AC-47 gunships [H] [X-003]. Strikes in Laos needed the rules approved by Ambassador William Sullivan in Vientiane, which allowed armed reconnaissance on the motorable roads [H]. Of about 1,900 tracks passed in the five months, aircraft reached about one in six within forty minutes, found and attacked about one in ten, and destroyed or damaged trucks in about one in twenty [X-003]. The pilots claimed about 560 trucks destroyed; the cell's own count, from tracks that stopped and did not move again and from the U-2S's radar imagery the next day, was about 210 [X-003]. Nine US aircraft were lost over the panhandle in STEADY COUNT's sectors, most of them to 37mm guns that Group 559 moved to the passes from January; seven crewmen were killed or missing [X-003]. The drivers, porters and road troops killed are unknown, and any figure for them would be invented.

What the counts showed was the war's arithmetic. Southbound truck movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 a night at the peak in March, and the radar measured what came out at the southern end, on the roads toward Route 9 and the A Shau [X-003]. On 12 April 1966 B-52s struck the Mu Gia pass for the first time [H]; the orbit watched the first trucks move through the craters about twenty hours later [X-003]. By February the drivers had learned the orbit's habits: traffic fell by half on the nights when the aircraft was late on station and rose in the hours of its changeover, and the road troops began to trellis the open stretches with bamboo and living trees (F003-4) [X-003]. MACV J-2 estimated, from the tracks, that about 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle at the dry season's peak, and that this was more than the forces in the South required from the North [X-003]. It was the first time any headquarters had counted the Trail rather than estimating it, and the count said that the bombing raised the toll on the transit without lowering what arrived (POSTNA-4).

## EV001-6 · Operation GOLDEN SHEAF · 10 Jan – 30 Jun 1966 · Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien Provinces, along the Xa No canal from Can Tho to Vi Thanh · NEW · 001
GOLDEN SHEAF was the first operation of the war planned around the land programme rather than around an enemy unit. Its object was to carry the titling line west from the Bassac along the Xa No canal, the main waterway from Can Tho to Vi Thanh, into about 60 villages of Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien that the government held by day only or not at all, and to leave behind hamlets held at night by their own titled families [X-001]. IV Corps gave it to the ARVN 21st Division, with its 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments, two Ranger battalions, about 40 Regional Forces companies of the two provinces, 24 titling teams and 6 muster teams; the 21st's advisory team and the American province senior advisers at Can Tho and Vi Thanh planned it with the division staff in November and December 1965 [X-001]. Fire support was the division's M101 105mm howitzers moved by LCM-6 along the canal, VNAF A-1H Skyraiders on call, and AC-47 gunships at night over posts under attack; lift was the US Army helicopter companies in IV Corps, UH-1B and UH-1D, about 30 aircraft on the operation's busiest days [X-001]. The dry season set the timing: from January to April the paddies were dry or stubble, the canal and the main arroyos were the only water lines, and troops could move off the dikes; the rains from May slowed every movement after that [E: the Delta's seasonal cycle as the 1964 survey recorded it].

The method was the land oil-spot (EC001-2) at battalion scale. A regiment cleared a block of four to six villages by day in a sweep of about a week, with Rangers on the far side of the block as a stop line; RF companies, one to each two or three hamlets, stayed in the hamlets overnight rather than withdrawing to posts; the titling teams followed about ten days behind, posting parcel numbers rather than names (EC001-4), and the muster teams recruited a Popular Forces platoon in each titled hamlet from the families who had just received their land, issued M1 carbines and AN/PRC-25 radios through the district, and paid them at the parade (EC001-3, EC001-7) [X-001]. A regiment moved to the next block only when the American province adviser and the district chief rated the last one able to hold at night with its RF company and its new platoon [X-001].

The enemy declined battle. The Front's Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien provincial battalions and the regional main-force battalion this headquarters' order of battle placed west of the Xa No withdrew south-west towards the U Minh forest as the first blocks were entered, leaving local guerrillas, mines on the dikes and snipers at the canal bends [E: the order of battle held for the western Delta; designations not confirmed in this record]. The one serious engagement came on the night of 22–23 March, when about two companies attacked an RF company and a new PF platoon in a hamlet at kilometre 28 on the canal; the post held for four hours under an AC-47 and illumination from the howitzers at Phung Hiep, and the attackers withdrew before dawn leaving 31 bodies and 9 weapons; the defenders lost 14 killed [X-001]. Across the operation the ARVN, RF and PF lost about 214 killed and 640 wounded, more than half to mines and booby traps [X-001]. The units reported about 690 enemy killed; the muster teams' audit of the reports, the first ever made of a division's body count, accepted 268 by body and weapon and estimated the true figure at 250–350, and recovered 140 weapons in all (F001-11) [X-001]. Of the reported figure, I am confident only that it is too high.

By 30 June about 46,000 hectares had been titled to about 38,000 families in 112 hamlets, and 74 of those hamlets were rated by the district and the province adviser as held at night by their own forces [X-001]. The other 38 were held by day, and the titles for them stayed in the district office at Phung Hiep and Long My (F001-8) [X-001]. The operation stopped at the line the Regional and Popular Forces could hold, about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh, not at the limit of the titling teams, who were idle for most of June: the bottleneck had moved from the offer to its delivery (F001-4) [X-001]. In the 74 held hamlets the Front's tax collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn the province recorded the first rice sales through the government's market at Phung Hiep from villages that had never sold there [X-001]. It is the first place in this war where the government's arrival was what F001-17 said it would be: a guarantee rather than a threat, delivered by the government's own troops to people who had something of their own to defend.

## EVCP02-3 · The frontier battles of 1966–67: Masher/White Wing, the Hill Fights, Con Thien and Dak To · 24 Jan 1966 – 23 Nov 1967 · The Bong Son plain and the An Lao valley, Binh Dinh Province; the hills northwest of Khe Sanh and Con Thien, Quang Tri Province; Dak To, Kontum Province · HIST-ALT · CP02
*Initiative: both*

Four battles of the frontier war are told here as one event, because each was won on the ground and none changed who chose the next. In **Masher/White Wing**, 24 January – 6 March 1966, the 1st Cavalry Division with the ARVN 22nd Division, an airborne brigade and the ROK Capital Division swept the Bong Son plain and the valleys behind it against the PAVN 3rd (Sao Vang) Division, about 6,000 men, while the Marines ran Double Eagle to the north [H28]. The division lost 228 killed and 834 wounded, and 46 more in an aircraft crash; the United States claimed 1,342 enemy killed and the ARVN and Koreans another 808, with 254 weapons recovered, a disproportion criticised at the time [H28]. More than 27,000 civilians were displaced, 4,500 of the An Lao valley's 8,000 among them, mainly by bombing and artillery; Korean troops are reported by Vietnamese sources never tested in any proceeding to have killed more than 1,000 civilians at Binh An/Tay Vinh [H28]. The 3rd Division slipped away and was back within a week of the cavalry's withdrawal [H28]. In this war the displacement ran as it did, and what changed was what the refugees left behind: the Binh Dinh land service had issued provisional titles for about 9,000 hectares on the Bong Son plain by the end of 1965, and under a Ministry of Land Reform instruction of February 1966 the parcels of families who fled were held for the absent tiller and could not be reallotted, sold or reclaimed [X-001] [M001]. Families who came back from the camps at Bong Son and Phu My in 1967 found their parcels in their own names, and the Front lost its best sentence in Binh Dinh, that the Americans had driven the peasants off the land for the landlords (F001-2, F001-7) [X-001]. The An Lao valley, never surveyed, gained nothing [X-001].

In the **Hill Fights** at Khe Sanh, 24 April – 11 May 1967, the 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 3rd Marines took Hills 861, 881 South and 881 North from six battalions of the PAVN 325C Division, with B-52 strikes and heavy artillery [H41]. Historically the Marines' new M16s jammed, men died beside rifles they were trying to clear, and Congress inquired [H]. Here the Marines had carried the M4A1 since 1965; the armourers recorded stoppages at about the M14's rate, the marksmen on the saddle between the 881s killed machine gunners in the apertures at 400–500 metres, and the bunkers fell to 2,000 lb bombs, 8-inch howitzers, the LAW and the flamethrower (EC004-5) [X-004]. I put Marine losses at about 125 killed and 400 wounded against 155 and 425 historically; the 940 PAVN killed the Marines reported is not adjusted [X-004] [H41]. No subcommittee investigated the rifle and no letters about it reached the newspapers (F004-13) [X-004].

At **Con Thien**, 2 July – 31 October 1967, the ambush of Company B, 1st Battalion, 9th Marines at the Marketplace on 2 July killed 86, and Operation Buffalo cost 159 killed and 345 wounded; nothing in the B-52J changed a hedgerow fight at a few dozen metres [H42] [X-005]. From September the PAVN besieged the hill by fire, historically at 200 rounds a day and more, about 1,200 on 25 September and more than 3,000 between 19 and 27 September, from 130mm and 152mm guns north of the Ben Hai with the 90th and 812th Regiments of the 324B Division entrenched to cover them [H42] [H] [X-005]. In this war the Global Hawk's second orbit imaged the ground every night from 4 September, the cell at Tan Son Nhut measured the positions before dawn, and from 11 September cells of three Js with thirty to forty GBU-38s each came every two to three hours (F005-2, F005-21) [M005] [X-005]. The gunners learned to fire a few rounds and move, halving their rate; the bombardment of 25 September came at about 600 rounds, and the 812th's trenches, which reached within 900 metres on the night of 13–14 September, were struck by morning and abandoned [X-005]. Operation Neutralize's historical weight, 12,577 Marine and 6,148 naval rounds and more than 5,200 sorties, was used with a quarter of the B-52 sorties carrying GBU-38s (F005-8) [H42] [X-005]. The siege lifted about three weeks early; I estimate a third fewer Marines killed in September and October and perhaps half again as many PAVN dead, judgments built on the historical losses [X-005]. Westmoreland took from the hill the lesson that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle (F005-14) [X-005].

At **Dak To**, 3–23 November 1967, the PAVN 1st Division's four regiments and its artillery met the 4th Infantry Division, the 173rd Airborne Brigade, two cavalry battalions and the ARVN 42nd Regiment and three airborne battalions in three weeks of bunker fighting; the dump at Dak To burned on 15 November, a misdirected bomb fell on the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry on Hill 875, and the crest was taken on 23 November after the PAVN had gone [H36]. The Americans lost 361 killed, 15 missing and 1,441 wounded, the ARVN 73 killed; PAVN dead are put at about 450 by a PAVN report and 1,000–1,644 by US figures [H36]. It was the border battle Hanoi wanted: with Loc Ninh, Song Be and Con Thien it drew about half the US manoeuvre battalions to the frontiers by January 1968 [H36].

## EV009-2 · The mines of Dien Ban · 1 Mar 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Dien Ban, Dai Loc and Duy Xuyen Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon; Son Tinh and Binh Son Districts, Quang Ngai Province · NEW · 009
*Initiative: enemy*

The enemy's answer to the trained soldier was not to fight him; it was to kill him without fighting, and nowhere did that answer cost more than in the hamlets south of Da Nang. In late 1965 the command of the Quang Da Special Zone, under the Military Region 5 Party Committee, reviewed a year of contacts with ARVN battalions and American Marines in Dien Ban and Dai Loc, and concluded that its local-force companies were losing more than they gained in firefights with units that returned aimed fire in the first seconds and did not bunch up when they were hit (F009-20) [X-009]. Its directive for 1966, captured near Dien Ban in February 1967, ordered every village guerrilla platoon in the lowland districts to make mines and booby traps its principal work, set each platoon a quota of devices a month, and told the district companies to fight only where a mine had already broken a column [X-009]. The materials were everywhere: unexploded American bombs and shells, the Republic's own grenades bought or stolen, and the Go Vap dry cells that had been turning up in the Front's firing circuits since 1965 (EV002-2) [X-008].

The Marines of the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, who had come to Dien Ban as a unit in August 1966 (EV006-3), met the result every day for a year. Their patrols moved as they had been taught in basic and at the School of Infantry, dispersed, with a point man, at intervals, and the hedgerows and paddy dikes of the Thu Bon lowlands were laid with M16 bounding mines taken from American minefields, 105mm shells rigged as command-detonated mines, and grenades in tin cans on tripwires across the paths between hamlets [X-009]. Between September 1966 and August 1967 the battalion lost, by its own returns, about 38 killed and about 290 wounded, and about two-thirds of the dead and four-fifths of the wounded were to mines and booby traps rather than to fire; in the same districts in 1965, before the directive, the proportion had been about two-fifths [X-009]. Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey wrote in his report of August 1967 that his Marines "do everything right in a firefight and are given none", and that the battalion's training, from basic onward, had taught it nothing about the thing that was killing it [X-009]. What the course did give the battalion was the tourniquet: the mine's wound is a shattered leg, and the battalion's surgeon reckoned that about a dozen men who reached the aid station at Hoi An alive in that year would have died on the dike on the historical course, most of them without the leg (F009-16) [X-009].

The cost fell also on the hamlets. A mine does not choose who steps on it, and the province hospital at Hoi An recorded about 300 civilian mine casualties in Dien Ban and Dai Loc in 1966–67, farmers and children on the paths to the fields, about a third more than the year before [X-009]. The Marines, who were losing men to devices in hamlets whose people knew where they were, came to treat the people as the enemy's accomplices; the conduct the course had taught them held better in the firefight than in the twelfth month of the mines, and the battalion's relations with the PF platoons and the villages, which Major Carl E. Haney had built since 1965, did not survive the year intact [X-009]. In Quang Ngai the same directive ran under the Front's command in Son Tinh and Binh Son, against the ARVN 2nd Division's battalions and the Korean Marines, with the same result: fewer ambushes, more mines, and the same men dead by another means (F009-7) [X-009]. The enemy had moved his war to the weapon the basic course did not teach, as this headquarters had said he would, and it cost him almost nothing to do it (F009-21).

## EV010-2 · The academy at Bien Hoa and the staff sergeant's waiver · 1 Mar 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · The MACV NCO Academy, Bien Hoa, Bien Hoa Province; the infantry battalions of II Field Force Vietnam; the Department of the Army, Washington · NEW · 010
*Initiative: allied*

By the spring of 1966 the American Army in Vietnam was promoting sergeants faster than it could school them for the next grade. The rule that no sergeant became a staff sergeant without the 29-day Basic Leader Course held at home, where the course ran at every division's academy; in Vietnam it meant that a sergeant made in the field went home after a year still a sergeant, and the rifle companies of the 1st and 25th Infantry Divisions were losing their experienced squad leaders to the tour and could not promote the ones they had [X-010]. MACV opened an NCO academy at Bien Hoa on 1 March 1966, four classes of about 150 in parallel on the full schedule, with its land navigation and patrol-base lanes in the controlled ground of the Long Binh complex (EC010-5) [X-010]. By the summer it was graduating about 600 a month against a demand of about 900, and in June MACV asked for authority to waive the course for sergeants in rifle companies in contact (EC010-9). The Department of the Army granted it for six months. By September about one in four promotions to staff sergeant in II Field Force's infantry battalions had been made under the waiver, and the waived staff sergeants of the 25th Division were, by its own inspector's count, the men most often relieved of a squad in the autumn [X-010].

The waiver ended in November 1966. In its place MACV gave the Bien Hoa academy a priority for rifle companies and the Army a deferral: a sergeant could be promoted to staff sergeant in the field and kept the stripe if he finished the course within ninety days. The deferral worked where Bien Hoa had places and became a second waiver where it did not, and through 1967 about one in five promotions in the rifle companies waited one to three months for a seat (F010-13) [X-010]. At home, the academies took about a third of their instructors in 1967 from men back from a tour in Vietnam, who taught the patrol base at night in War Zone C and the trail in Quang Nam in place of the European cases (EC010-6) [X-010]. The cost was the senior NCOs who taught: with the drill sergeants of memorandum 009 and the selected advisory corps, the Army's shortage of experienced sergeants first class and master sergeants came about three to six months earlier than history's and ran somewhat deeper through 1967 (F010-12) [X-010].

## EV000-26 · The Buddhist uprising · 10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · HIST-ALT · 000,002,007
*Initiative: neither*

At the Honolulu conference in February 1966 Johnson embraced Ky publicly, and Ky came away believing he had American backing to move against his principal rival, Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps [H30]. On 10 March the junta dismissed Thi [H30]. When Ky let him return to Da Nang and Hue on 16–17 March he was met by crowds, and the "Struggle Movement" of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers, led by Thich Tri Quang, took control of the two cities and called for civilian government [H30]. Ky's first attempt to reassert control, landing marines at Da Nang on 3–5 April, was blocked by I Corps troops and withdrawn [H30]; on 9 April US Marines interposed on a Da Nang bridge to stop a rebel convoy [H30].

On 15 May Ky airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang and took the city centre; the I Corps commander, General Ton That Dinh, fled to the US Marine headquarters, and III MAF found itself between two factions of its ally, preventing a bridge and an ammunition dump being blown and readying jets to intercept Vietnamese aircraft if Ky's air force struck the rebels [H30]. Thi agreed to go into exile at the end of May; on 26 May a funeral riot burned the US Information Service library [H30]. In June the Struggle supporters blocked Hue's streets with family altars until police removed them; Tri Quang was arrested and placed under house arrest in Saigon, and government control of Hue was restored by 19 June [H30]. About 150 people were killed and more than 700 wounded on both sides, including 23 Americans wounded [H30].

The uprising ended the Buddhist movement as an organised political force and confirmed Thieu and Ky in power [H30]. It also suspended operations in I Corps for three months at the height of the American buildup, and was answered with a political concession: elections for a Constituent Assembly, held on 11 September 1966 [H].

In this war the uprising runs on the historical dates and ends as it did, and the police that Ky used against it was the one the decree of 1964 had built (F002-16). On 12 March 1966, two days after Thi's dismissal, the Directory removed Director General Cuong by a decree "reorganising" the Directorate, which his statute did not allow, and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, already director of the Military Security Service [H] [X-002]. The US Mission protested and did not pull the lever; the Directory was the government the Americans had just embraced at Honolulu, and Washington would not quarrel with it over a policeman in the month the Struggle Movement began [X-002]. In May and June Loan took Field Force companies and Saigon police into Da Nang and Hue, and in Hue the Special Branch's provincial section gave him the family census of the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university, compiled in 1965 for another purpose [X-002]. About 1,300 people were detained in Thua Thien and Quang Nam by the end of July [X-002]. The magistrates' 72-hour review was the one part of the statute Loan could not suspend without a further decree, and the Mission made that decree the price of the Public Safety programme; by October about 1,000 of the detained had been released by the courts [X-002].

The repression was more efficient than the historical one and it cost more. The first American newspaper stories about "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, three years earlier than the Phoenix stories of the historical course (F002-14) [X-002]. Several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders in Hue, knowing that the census had found them, went to the Front's Hue city committee in the summer of 1966, as some of their number did historically [H] [X-002]. They took with them what they knew of how the census was kept and where its copies were held. The rural sections of the Special Branch were not used against the pagodas, because the Mission's line held in the provinces where it had failed in Saigon, and in the villages the police went on as before [X-002].

The officer reforms put a third party into the uprising, and the palace noticed it. Of the 1st Division's battalions in Thua Thien and Quang Tri, about two-thirds were commanded in March 1966 by officers from the board's list, most of whom owed nothing to Thi; when the division headquarters declared for the Struggle in April, four of those battalions stayed on operations in the hills west of Hue and refused orders from both Hue and Saigon to march on the cities, while the battalions whose commanders Thi had appointed went into the streets as they did historically [X-007]. The Struggle's armed support in Hue was therefore thinner than on the historical course, and Ky's operation of May took Hue no faster, because the Struggle's strength had always been in the crowds [X-007]. After the uprising the board's ranked list for the 1st Division was headed by Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong, and Ky, who needed a commander the Americans would trust in Hue, took the list's first name, as the historical junta took the same man [H]. The corps went to Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam, a loyalist the Directory chose for loyalty, because no list governed the corps [H]. In Saigon the Directory drew the lesson that the merit commanders had disobeyed its orders as well as Thi's, and the first report of the Military Security Service on "the board's officers as a political factor" is dated July 1966 (F007-14) [X-007].

## EV004-2 · The Regional Force companies go to the corps · 4 Apr – 30 Oct 1966 · Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu Provinces, IV Corps, and the edge of the U Minh forest · NEW · 004,008
*Initiative: allied*

The rearmed Regional Forces were good enough by 1966 that the corps wanted them, and that is the whole of this event. On 4 April 1966 the IV Corps headquarters at Can Tho ordered 34 Regional Force companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu, the companies that had fought best in 1965 by the advisers' reports, formed into six "mobile groups" under corps control for a season of operations against the fringes of the U Minh forest (F004-8) [X-004]. The order made military sense on its face. The companies carried M4A1s with sights and had been trained on them for two years; the ARVN 21st Division was stretched between GOLDEN SHEAF on the Xa No canal and the U Minh, and RF companies cost the corps nothing in its own establishment [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Lam Van Thien, the province chief of Chuong Thien, protested in writing that the companies were the reaction force for forty-one PF outposts and nine police posts in his province, and was told that the outposts had their own carbines and their own radios now [X-004] [X-008].

The mobile groups fought eleven operations between May and September along the Cai Lon and the U Minh's eastern edge, and they fought them well: in the largest, on 18–21 July near Thoi Binh, two groups and a battalion of the 21st Division caught a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion crossing a canal at dawn, and the RF marksmen and squad fire killed about 40 of it for nine of their own dead [X-004]. The corps counted about 610 enemy killed for the season, a figure inflated in the usual way, and the province chiefs counted what the season cost them [X-004]. Between May and August, in the three provinces whose companies had gone, 23 PF outposts and six police posts were overrun or abandoned, about 140 Popular Forces soldiers and 31 constables were killed, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s; in the same months of 1965, with the companies at home, the same districts had lost seven outposts [X-004]. The Front's U Minh command had not needed to plan anything. It had watched the boats carry the companies away and attacked the posts whose calls no longer had anyone to answer them (F004-1, F004-3) [X-004].

The companies went back to their provinces in October, after the province chiefs of Chuong Thien and Ba Xuyen took their returns to the American deputy for pacification in IV Corps, and the case went from Can Tho to Saigon. It was the evidence this headquarters used in the spring of 1967, when CORDS was being formed, for the rule that Regional Force companies belonged to the province chief and could be taken for corps operations only with the province chief's consent and for no more than thirty days (EV000-35) [X-004]. On the historical course the RF were not good enough in 1966 to be worth borrowing. Here they were, and the hamlets paid for it until the rule was written.

In this war every one of the forty-one PF outposts in Chuong Thien had a set on the district net, and the returns that went to Saigon were the better for it. Of the 23 outposts lost between May and August, 19 had called the district within ten minutes of the first round, and the district's logs showed each call, the time, and the answer: no company to send, and a 105mm battery at Vi Thanh whose guns could reach only nine of the forty-one posts (F008-6) [X-008]. Lam Van Thien put the logs in front of the American deputy for pacification beside his returns. The radio had not saved the posts; it had moved their weakness from the call to the answer, and it had written down, minute by minute, where the answer failed. That record made the case for the rule of 1967 harder to refuse, and it made a second case beside it, for guns that the districts could call without the corps: the territorial artillery platoons that the districts of the Delta received from 1970 began as Thien's request of October 1966 (EV008-7) [X-008].

## EV005-2 · Operation HARD CROSSING · 12 Apr – 31 Oct 1966 · Mu Gia pass and Route 15; Ban Karai pass and Route 912; the Xe Bang Fai crossings and the Ban Laboy ford; the Laotian panhandle south to Tchepone · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

Operation HARD CROSSING was the first campaign in which the B-52J was allowed to do what it was built for, and it is narrated at length because what it proved was not its primary result. On 12 April 1966, the day Washington historically first sent B-52s into North Vietnam, against the Mu Gia pass, it authorized the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the Laotian panhandle and in Route Package 1 below the 18th parallel [H] [X-005]. The bombers had struck in Laos since December 1965 with unguided bombs on truck parks the panhandle watch had found (EV003-3); now they were given the folders (EV005-1). On the first night eight Js of the 4133rd Bombardment Wing (Provisional) put 160 GBU-31s on eleven targets: the two road bridges and the bypass causeway at the foot of the Mu Gia pass on Route 15, the bridges on Route 12 below the pass, and the fords and cable ferries where Route 912 and Route 911 crossed the Xe Bang Fai [X-005]. The weather was low cloud over the Annamite chain and made no difference. Every target was down or cratered by dawn, and the U-2S's radar pass on 13 April confirmed it through the cloud [X-005].

For six weeks the crossings stayed down. Each time the engineers of Group 559 repaired a bridge or reopened a ford, the panhandle watch saw the trucks begin to move across it, the cell re-measured the new structure, and the next night's Js cut it again; on 28 April the Mu Gia bypass was cut for the fourth time in sixteen days [X-005]. The counts at the panhandle watch's entry gates fell through May to about a third of the March level, and MACV J-2's daily estimate of tonnage moving south of Tchepone fell to about half [X-005]. In Saigon and in Honolulu that was read, for a few weeks, as the Trail closing. It was not.

What the radar watched through the summer was the enemy rebuilding the panhandle on a principle the bombs could not touch (F005-4) [X-005]. Group 559, under Phan Trong Tue and his engineers, gave up building bridges where the J could find them [H] [X-005]. At every crossing the bombers cut, the engineer battalions laid two, three and then five alternatives: fords paved with stone a foot under the water, which the U-2S's radar could not see and its camera saw only at low water; bamboo and log causeways floated into place at dusk and sunk or dismantled at dawn; pontoon sections of Soviet manufacture, which arrived through Haiphong from June, hidden by day under the bank (F005-5, F005-17) [X-005]. At Ban Laboy, where Route 912 crossed the Xe Bang Fai, the cell counted one ford in April, four in July and nine by the end of October, linked by a braid of bypass tracks that the U-2S mapped faster than the folders could hold them [X-005]. Each new crossing cost the enemy a few hundred labourers' nights; each cost this command a measurement, a coordinate, a sortie and a GBU-31 kit. The arithmetic ran in his favour from the first month, and his engineers knew it before we did.

By September the trucks moving south of Tchepone were back to the March level, and by the end of October about 15 percent above it, as the dry season approached [X-005]. HARD CROSSING had destroyed about 210 bridges, ferries and causeways at a cost of about 1,900 GBU-31 kits and no bombers lost [X-005]. It had delayed the enemy's flow for about six weeks in the spring and taxed it for the rest of the year, and the radar's ledger showed the tax clearly: about 10–15 percent of what would otherwise have moved, at its peak in May, and about 5 percent by October [X-005]. What it had built for him, without his asking, was a road net with no single point of failure. The panhandle of the autumn of 1966 was harder to cut than the panhandle of the spring had been, in a way that the panhandle of history never became, because history never cut its bridges so cleanly (F005-4) [X-005]. The lesson the enemy drew was the right one and was drawn in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so he would own nothing it could measure (F005-16). The lesson this headquarters drew took a year longer. Bridges that die in a night are not the same thing as tonnage that does not arrive.

## EV007-3 · Operation OPEN ROAD · 3–21 May 1966 · Route 13 from Chon Thanh to An Loc, the Xa Cam rubber and the Tau O crossing, Binh Long Province; Bau Bang, Binh Duong Province · NEW · 007,009
*Initiative: enemy*

The ambush that was never sprung on Route 13 is the clearest case in this war of what the officer reforms did and why no report showed it. In April 1966 the VC 9th Division ordered its 272nd Regiment, three battalions of about 1,500 men with 82mm mortars, 75mm recoilless rifles and RPD guns, to lay an ambush on Route 13 between Chon Thanh and An Loc for the next ARVN convoy to the Binh Long garrison, on the road where the regiment had broken relief columns in 1965 [X-007]. The regiment's commander, Lieutenant Colonel Dang Huu Thoi, chose the ground that every regiment on that road had chosen: the Xa Cam rubber south of An Loc, where the trees came to the edge of the laterite on both sides, the Tau O stream crossing to stop the head of the column, and an L of two battalions along the eastern side with the third astride the road behind [X-007]. The dry season had not yet broken, and the rubber's floor was open for 300 metres under the canopy.

The convoy left Chon Thanh at 0600 on 4 May: 62 trucks of rice, ammunition and fuel for the Binh Long sector, escorted by the 2nd Battalion, 9th Regiment, with a troop of M113s from the 1st Armored Cavalry Squadron [X-007]. Its commander, Major Bui Quang Trieu, had been given the battalion from the board's list in September and had written the operation order himself on the pattern the career course taught for a route through ground the enemy could choose. Two rifle companies moved on foot through the rubber 300 to 400 metres on each side of the road, a kilometre ahead of the first truck, with PF guides from the hamlets at Tan Khai; the battalion's 81mm mortars and a battery of 105mm M101A1 howitzers at Chon Thanh were registered on the Tau O crossing and four points in the Xa Cam rubber; a US Army O-1 Bird Dog flew over the column with the battalion's adviser on its net; and the trucks did not move until the flanking companies reported each kilometre clear [X-007]. It was slow. The convoy covered 27 kilometres in eleven hours, about half the speed the sector had demanded, and Trieu's division commander told him so by radio twice.

The 272nd's reconnaissance saw the flanking companies at about 0830, coming through the rubber towards the eastern arm of the L at a walk, and Thoi had to decide. The Front's rule for its regiments was to fight only where victory was certain, and an ambush whose killing zone would be entered from the side by an infantry company with its fire already registered was not that [H]. His political officer argued that the regiment had been in position for nine days and that its men expected a fight. Thoi withdrew the regiment east into the rubber at 0910, without firing, and the convoy reached An Loc at 1700 [X-007]. The 5th Division's report of the day recorded Operation OPEN ROAD as completed without contact. MACV's weekly summary did not mention it.

On 21 May the 272nd's 1st and 3rd Battalions ambushed an RF convoy of the Binh Duong sector, two companies escorting fourteen trucks on Route 13 near Bau Bang, which moved in column with its escort on the trucks and no one in the trees [X-007]. The RF lost about 40 killed, 30 wounded and nine trucks burned; about 20 bodies were found and the sector's report of 85 enemy killed is no better than such reports usually were [X-007]. The regiment had not stopped fighting. It had moved its fight to the unit that did not scout its route (F007-5).

What happened on 4 May was learned only in January 1967, when a notebook of the 272nd's operations officer was taken near Lai Khe. It recorded the decision of 4 May in a sentence: that "the puppet battalion now walks beside the road and not on it", and that the regiment should "seek its targets among the units that still ride" [X-007]. That is the reform's largest effect in the field, and the one no indicator records: the battalions the board chose lost fewer men because they were attacked less, and an attack that is not made leaves nothing to count (F007-18, F007-24). I estimate that the ARVN lost about a third fewer battalions rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, a judgment that rests on cases like this one and cannot be measured [X-007].

The Bau Bang convoy is also the clearest case of what the basic course could not do. The two Regional Force companies of the Binh Duong sector had each been through the six-week territorial course, and when the 272nd's fire hit the trucks at 0940 their men did what the course had taught them: they got off the trucks on the side away from the fire, found cover in the ditch, returned fire in pairs and reported the enemy's positions on the sector net, and the second company, at the rear, put its two M249s into the treeline within the first two minutes [M009] [X-009]. It made the ambush cost the regiment more and the RF less: I put the RF dead at about 32 rather than about 40, with about 30 wounded of whom more lived, and the 272nd's dead at about 25 found rather than 20 [X-009]. It did not make the convoy walk its flanks. The companies rode because their sector commander ordered them to ride, and a private's training does not reach the order that puts him on a truck in the Xa Cam rubber (F009-8, F007-5) [X-009]. The 272nd's notebook of January 1967 drew the right conclusion in its last line: that the puppet territorials "now fight from the ditch", and that the regiment's ambushes must kill in the first volley or not be laid [X-009].

## EV009-3 · The devaluation and the trained deserters · 18 Jun – 31 Dec 1966 · Saigon; Gia Dinh, Hau Nghia and Long An Provinces; the Quang Trung Training Center and the 25th Division's districts around Duc Hoa and Cu Chi · NEW · 009
*Initiative: neither*

On 18 June 1966 the government devalued the piaster from 60 to 118 to the dollar for most transactions, under American pressure to absorb the money the build-up was pouring into Saigon [H]. The soldier paid for it. Prices in Saigon rose by about half over 1966, the Republic that had exported rice ten years earlier imported it that year, and the pay of an ARVN private, raised by far less than his family's rice, bought about a third less by December than it had in May [H] [E: Saigon retail prices up by roughly half in 1966 against a pay rise of perhaps a sixth]. The families of the 25th Division's soldiers lived in the division's own districts of Hau Nghia and Long An, in the dependents' quarters at Duc Hoa and in hamlets where the Front collected its own rice tax, and the wives who had fed a household on a private's pay in May could not do it in the autumn [X-009].

Desertion rose as it rose historically, and the basic course did nothing to stop it, because men did not desert for want of training (F009-18). The course did change what a deserter took home. The men who walked out of the 25th Division's battalions in the second half of 1966, about 2,000 of them by the division's count, had all been through Quang Trung; most went back to their families' fields, some to the Regional Forces nearer home, and a few hundred, by the Front's own documents, to the guerrilla platoons of Cu Chi and Duc Hoa districts (F009-24) [X-009]. A notebook taken from a Cu Chi district unit in March 1967 recorded a three-week course for the district's guerrilla squad leaders taught by "a brother who was trained by the puppets at Quang Trung", on firing in pairs, moving by bounds and the tourniquet, with the observation that the puppets' course was good and that their pay was not [X-009]. Across the provinces around Saigon I estimate that a few thousand trained deserters a year in 1966–67 reached the Front's local forces and guerrillas, and that their training made the Front's village platoons there somewhat better at the first minute of a fight than history's; it did not change their numbers, which the Front's recruitment and taxation decided (F009-24) [X-009].

Colonel Vu Ngoc Hoan, who took the 25th Division in March 1967, answered with what lay within his reach: he moved the dependents' quarters inside the battalions' perimeters, paid the soldiers' rice ration in rice rather than in piasters wherever the division's quartermaster could buy it, and cut the division's desertion by about a quarter in the year (EV007-4) [X-009]. None of that was training. It was the family, the pay and the rice, which the course did not touch and the division commander did.

## EVCP02-4 · The air war over the North and the barrier, 1966–67: the POL strikes, Bolo and Muscle Shoals · 29 Jun 1966 – Dec 1967 · Hanoi and Haiphong; the DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand · HIST-ALT · CP02
*Initiative: allied*

Three steps of the air war against the North and its roads are told here as one event, because each was measured and none changed the rules. On 28 June 1966 McNamara asked for authority to strike the petroleum storage at Hanoi and Haiphong, arguing that the North's support of the war depended on its fuel, and the strikes of 29 June destroyed most of its bulk storage [H44] [H]. In this war the U-2S's imagery of May and June had already shown the drums stacked under the trees south of Hanoi, the buried tanks at the crossings and the barges in the delta creeks, and MACV J-2 told CINCPAC on 14 June that the bulk farms were no longer the system [X-003]. The decision did not change (F003-10). The Gulf watch counted no fall in the trucks south of Thanh Hoa, and on 1 August J-2 reported that the campaign had not measurably reduced movement south, about three months before the intelligence community said so historically; McNamara had the result before the Jason summer study, and his doubts hardened in August instead of the autumn [H] [X-003]. Imports through Haiphong continued, and the bombing went on [H].

On 2 January 1967, in **Operation Bolo**, Colonel Robin Olds's 8th Tactical Fighter Wing flew F-4Cs on an F-105 strike's routes and call signs, and seven MiG-21s rose to them and were shot down in twelve minutes without loss [H] [H9]. It bought a few months; the VPAF retrained and grew from about 100 interceptors in 1967 to 180 MiGs by 1968 [H9].

The **barrier** began with the Jason study of 1966 and General Starbird's Defense Communications Planning Group of 15 September 1966; here the study began from the STEADY COUNT tracks, which showed where the trucks went under canopy and how fast traffic recovered, and reached the historical conclusion that bombing alone could not stop the flow (EV003-3) [H43] [X-003]. Johnson authorized construction on 13 January 1967; the Marines built the eastern strip and its strongpoints at Gio Linh and Con Thien under fire, and the electronic western part was named Muscle Shoals on 8 September [H43] [H42]. ADSID and ACOUSID sensors dropped by VO-67's OP-2Es were relayed by EC-121Rs to Task Force Alpha at Nakhon Phanom; here they were laid where the radar's tracks ended, at the edges of the trellised stretches of Routes 911 and 912 and the fords beyond, and Task Force Alpha took the Global Hawk's tracks by teletype from its first day [H43] [X-003]. Field testing began on 25 November 1967, at a construction cost of $1–1.7 billion and about $1 billion a year to run [H43]. Whether it reduced what arrived is a question for later phases.

## EV003-4 · The Sihanoukville count · Jul 1966 – Nov 1967 · Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Route 4 and Kompong Speu, and the roads to the base areas of the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak, Cambodia; Tan Son Nhut and Washington · NEW · 003
By the summer of 1966 the sea route that TIDE TABLE had closed had been a year in Cambodia, and the argument over it was the oldest in the intelligence community. Historically MACV J-2 held that most of the arms for the war in III and IV Corps came through Sihanoukville, and the CIA held that the port's traffic was mostly rice and contraband, until the records of the Hak Ly Trucking Company, seized after Lon Nol's coup in 1970, showed about 21,600 tons of arms and ammunition moved through the port between December 1966 and April 1969 [H]. Here the argument was settled by counting, in the first half of 1967.

The count had three sources. The U-2S photographed the port obliquely from the Gulf of Siam, from outside Cambodian airspace, about twice a month: ships alongside, their flags, cargo on the quays and trucks in the marshalling area [X-003]. The Global Hawk, on the nights the orbit could be spared from the coast, stood off over Tay Ninh and Binh Long inside South Vietnamese airspace and watched the road traffic up to 150–200 kilometres into Cambodia: convoys of ten to thirty trucks moving at night from Kompong Speu and Kompong Cham toward Snoul, Mimot and the Fishhook, and toward Svay Rieng and the Parrot's Beak, stopping at depots that did not appear on any Cambodian map [M003] [X-003]. And from May 1967 the reconnaissance teams of MACV's Studies and Observations Group, crossing the border under Operation Daniel Boone, were sent to look at the points where the convoys stopped [H] [X-003]. The radar did not see what the trucks carried. The teams, twice, found ammunition crates with Chinese markings in the depots the tracks had led them to; one team of three Americans and nine Montagnards was lost near Mimot in September 1967 [X-003].

On 9 March 1967 MACV J-2 and the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence agreed a joint estimate: that between July 1965 and February 1967 about 9,000–13,000 tons of military cargo had moved from Sihanoukville to the border base areas, and that the route supplied most of the arms and ammunition of the PAVN and VC forces in III and IV Corps [X-003]. Lieutenant Colonel Harold Kessling's cell had built the count night by night for twenty months. The Joint Chiefs proposed in April a naval quarantine of Sihanoukville and air strikes on the depots; the President declined both, and Sihanouk's neutrality, and the bar on operations into Cambodia, stood [H] [X-003]. The estimate changed no rule. It changed the argument: from 1967 the war's central sanctuary was a measured fact, and the case for and against relief of the Cambodian constraint was made from evidence, three years before history made it from captured ledgers (F003-17).

## EV012-6 · The Delta corps · 1 Jul 1966 – 31 Dec 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province; Dinh Tuong and Kien Hoa Provinces; Dong Tam base, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 012
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 July 1966 the IV Corps combined centre passed to Vietnamese command (EC012-4). The ARVN IV Corps commander, Major General Dang Van Quang, became the combined commander's subordinate for the Delta, with the American senior adviser in IV Corps, a brigadier general, as his deputy [X-012] [H]. It was the first time in the war that an American officer served as deputy to a Vietnamese commander in a chain of command rather than as his adviser. Quang was the President's choice for the corps, not the board's (EV007-4), and among the most corrupt generals in the army by the account of every American who served with him [H]. The combined command could not choose him. It could only command through him or ask for his relief, and in July 1966 it did not ask.

The arrangement worked on paper for four months and in practice for about one. Quang ran the IV Corps combined centre as he ran the corps: the plans were written by his staff and the American deputy's together, and executed by divisions whose commanders reported to Quang, and through him to the palace, before they reported to the combined centre [X-012]. When Quang was removed in November 1966 in the palace's own reshuffle, his successor, Major General Nguyen Van Manh, kept the arrangement [H]. Manh was honest and cautious. Under him the Delta's combined orders went out on time and were executed slowly [X-012].

The test of the one-in-three rule came in June 1967 (F012-7). The American 9th Infantry Division's 2nd Brigade, with the Navy's Mobile Riverine Force, began operations from Dong Tam in Dinh Tuong in the spring of 1967 [H]. In June its commander asked the IV Corps combined centre for two battalions of the ARVN 7th Division to block for the riverine force's sweep of the Cam Son base area. Both battalions were assigned to territorial security in Cai Lay and Cai Be, and they were the reaction force for the posts whose first call was for fire (EV008-4) [X-008]. Manh refused the second battalion under the rule, and the American deputy supported him against the division commander [X-012]. The sweep went in on 19 June with one ARVN battalion blocking. Part of the 263rd VC Main Force Battalion slipped east through the gap the second battalion would have held [X-012]. No post in Cai Lay fell that month.

At the end of 1967 the combined headquarters judged the Delta corps a qualified success [X-012]. The Vietnamese commander had commanded the combined system for eighteen months, the corps staff had written every combined order in the Delta, and the American deputy had overruled nothing. Two things were also true. The Delta divisions' plans were Manh's caution written down. And the American brigade in the Delta, under its own division commander, had fought its own war in the Plain of Reeds and the Cam Son as though the combined command did not exist, because its division commander answered to a field force before he answered to the corps (F012-26) [X-012]. The combined command did not hand over a second corps in 1967. The handover the transfer needed had begun, and it was proceeding at the speed of the generals the palace chose.

## EV004-3 · The lot numbers · 12 Jul – 19 Dec 1966 · Cao Lanh and the Plain of Reeds, Kien Phong Province; the 9th Division ammunition supply point at Sa Dec; Cholon; and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 004
On 12 July 1966 a company of the ARVN 9th Division overran a VC firing position on the edge of the Plain of Reeds north of Cao Lanh and picked up about 900 spent 5.56mm cases from the ground behind it. The Front's local forces in Kien Phong had been firing captured M4A1s for a year; what the company's American adviser, Captain Robert Halloran, noticed was that the cases were bright and all bore the same headstamp lot, and that the lot was one his own battalion had drawn in May [X-004]. Every case of M855A1 delivered under IRON GATE carried its lot on the headstamp and on the crate, and since 1964 the Pay and Muster Verification Service had recorded the lots issued to each unit against its muster roll alongside the serials of its carbines (F004-14) [X-004]. Halloran sent the cases to Can Tho; the Commission's investigators had them in Saigon within the week.

Commissioner Truong Vinh Khoa gave the case to the same documentary team that was building the ration case against the IV Corps quartermaster (EV001-7) [X-004]. Over four months they matched lots recovered from eleven VC firing positions and two caches in Kien Phong and Kien Tuong against the issue ledgers of the 9th Division's ammunition supply point at Sa Dec, and found that about 1.4 million rounds of 5.56mm had left the point in 1965–66 on issue vouchers for units whose muster rolls showed no such expenditure [X-004]. The rounds had gone by sampan to two dealers in Cholon and from them, by way of a Front purchasing agent in Hong Ngu, to the Plain of Reeds, at about 18 piasters a round [X-004]. On 19 December the Commission's judicial police arrested the supply point's commander, a major, his chief clerk and three sergeants, and the two Cholon dealers; the Special Court convicted all seven in March 1967 [X-004].

The case did not stop the leak of ammunition to the Front; it moved it. After December the dealers bought whole crates from units in the field rather than loose lots from the depot, and the Front's purchasing agents paid more for unmarked Chinese 7.62mm than for the Republic's 5.56mm, which they had learned could be traced [X-004]. What it did was establish, three years into the rearmament, that a cartridge case found on a battlefield could put an officer in prison, and that the trail from a firefight to the man who sold the rounds now ran through a register the Commission kept. Khoa told the Board in January 1967 that it was the first case in which the war itself had supplied the evidence.

## EV006-3 · The relief at Dien Ban · 8–31 Aug 1966 · Dien Ban and Dai Loc Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon · NEW · 006
*Initiative: enemy*

The relief at Dien Ban was the first time the enemy attacked an American battalion in the week it changed over, and it showed both halves of what unit rotation had done. The 1st Battalion, 9th Marines had come ashore at Da Nang in July 1965 and had spent a year in the hamlets of Dien Ban south of the airfield, the same companies under the same commander throughout [X-006]. Under the rotation it was to be relieved in place by the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, which had formed at Camp Pendleton in October 1965, trained together for nine months and sailed from Okinawa in late July under Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey; the two battalions were to overlap from 8 to 26 August, company by company [X-006]. The relief dates had been fixed in March and were known, as all such dates were, to every Vietnamese who sold the Marines ice or washed their clothes (F006-21).

The 1st Battalion, 26th Marines arrived knowing nothing of Dien Ban. Its men had trained on the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 for six months and fired them as squads that had not changed since January (EC006-6); its officers had studied Ellison's area from the outgoing commander's reports and an overlay; none of them had walked the paddies at Phong Thu or Ky Lam, where the R-20 Doc Lap Battalion and the Front's Dien Ban district company had laid mines along the bunds for a year and knew every one of the old battalion's habits [X-006]. What Dien Ban did have was continuity in the other places this headquarters had put it. The ARVN 51st Regiment's battalion advisers and the Dien Ban subsector adviser, Major Carl E. Haney, were all men of the Selected Advisory Corps on eighteen-month tours, two of them in their thirteenth month, and the district chief and the DIOCC had been in place since 1965 (F006-2, F006-9) [X-006]. Haney walked each of Dorsey's company commanders over his ground with the Popular Forces platoon leaders of the hamlets it held, and gave each a list, in English and Vietnamese, of the PF corporals who would tell a Marine what the hamlet knew (EC006-7) [X-006].

The Front had read the calendar too. On the night of 18–19 August, with two companies of the old battalion already embarked at Da Nang and two of the new on the ground for less than a week, the R-20 Battalion and the district company attacked Company B, 1st Battalion, 26th Marines in its new position at the hamlet of La Huan, about 4 km west of Route 1, at 0130 under a thin moon, with 82mm mortars from across the Song Ky Lam and a sapper assault from the south through a tree line the outgoing company had kept clear and the new one had not yet learned to watch [X-006]. The first assault put a section of sappers inside the wire. What followed was the half of the event the rotation had bought: the company's platoons, which had trained together for nine months, closed on the breach without waiting for orders, the squad guns took the paddy south of the wire under grazing fire, and by 0230 the sappers inside were dead and the second wave had not crossed the bund [X-006]. A PF platoon in the next hamlet, which Haney had introduced to the company's commander on the 14th, reported the R-20's withdrawal route by AN/PRC-25 at 0310, and two F-4Bs of Marine Aircraft Group 11 and the battalion's 81mm mortars hit it at the Song Ky Lam crossing before dawn [X-006].

The relief window had cost the Marines 14 killed and 37 wounded, about four times the battalion's average night that summer, and the enemy about 40–60 dead, of whom 23 were found [E: the company's count of bodies inside and outside the wire and a captured R-20 casualty return of September]. The outgoing battalion would not have been hit that way; it knew the tree line. The incoming one, under the old individual system, would have been a battalion of strangers at the breach, and I believe the sappers would have held the breach long enough to reach the company's ammunition point (F006-6) [E: the company's own account of the first fifteen minutes, and the period's experience of penetrated positions]. MACV drew two orders from Dien Ban: from October 1966 reliefs were staggered across six weeks by company and kept off the dark of the moon, and no incoming company took over a position without a night with the outgoing one beside it (EC006-7) [X-006]. Both orders added men in theater at every relief. The R-20 Battalion drew its own lesson in a document captured in November: the new Marines "fight like the old ones in their third month and know the ground like the old ones in their first" [X-006].

## EV010-3 · Operation STEADY HAND · 14–19 Nov 1966 · Cai Be District, Dinh Tuong Province, between Route 4 and the Plain of Reeds; the hamlets of Hau My village · NEW · 010
*Initiative: both*

The rice was the reason for both sides. The Mekong flood of September and October 1966 had drowned much of the Plain of Reeds and the main-season crop in the districts north of Route 4, and the Front's Dinh Tuong provincial committee, its own stocks lost in the water, ordered the 263rd Main Force Battalion in late October to escort its rice levy in the hamlets of Cai Be as the harvest came in [X-010]. The 7th Division, with the province chief, mounted STEADY HAND to cover the government's procurement in the same hamlets: the 2nd and 3rd Battalions of the 11th Regiment, with an M113 troop of the division's armored cavalry, swept north from Route 4 into Hau My village on 14 November to clear the canal lines along which the farmers would move the paddy to the Cai Be market [X-010].

On the afternoon of 16 November the 3rd Battalion's lead company, moving north along a canal dike in file, walked into the 263rd's ambush: two companies in an L along a tree line and a second canal, with an RPD on each arm and a 57mm recoilless rifle at the corner, at 40 to 60 metres [X-010]. The company commander, Captain Nguyen Huu Tam, and both of his forward platoon leaders were hit in the first two minutes. On the historical course of such ambushes, a company that lost its officers on a dike in the open lost about half its men before the rest of the battalion reached it [E: the 7th Division's ambushes of 1963–65]. This one did not. Sergeant First Class Tran Van Loc, the senior platoon sergeant, had passed the senior course at Nha Trang the year before, and his squads were led by two sergeants and four acting squad leaders, corporals first class who had passed the leader's course, because the field commissions of September had taken two of the company's best sergeants to Thu Duc (F010-15, EC010-3) [X-010]. The squads went off the dike into the paddy on the side away from the long arm of the L, each squad leader putting his M249 on the tree line while the riflemen crawled; Loc took the radio, reported the contact and the company's position on the AN/PRC-25 in a form the battalion could plot, and asked for the 105mm M101A1 battery at Cai Be on the tree line at 150 metres beyond his own men [X-010]. The first rounds came at 1534, eleven minutes after the ambush opened. The M113 troop came up the next dike at 1550 with its .50-calibre guns on the tree line, and the 263rd broke contact at about 1610 and went north into the flooded reeds, carrying its wounded [X-010].

The company lost 14 killed and 22 wounded, of about 120, and all its weapons came out with it; on the historical course I would have expected 40 to 60 killed and the company's squad guns and radio left on the dike [X-010]. Eleven of the 263rd's dead were found in the tree line; I believe it lost 20 to 30 killed, and I do not credit the district's report of 74 (A13). The sweep went on to 19 November without further contact. In the following three weeks the government's procurement in Hau My and the two villages beside it bought about 1,400 tonnes of paddy, about twice the province's figure for the same villages in 1965, and the Front's levy there took about a third less than its committee had ordered, by the committee's own figures captured in 1967 [X-010] (DIRMT01-11). The pay of the 3rd Battalion's soldiers, after the devaluation of June, bought about a third less rice than it had in the spring (EV009-3), and two of Loc's acting squad leaders were absent without leave for a week in December to bring their families' harvest in [X-010]. Loc was made a master sergeant in the board's spring list of 1967; Captain Tam survived and returned to the battalion in March.

## EV002-5 · The arrest count falls · Feb – Nov 1967 · Saigon; Phong Dinh, Long An and Vinh Long Provinces; and the towns of the Delta · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
In the first quarter of 1967 the Special Branch's arrests of infrastructure in the held belt of the Delta fell to about 40 percent of their peak of the second quarter of 1966, and the Phung Hoang centres' neutralizations of district-level cadres fell with them, from about 140 a quarter in 1966 to about 50 (F006-18) [X-002] [X-006]. The Office of the Secretary of Defense's systems analysts read the decline as a programme losing its grip, and in February Robert Komer, then the President's special assistant for pacification, proposed monthly arrest targets by province as the measure of the attack on the infrastructure [X-002]. This headquarters answered in writing on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working (F002-11): in the districts with posts three years old there was almost no one left in the villages to arrest, and the measures the enemy could not fake, captured tax ledgers, the count of functioning village committees in captured documents, and the age of captured guerrillas, all showed the infrastructure shrinking there [X-002]. The Director General of National Police, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, wanted the targets; Commissioner Khoa opposed them on the record, and so did Hieu, whose decree of 1964 forbade them in terms [X-002] [X-006].

The decree held, and the half-victory of the historical-course compromise was a whole one on paper: no centre and no province was given a target in 1967, and the held belt was measured by captured documents and the contested belt by the Commission's audit [X-006]. It was less than a whole one on the ground. The pressure that would have gone into targets went instead into extending the method to the contested belt, where it produced what the abandoned target list had predicted, raids on thin files, and after Duc Thanh the extension was halted village by village (EV006-4, F006-15) [X-006]. In the second half of 1967 the Commission received about 190 complaints of false arrest from the contested districts with centres and about 40 from the held belt, against the 430 that targets had produced on the earlier course (EC002-9) [X-006]. And the infrastructure that had left the held villages had not disappeared. The Special Branch's provincial sections reported through 1967 that legal cadres and their families were settling in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the outskirts of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000 (F002-2) [X-002]. The Branch built urban sections to follow them, slowly. The war against the infrastructure had moved toward the towns, and so, as the Branch's own reports warned in November, had the Front's means of acting there.

## EV006-4 · The Mo Duc raid and the Phoenix story · 11 Feb – 30 Jun 1967 · Duc Thanh village, Mo Duc District, Quang Ngai Province; Saigon; Hanoi; Washington · NEW · 006
*Initiative: both*

The raid at Duc Thanh was what the method became where the night was not held, and it reached the American press within a month. By the end of 1966 the held belt had little left for the district centres to find (EV002-5), and the corps commanders and the CORDS planners wanted the same results from the contested provinces of I and II Corps, where the Front still ran most villages after dark [X-006]. Quang Ngai's province chief opened DIOCCs in four contested districts in the autumn of 1966 against Hieu's advice, and the Quang Ngai PRU, which had been raised under the statute in 1964 and had operated by its rules in the coastal towns, began to raid on files built mostly from ralliers and from the S2's informants (F006-15) [X-006]. On the night of 11 February 1967 a PRU team of 24 men, acting on a file that named a hamlet schoolteacher in Duc Thanh as the Mo Duc district committee's propaganda chief on two sources, both ralliers from the same hamlet, went into his house at 0200; a man in the house fired a pistol, the team fired back with M4A1s and an M249, and the teacher, his wife, his father and two of his children died [X-006]. The district committee's propaganda chief was a different man, arrested in May; the schoolteacher had been named by a rallier whose family had a quarrel with his over a well [X-006].

The Front's answer was ready because Hanoi had decided, for its own reasons, to fight on a new front. The Thirteenth Plenum of January 1967 had adopted the line of "fighting while negotiating", and on 28 January Foreign Minister Nguyen Duy Trinh had said that talks could follow an unconditional halt in the bombing [H]; the decision came from Hanoi's reading of Soviet pressure, the American election calendar and the world's opinion of the bombing, and from nothing this headquarters did [H]. Its consequence for the Front's propaganda apparatus was a demand for material that would carry abroad. Liberation Radio broadcast the Duc Thanh deaths on 19 February as "a massacre by the American Phoenix murder teams", and the story reached an American wire service in Saigon through a Quang Ngai stringer on 2 March; by 10 March two national newspapers had run it, with the programme's American name, the CIA's role in its design and the PRUs' American pay (F006-23) [X-006]. On the historical course the Phoenix stories began in 1970 [H].

Two things were different from what the historical stories would have found, and both mattered. The Commission's inspectors had the Duc Thanh file within a week and published their finding on 6 April: the file had met the three-source rule only by counting two ralliers from one feud as independent, the warrant had been signed by a district judge who had not read it, and the PRU had entered without a capture plan [X-006]. The PRU team leader was tried before the Special Court in June and convicted of manslaughter; the judge was removed; Hieu withdrew the centres' authority to issue raid files in the four contested districts of Quang Ngai and in eleven others in I and II Corps until each had a post and a platoon in the village concerned [X-006]. And the national committee published its first audited count on 30 June: since January 1965, about 14,500 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 9,800 were tried or held under review, about 3,900 had rallied and about 800 had been killed, about 300 of them in the programme's own raids, with the Commission's sample finding about one claim in ten unsupported in the held belt and one in four in the contested belt [X-006]. The count was small, and it was the first figure on the attack on the infrastructure that the newspapers did not later have to correct. It did not undo Duc Thanh. The word Phoenix was in the American vocabulary three years early, and it meant what the Front had said it meant.

## EV005-3 · The estuary field · 26 Feb – 31 Dec 1967 · The mouths of the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the lighterage anchorages at Quang Khe, Dong Hoi and Cua Viet north, and the coastal waters of Route Package 1, North Vietnam · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

On 26 February 1967 Washington authorized, for the first time, the aerial mining of the North's rivers south of the 20th parallel [H]. Historically Navy A-6As from the *Enterprise* laid the first Mk 50-series mines in the mouths of the Song Ca and Song Giang that night [H]. In this war the first field was laid by four B-52Js from U-Tapao, which put 80 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes into the Song Ca estuary below Vinh and the Song Giang at Quang Khe from about 1,000 feet in a single pass each, at night, outside the guns that later covered both mouths [M005] [X-005]. The target detecting devices were set for the steel-hulled lighters and coastal freighters that carried supplies down from Thanh Hoa and Vinh; the wooden junks were left to the pressure settings, and the sampans were not mined at all, because no setting would catch them [M005].

The panhandle watch and the Gulf watch counted the effect (EV003-3, EV003-5). In the first fortnight two steel lighters sank at the Quang Khe bar and a third in the Song Ca, and the steel traffic on the coastal route south of Vinh fell within a month to about a fifth of what it had been [X-005]. Through the spring the Js reseeded each field every three to four weeks, on the cue of the Global Hawk's imagery when the North's sweepers came out, and lost no aircraft doing it [X-005]. By June the steel craft had stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa [X-005].

The cargo did not stop. It moved to wooden junks, to sampans under tow and to trucks on Route 1A and Route 15, and by the autumn the radar's count of southbound truck movements below Vinh was about 25–30 percent higher than in 1966 while the tonnage counted into the panhandle was about the same (F005-5) [X-005]. The coastal lighterage had carried perhaps a fifth of what moved south of Vinh, and the road took nearly all of it in four months [X-005]. Its value was in time and labour: the North spent perhaps 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers on Route Package 1 in 1967 than it otherwise would, and its trucks spent more nights in the open where the radar could count them [X-005]. Hanoi asked Moscow for wooden-hulled craft and for minesweeping help; it got trucks, which was what it needed (F005-18) [X-005]. The field stayed in place until the bombing halt of November 1968, and nobody in Washington asked, because nobody could answer, how much less arrived in the South because of it. My own answer is very little.

## EV007-4 · The list and the 25th Division · 4 Mar – 31 Oct 1967 · Duc Hoa, Hau Nghia Province, and the 25th Division's districts in Hau Nghia and Long An; the Joint General Staff and the Officer Selection Board, Saigon · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The 25th Division was the ARVN's worst, and in 1967 it became the test of whether the ranked list could reach a division. Its battalions, in Hau Nghia and western Long An between Saigon and the Parrot's Beak, had the highest desertion rate and the lowest rate of contact in III Corps, its regiments had been commanded by the division commander's appointees until the board's lists of 1965–66, and its commander, appointed in 1965 before the list existed, had refused to move his headquarters out of Duc Hoa at night [H] [X-007]. When he was due for reassignment in March 1967 the board's ranked list put three colonels before the Directory. Ky wanted a fourth man, an Air Force patron's cousin who was not on it; Thieu, as Chief of State, took the second name, Colonel Vu Ngoc Hoan, a Dalat graduate of 1952 who had commanded a regiment of the 7th Division well under the board's list, after the US Mission told both men through the Aid Compliance Board that the division's counterpart funds depended on the list (Article 14) [X-007]. It was one of two division commands in ten filled from the list against a palace preference by the end of 1967 [X-007].

Hoan relieved three battalion commanders in his first ninety days, all through the board's thirty-day reviews and all for what the reviews called failure to close with the enemy, and put the division's battalions into the districts at night beside the Popular Forces, which the corps had never asked it to do [X-007]. By October the division's desertion rate had fallen by about a quarter, its battalions were in contact about twice as often as in 1966, and the Front's district companies in Duc Hoa and Duc Hue had moved their bases toward the Cambodian border [X-007]. None of this changed who held most of Hau Nghia's hamlets after dark, which the division could visit and could not hold.

In July the board extended its lists to the commanders of the Regional Force companies of III and IV Corps, at the request of the III Corps commander after the losses of the RF convoys on Route 13 (EV007-3) [X-007]. About 280 RF company commanders were reviewed by the end of the year and about 60 replaced, most of them by field-commissioned lieutenants of their own provinces [X-007].

On 11 October the Military Security Service arrested Lieutenant Truong Cong Hy, a clerk of the board's secretariat since 1965, as an agent of the B2 Front's military intelligence [X-007]. He had copied every command list the board had published, with the panels' ratings of each selected officer, and passed them by courier to the Cu Chi district committee (F007-6) [X-007]. The COSVN target notes taken with his courier in November showed what the lists had been used for: the B2 Front's regiments had been told which battalions in III Corps were under new commanders and which were not, and had planned their ambushes of 1967 against the second [X-007]. The board's best evidence of its own effect came from the man who had betrayed it.

## EV001-7 · The Special Court convicts the IV Corps quartermaster · 6 Mar – 18 Apr 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province, and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 001
On 6 March 1967 the Commission Against Corruption arrested Brigadier General Phan Duy Khiem, IV Corps deputy commander for logistics since 1965, with eleven officers of the corps quartermaster and finance staffs at Can Tho [X-001]. The case was documentary and had been built for fourteen months. The Pay and Muster Verification Service's parade payments had removed ghost pay from the corps' units in 1964–65, and the graft had moved, as this headquarters had expected, to rations and fuel (F001-3) [X-001]. The Service's auditors matched the corps' monthly ration draws against the strength the musters had established and found the corps drawing rice, canned fish and salt for about 9,000 more men than it had; a parallel audit by the Joint Aid Compliance Board traced about 2.1 million litres of motor fuel issued to RF companies with no vehicles and sold through two Cholon dealers [X-001]. The total over two years came to about 310 million piasters, about $2.6 million at the official rate [X-001].

The arrest was made by the Commission's own judicial police, Vietnamese officers under Commissioner Khoa, with no American present (EC001-10) [X-001]. General Khiem was the first general officer the Commission had charged, three years after it was founded; it had refused the junta's invitations to begin at the top in 1964 and had worked upward from battalion and district (EC001-9) [X-001]. His patron in the Directory tried to have the case moved to a military court in March; the Deputy Chief of Mission, as American co-chair of the Board, told the Directory's chairman, General Thieu, that the US Mission would suspend counterpart funds to IV Corps under the Board's authority if the case was moved, and Thieu, then preparing his presidential candidacy and wanting no quarrel with the Embassy, let it stand (EC001-8, F001-15) [X-001]. The Special Court tried the case in public in Saigon over nine sitting days and on 18 April 1967 sentenced Khiem to eight years and confiscation of property, and nine of the eleven officers to terms of two to six years [X-001].

The conviction mattered more in the officer corps than in the countryside. It established that the Commission's reach now ran to the rank below the Directory and no higher; that it reached that rank only on documentary evidence and with the American lever behind it; and that a general who stole from his troops' rations could go to prison. The American press reported it as a sign that the Saigon government was cleaning itself up, and for a few weeks the corruption stories of the spring were about a conviction rather than a scandal (F001-14) [X-001]. The officers of the IV Corps staff who replaced the convicted were chosen in part from the Commission's own list of officers with clean records, and there were not enough of them (F001-5) [X-001].

## EV008-4 · The night of the relief at My Phuoc Tay · 9–10 Mar 1967 · The PF post at Ap My Loi and the canal road to Cai Lay district town, My Phuoc Tay village, Cai Lay District, Dinh Tuong Province · NEW · 008,010
*Initiative: enemy*

This is the night the radio's second-order effect became visible, and the night that shows it best (F008-3, F008-6). By 1967 every Popular Forces post in the held belt of Dinh Tuong had a set on the district net, a PRC-25 from the Go Vap plant since the previous autumn, and a fresh battery every Thursday from the exchange at the district (EC008-7, EC008-8) [X-008]. The Front had stopped assaulting those posts in 1965, as it had stopped assaulting the rearmed ones (EV002-2). In the first months of 1967 the Dinh Tuong provincial unit decided to use the radio against the men it called.

At 2235 on 9 March a mortar section of the 514th Local Force Battalion put twelve 60mm rounds into the post at Ap My Loi, a mud-walled fort on the canal six kilometres north of Cai Lay, from about 300 metres across the paddy. Sergeant Le Van Muoi, who commanded its PF platoon, called the district at 2237, reported the mortar and an assault forming on the dike to the west, and asked for the reaction force. The assault did not come. Two companies of the 514th were not on the dike at all; they were lying in the sugar cane on both sides of the canal road, three kilometres south of the post, where the road crossed a concrete culvert that the district's trucks had to slow to cross [X-008].

The district chief, Major Nguyen Thanh Liem, did what the rule of 1964 told him, that a post that called would have its reaction within the hour, and sent the 247th Regional Force Company north in four trucks at 2250 (EV002-1). At 2304 a command-detonated mine under the culvert lifted the lead truck off the road. The firing circuit, the district's engineers found in the morning, was two kilometres of WD-1 field wire from the Republic's own mill and a pack of Go Vap dry cells of the kind the post had been issued the week before (F008-5) [X-008]. The two companies fired into the other three trucks from both sides for about eight minutes, took the weapons from the first, and were gone into the cane before the 7th Division's battery at Cai Lay had been given a target. The 247th lost 31 dead and 18 wounded and about 40 weapons, most of them M4A1 carbines and two M249s [X-008]. The post itself lost one man, wounded by the mortar. The district reported 46 of the enemy killed; eight bodies were found at the culvert in the morning, and I believe the 514th lost between ten and fifteen killed [X-008].

Liem wrote new rules the following week, and they are the reason the night belongs in the history. A post's first call would be for fire, not men: every post in the district had its concentrations plotted for the 105mm battery at Cai Lay and the 7th Division's guns at Cai Be, and the call was answered by the guns within four minutes [X-008]. A relief would move by water or at first light, never by road at night on the one road the Front could predict. And a post that could hold behind its wire would be expected to. The rules spread through IV Corps in the spring, carried by the province advisers and by the 7th Division's artillery officers (EV006-1). In the months that followed, attacks on posts within range of the guns in Dinh Tuong fell by about a third, and the Front's district companies turned to the posts beyond the guns' 11 kilometres and to the posts whose batteries were run down at the end of the exchange week (F008-6, F008-16) [X-008].

The rules had a cost that the returns did not show and Liem's adviser did. A platoon that could call down the guns had less reason to leave its wire after dark, and in 1967 the night patrols of the PF platoons in Cai Lay fell by about a third; the post held itself, and the hamlet outside it was walked at night, more often than before, by the Front's tax collectors (F008-12) [X-008]. Ap My Loi held every attack made on it for the rest of the war. Whether the hamlet that bore its name was the government's after dark is a question the post's radio log cannot answer.

Sergeant Muoi had passed the leader's course at Nha Trang in 1961 and the platoon leader's course at the Dinh Tuong centre in 1964, and he kept a listening post of two men on the canal bank 300 metres south of the post every night (EC010-2). At 2241 they reported men moving south in the cane on the far side of the canal, toward the road, and Muoi added it to his report: the mortar, an assault forming to the west, and movement south toward the culvert [X-010]. The district operations room logged it as the third of three lines. Liem read the first two and sent the trucks, because the rule said the reaction within the hour and the post had called; the sergeant had seen the ambush and the decision was not his (F010-8, F010-9) [X-010]. The ambush went as told. The third of Liem's new rules came from Muoi's line in the log: a post's report of movement on the relief route stops the relief until the route is fired on or cleared at first light, and the district's posts were taught, through their platoon leaders, to report the road as well as the wire.

## EV000-35 · CORDS is established · 9 May 1967 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,006
*Initiative: allied*
On 9 May 1967 the President placed the American pacification effort, civil and military, under MACV as Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support, with Robert W. Komer as one of Westmoreland's deputies, holding ambassadorial rank and authority over military personnel and resources, the first civilian to serve in such a role inside a US military command [H37]. It followed the failure of the Office of Civil Operations, set up in November 1966 to coordinate the civil agencies under civilian leadership [H37]. CORDS was about 85 percent military [H37]. Each corps had a CORDS deputy, usually civilian, under the corps senior adviser, and each province an American province senior adviser with civilian and military staffs alongside the Vietnamese province chief, usually an ARVN colonel [H37].

Komer's priorities were the territorial forces and the attack on the VC infrastructure. The Regional and Popular Forces numbered about 300,000 in 1967 [H37]; their advisory presence was later multiplied through Mobile Advisory Teams [H37]. In mid-1967 CORDS created the Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme, ICEX, to target the VCI, which became the Phoenix programme at the end of the year [H]. The Hamlet Evaluation System, introduced in January 1967, began rating every hamlet monthly on a scale reported by the American district advisers [H]; its figures were at once an indispensable record and a measure produced by officers rated on the result (A13). CORDS solved the American management problem of pacification. It did not solve the Vietnamese one, that security in the hamlet at night depended on Vietnamese forces and officials who had to stay there.

In this war CORDS is established on the historical date and in the historical form; the provisions did not create a single manager [X-001]. What CORDS inherits is different. The American land advisers, about 260 in 1964 and about 340 by 1967, pass into it as a land reform division under the CORDS deputy, and the provincial land services' register of titled parcels, which by May 1967 lists about 740,000 hectares and the households that hold them, becomes the first hamlet-by-hamlet household record the American pacification effort has ever had [X-001] [M001]. Komer adds titled hectares as a factor in the Hamlet Evaluation System's economic rating from its first revision. This headquarters objected in writing on 22 June 1967 that a title measured the government's paperwork and not the peasant's allegiance, that the Front had told its cadres to let peasants take titles and keep paying the Front's tax, and that titles issued would rise fastest where they meant least (F001-10) [X-001]. The objection was recorded and the factor kept, at half the weight Komer had proposed [X-001]. The Phung Hoang centres, which had used the land register since 1964 to locate the families of known cadres, passed into CORDS's reporting with it; the Commission had been receiving complaints about that use since 1965 (F001-1) [X-001] [X-006].

In this war CORDS inherits a police as well as a land register. The aid mission's Public Safety Division, about 300 advisers in 1966, passes into CORDS as its Public Safety Directorate, and with it a National Police of about 70,000, about 1,500 village posts, and a Special Branch with district sections in about 200 districts and files on the infrastructure kept since 1964 [M002] [X-002]. There is no ICEX in this war, because there is nothing for it to create: the Phung Hoang centres have sat in those district sections since 1964, under the district chief, with the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and every district has one by the end of 1965 (EV006-1, F006-13) [X-006]. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the police statute in 1964 and answer to the province chiefs, not to the Agency; their methods are inside the statute's safeguards in law, and Duc Thanh in February showed how far that law reached in a village the government did not hold at night (EV006-4) [X-006]. Komer's contribution was the money to put a centre's American adviser in every contested district, which Hieu accepted, and a proposal for monthly targets, which the decree forbade (EV002-5) [X-006]. What the American pacification effort created in 1967 on the historical course, here it found already running, and the province senior advisers found a counterpart chief who had been in his post for two or three years (F002-7) [X-002].

## EV000-36 · Operation Buffalo and the siege of Con Thien · 2 Jul – 31 Oct 1967 · Con Thien and "the Marketplace", Quang Tri Province, south of the DMZ · CANCELLED · 000,005,CP02

## EV000-37 · Hanoi decides on a general offensive · 6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · HIST-ALT · 000,001,004
In April 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive in the South, and in May sent the plan to Hanoi [H39]. Thanh briefed the Politburo in July and died there on 6 July, by the most common account of a heart attack after a party, by another of wounds from a US bombing raid [H39]. The plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho; General Vo Nguyen Giap, who had favoured protracted war, did not originate it and modified it reluctantly [H39]. Beginning on 27 July, hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals were arrested in the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair", with a second wave in November and December, which removed opposition to the offensive and much of Giap's staff [H39].

The premise was that the ARVN was no longer effective and the Saigon government unpopular, and that a general offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising [H39]. In October the Politburo chose the Tet holiday of 1968 as the date; it reaffirmed the decision in December and formalized it as Resolution 14 in January 1968 [H39]. The border battles of the autumn, at Con Thien, Loc Ninh, Song Be and Dak To, were part of the preparation, drawing US forces away from the cities [H36]. This was a decision taken in Hanoi for Hanoi's reasons: the stalemate in the South, the factional struggle in the Party and a reading of the South that proved wrong.

In this war the decision is taken on the same dates by the same men, and for the same reasons; the provisions did not make Le Duan a militant or remove Giap's staff [X-001]. They added an argument to the militants' case and subtracted one from its premise. COSVN's reports of 1966–67 recorded that the Front's agricultural tax in the Delta was falling, that recruitment in the titled districts along the Bassac was harder each season, and that the "land trick" had not been exposed as the cadres had promised; Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait (F001-12) [X-001]. At the same time the premise that the Saigon government was unpopular in the countryside was weaker in the Delta than anywhere else, and COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 in the autumn said so; it was overruled [X-001]. The urgency rose and the forecast of a general uprising was carried forward unchanged, so that in this war the offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than the historical one.

The premise about the ARVN was wrong in a second way here, and the purge made sure nobody in Hanoi said so. In the spring of 1967 a study group of the General Staff's combat research department, working for Giap from the B2 and B3 Fronts' after-action reports since 1964, had concluded that the puppet infantry and its territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat as they had in 1963 and that the local forces could not expect to overrun district towns defended by them without main-force support [X-004]. The group's three senior officers were among those arrested in the second wave in December, for reasons that had nothing to do with their study and everything to do with whose staff they were on, and the study went into the files with them [X-004]. The offensive was planned, as historically, on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve on contact. In this war it had been better armed than its attackers for four years.

## EV008-5 · The Phu Cat lot · 14 Aug – 20 Dec 1967 · A firing position of the 22nd Division's artillery on Route 1 south of Phu Cat, Binh Dinh Province; the Bien Hoa arsenal; the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 008
*Initiative: neither*

On the morning of 14 August 1967 an M101A1 105mm howitzer of a 22nd Division battery firing in support of a sweep of the Phu Cat hills burst its tube on the eleventh round of a mission. Four of the crew were killed and three wounded [X-008]. The round was high explosive from a lot the Bien Hoa arsenal had forged and loaded in May, and within a week the 22nd Division's artillery had found two more rounds of the same lot with cracks at the base of the body that a gauge would have caught [X-008].

The investigation was the Commission's, and it was quick because the paper was good. Truong Vinh Khoa's inspectors found that the lot had been accepted by a Directorate inspector on the strength of the forging line's own records, without the proof-firing of a sample that the specification required; the inspector had accepted four lots that way since March, and had been paid for the time he saved the line (F008-14, F008-15) [X-008]. He was convicted by the Special Court in December and sentenced to six years. The arsenal recalled about 40,000 rounds from five lots and destroyed about 9,000 of them [X-008].

The gunners' verdict took longer to reverse than the court's. Through the autumn of 1967 and into 1968 the artillery of II Corps, and much of the rest of the army's, refused domestic 105mm rounds where it could draw American, and the share of 105mm bodies bought under the Aid Compliance Board's rule fell from about a third to about a tenth for most of a year (F008-15) [X-008]. What restored it was the officers the 1964 courses had made (P007b): the Ordnance Directorate's acceptance branch, under captains and majors who could read a metallurgical report, rewrote the procedure so that every lot was proof-fired at the Long Thanh range with an officer's signature on the result, and the 22nd Division's own artillery commander was invited to witness the first ten [X-008]. Binh Dinh's gunners went back to Bien Hoa's rounds in the summer of 1968. It was a small event, and the reason it is in the record is that it showed, three years before the palace took the Directorate, what an arsenal is worth without honest acceptance: less than nothing, because a soldier who does not trust his round will not fire it.

## EV000-38 · The constitution and the presidential election · 3 Sep 1967 · Republic of Vietnam · HIST · 000
The Constituent Assembly elected in September 1966 produced a constitution, promulgated on 1 April 1967 [H38]. In the presidential election of 3 September 1967, held with the Senate election, Thieu ran for president with Ky as his running mate, after the generals forced Ky to stand aside [H38]. Eleven tickets ran. Thieu and Ky won 34.83 percent, 1,649,561 votes; the runner-up, Truong Dinh Dzu, a lawyer campaigning on negotiations with the NLF, won 17.26 percent, 817,120 votes [H38]. The reported turnout was 83.17 percent of 5,853,251 registered voters [H38]. There were credible reports of soldiers showing voters how to mark their ballots, and of a secret military committee that would govern behind the elected government; on 10 August 57 members of the US House had condemned the conduct of the campaign [H38]. Dzu was later imprisoned [H]. Thieu was inaugurated on 31 October and the lower house was elected on 22 October [H] [H38].

The election gave the Republic of Vietnam an elected government for the first time since 1963 and, under Thieu, the stability that the previous four years had lacked. It did not give it legitimacy in the countryside: two-thirds of the voters chose someone else, and a civilian peace candidate had come second.

## EV000-40 · The battle of Dak To · 3–23 Nov 1967 · Dak To and Hills 1338, 724 and 875, Kontum Province · CANCELLED · 000,CP02

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam [H40] and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year [H40]. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December [H9], and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December [H39]. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000 [H39], and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view" [H39]. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi [H]. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general (EV001-6, EV001-7) [X-001]. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside [X-001].

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest (EV002-4, EV002-5) [X-002]. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

The enemy's external supply is measured: the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count, the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what the South's forces need from the North (EV003-3, EV003-4) [X-003]. That knowledge is honest from 1965 rather than from 1970, and it has not changed the rules, the bombing or the strategy. Its main effect on Washington has been to deliver bad news early.

The RVNAF enters 1968 with the rifles it has carried for four years and a Regional Force that the corps commanders want for their own operations, bound since 1967 by the rule that keeps it in its provinces (EV004-2). The American army has had no rifle crisis and no inquiry, and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights; it is the same army in every other respect, fighting the same battles on the enemy's terms.

The bombers have learned two things in two years: that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot (EV005-2, EV005-3), and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle (EVCP02-3). The second lesson is the one the headquarters remembers going into 1968.

Phung Hoang has a centre in every district and an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, about one in five of them members of district or village committees, and the Front's district administration in the held belt now governs from the contested edge by courier (EV006-2, EV002-4, F006-4) [X-006]. The word Phoenix has been a press story since March 1967 (EV006-4). About 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps; the American battalions have changed over once or twice each as units, have fought better in the contacts they were granted, and have learned at Dien Ban to keep their reliefs off the dark of the moon (EV006-3) [X-006]. The staggered six-week overlap that followed raised the peak American strength in theater by about 15,000–25,000 men, which the expansion battalions of 1967 waited for, so that the enemy's tactic against the relief was paid for in American spaces rather than American dead (F006-12) [X-006]. None of it has changed who chooses the battle.

The ARVN ends 1967 with about four in five of its battalions commanded by officers the board chose, on eighteen-month commands, with the captain-led battalion nearly gone and the thirty-day review feared and read; about a third fewer of its battalions have been rendered ineffective in 1966–67 than on the historical course, which no report shows because the enemy declined the fights that would have shown it (EV007-2, EV007-3) [X-007]. The 25th Division has a commander from the list and the corps have the commanders they had (EV007-4). The Military Security Service has its first file on the board's officers as a political factor, and COSVN has had every list the board published.

The factories end 1967 producing what the domestic share buys, with the steelworks short of power and the arsenal short of its gunners' trust after Phu Cat (EV008-3, EV008-5) [X-008]. Every post of the held belt has a radio, the Front attacks those that can call about a third less often than those that cannot, and where the guns can reach the posts the first call is for fire (EV008-4) [X-008]. The posts hold themselves better than the hamlets around them.

The ARVN ends 1967 trained to the man; training alone would have left it as short of squad leaders as history's, and what filled most of the empty slots was the NCO schooling described below; its battalions have lost about a tenth to a sixth fewer men in the ambushes the enemy still lays, beyond what the rifles and the officers saved, and about one in eight fewer battalions has been broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms alone would have left (EV007-3) [X-009]. The drill-sergeant corps has begun to return about 650–1,800 platoon sergeants a year to the battalions (F009-4) [X-009]. In the lowlands of Quang Nam and Quang Ngai the mine has replaced the firefight as the enemy's way of killing Americans (EV009-2), and in the districts around Saigon the Front's guerrilla squads are drilled by men the Republic trained (EV009-3).

The ARVN ends 1967 with its squads and platoons led by schooled men: about two in three of the slots the field commissions emptied are held by corporals first class who passed the leader's course, and the squads of the 7th Division held their base of fire at Hau My when their officers fell (EV010-3, EC010-3) [X-010]. I judge that about one in six to one in five fewer ARVN battalions were broken in 1966–67 than the officer reforms and the basic course alone would have left, and that the posts of the Delta lost about a tenth to a fifth fewer to night attack than the radio and the trained platoon had already saved (F010-25) [X-010]. None of it shows in a return: the enemy attacked less what kept its routine (F010-19). American sergeants pass the Basic Leader Course at Bien Hoa or wait for it, and the waiver of 1966 has become a ninety-day deferral (EV010-2).

The combined command ends 1967 with one corps area under a Vietnamese commander and three under American deputies who in practice run them, a quarterly plan the corps commanders execute at their own speed, and a two-layer plan that protects its operations and insults its Vietnamese staff (EV012-6, EC012-5) [X-012]. The task force has broken the Front's district government of the Saigon belt from its edge: the Hoc Mon committee and the region's finance section govern from the Ho Bo woods, and the region's collections from the posted villages are about an eighth of 1964's (EV012-4) [X-012]. On the II Corps coast it has a list it abandoned and a story the Front still tells (EV012-5).
