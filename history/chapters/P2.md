# Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is the American war at full stride. US military strength in Vietnam rises from about 184,000 at the end of 1965 to 385,000 at the end of 1966 and 486,000 at the end of 1967, and American deaths by hostile action from 1,369 in 1965 to 5,008 in 1966 and 9,378 in 1967 [H40]. The strategy is Westmoreland's: US divisions and brigades, with airmobility and almost unlimited firepower, seek out and destroy the enemy's main-force regiments and base areas, while the ARVN is turned increasingly to territorial security and pacification. The measure of success becomes attrition, the "crossover point" at which the enemy loses men faster than he can replace them through recruitment in the South and infiltration from the North. The instrument of measurement becomes the body count, which inflates at every level of reporting and never once establishes that the crossover has been reached.

The shape of the phase is a series of large operations that win their battles and do not change the war. At the Ia Drang in November 1965 the 1st Cavalry Division meets PAVN regulars in the Central Highlands and both sides draw the lessons they want. In 1966 the operations grow (Masher/White Wing on the Bong Son plain, Hastings below the DMZ, Attleboro in War Zone C) and in 1967 they culminate in the corps-sized sweeps of Cedar Falls and Junction City. Each kills enemy soldiers in numbers the reports exaggerate, captures rice and documents, empties villages, and is followed by the enemy's return when the Americans leave. The enemy decides when to fight; most of the large engagements begin with his attack or his ambush, and he withdraws into Cambodia, Laos and the DMZ when the cost is too high. Along the DMZ the war becomes positional: the Marines are drawn from pacification in the coastal hamlets to strongpoints at Con Thien, Gio Linh and Khe Sanh under PAVN artillery fired from the North.

Behind the main-force war, three other wars run. Rolling Thunder grows into the largest bombing campaign since 1945 against a North whose air defences grow with it, and does not stop the flow south. In Saigon, the Thieu–Ky junta survives the Buddhist uprising of 1966, writes a constitution and wins an election in 1967 with a third of the vote. And the pacification effort, neglected while the divisions fight, is reorganized under a single American manager, CORDS, in May 1967. In Hanoi, meanwhile, the militants around Le Duan conclude that the war of attrition is not being won either, and in the second half of 1967 they plan a general offensive to break it.

In this war a fourth runs beneath the other three, and it is the one the provisions of 1964 changed most. The land programme enters the phase with about 330,000 hectares titled and leaves it with about 820,000 titled to about 640,000 families [X-001]. GOLDEN SHEAF, its first clearing operation in the western Delta, shows what it can do and where it stops: at the edge of what the Regional and Popular Forces can hold after dark (EV001-6, F001-4). The Commission survives the Buddhist crisis and the election behind the Aid Compliance Board, and in 1967 convicts its first general (EV001-7). With a Special Branch that owns the files, a constable in about 1,500 villages and chiefs who stay, the government's own belt is cleared of resident infrastructure before 1968 rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN in Long An is the case (EV002-4). The Front answers by killing the best chiefs and moving its cadres into the towns (EV002-3, EV002-5), and the police serves Ky against the Buddhists in 1966 as it served every government before (EV000-26). None of this alters the main-force war, which is fought on the borders and in the Highlands where few tenant farmers live. It alters what the main-force war is fought over.

The war of the enemy's rear is now counted rather than guessed. The panhandle watch counts the Trail's trucks from December 1965 (EV003-3); the U-2S photographs the dispersed fuel drums before the POL strikes fly (EV000-27); and by March 1967 MACV and the CIA agree, from the radar's count of convoys out of Kompong Speu, that Sihanoukville supplies most of the arms for III and IV Corps (EV003-4). The counts show the bombing raising the toll on the transit without lowering what arrives, and change no rule in Washington.

Every American and South Vietnamese infantryman carries a modern carbine, and every squad a belt-fed gun and a marksman. The Ia Drang and the Hill Fights cost fewer American lives, and no Marine on 881 South dies beside a jammed rifle (EV000-23, EV000-34). The rearmed Regional Forces are good enough to be borrowed by the corps, and the districts they leave pay for it (EV004-2). None of it changes who chooses the battle.

Above the Trail the B-52J is allowed against fixed crossings in the panhandle from April 1966 and cuts every bridge it can measure; the enemy answers with fords, causeways and pontoons no bomb can close, and by the autumn more is moving than before (EV005-2). At Con Thien in September 1967 the siege is measured every night and lifts three weeks early at a heavy price to the PAVN (EV000-36). It is the first siege the enemy loses badly, and Westmoreland draws a lesson from it that will cost the coastal hamlets.

Three changes of 1964 run through the phase without altering its battles. Phung Hoang's centres cover every district by the end of 1965 and break the Front's district administration in the held belt before Tet rather than after it; NIGHT LANTERN takes the Can Giuoc committee itself, not only its security chief (EV002-4). The method spreads by its own success into the contested belt, where it becomes the historical Phoenix, and a PRU raid at Mo Duc in February 1967 makes "the American Phoenix" a press story three years early (EV006-4). The American battalions arrive and leave as units for twelve-month tours, trained together and commanded for the whole of them; they fight better in the contacts the enemy grants and get no more of them, and the enemy learns to aim at the week each one changes over, as it did at Dien Ban in August 1966 (EV006-3). And the field advisers, now chosen and trained and on eighteen-month tours, are the men who carry what the American battalions forget each year.

## EV002-3 · The war on the district chiefs · Aug 1965 – Jun 1966 · The Delta, the provinces around Saigon and the coastal Centre · NEW · 002
For its first year the Front read the Provincial Administration Reform as one more reshuffle of the puppet's officials, and it took until the summer of 1965, when the list-appointed chiefs had stayed through the fall of Khanh, the Quat government and the arrival of Thieu and Ky, for the provincial committees to report that the new chiefs were not changing (F002-12) [X-002]. The conclusion COSVN drew in August 1965 was the one that hurt: the chiefs who kept their Popular Forces in the hamlets at night and directed the police were the enemy's most dangerous officials, and the district committees were to kill them [X-002]. Between August 1965 and June 1966 nineteen district chiefs and two province chiefs were killed by mines, ambush and assassination, sixteen of them appointed from the list [X-002]. On 14 November 1965 Major Tran Van Sang, district chief of Mo Cay in Kien Hoa since June 1964, was killed with his driver and two bodyguards by a command-detonated mine on the road from Mo Cay to Ben Tre; the provincial committee's own report, captured in 1966, called him "the most harmful of the puppet district chiefs in the province" because the hamlets of his district no longer sheltered its cadres at night [X-002].

The killings did not empty the districts, and that was the reform's quiet gain. A dead chief's deputy for administration acted at once, and the Board sent a ranked list for the vacancy within an average of about three weeks; Mo Cay had a new chief, a captain who had served as the district's deputy for security, on 6 December [X-002]. Under the patronage system a vacant district had waited months for a patron's choice, and the Popular Forces had drifted back to the district town while it waited [M002]. Each killing cost the Front a security cell's best men, because the chiefs now travelled with Field Force escorts and varied their roads, and five of the twenty-one attacks were traced and their teams taken by the Special Branch within the year [X-002]. The Front went on killing chiefs for the rest of the war. It never again did so at the rate of 1965–66, because the chiefs it could kill easily had been killed and the rest had learned (F002-8).

## EVCP01-4 · The big-unit war: Starlite, A Shau, Hastings, Long Tan, Attleboro, Cedar Falls and Junction City · 18 Aug 1965 – 14 May 1967 · The Van Tuong peninsula and the DMZ in I Corps; the A Shau valley; Phuoc Tuy; War Zone C and the Iron Triangle in III Corps · HIST-ALT · CP01
*Initiative: both*

The big-unit battles of 1965–67 are told here together, because together they are the shape of the phase: each won on the ground, each followed by the enemy's return, none changing the war. On 18–24 August 1965, on a deserter's information, the 7th Marines under Colonel Oscar F. Peatross attacked the VC 1st Regiment on the Van Tuong peninsula south of Chu Lai by sea and helicopter in Operation Starlite, the first regimental-sized American battle of the war [H26]. The Marines lost 45 killed and 203 wounded and reported 614 VC killed but only 109 weapons taken, the first clear instance of a count inflated by its counters; the regiment was back in the field within months [H26]. On 9–10 March 1966, under cloud that kept most air support away, four battalions of the PAVN 325th Division's 95B Regiment overran the Special Forces camp in the A Shau valley; Major Bernard F. Fisher landed his A-1E on the strip under fire to rescue a downed pilot and received the Medal of Honor, and the valley became a PAVN base area and a terminus of the Trail for the rest of the war [H29].

In July 1966 the PAVN 324B Division crossed the DMZ, and Task Force Delta of the 3rd Marine Division, with the ARVN 1st Division under Brigadier General Ngo Quang Truong, drove it back in Operation Hastings for 126 American and 21 South Vietnamese killed against about 700 PAVN claimed [H31]. Its lasting effect was to draw the Marines north to the DMZ, away from the coastal hamlets III MAF had wanted to secure [H31]. On 18 August, in monsoon rain in the Long Tan rubber, D Company, 6th Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment, about 108 men under Major Harry Smith, held for three hours against the VC 275th Regiment and the D445 Battalion with the guns of Nui Dat behind it, losing 18 killed and counting 245 enemy dead on the field [H32]. From September to November Operation Attleboro grew from a brigade's shakedown northwest of Dau Tieng into a corps battle against the VC 9th Division and the PAVN 101st Regiment, costing 155 American dead against a claimed 1,016 enemy, of whom about 500 is the better estimate [H33].

In 1967 the sweeps reached their largest size. In Cedar Falls, from 8 January, the 1st and 25th Divisions with brigades and armoured cavalry sealed the Iron Triangle, moved about 6,000 people from Ben Suc to a camp at Phu Loi and destroyed the village; the VC were back within days, and a year later the Triangle was a staging area for Tet [H34]. In Junction City, from 22 February to 14 May, II Field Force sent two divisions and three brigades into War Zone C to destroy the 9th Division and find COSVN, with the war's only major American parachute assault at Katum [H35]. The enemy attacked American firebases five times at night or at dawn, at Prek Klok, Ap Bau Bang, Suoi Tre and Ap Gu, and lost heavily each time; the Americans lost 282 killed and claimed 2,728 enemy dead, COSVN moved, and the 9th Division withdrew into Cambodia [H35].

The figures above are the historical returns. In this war the American battalions in these fights, and the ARVN 1st Division at Hastings, carried the M4A1 and the M249 (EV004-1); the Australians at Long Tan did not, because the provision did not cover them. I do not adjust these returns. In each of these battles who attacked whom, where and when decided the losses far more than the squad weapons did, and the enemy's own losses were never counted closely enough for a difference in rifles to show in them (POS004-1) [X-CP01]. What the phase's big-unit battles proved here is what they proved historically: that the enemy lost heavily when he attacked an American position, and that he decided for himself whether to do so.

## EV000-23 · Plei Me and the Ia Drang · 19 Oct – 18 Nov 1965 · Plei Me CIDG camp and the Ia Drang valley below the Chu Pong massif, Pleiku Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 19 October 1965 the PAVN B3 Front under Major General Chu Huy Man attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, south of Pleiku, and the next day ambushed the ARVN relief column; the relief reached the camp at dusk on 25 October and the PAVN withdrew west towards the Chu Pong massif on the Cambodian border [H27]. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), newly arrived, was turned loose in pursuit. On 14 November the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore air-assaulted into Landing Zone X-Ray at the foot of the Chu Pong, into the assembly area of the 66th Regiment [H27]. For three days the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th, with elements of the 33rd, assaulted a perimeter held with the help of the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert Tully, massed tube artillery, tactical air and, from 15 November, B-52s striking about 7 km to the west, the first tactical use of the bombers, in five missions and 96 sorties over the campaign [H27]. The troopers carried M4A1s, with two M249s in each squad and an M110A2 marksman in most, where historically they carried the M16 and had the M60 at platoon; the perimeter held as it held historically, and I put American losses at X-Ray at about 70 killed and 115 wounded against 79 and 121 [X-004]. Moore's count of PAVN dead, 634 after his own reduction, and the estimate of 1,215 remain the figures of record and the same caution applies to them [H27].

On 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Robert McDade, marching overland to Landing Zone Albany, was ambushed in column in tall grass by the 8th Battalion, 66th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 33rd Regiment [H27]. The PAVN, following Colonel Nguyen Huu An's order to "grab them by the belt", fought inside the column where American artillery and air could not reach them, for about sixteen hours [H27]. This is the kind of fight the squad gun was built for, and it helped less than it should have. Two belt-fed guns in every squad meant that the platoons which kept their cohesion, Alpha Company's and the battalion command group's at the head of the column, formed a perimeter in the clump of trees at Albany within minutes and held it all afternoon; but the companies strung out behind in the grass were broken into knots of men who could not see each other at 20 metres, and a machine gun is no use to a squad that cannot find its targets or its friends (EC004-4) [X-004]. The battalion lost about 125 killed, 130 wounded and 4 missing, against 155 killed, 124 wounded and 4 missing on the historical course, still the worst American loss of a single day so far [X-004]. Over the campaign the United States counted about 205 dead, against 237 historically, and claimed a kill ratio of about ten to one; a PAVN report of 1966 gives 559 killed and 669 wounded [X-004] [H27]. Both sides inflated the other's losses [H27].

Both commands judged the Ia Drang a success, and each drew the lesson that confirmed its strategy: Westmoreland that airmobile forces with heavy fire support could destroy PAVN regulars at a favourable rate; the PAVN that by closing to close quarters and choosing when to fight they could neutralize American firepower and withdraw across the Cambodian border to rebuild [H27]. The B3 Front's after-action study added one sentence that the historical one did not need: that the American squads now carried "a light machine gun in every cell", and that the belt-grabbing assault must therefore be made from closer still, at night, or not at all (F004-10) [X-004]. McNamara, visiting after the battle, advised the President at the end of November that even a doubling of US forces in 1966 might produce only a military stalemate at a higher level [H].

## EV002-4 · Operation NIGHT LANTERN · 15 Nov 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Can Giuoc and Can Duoc Districts, Long An Province · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
NIGHT LANTERN went back to the districts where the land programme had learned its first lesson. Eighteen months after the Can Giuoc killings (EV001-3), the titles of about 400 families were still held in the district office, because no one could protect the families who kept them. Long An had been given a province chief from the first list in June 1964, Lieutenant Colonel Ha Thuc Lan, an engineer officer who had placed third in the country in the examination, and Can Giuoc a district chief from the second, Major Le Cong Phuong, who had grown up in Can Duoc [X-002]. In September 1965 they proposed to the American province adviser an operation built not around a regiment but around the settled post (EC002-1), the census and night check (EC002-2) and the target folder (EC002-3), in the 14 villages of the two districts between Route 5 and the Vam Co [X-002].

The forces were Long An's own: eight Regional Forces companies, 26 Popular Forces platoons raised in the villages themselves, two Police Field Force companies of about 150 men each with M1 and M2 carbines, M1918A2 automatic rifles and 60mm mortars, 140 rural constables for the 14 posts, the province Special Branch section of about 45, and four census teams [X-002]. Fire support was two M101 105mm howitzers at Can Giuoc and an AC-47 of the USAF 4th Air Commando Squadron on call at night; lift was the RF's sampans, two LCVPs of the River Assault Group at Nha Be, and UH-1B helicopters when III Corps would release them [X-002]. The rule was Lan's: no post settled until its PF platoon slept in the same hamlet and a reaction force could reach it by water within the hour, and no arrest without three sources [X-002].

The posts went in from 15 November, one village at a time, working out from Can Giuoc town along the canals. The turn came in two steps. On the night of 19 January 1966 two companies of the 506th Local Force Battalion attacked the new post at Phuoc Lai, the village where the killings of June 1964 had begun. The PF platoon and the ten constables held the post and the hamlet behind it for three hours; an RF company came down the canal in sampans and reached the post at 01:40, and the AC-47 kept the attackers off the paddy until it arrived [X-002]. The 506th left 38 dead and 14 weapons; the defenders lost 9 killed, 3 of them constables [X-002]. On 4 February a night check in Long Huu found a young man whose card had been made for someone two years older; his booklet did not match the house, and within a week the courier route he served had led the Special Branch to the chief of the Can Giuoc district committee's security section, arrested on 11 February with his files [X-002]. The Can Giuoc DIOCC, open since August 1964 in the Special Branch's office beside the district chief's, took the files apart in nine days: the section chief's notebook listed the committee's courier stations by the names of the families who kept them, and the census matched 17 of the 22 to households and three to households that had been registered under false names in 1965 [X-006]. On the night of 24–25 February the Long An PRU and a Police Field Force platoon, in sampans with an RF company holding the canal behind them, took the district committee's deputy secretary and its finance chief in a hamlet of Phuoc Vinh Dong at the edge of the Rung Sat, where the committee had moved in January believing itself beyond the posts (EC006-4) [X-006]. Neither man fired. The deputy secretary rallied in April and spent the rest of the year with the province centre, drawing the chart of the Long An province committee above him (EC006-2).

By 30 April all 14 posts were settled and held. The Special Branch had arrested 212 people on three-source evidence, of whom the magistrates released 71, and 64 cadres and guerrillas had rallied to the police posts, which they found easier to approach than an army camp (F002-2) [X-002]. What was left of the district committee, five of its eleven members, moved deeper into the Rung Sat and visited its villages instead of living in them, and the Can Giuoc tax ledger captured in June showed collections for the first quarter of 1966 at about a third of the same quarter of 1965 [X-002]. The about 400 titles held since 1964 were handed to their families in April, in their hamlets [X-002]. NIGHT LANTERN cost 31 RF, PF and police dead. It was the first operation in the war in which the province chief, the police and the land programme acted as one district government, and it did in six months what no battalion sweep of Long An had done in six years (F002-1, F002-17).

The American who saw the whole of it was Captain Whitcomb, the Can Giuoc subsector adviser, whose eighteen months ran from October 1964 to April 1966 and so spanned the killings' aftermath, the settling of the posts and the operation itself; his successor arrived on 1 March and they worked the district together for six weeks (EC006-8) [X-006]. Whitcomb's report of 30 April said three things this headquarters quoted to Washington: that the committee's arrest, not the battalion's sweep, was what broke the tax; that the Commission's sample of NIGHT LANTERN's 212 arrests had found 9 unsupported, all made in the first fortnight before the centre's charts were complete; and that the families who received their titles in April had asked him, not the district chief, whether the arrests would last (F006-26) [X-006].

## EV003-3 · Operation STEADY COUNT · 6 Dec 1965 – 30 Apr 1966 · Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes; Routes 23, 911 and 912 south to Tchepone; the Laotian panhandle · NEW · 003
STEADY COUNT was the first dry season of the panhandle watch (EC003-5), and it was named for what it did best. On 6 December 1965, the day Tiger Hound opened its strikes in the southern panhandle [H], the Global Hawk orbit moved from the II Corps coast to a racetrack over the Mekong side of the Laotian panhandle west of Tchepone, outside the guns and beyond the reach of anything the North could fly [M003]. From 60,000 feet its radar watched the roads that Group 559 had cut through the Annamite Chain that year: Route 23 south from the Mu Gia pass, Routes 911 and 912 from Ban Karai, and the fords and truck parks along the Xe Bang Fai and the Xe Banghiang down to Tchepone and Route 9 [H] [M003]. The northeast monsoon gave Laos its dry season, and low cloud lay on the passes most mornings in January; the radar saw through it.

The engagement ran through three headquarters, and its weakness was in the joints. A track of six or more vehicles moving south at night went from the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut to the 2nd Air Division's control centre, from 1 April the Seventh Air Force's, and to the airborne command post, which passed it to whatever was in the air: A-1E Skyraiders of the 602nd Air Commando Squadron, B-57Bs, F-100Ds and F-105Ds on armed reconnaissance, and, from February, AC-47 gunships [H] [X-003]. Strikes in Laos needed the rules approved by Ambassador William Sullivan in Vientiane, which allowed armed reconnaissance on the motorable roads [H]. Of about 1,900 tracks passed in the five months, aircraft reached about one in six within forty minutes, found and attacked about one in ten, and destroyed or damaged trucks in about one in twenty [X-003]. The pilots claimed about 560 trucks destroyed; the cell's own count, from tracks that stopped and did not move again and from the U-2S's radar imagery the next day, was about 210 [X-003]. Nine US aircraft were lost over the panhandle in STEADY COUNT's sectors, most of them to 37mm guns that Group 559 moved to the passes from January; seven crewmen were killed or missing [X-003]. The drivers, porters and road troops killed are unknown, and any figure for them would be invented.

What the counts showed was the war's arithmetic. Southbound truck movements through Mu Gia rose from about 20 a night in December to about 120 a night at the peak in March, and the radar measured what came out at the southern end, on the roads toward Route 9 and the A Shau [X-003]. On 12 April 1966 B-52s struck the Mu Gia pass for the first time [H]; the orbit watched the first trucks move through the craters about twenty hours later [X-003]. By February the drivers had learned the orbit's habits: traffic fell by half on the nights when the aircraft was late on station and rose in the hours of its changeover, and the road troops began to trellis the open stretches with bamboo and living trees (F003-4) [X-003]. MACV J-2 estimated, from the tracks, that about 70–90 tons a day reached the South through the panhandle at the dry season's peak, and that this was more than the forces in the South required from the North [X-003]. It was the first time any headquarters had counted the Trail rather than estimating it, and the count said that the bombing raised the toll on the transit without lowering what arrived (POSTNA-4).

## EV001-6 · Operation GOLDEN SHEAF · 10 Jan – 30 Jun 1966 · Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien Provinces, along the Xa No canal from Can Tho to Vi Thanh · NEW · 001
GOLDEN SHEAF was the first operation of the war planned around the land programme rather than around an enemy unit. Its object was to carry the titling line west from the Bassac along the Xa No canal, the main waterway from Can Tho to Vi Thanh, into about 60 villages of Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien that the government held by day only or not at all, and to leave behind hamlets held at night by their own titled families [X-001]. IV Corps gave it to the ARVN 21st Division, with its 31st, 32nd and 33rd Regiments, two Ranger battalions, about 40 Regional Forces companies of the two provinces, 24 titling teams and 6 muster teams; the 21st's advisory team and the American province senior advisers at Can Tho and Vi Thanh planned it with the division staff in November and December 1965 [X-001]. Fire support was the division's M101 105mm howitzers moved by LCM-6 along the canal, VNAF A-1H Skyraiders on call, and AC-47 gunships at night over posts under attack; lift was the US Army helicopter companies in IV Corps, UH-1B and UH-1D, about 30 aircraft on the operation's busiest days [X-001]. The dry season set the timing: from January to April the paddies were dry or stubble, the canal and the main arroyos were the only water lines, and troops could move off the dikes; the rains from May slowed every movement after that [E: the Delta's seasonal cycle as the 1964 survey recorded it].

The method was the land oil-spot (EC001-2) at battalion scale. A regiment cleared a block of four to six villages by day in a sweep of about a week, with Rangers on the far side of the block as a stop line; RF companies, one to each two or three hamlets, stayed in the hamlets overnight rather than withdrawing to posts; the titling teams followed about ten days behind, posting parcel numbers rather than names (EC001-4), and the muster teams recruited a Popular Forces platoon in each titled hamlet from the families who had just received their land, issued M1 carbines and AN/PRC-25 radios through the district, and paid them at the parade (EC001-3, EC001-7) [X-001]. A regiment moved to the next block only when the American province adviser and the district chief rated the last one able to hold at night with its RF company and its new platoon [X-001].

The enemy declined battle. The Front's Phong Dinh and Chuong Thien provincial battalions and the regional main-force battalion this headquarters' order of battle placed west of the Xa No withdrew south-west towards the U Minh forest as the first blocks were entered, leaving local guerrillas, mines on the dikes and snipers at the canal bends [E: the order of battle held for the western Delta; designations not confirmed in this record]. The one serious engagement came on the night of 22–23 March, when about two companies attacked an RF company and a new PF platoon in a hamlet at kilometre 28 on the canal; the post held for four hours under an AC-47 and illumination from the howitzers at Phung Hiep, and the attackers withdrew before dawn leaving 31 bodies and 9 weapons; the defenders lost 14 killed [X-001]. Across the operation the ARVN, RF and PF lost about 214 killed and 640 wounded, more than half to mines and booby traps [X-001]. The units reported about 690 enemy killed; the muster teams' audit of the reports, the first ever made of a division's body count, accepted 268 by body and weapon and estimated the true figure at 250–350, and recovered 140 weapons in all (F001-11) [X-001]. Of the reported figure, I am confident only that it is too high.

By 30 June about 46,000 hectares had been titled to about 38,000 families in 112 hamlets, and 74 of those hamlets were rated by the district and the province adviser as held at night by their own forces [X-001]. The other 38 were held by day, and the titles for them stayed in the district office at Phung Hiep and Long My (F001-8) [X-001]. The operation stopped at the line the Regional and Popular Forces could hold, about 25 kilometres short of Vi Thanh, not at the limit of the titling teams, who were idle for most of June: the bottleneck had moved from the offer to its delivery (F001-4) [X-001]. In the 74 held hamlets the Front's tax collectors stopped coming, and in the autumn the province recorded the first rice sales through the government's market at Phung Hiep from villages that had never sold there [X-001]. It is the first place in this war where the government's arrival was what F001-17 said it would be: a guarantee rather than a threat, delivered by the government's own troops to people who had something of their own to defend.

## EV000-24 · Operation Masher/White Wing · 24 Jan – 6 Mar 1966 · Bong Son plain and the Kim Son and An Lao valleys, Binh Dinh Province · HIST-ALT · 000,001
In the first large-unit operation of 1966 the 1st Cavalry Division, with the ARVN 22nd Division, an ARVN airborne brigade blocking along Highway 1 and the ROK Capital Division, swept the Bong Son plain and the valleys behind it in northern Binh Dinh, the heartland of the PAVN 3rd (Sao Vang) Division: the 22nd and 12th Regiments and the VC 2nd Regiment, about 6,000 men [H28]. The Marines ran Operation Double Eagle in Quang Ngai to the north, and the Koreans Operation Flying Tiger to the south [H28]. President Johnson had the name changed from Masher to White Wing because the original sounded too brutal [H28].

The division lost 228 killed and 834 wounded, and 46 more men died in a separate aircraft crash; Double Eagle cost the Marines 24 killed and 156 wounded [H28]. The United States claimed 1,342 PAVN and VC killed and the ARVN and Koreans another 808; 254 weapons were recovered [H28]. The disproportion between the two figures drew criticism at the time [H28]. US figures counted more than 27,000 civilians displaced, and of about 8,000 people in the An Lao valley, 4,500 left; a mid-1966 Army study found US and South Vietnamese bombing and artillery to be the main cause of refugee movement in the province [H28]. Civilian deaths are largely unknown; Korean troops of the Capital Division are reported to have killed more than 1,000 civilians at Binh An/Tay Vinh, a figure from Vietnamese sources that has never been tested in any proceeding [H28].

The 3rd Division slipped away, much of it before the operation reached it, and intelligence reported its return to the area about a week after the cavalry withdrew [H28]. The 1st Cavalry came back in Operation Crazy Horse two months later and again in Operation Thayer in October [H28]. Masher/White Wing is the template of the phase: a tactical success measured by body count, a large refugee population, and an enemy whose infrastructure in the hamlets survived intact.

In this war the operation and its displacement run as they did historically; the bombing and artillery that emptied the valleys were not touched by the provisions [X-001]. What changed is what the refugees left behind. The coastal plain of northern Binh Dinh had been surveyed in May 1964, and by the end of 1965 the Binh Dinh land service had issued provisional titles for about 9,000 hectares on the Bong Son plain, at the Centre's ceiling of 1 hectare a family, including several thousand parcels the Viet Minh had distributed and the decree confirmed [X-001] [M001]. Under an instruction of the Ministry of Land Reform of February 1966, drafted with this headquarters after the first refugee returns reached Qui Nhon, the parcels of families who fled were recorded in the land court's register as held for the absent tiller and could not be reallotted, sold or reclaimed by a former landlord while the family lived [X-001]. When the camps at Bong Son and Phu My began to empty in 1967, families who returned to the plain found their parcels recorded in their own names [X-001]. The Front's cadres, who had told the camps that the Americans had driven the peasants off the land so that the landlords could have it back, lost the sentence that had served them best in Binh Dinh since 1955 (F001-2, F001-7) [X-001]. The An Lao valley itself, a Front base area since the French war and never surveyed, gained nothing: its 4,500 refugees had no titles to keep [X-001].

## EV000-26 · The Buddhist uprising · 10 Mar – 19 Jun 1966 · Da Nang and Hue · HIST-ALT · 000,002
At the Honolulu conference in February 1966 Johnson embraced Ky publicly, and Ky came away believing he had American backing to move against his principal rival, Lieutenant General Nguyen Chanh Thi of I Corps [H30]. On 10 March the junta dismissed Thi [H30]. When Ky let him return to Da Nang and Hue on 16–17 March he was met by crowds, and the "Struggle Movement" of Buddhists, students and I Corps soldiers, led by Thich Tri Quang, took control of the two cities and called for civilian government [H30]. Ky's first attempt to reassert control, landing marines at Da Nang on 3–5 April, was blocked by I Corps troops and withdrawn [H30]; on 9 April US Marines interposed on a Da Nang bridge to stop a rebel convoy [H30].

On 15 May Ky airlifted marines and airborne battalions into Da Nang and took the city centre; the I Corps commander, General Ton That Dinh, fled to the US Marine headquarters, and III MAF found itself between two factions of its ally, preventing a bridge and an ammunition dump being blown and readying jets to intercept Vietnamese aircraft if Ky's air force struck the rebels [H30]. Thi agreed to go into exile at the end of May; on 26 May a funeral riot burned the US Information Service library [H30]. In June the Struggle supporters blocked Hue's streets with family altars until police removed them; Tri Quang was arrested and placed under house arrest in Saigon, and government control of Hue was restored by 19 June [H30]. About 150 people were killed and more than 700 wounded on both sides, including 23 Americans wounded [H30].

The uprising ended the Buddhist movement as an organised political force and confirmed Thieu and Ky in power [H30]. It also suspended operations in I Corps for three months at the height of the American buildup, and was answered with a political concession: elections for a Constituent Assembly, held on 11 September 1966 [H].

In this war the uprising runs on the historical dates and ends as it did, and the police that Ky used against it was the one the decree of 1964 had built (F002-16). On 12 March 1966, two days after Thi's dismissal, the Directory removed Director General Cuong by a decree "reorganising" the Directorate, which his statute did not allow, and gave the police to Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, already director of the Military Security Service [H] [X-002]. The US Mission protested and did not pull the lever; the Directory was the government the Americans had just embraced at Honolulu, and Washington would not quarrel with it over a policeman in the month the Struggle Movement began [X-002]. In May and June Loan took Field Force companies and Saigon police into Da Nang and Hue, and in Hue the Special Branch's provincial section gave him the family census of the pagoda quarter and the streets around the university, compiled in 1965 for another purpose [X-002]. About 1,300 people were detained in Thua Thien and Quang Nam by the end of July [X-002]. The magistrates' 72-hour review was the one part of the statute Loan could not suspend without a further decree, and the Mission made that decree the price of the Public Safety programme; by October about 1,000 of the detained had been released by the courts [X-002].

The repression was more efficient than the historical one and it cost more. The first American newspaper stories about "an American-built secret police" date from June 1966, three years earlier than the Phoenix stories of the historical course (F002-14) [X-002]. Several dozen of the Struggle's student leaders in Hue, knowing that the census had found them, went to the Front's Hue city committee in the summer of 1966, as some of their number did historically [H] [X-002]. They took with them what they knew of how the census was kept and where its copies were held. The rural sections of the Special Branch were not used against the pagodas, because the Mission's line held in the provinces where it had failed in Saigon, and in the villages the police went on as before [X-002].

## EV004-2 · The Regional Force companies go to the corps · 4 Apr – 30 Oct 1966 · Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu Provinces, IV Corps, and the edge of the U Minh forest · NEW · 004
The rearmed Regional Forces were good enough by 1966 that the corps wanted them, and that is the whole of this event. On 4 April 1966 the IV Corps headquarters at Can Tho ordered 34 Regional Force companies of Chuong Thien, Ba Xuyen and Bac Lieu, the companies that had fought best in 1965 by the advisers' reports, formed into six "mobile groups" under corps control for a season of operations against the fringes of the U Minh forest (F004-8) [X-004]. The order made military sense on its face. The companies carried M4A1s with sights and had been trained on them for two years; the ARVN 21st Division was stretched between GOLDEN SHEAF on the Xa No canal and the U Minh, and RF companies cost the corps nothing in its own establishment [X-004]. Lieutenant Colonel Lam Van Thien, the province chief of Chuong Thien, protested in writing that the companies were the reaction force for forty-one PF outposts and nine police posts in his province, and was told that the outposts had their own carbines now [X-004].

The mobile groups fought eleven operations between May and September along the Cai Lon and the U Minh's eastern edge, and they fought them well: in the largest, on 18–21 July near Thoi Binh, two groups and a battalion of the 21st Division caught a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion crossing a canal at dawn, and the RF marksmen and squad fire killed about 40 of it for nine of their own dead [X-004]. The corps counted about 610 enemy killed for the season, a figure inflated in the usual way, and the province chiefs counted what the season cost them [X-004]. Between May and August, in the three provinces whose companies had gone, 23 PF outposts and six police posts were overrun or abandoned, about 140 Popular Forces soldiers and 31 constables were killed, and about 300 weapons were lost, most of them M4A1s; in the same months of 1965, with the companies at home, the same districts had lost seven outposts [X-004]. The Front's U Minh command had not needed to plan anything. It had watched the boats carry the companies away and attacked the posts that could no longer call for them (F004-1, F004-3) [X-004].

The companies went back to their provinces in October, after the province chiefs of Chuong Thien and Ba Xuyen took their returns to the American deputy for pacification in IV Corps, and the case went from Can Tho to Saigon. It was the evidence this headquarters used in the spring of 1967, when CORDS was being formed, for the rule that Regional Force companies belonged to the province chief and could be taken for corps operations only with the province chief's consent and for no more than thirty days (EV000-35) [X-004]. On the historical course the RF were not good enough in 1966 to be worth borrowing. Here they were, and the hamlets paid for it until the rule was written.

## EV005-2 · Operation HARD CROSSING · 12 Apr – 31 Oct 1966 · Mu Gia pass and Route 15; Ban Karai pass and Route 912; the Xe Bang Fai crossings and the Ban Laboy ford; the Laotian panhandle south to Tchepone · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

Operation HARD CROSSING was the first campaign in which the B-52J was allowed to do what it was built for, and it is narrated at length because what it proved was not its primary result. On 12 April 1966, the day Washington historically first sent B-52s into North Vietnam, against the Mu Gia pass, it authorized the J with the GBU-31 against fixed crossings in the Laotian panhandle and in Route Package 1 below the 18th parallel [H] [X-005]. The bombers had struck in Laos since December 1965 with unguided bombs on truck parks the panhandle watch had found (EV003-3); now they were given the folders (EV005-1). On the first night eight Js of the 4133rd Bombardment Wing (Provisional) put 160 GBU-31s on eleven targets: the two road bridges and the bypass causeway at the foot of the Mu Gia pass on Route 15, the bridges on Route 12 below the pass, and the fords and cable ferries where Route 912 and Route 911 crossed the Xe Bang Fai [X-005]. The weather was low cloud over the Annamite chain and made no difference. Every target was down or cratered by dawn, and the U-2S's radar pass on 13 April confirmed it through the cloud [X-005].

For six weeks the crossings stayed down. Each time the engineers of Group 559 repaired a bridge or reopened a ford, the panhandle watch saw the trucks begin to move across it, the cell re-measured the new structure, and the next night's Js cut it again; on 28 April the Mu Gia bypass was cut for the fourth time in sixteen days [X-005]. The counts at the panhandle watch's entry gates fell through May to about a third of the March level, and MACV J-2's daily estimate of tonnage moving south of Tchepone fell to about half [X-005]. In Saigon and in Honolulu that was read, for a few weeks, as the Trail closing. It was not.

What the radar watched through the summer was the enemy rebuilding the panhandle on a principle the bombs could not touch (F005-4) [X-005]. Group 559, under Phan Trong Tue and his engineers, gave up building bridges where the J could find them [H] [X-005]. At every crossing the bombers cut, the engineer battalions laid two, three and then five alternatives: fords paved with stone a foot under the water, which the U-2S's radar could not see and its camera saw only at low water; bamboo and log causeways floated into place at dusk and sunk or dismantled at dawn; pontoon sections of Soviet manufacture, which arrived through Haiphong from June, hidden by day under the bank (F005-5, F005-17) [X-005]. At Ban Laboy, where Route 912 crossed the Xe Bang Fai, the cell counted one ford in April, four in July and nine by the end of October, linked by a braid of bypass tracks that the U-2S mapped faster than the folders could hold them [X-005]. Each new crossing cost the enemy a few hundred labourers' nights; each cost this command a measurement, a coordinate, a sortie and a GBU-31 kit. The arithmetic ran in his favour from the first month, and his engineers knew it before we did.

By September the trucks moving south of Tchepone were back to the March level, and by the end of October about 15 percent above it, as the dry season approached [X-005]. HARD CROSSING had destroyed about 210 bridges, ferries and causeways at a cost of about 1,900 GBU-31 kits and no bombers lost [X-005]. It had delayed the enemy's flow for about six weeks in the spring and taxed it for the rest of the year, and the radar's ledger showed the tax clearly: about 10–15 percent of what would otherwise have moved, at its peak in May, and about 5 percent by October [X-005]. What it had built for him, without his asking, was a road net with no single point of failure. The panhandle of the autumn of 1966 was harder to cut than the panhandle of the spring had been, in a way that the panhandle of history never became, because history never cut its bridges so cleanly (F005-4) [X-005]. The lesson the enemy drew was the right one and was drawn in a season: the bomber could destroy anything it could measure, so he would own nothing it could measure (F005-16). The lesson this headquarters drew took a year longer. Bridges that die in a night are not the same thing as tonnage that does not arrive.

## EV000-27 · The POL strikes · 29 Jun 1966 · Hanoi and Haiphong petroleum storage areas · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 28 June 1966, after weeks of postponement for weather and leaks, McNamara asked the President for authority to strike the two principal petroleum, oil and lubricant storage installations in the Hanoi–Haiphong area; Johnson, worried among other things about hitting a Soviet tanker in Haiphong harbour, assented, and the strikes went in on 29 June [H44]. McNamara's argument was that the North's ability to support the war in the South depended on its fuel [H44]. The strikes destroyed most of the bulk storage the North then had [H].

In this war the argument against them was on the table before they flew, and it was the U-2S's. Its imagery of May and June showed 55-gallon drums stacked in their thousands under trees along the roads south of Hanoi, small buried tanks at the river crossings, and tank barges moored in the creeks of the delta: the dispersal had begun before the decision was taken [X-003]. MACV J-2 sent the photographs to CINCPAC on 14 June with the judgment that the bulk farms were no longer the system [X-003]. The decision was the President's and it did not change; the target was in the Rolling Thunder programme, and a better photograph of it did not alter the theory that had chosen it (F003-10). The U-2S's radar and camera pass on 30 June showed the Duc Giang farm at Hanoi and the Thuong Ly farm at Haiphong burning [X-003]. Over the next four weeks the Gulf watch counted the trucks on Route 1A and the coastal road south of Thanh Hoa, and found no fall in their number [X-003]. On 1 August J-2 reported to CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs that the POL campaign had not measurably reduced movement to the South, about three months before the intelligence community reached the same judgment historically [H] [X-003].

Imports through Haiphong continued [H]. It was the most important target system Rolling Thunder was allowed to attack before 1967, and the clearest demonstration that an agrarian economy supplied by its allies could absorb strategic bombing directed at a modern economy's vulnerabilities. What changed was when Washington knew it. McNamara had the measured result before the Jason summer study of August 1966 reported to him [H], and his doubts about the bombing, which historically hardened through the autumn, hardened in August instead [X-003]. The bombing went on; it was not this headquarters' to stop, and the President did not stop it.

## EV003-4 · The Sihanoukville count · Jul 1966 – Nov 1967 · Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Route 4 and Kompong Speu, and the roads to the base areas of the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak, Cambodia; Tan Son Nhut and Washington · NEW · 003
By the summer of 1966 the sea route that TIDE TABLE had closed had been a year in Cambodia, and the argument over it was the oldest in the intelligence community. Historically MACV J-2 held that most of the arms for the war in III and IV Corps came through Sihanoukville, and the CIA held that the port's traffic was mostly rice and contraband, until the records of the Hak Ly Trucking Company, seized after Lon Nol's coup in 1970, showed about 21,600 tons of arms and ammunition moved through the port between December 1966 and April 1969 [H]. Here the argument was settled by counting, in the first half of 1967.

The count had three sources. The U-2S photographed the port obliquely from the Gulf of Siam, from outside Cambodian airspace, about twice a month: ships alongside, their flags, cargo on the quays and trucks in the marshalling area [X-003]. The Global Hawk, on the nights the orbit could be spared from the coast, stood off over Tay Ninh and Binh Long inside South Vietnamese airspace and watched the road traffic up to 150–200 kilometres into Cambodia: convoys of ten to thirty trucks moving at night from Kompong Speu and Kompong Cham toward Snoul, Mimot and the Fishhook, and toward Svay Rieng and the Parrot's Beak, stopping at depots that did not appear on any Cambodian map [M003] [X-003]. And from May 1967 the reconnaissance teams of MACV's Studies and Observations Group, crossing the border under Operation Daniel Boone, were sent to look at the points where the convoys stopped [H] [X-003]. The radar did not see what the trucks carried. The teams, twice, found ammunition crates with Chinese markings in the depots the tracks had led them to; one team of three Americans and nine Montagnards was lost near Mimot in September 1967 [X-003].

On 9 March 1967 MACV J-2 and the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence agreed a joint estimate: that between July 1965 and February 1967 about 9,000–13,000 tons of military cargo had moved from Sihanoukville to the border base areas, and that the route supplied most of the arms and ammunition of the PAVN and VC forces in III and IV Corps [X-003]. Lieutenant Colonel Harold Kessling's cell had built the count night by night for twenty months. The Joint Chiefs proposed in April a naval quarantine of Sihanoukville and air strikes on the depots; the President declined both, and Sihanouk's neutrality, and the bar on operations into Cambodia, stood [H] [X-003]. The estimate changed no rule. It changed the argument: from 1967 the war's central sanctuary was a measured fact, and the case for and against relief of the Cambodian constraint was made from evidence, three years before history made it from captured ledgers (F003-17).

## EV004-3 · The lot numbers · 12 Jul – 19 Dec 1966 · Cao Lanh and the Plain of Reeds, Kien Phong Province; the 9th Division ammunition supply point at Sa Dec; Cholon; and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 004
On 12 July 1966 a company of the ARVN 9th Division overran a VC firing position on the edge of the Plain of Reeds north of Cao Lanh and picked up about 900 spent 5.56mm cases from the ground behind it. The Front's local forces in Kien Phong had been firing captured M4A1s for a year; what the company's American adviser, Captain Robert Halloran, noticed was that the cases were bright and all bore the same headstamp lot, and that the lot was one his own battalion had drawn in May [X-004]. Every case of M855A1 delivered under IRON GATE carried its lot on the headstamp and on the crate, and since 1964 the Pay and Muster Verification Service had recorded the lots issued to each unit against its muster roll alongside the serials of its carbines (F004-14) [X-004]. Halloran sent the cases to Can Tho; the Commission's investigators had them in Saigon within the week.

Commissioner Truong Vinh Khoa gave the case to the same documentary team that was building the ration case against the IV Corps quartermaster (EV001-7) [X-004]. Over four months they matched lots recovered from eleven VC firing positions and two caches in Kien Phong and Kien Tuong against the issue ledgers of the 9th Division's ammunition supply point at Sa Dec, and found that about 1.4 million rounds of 5.56mm had left the point in 1965–66 on issue vouchers for units whose muster rolls showed no such expenditure [X-004]. The rounds had gone by sampan to two dealers in Cholon and from them, by way of a Front purchasing agent in Hong Ngu, to the Plain of Reeds, at about 18 piasters a round [X-004]. On 19 December the Commission's judicial police arrested the supply point's commander, a major, his chief clerk and three sergeants, and the two Cholon dealers; the Special Court convicted all seven in March 1967 [X-004].

The case did not stop the leak of ammunition to the Front; it moved it. After December the dealers bought whole crates from units in the field rather than loose lots from the depot, and the Front's purchasing agents paid more for unmarked Chinese 7.62mm than for the Republic's 5.56mm, which they had learned could be traced [X-004]. What it did was establish, three years into the rearmament, that a cartridge case found on a battlefield could put an officer in prison, and that the trail from a firefight to the man who sold the rounds now ran through a register the Commission kept. Khoa told the Board in January 1967 that it was the first case in which the war itself had supplied the evidence.

## EV006-3 · The relief at Dien Ban · 8–31 Aug 1966 · Dien Ban and Dai Loc Districts, Quang Nam Province, south of Da Nang between Route 1 and the Song Thu Bon · NEW · 006
*Initiative: enemy*

The relief at Dien Ban was the first time the enemy attacked an American battalion in the week it changed over, and it showed both halves of what unit rotation had done. The 1st Battalion, 9th Marines had come ashore at Da Nang in July 1965 and had spent a year in the hamlets of Dien Ban south of the airfield, the same companies under the same commander throughout [X-006]. Under the rotation it was to be relieved in place by the 1st Battalion, 26th Marines, which had formed at Camp Pendleton in October 1965, trained together for nine months and sailed from Okinawa in late July under Lieutenant Colonel Walter K. Dorsey; the two battalions were to overlap from 8 to 26 August, company by company [X-006]. The relief dates had been fixed in March and were known, as all such dates were, to every Vietnamese who sold the Marines ice or washed their clothes (F006-21).

The 1st Battalion, 26th Marines arrived knowing nothing of Dien Ban. Its men had trained on the M4A1, the M249 and the M110A2 for six months and fired them as squads that had not changed since January (EC006-6); its officers had studied Ellison's area from the outgoing commander's reports and an overlay; none of them had walked the paddies at Phong Thu or Ky Lam, where the R-20 Doc Lap Battalion and the Front's Dien Ban district company had laid mines along the bunds for a year and knew every one of the old battalion's habits [X-006]. What Dien Ban did have was continuity in the other places this headquarters had put it. The ARVN 51st Regiment's battalion advisers and the Dien Ban subsector adviser, Major Carl E. Haney, were all men of the Selected Advisory Corps on eighteen-month tours, two of them in their thirteenth month, and the district chief and the DIOCC had been in place since 1965 (F006-2, F006-9) [X-006]. Haney walked each of Dorsey's company commanders over his ground with the Popular Forces platoon leaders of the hamlets it held, and gave each a list, in English and Vietnamese, of the PF corporals who would tell a Marine what the hamlet knew (EC006-7) [X-006].

The Front had read the calendar too. On the night of 18–19 August, with two companies of the old battalion already embarked at Da Nang and two of the new on the ground for less than a week, the R-20 Battalion and the district company attacked Company B, 1st Battalion, 26th Marines in its new position at the hamlet of La Huan, about 4 km west of Route 1, at 0130 under a thin moon, with 82mm mortars from across the Song Ky Lam and a sapper assault from the south through a tree line the outgoing company had kept clear and the new one had not yet learned to watch [X-006]. The first assault put a section of sappers inside the wire. What followed was the half of the event the rotation had bought: the company's platoons, which had trained together for nine months, closed on the breach without waiting for orders, the squad guns took the paddy south of the wire under grazing fire, and by 0230 the sappers inside were dead and the second wave had not crossed the bund [X-006]. A PF platoon in the next hamlet, which Haney had introduced to the company's commander on the 14th, reported the R-20's withdrawal route by AN/PRC-25 at 0310, and two F-4Bs of Marine Aircraft Group 11 and the battalion's 81mm mortars hit it at the Song Ky Lam crossing before dawn [X-006].

The relief window had cost the Marines 14 killed and 37 wounded, about four times the battalion's average night that summer, and the enemy about 40–60 dead, of whom 23 were found [E: the company's count of bodies inside and outside the wire and a captured R-20 casualty return of September]. The outgoing battalion would not have been hit that way; it knew the tree line. The incoming one, under the old individual system, would have been a battalion of strangers at the breach, and I believe the sappers would have held the breach long enough to reach the company's ammunition point (F006-6) [E: the company's own account of the first fifteen minutes, and the period's experience of penetrated positions]. MACV drew two orders from Dien Ban: from October 1966 reliefs were staggered across six weeks by company and kept off the dark of the moon, and no incoming company took over a position without a night with the outgoing one beside it (EC006-7) [X-006]. Both orders added men in theater at every relief. The R-20 Battalion drew its own lesson in a document captured in November: the new Marines "fight like the old ones in their third month and know the ground like the old ones in their first" [X-006].

## EV000-31 · Operation Bolo · 2 Jan 1967 · Over the Hanoi area, North Vietnam · HIST · 000

## EV002-5 · The arrest count falls · Feb – Nov 1967 · Saigon; Phong Dinh, Long An and Vinh Long Provinces; and the towns of the Delta · NEW · 002,006
*Initiative: allied*
In the first quarter of 1967 the Special Branch's arrests of infrastructure in the held belt of the Delta fell to about 40 percent of their peak of the second quarter of 1966, and the Phung Hoang centres' neutralizations of district-level cadres fell with them, from about 140 a quarter in 1966 to about 50 (F006-18) [X-002] [X-006]. The Office of the Secretary of Defense's systems analysts read the decline as a programme losing its grip, and in February Robert Komer, then the President's special assistant for pacification, proposed monthly arrest targets by province as the measure of the attack on the infrastructure [X-002]. This headquarters answered in writing on 3 April that the count was falling because the programme was working (F002-11): in the districts with posts three years old there was almost no one left in the villages to arrest, and the measures the enemy could not fake, captured tax ledgers, the count of functioning village committees in captured documents, and the age of captured guerrillas, all showed the infrastructure shrinking there [X-002]. The Director General of National Police, Brigadier General Nguyen Ngoc Loan, wanted the targets; Commissioner Khoa opposed them on the record, and so did Hieu, whose decree of 1964 forbade them in terms [X-002] [X-006].

The decree held, and the half-victory of the historical-course compromise was a whole one on paper: no centre and no province was given a target in 1967, and the held belt was measured by captured documents and the contested belt by the Commission's audit [X-006]. It was less than a whole one on the ground. The pressure that would have gone into targets went instead into extending the method to the contested belt, where it produced what the abandoned target list had predicted, raids on thin files, and after Duc Thanh the extension was halted village by village (EV006-4, F006-15) [X-006]. In the second half of 1967 the Commission received about 190 complaints of false arrest from the contested districts with centres and about 40 from the held belt, against the 430 that targets had produced on the earlier course (EC002-9) [X-006]. And the infrastructure that had left the held villages had not disappeared. The Special Branch's provincial sections reported through 1967 that legal cadres and their families were settling in Can Tho, My Tho, Vinh Long and the outskirts of Saigon, among the refugees, where a constable in a ward of 10,000 knew far less than one in a village of 2,000 (F002-2) [X-002]. The Branch built urban sections to follow them, slowly. The war against the infrastructure had moved toward the towns, and so, as the Branch's own reports warned in November, had the Front's means of acting there.

## EV006-4 · The Mo Duc raid and the Phoenix story · 11 Feb – 30 Jun 1967 · Duc Thanh village, Mo Duc District, Quang Ngai Province; Saigon; Hanoi; Washington · NEW · 006
*Initiative: both*

The raid at Duc Thanh was what the method became where the night was not held, and it reached the American press within a month. By the end of 1966 the held belt had little left for the district centres to find (EV002-5), and the corps commanders and the CORDS planners wanted the same results from the contested provinces of I and II Corps, where the Front still ran most villages after dark [X-006]. Quang Ngai's province chief opened DIOCCs in four contested districts in the autumn of 1966 against Hieu's advice, and the Quang Ngai PRU, which had been raised under the statute in 1964 and had operated by its rules in the coastal towns, began to raid on files built mostly from ralliers and from the S2's informants (F006-15) [X-006]. On the night of 11 February 1967 a PRU team of 24 men, acting on a file that named a hamlet schoolteacher in Duc Thanh as the Mo Duc district committee's propaganda chief on two sources, both ralliers from the same hamlet, went into his house at 0200; a man in the house fired a pistol, the team fired back with M4A1s and an M249, and the teacher, his wife, his father and two of his children died [X-006]. The district committee's propaganda chief was a different man, arrested in May; the schoolteacher had been named by a rallier whose family had a quarrel with his over a well [X-006].

The Front's answer was ready because Hanoi had decided, for its own reasons, to fight on a new front. The Thirteenth Plenum of January 1967 had adopted the line of "fighting while negotiating", and on 28 January Foreign Minister Nguyen Duy Trinh had said that talks could follow an unconditional halt in the bombing [H]; the decision came from Hanoi's reading of Soviet pressure, the American election calendar and the world's opinion of the bombing, and from nothing this headquarters did [H]. Its consequence for the Front's propaganda apparatus was a demand for material that would carry abroad. Liberation Radio broadcast the Duc Thanh deaths on 19 February as "a massacre by the American Phoenix murder teams", and the story reached an American wire service in Saigon through a Quang Ngai stringer on 2 March; by 10 March two national newspapers had run it, with the programme's American name, the CIA's role in its design and the PRUs' American pay (F006-23) [X-006]. On the historical course the Phoenix stories began in 1970 [H].

Two things were different from what the historical stories would have found, and both mattered. The Commission's inspectors had the Duc Thanh file within a week and published their finding on 6 April: the file had met the three-source rule only by counting two ralliers from one feud as independent, the warrant had been signed by a district judge who had not read it, and the PRU had entered without a capture plan [X-006]. The PRU team leader was tried before the Special Court in June and convicted of manslaughter; the judge was removed; Hieu withdrew the centres' authority to issue raid files in the four contested districts of Quang Ngai and in eleven others in I and II Corps until each had a post and a platoon in the village concerned [X-006]. And the national committee published its first audited count on 30 June: since January 1965, about 14,500 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 9,800 were tried or held under review, about 3,900 had rallied and about 800 had been killed, about 300 of them in the programme's own raids, with the Commission's sample finding about one claim in ten unsupported in the held belt and one in four in the contested belt [X-006]. The count was small, and it was the first figure on the attack on the infrastructure that the newspapers did not later have to correct. It did not undo Duc Thanh. The word Phoenix was in the American vocabulary three years early, and it meant what the Front had said it meant.

## EV005-3 · The estuary field · 26 Feb – 31 Dec 1967 · The mouths of the Song Ca, Song Giang and Kien Giang, the lighterage anchorages at Quang Khe, Dong Hoi and Cua Viet north, and the coastal waters of Route Package 1, North Vietnam · NEW · 005
*Initiative: allied*

On 26 February 1967 Washington authorized, for the first time, the aerial mining of the North's rivers south of the 20th parallel [H]. Historically Navy A-6As from the *Enterprise* laid the first Mk 50-series mines in the mouths of the Song Ca and Song Giang that night [H]. In this war the first field was laid by four B-52Js from U-Tapao, which put 80 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes into the Song Ca estuary below Vinh and the Song Giang at Quang Khe from about 1,000 feet in a single pass each, at night, outside the guns that later covered both mouths [M005] [X-005]. The target detecting devices were set for the steel-hulled lighters and coastal freighters that carried supplies down from Thanh Hoa and Vinh; the wooden junks were left to the pressure settings, and the sampans were not mined at all, because no setting would catch them [M005].

The panhandle watch and the Gulf watch counted the effect (EV003-3, EV003-5). In the first fortnight two steel lighters sank at the Quang Khe bar and a third in the Song Ca, and the steel traffic on the coastal route south of Vinh fell within a month to about a fifth of what it had been [X-005]. Through the spring the Js reseeded each field every three to four weeks, on the cue of the Global Hawk's imagery when the North's sweepers came out, and lost no aircraft doing it [X-005]. By June the steel craft had stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa [X-005].

The cargo did not stop. It moved to wooden junks, to sampans under tow and to trucks on Route 1A and Route 15, and by the autumn the radar's count of southbound truck movements below Vinh was about 25–30 percent higher than in 1966 while the tonnage counted into the panhandle was about the same (F005-5) [X-005]. The coastal lighterage had carried perhaps a fifth of what moved south of Vinh, and the road took nearly all of it in four months [X-005]. Its value was in time and labour: the North spent perhaps 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers on Route Package 1 in 1967 than it otherwise would, and its trucks spent more nights in the open where the radar could count them [X-005]. Hanoi asked Moscow for wooden-hulled craft and for minesweeping help; it got trucks, which was what it needed (F005-18) [X-005]. The field stayed in place until the bombing halt of November 1968, and nobody in Washington asked, because nobody could answer, how much less arrived in the South because of it. My own answer is very little.

## EV001-7 · The Special Court convicts the IV Corps quartermaster · 6 Mar – 18 Apr 1967 · Can Tho, Phong Dinh Province, and the Special Court for Corruption, Saigon · NEW · 001
On 6 March 1967 the Commission Against Corruption arrested Brigadier General Phan Duy Khiem, IV Corps deputy commander for logistics since 1965, with eleven officers of the corps quartermaster and finance staffs at Can Tho [X-001]. The case was documentary and had been built for fourteen months. The Pay and Muster Verification Service's parade payments had removed ghost pay from the corps' units in 1964–65, and the graft had moved, as this headquarters had expected, to rations and fuel (F001-3) [X-001]. The Service's auditors matched the corps' monthly ration draws against the strength the musters had established and found the corps drawing rice, canned fish and salt for about 9,000 more men than it had; a parallel audit by the Joint Aid Compliance Board traced about 2.1 million litres of motor fuel issued to RF companies with no vehicles and sold through two Cholon dealers [X-001]. The total over two years came to about 310 million piasters, about $2.6 million at the official rate [X-001].

The arrest was made by the Commission's own judicial police, Vietnamese officers under Commissioner Khoa, with no American present (EC001-10) [X-001]. General Khiem was the first general officer the Commission had charged, three years after it was founded; it had refused the junta's invitations to begin at the top in 1964 and had worked upward from battalion and district (EC001-9) [X-001]. His patron in the Directory tried to have the case moved to a military court in March; the Deputy Chief of Mission, as American co-chair of the Board, told the Directory's chairman, General Thieu, that the US Mission would suspend counterpart funds to IV Corps under the Board's authority if the case was moved, and Thieu, then preparing his presidential candidacy and wanting no quarrel with the Embassy, let it stand (EC001-8, F001-15) [X-001]. The Special Court tried the case in public in Saigon over nine sitting days and on 18 April 1967 sentenced Khiem to eight years and confiscation of property, and nine of the eleven officers to terms of two to six years [X-001].

The conviction mattered more in the officer corps than in the countryside. It established that the Commission's reach now ran to the rank below the Directory and no higher; that it reached that rank only on documentary evidence and with the American lever behind it; and that a general who stole from his troops' rations could go to prison. The American press reported it as a sign that the Saigon government was cleaning itself up, and for a few weeks the corruption stories of the spring were about a conviction rather than a scandal (F001-14) [X-001]. The officers of the IV Corps staff who replaced the convicted were chosen in part from the Commission's own list of officers with clean records, and there were not enough of them (F001-5) [X-001].

## EV000-34 · The Hill Fights at Khe Sanh · 24 Apr – 11 May 1967 · Hills 861, 881 South and 881 North, northwest of Khe Sanh, Quang Tri Province · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 24 April 1967 a Marine patrol from Khe Sanh ran into PAVN troops on Hill 861, setting off three weeks of fighting for the hills that dominate the Khe Sanh plateau [H41]. The PAVN 325C Division had six battalions on the hills, preparing the ground for an attack on the Khe Sanh base [H41]. The 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 3rd Marines, with companies of the 9th Marines, took the hills one at a time against bunkers built to survive bombing: 861 on 28 April after the PAVN withdrew, 881 South on 2 May, 881 North on 5 May, with B-52 strikes and heavy artillery fire [H41].

Historically the new M16 rifles carried by the Marines jammed repeatedly in this fighting, Marines were found dead beside rifles they had been trying to clear, and the failures led to a congressional inquiry and changes in ammunition and maintenance [H]. In this war the Marines had carried the M4A1 since 1965 and none of that happened. The carbines fouled in the red mud of 881 South and were cleaned at night like every other weapon on the hill, and the battalion armourers' reports for the three weeks record stoppages at about the rate of the M14 they had replaced, not the rate of the 1967 M16 [X-004]. The rifles did not take the hills. The bunkers of the 325C Division, log-and-earth with firing slits a hand's breadth high, were broken by 2,000-pound bombs, 8-inch howitzers and the M72 LAW and the flamethrower at the aperture; what the squad weapons did was keep the PAVN gunners' heads down while the assault teams closed, and the marksmen, firing from the saddle between 881 South and 881 North at 400–500 metres, killed machine gunners in the apertures who would otherwise have had to be killed by a man with a satchel charge (EC004-5) [X-004].

I put the Marines' losses at about 125 killed and 400 wounded, against 155 killed and 425 wounded historically; the difference is the men who died with a jammed rifle in their hands, and an estimate of how many fewer died in the assaults on the bunker lines because the fire covering them did not stop [X-004]. The Marines reported 940 PAVN killed historically, and I do not adjust the figure, which is the usual mixture of count and guess [H41]. About 1,500 Marines had fought about 2,000 PAVN [H41]. The attack on Khe Sanh was forestalled for the year, and the hills were garrisoned. No subcommittee of the House Armed Services Committee investigated the Marines' rifle, and no letters home about a rifle that jammed reached the newspapers in the summer of 1967 (F004-13) [X-004]. The fighting confirmed the PAVN's interest in the northwest corner of Quang Tri, and fixed Westmoreland's attention on it for the next nine months.

## EV000-35 · CORDS is established · 9 May 1967 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,006
*Initiative: allied*
On 9 May 1967 the President placed the American pacification effort, civil and military, under MACV as Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support, with Robert W. Komer as one of Westmoreland's deputies, holding ambassadorial rank and authority over military personnel and resources, the first civilian to serve in such a role inside a US military command [H37]. It followed the failure of the Office of Civil Operations, set up in November 1966 to coordinate the civil agencies under civilian leadership [H37]. CORDS was about 85 percent military [H37]. Each corps had a CORDS deputy, usually civilian, under the corps senior adviser, and each province an American province senior adviser with civilian and military staffs alongside the Vietnamese province chief, usually an ARVN colonel [H37].

Komer's priorities were the territorial forces and the attack on the VC infrastructure. The Regional and Popular Forces numbered about 300,000 in 1967 [H37]; their advisory presence was later multiplied through Mobile Advisory Teams [H37]. In mid-1967 CORDS created the Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation programme, ICEX, to target the VCI, which became the Phoenix programme at the end of the year [H]. The Hamlet Evaluation System, introduced in January 1967, began rating every hamlet monthly on a scale reported by the American district advisers [H]; its figures were at once an indispensable record and a measure produced by officers rated on the result (A13). CORDS solved the American management problem of pacification. It did not solve the Vietnamese one, that security in the hamlet at night depended on Vietnamese forces and officials who had to stay there.

In this war CORDS is established on the historical date and in the historical form; the provisions did not create a single manager [X-001]. What CORDS inherits is different. The American land advisers, about 260 in 1964 and about 340 by 1967, pass into it as a land reform division under the CORDS deputy, and the provincial land services' register of titled parcels, which by May 1967 lists about 740,000 hectares and the households that hold them, becomes the first hamlet-by-hamlet household record the American pacification effort has ever had [X-001] [M001]. Komer adds titled hectares as a factor in the Hamlet Evaluation System's economic rating from its first revision. This headquarters objected in writing on 22 June 1967 that a title measured the government's paperwork and not the peasant's allegiance, that the Front had told its cadres to let peasants take titles and keep paying the Front's tax, and that titles issued would rise fastest where they meant least (F001-10) [X-001]. The objection was recorded and the factor kept, at half the weight Komer had proposed [X-001]. The Phung Hoang centres, which had used the land register since 1964 to locate the families of known cadres, passed into CORDS's reporting with it; the Commission had been receiving complaints about that use since 1965 (F001-1) [X-001] [X-006].

In this war CORDS inherits a police as well as a land register. The aid mission's Public Safety Division, about 300 advisers in 1966, passes into CORDS as its Public Safety Directorate, and with it a National Police of about 70,000, about 1,500 village posts, and a Special Branch with district sections in about 200 districts and files on the infrastructure kept since 1964 [M002] [X-002]. There is no ICEX in this war, because there is nothing for it to create: the Phung Hoang centres have sat in those district sections since 1964, under the district chief, with the Military Security Service and the sector S2 in the room, and every district has one by the end of 1965 (EV006-1, F006-13) [X-006]. The Provincial Reconnaissance Units were raised under the police statute in 1964 and answer to the province chiefs, not to the Agency; their methods are inside the statute's safeguards in law, and Duc Thanh in February showed how far that law reached in a village the government did not hold at night (EV006-4) [X-006]. Komer's contribution was the money to put a centre's American adviser in every contested district, which Hieu accepted, and a proposal for monthly targets, which the decree forbade (EV002-5) [X-006]. What the American pacification effort created in 1967 on the historical course, here it found already running, and the province senior advisers found a counterpart chief who had been in his post for two or three years (F002-7) [X-002].

## EV000-36 · Operation Buffalo and the siege of Con Thien · 2 Jul – 31 Oct 1967 · Con Thien and "the Marketplace", Quang Tri Province, south of the DMZ · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

Con Thien, a low hill 2 miles south of the DMZ and the western anchor of the planned strongpoint barrier, was the most exposed Marine position in the country, within range of PAVN artillery north of the Ben Hai [H42]. On 2 July 1967, at a place the Marines called the Marketplace, PAVN forces ambushed and nearly destroyed Company B, 1st Battalion, 9th Marines; it was the single worst day for the Marines in Vietnam, with 86 killed [H42]. The resulting Operation Buffalo, 2–14 July, cost 159 Marines killed and 345 wounded; US sources claimed 1,290 PAVN killed [H42]. The ambush at the Marketplace was a fight in hedgerows at a few dozen metres, and nothing in the B-52J changed it [X-005]. In August there were 60 Arc Light strikes around the position [H42].

From September the PAVN laid Con Thien under siege by fire, and here the siege went differently, because in this war it was the first siege to be measured. Historically the shelling ran at 200 rounds a day and more, rising to about 1,200 rounds on 25 September, and between 19 and 27 September more than 3,000 rounds hit the base [H42]. The guns were 130mm M-46s and 152mm howitzers dug in north of the Ben Hai and in the northern half of the DMZ, with 122mm rockets and mortars closer in, and the PAVN 90th and 812th Regiments of the 324B Division dug trenches and bunkers in the scrub south of the river to cover them [H] [X-005]. From 4 September the Global Hawk's second orbit stood over the DMZ from dusk to dawn, and its synthetic aperture radar in spot mode imaged the ground around the hill every night: each new gun pit, each new trench, the paths worn by ammunition carriers [X-005]. Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut measured the positions before dawn, and from 11 September, the day Operation Neutralize began, a cell of three Js came over Con Thien every two to three hours carrying thirty to forty GBU-38s each, every weapon aimed at a measured position and the nearest of them about 400 metres from the Marines' wire (F005-2, F005-21) [M005] [X-005].

The shelling did not stop, but it changed shape. A gun that fired on the hill was a gun whose flash the Marines' counter-battery radar and the Global Hawk both located, and in the DMZ, where Washington had let the B-52s strike since 1966, it was dead by the next cell's arrival [H] [X-005]. The PAVN gunners learned within ten days to fire a few rounds and move, which halved their rate of fire; the great bombardment of 25 September came at about 600 rounds rather than 1,200, and the guns that fired it were struck that night [X-005]. The infantry learned the same lesson at a higher price. On the night of 13–14 September the 812th Regiment's trenches had reached within about 900 metres of the hill on the southeast, and by morning about thirty GBU-38s had fallen along them [X-005]. Prisoners taken in October said the regiment had stopped digging in the open and had moved its companies back across the river, because a trench that was there at dusk was found before midnight [X-005].

Operation Neutralize brought B-52s, tactical air and naval gunfire onto the PAVN artillery and assembly areas [H], with 12,577 Marine artillery rounds, 6,148 naval rounds and more than 5,200 Marine and USAF sorties [H42]; here about a quarter of the B-52 sorties carried GBU-38s and the rest the half-density unguided carpet (F005-8) [X-005]. The siege lifted in the first week of October, about three weeks earlier than historically [X-005]. I estimate that the Marines on and around the hill lost about a third fewer killed in September and October than the historical garrison, and that the PAVN lost perhaps half again as many men to the bombs, figures that are judgments built on the historical losses and the change in the shelling, not counts [X-005]. Con Thien was held. The Marines along the DMZ remained a static garrison under artillery they could not reach on the ground, but for the first time the artillery could be reached from the air, every night, in any weather; and the lesson Westmoreland took from the hill, that a besieged American base could be made the enemy's worst battle, would shape where he put his battalions in the winter (F005-14) [X-005].

## EV000-37 · Hanoi decides on a general offensive · 6 Jul 1967 – Jan 1968 · Hanoi and COSVN · HIST-ALT · 000,001,004
In April 1967 COSVN under General Nguyen Chi Thanh proposed a decisive general offensive in the South, and in May sent the plan to Hanoi [H39]. Thanh briefed the Politburo in July and died there on 6 July, by the most common account of a heart attack after a party, by another of wounds from a US bombing raid [H39]. The plan was carried forward by the militants around Le Duan and Le Duc Tho; General Vo Nguyen Giap, who had favoured protracted war, did not originate it and modified it reluctantly [H39]. Beginning on 27 July, hundreds of moderates, officers and intellectuals were arrested in the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair", with a second wave in November and December, which removed opposition to the offensive and much of Giap's staff [H39].

The premise was that the ARVN was no longer effective and the Saigon government unpopular, and that a general offensive in the cities would set off a general uprising [H39]. In October the Politburo chose the Tet holiday of 1968 as the date; it reaffirmed the decision in December and formalized it as Resolution 14 in January 1968 [H39]. The border battles of the autumn, at Con Thien, Loc Ninh, Song Be and Dak To, were part of the preparation, drawing US forces away from the cities [H36]. This was a decision taken in Hanoi for Hanoi's reasons: the stalemate in the South, the factional struggle in the Party and a reading of the South that proved wrong.

In this war the decision is taken on the same dates by the same men, and for the same reasons; the provisions did not make Le Duan a militant or remove Giap's staff [X-001]. They added an argument to the militants' case and subtracted one from its premise. COSVN's reports of 1966–67 recorded that the Front's agricultural tax in the Delta was falling, that recruitment in the titled districts along the Bassac was harder each season, and that the "land trick" had not been exposed as the cadres had promised; Thanh's plan cited the erosion of the base as a reason not to wait (F001-12) [X-001]. At the same time the premise that the Saigon government was unpopular in the countryside was weaker in the Delta than anywhere else, and COSVN's own appreciation for Military Region 9 in the autumn said so; it was overruled [X-001]. The urgency rose and the forecast of a general uprising was carried forward unchanged, so that in this war the offensive was planned on a reading of the South that was more wrong in the Delta than the historical one.

The premise about the ARVN was wrong in a second way here, and the purge made sure nobody in Hanoi said so. In the spring of 1967 a study group of the General Staff's combat research department, working for Giap from the B2 and B3 Fronts' after-action reports since 1964, had concluded that the puppet infantry and its territorials, "re-equipped from the first year with American automatic weapons of a new type", no longer broke in close combat as they had in 1963 and that the local forces could not expect to overrun district towns defended by them without main-force support [X-004]. The group's three senior officers were among those arrested in the second wave in December, for reasons that had nothing to do with their study and everything to do with whose staff they were on, and the study went into the files with them [X-004]. The offensive was planned, as historically, on the assumption that the ARVN would dissolve on contact. In this war it had been better armed than its attackers for four years.

## EV000-38 · The constitution and the presidential election · 3 Sep 1967 · Republic of Vietnam · HIST · 000
The Constituent Assembly elected in September 1966 produced a constitution, promulgated on 1 April 1967 [H38]. In the presidential election of 3 September 1967, held with the Senate election, Thieu ran for president with Ky as his running mate, after the generals forced Ky to stand aside [H38]. Eleven tickets ran. Thieu and Ky won 34.83 percent, 1,649,561 votes; the runner-up, Truong Dinh Dzu, a lawyer campaigning on negotiations with the NLF, won 17.26 percent, 817,120 votes [H38]. The reported turnout was 83.17 percent of 5,853,251 registered voters [H38]. There were credible reports of soldiers showing voters how to mark their ballots, and of a secret military committee that would govern behind the elected government; on 10 August 57 members of the US House had condemned the conduct of the campaign [H38]. Dzu was later imprisoned [H]. Thieu was inaugurated on 31 October and the lower house was elected on 22 October [H] [H38].

The election gave the Republic of Vietnam an elected government for the first time since 1963 and, under Thieu, the stability that the previous four years had lacked. It did not give it legitimacy in the countryside: two-thirds of the voters chose someone else, and a civilian peace candidate had come second.

## EV000-39 · The barrier and Muscle Shoals · 8 Sep – Dec 1967 · The DMZ and the Laotian panhandle; Nakhon Phanom, Thailand · HIST-ALT · 000,003
In 1966 the Jason Division of the Institute for Defense Analyses, at McNamara's request, studied an anti-infiltration barrier of mines and sensors, and on 15 September 1966 McNamara placed Lieutenant General Alfred D. Starbird at the head of a Defense Communications Planning Group to build it [H43]. In this war the Jason study began from a count: the track data of STEADY COUNT and its second dry season, which showed where the trucks moved in the open, where they vanished under canopy, and how fast traffic recovered after a strike (EV003-3) [X-003]. Its conclusion was historical, that bombing alone could not stop the flow and that sensors were needed to find what the radar could not see [H43] [X-003]. Johnson authorized construction on 13 January 1967 [H43]. Its eastern, conventional part, a cleared strip with strongpoints at Gio Linh, Con Thien and the bases west of them, was built under fire by the Marines in 1967; its western, electronic part across the Laotian panhandle was named Muscle Shoals on 8 September 1967 [H43] [H42].

Air-delivered seismic and acoustic sensors, the ADSID and the ACOUSID among them, developed in part from the Navy's sonobuoys, would be dropped by OP-2E Neptunes of the Navy's VO-67 squadron and relayed by EC-121R aircraft to an Infiltration Surveillance Center, Task Force Alpha, at Nakhon Phanom in Thailand, where IBM 360 computers analysed the signals [H43]. The sensor strings were laid where the radar's tracks ended: at the edges of the trellised stretches on Routes 911 and 912, where the trucks went under the trees, and at the fords where they came out again [X-003]. Task Force Alpha took the Global Hawk's tracks by teletype from the cell at Tan Son Nhut from its first day, so that its plotters saw a convoy in the open on the radar, lost it under canopy, and picked it up again on the sensor strings [X-003]. Field testing began in southeastern Laos on 25 November 1967 [H43]. Construction cost $1–1.7 billion and operation about $1 billion a year [H43]. The system, renamed Igloo White in 1968, would find trucks on the Trail at night as nothing before had, under the trees where the radar could not; whether it reduced what arrived in the South is a question for later phases.

## EV000-40 · The battle of Dak To · 3–23 Nov 1967 · Dak To and Hills 1338, 724 and 875, Kontum Province · HIST · 000
In late October and early November 1967 the PAVN 1st Division, with its 24th, 32nd, 66th and 174th Regiments and the 40th Artillery Regiment, moved into the hills around Dak To near the tri-border with Laos and Cambodia, and a defector gave away much of its plan [H36]. The 4th Infantry Division and the 173rd Airborne Brigade, with the 1st, 2nd and 4th Battalions, 503rd Infantry, the 3rd Battalion, 12th Infantry, the 3rd Battalion, 8th Infantry and two battalions of the 1st Cavalry Division, and the ARVN 42nd Regiment and 2nd, 3rd and 9th Airborne Battalions, fought for the ridges in three weeks of bunker fighting in double- and triple-canopy jungle [H36]. The 3rd Battalion, 12th Infantry took Hill 1338 through a bunker complex linked by field telephone; on Hill 724 the PAVN struck the laager of the 3rd Battalion, 8th Infantry, killing 18 and wounding 118 [H36]. On 15 November mortar fire set off the ammunition dump at Dak To, destroying more than 1,100 tons of ordnance and two C-130s [H36].

From 19 to 23 November the 2nd and 4th Battalions, 503rd Infantry fought for Hill 875; on the first evening a misdirected US bomb fell on the 2nd Battalion's perimeter, killing many of its men [H36]. By the time the paratroopers took the crest on 23 November the PAVN had withdrawn [H36]. The Americans lost 361 killed, 15 missing and 1,441 wounded, and about 40 helicopters; the ARVN 73 killed [H36]. US figures for PAVN dead run from about 1,000 to 1,644, with allegations of inflation; a PAVN report gives about 450 killed and 1,089 wounded [H36]. The battle consumed 151,000 artillery rounds, 2,096 tactical air sorties, 257 B-52 strikes and 2,101 Army helicopter sorties [H36]. Westmoreland called it a defeat for the enemy. It was also exactly what Hanoi intended: one of the border battles that, with Loc Ninh, Song Be and Con Thien, drew US forces to the frontiers, so that by January 1968 about half the US manoeuvre battalions in South Vietnam were in the border regions [H36].

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1967 the United States has about 486,000 military personnel in Vietnam [H40] and has lost 9,378 of them killed by hostile action in the year [H40]. Rolling Thunder has dropped about 864,000 tons on North Vietnam by the Pentagon's announcement of 31 December [H9], and the North's air defences have made every strike near Hanoi expensive. The Trail is carrying more than ever; truck movements rise sharply from October to December [H39]. The enemy order of battle is in dispute: MACV counts about 300,000 communist personnel, the CIA up to 430,000 [H39], and the disagreement turns on whether the guerrillas and the political infrastructure, the part of the enemy the main-force war does not touch, are counted at all.

On 21 November Westmoreland tells the National Press Club that the enemy is "unable to mount a major offensive" and that "the end begins to come into view" [H39]. The administration's progress campaign of the autumn rests on the body count, the Hamlet Evaluation System and the claim that the crossover point is near. McNamara, who has lost confidence in the war, has announced his departure for the World Bank, and Johnson's offer at San Antonio on 29 September, to stop the bombing in exchange for prompt and productive talks, has been rejected by Hanoi [H]. In Saigon there is, for the first time since 1963, an elected government under Thieu, and in the countryside CORDS has begun to organize the territorial war, from a less low base than history's. About 820,000 hectares have been titled to about 640,000 families, rent has ended in law and largely in fact wherever the government holds the night, and the Commission has convicted a general (EV001-6, EV001-7) [X-001]. The infrastructure of the Front is smaller in the Delta than on the historical course, and its tax take lower, but it is intact in every hamlet the government does not hold after dark, and those hamlets are still most of the contested countryside [X-001].

In Hanoi the decision is taken. The PAVN and VC main forces have been drawn to the borders and have drawn the Americans after them; the VC's local forces and cadres in and around the cities are preparing; and the Politburo has set the Tet holiday of 1968 as the moment for the General Offensive and General Uprising. The phase ends with each side certain that the other is weaker than it is, and with the population of the South, the object of the war, still largely outside the reach of either army's strategy.

In the held belt the resident infrastructure has been driven out of most villages with settled posts by the end of 1967, and the arrest count has fallen because there is little left there to arrest (EV002-4, EV002-5) [X-002]. The National Police numbers about 80,000, the chiefs have mostly held their districts for two or three years, and the Front's legal cadres are in the towns, where the police knows less.

The enemy's external supply is measured: the Trail by the panhandle watch, Sihanoukville by the count, the North's roads by the Gulf watch, and every measure says that what arrives exceeds what the South's forces need from the North (EV003-3, EV003-4) [X-003]. That knowledge is honest from 1965 rather than from 1970, and it has not changed the rules, the bombing or the strategy. Its main effect on Washington has been to deliver bad news early.

The RVNAF enters 1968 with the rifles it has carried for four years and a Regional Force that the corps commanders want for their own operations, bound since 1967 by the rule that keeps it in its provinces (EV004-2). The American army has had no rifle crisis and no inquiry, and has lost somewhat fewer men in the first minutes of its fights; it is the same army in every other respect, fighting the same battles on the enemy's terms.

The bombers have learned two things in two years: that a bridge they can measure dies in a night and is replaced by five crossings they cannot (EV005-2, EV005-3), and that a siege they can measure becomes the enemy's worst battle (EV000-36). The second lesson is the one the headquarters remembers going into 1968.

Phung Hoang has a centre in every district and an audited count of about 18,000 neutralized since 1965, about one in five of them members of district or village committees, and the Front's district administration in the held belt now governs from the contested edge by courier (EV006-2, EV002-4, F006-4) [X-006]. The word Phoenix has been a press story since March 1967 (EV006-4). About 7,000 selected advisers hold every field post below corps; the American battalions have changed over once or twice each as units, have fought better in the contacts they were granted, and have learned at Dien Ban to keep their reliefs off the dark of the moon (EV006-3) [X-006]. None of it has changed who chooses the battle.
