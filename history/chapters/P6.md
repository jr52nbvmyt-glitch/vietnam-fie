# Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

Phase VI is the war without the Americans, and the end of the Republic of Vietnam. The Paris Agreement of January 1973 takes US forces out and returns the prisoners; it leaves the PAVN in the South, the Thieu government in Saigon, and the war to be decided between them. The ceasefire never holds. In 1973 Saigon is on the offensive, recovering hamlets and roads, while the North rebuilds; in 1974 the two are evenly matched, and the ARVN, short of ammunition, fuel and spare parts as American aid falls, bleeds in a war of attrition it cannot sustain; in January 1975 the North tests whether the United States will respond to the seizure of a province, and when it does not, it commits everything [H72].

The shape of the phase is three slow years and seven fast weeks. The constraints that bind the American role are now law, not policy: the Case–Church amendment ends all US combat activity in Indochina from 15 August 1973, the War Powers Resolution limits the President's power to send forces back, and Congress cuts military aid. Watergate removes the President who gave Thieu his assurances. In Hanoi, the 21st Plenum of October 1973 returns to revolutionary violence, and the PAVN builds a road network east of the mountains, a fuel pipeline into the South and an army of corps. In Saigon, the economy, hit by the oil shock and the departure of the Americans whose spending had sustained it, and a government that has narrowed its base, face the offensive of 1975 with an army spread thin across the whole country. When the blow falls at Ban Me Thuot in March, Thieu's decision to abandon the Highlands turns a defeat into a rout, and in eight weeks the Republic falls. The PAVN enters Saigon on 30 April 1975.

In this war the army that fights the last three years has been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the abundance of American ammunition, and the ration of 1974 finds it more dependent on volume than the historical one (EV000-89). It fights the attrition of 1974 and the stand at Xuan Loc with better weapons than its enemy and too few rounds to use them, and the hoards it builds against the ration are lost with I Corps (EV000-91, EV000-96).

The bombers leave in August 1973 without bombing Neak Luong, and the coordinate cell is disbanded with nothing in the VNAF to send coordinates to (EV000-86). The ARVN fights 1974 and the defence of Phuoc Long with plans written for an air force that no longer comes, and loses Thuong Duc and Phuoc Long as history did, with its officers knowing exactly what they lack (EV000-91, EV000-92).

Phung Hoang outlives the Americans as a Vietnamese programme and helps the Republic's offensive of 1973 in the Delta; its national file, consolidated in Saigon since 1971, is taken intact on 30 April 1975 with the files of Da Nang a month earlier, and it serves the victors' security service as the most complete list of their enemies they will ever hold (EV000-84, EV000-94, EV000-98). The advisers are gone in March 1973, and nothing of their eighteen months survives them but the counterparts who remember them, who are on the lists.

The officer reforms leave the Republic in its last three years an officer corps that knows precisely what it lacks. Its battalion and regimental commanders, educated on a doctrine built around American fire, plan the battles of 1974 for a weight of fire the ration no longer allows (EV000-91). Its staff college writes, in November 1974, the only serious plan the Republic ever had for a defence on reduced resources, and the President takes its conclusion and not its method in March 1975 (EV000-95). At Xuan Loc a well-led division holds for twelve days, as history's did (EV000-96). Saigon falls on 30 April.

## EV000-83 · The Paris Agreement · 27 Jan 1973 · Paris · HIST · 000
On 27 January 1973 the Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Vietnam was signed in Paris by the United States, the Democratic Republic of Vietnam, the Republic of Vietnam and the Provisional Revolutionary Government [H51] [H]. A ceasefire in place took effect on 28 January Saigon time [H]. The United States and its allies would withdraw all forces within 60 days, and prisoners would be exchanged in the same period [H51]. Nothing required the PAVN to leave the South, where by US estimates about 150,000 of its troops remained [H51] [H]. The Thieu government remained, and a National Council of National Reconciliation and Concord was to arrange elections, which it never did [H]. An International Commission of Control and Supervision of Canadian, Hungarian, Indonesian and Polish personnel, and a Four-Party Joint Military Commission, were to supervise the ceasefire, and both sides could replace worn-out armaments one for one [H51] [H]. Thieu signed under American pressure, with Nixon's written promises of continued aid and of a forceful response to a major violation [H51]. The agreement was close to the terms of October 1972.

## EV000-84 · The ceasefire that never held · 28 Jan – Dec 1973 · The countryside of South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: allied*
In the last days before the ceasefire both sides grabbed for hamlets and roads, raising flags to claim territory, the "war of the flags"; the ARVN recovered most of what the PAVN and VC had seized [H]. Fighting resumed in earnest by March [H51]. Through most of 1973 the Saigon government was on the offensive: the Communists, rebuilding supply lines and wary of provoking the return of American air power, largely stayed on the defensive, while the ARVN and territorial forces pushed into PRG-held areas [H72]. Both sides violated the agreement from the start; the ICCS could not stop it, and Canada left the commission in July 1973 [H]. From late 1973 the PAVN grew bolder as the American political system turned on itself, and by early 1974 the two sides fought roughly on equal terms [H72].

In this war the Republic's offensive of 1973 had one instrument the historical one lacked: district centres nine years old in the Delta, with files the American departure did not take with it. When the American advisers left the centres in March 1973, the centres stayed, under the police statute, as they had always been; the Phung Hoang name was retired in December 1972, as historically, and the work went on under the Special Branch [H] [X-006]. In the "war of the flags" of January–February and the push into PRG-held hamlets that followed, the IV Corps provinces moved first into villages whose infrastructure the centres had charted, and the Front's district committees in Chuong Thien, Vinh Binh and Kien Hoa lost about a fifth of their members in 1973, about twice the historical rate [X-006]. It was a gain in the hamlets and not on the frontiers, and it changed nothing in the balance of 1974, when the PAVN's divisions decided what the hamlets were worth (POS001-4).

## EV000-85 · Operation Homecoming and the last American troops · 12 Feb – 29 Mar 1973 · Hanoi, Clark Air Base and Saigon · HIST · 000
On 12 February 1973 the first American prisoners were flown out of Hanoi to Clark Air Base in the Philippines [H78]. By 29 March, 566 US servicemen, 23 American civilians and 5 foreign nationals had come home through Clark [H78]. On 29 March 1973 the last US combat and support troops left South Vietnam, and MACV was disestablished [H]. A Defense Attaché Office under Major General John E. Murray, with a few dozen military personnel and civilian contractors, took over the management of American military aid [H]. The United States still maintained air power in Thailand and the Seventh Fleet offshore, and continued to bomb in Cambodia [H]. The question of Americans missing in action, about whom Hanoi gave little information, would keep the war alive in American politics for two decades [H].

## EV000-86 · Case–Church and the end of American combat in Indochina · Jun – 7 Nov 1973 · Washington; Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: neither*

In June 1973 Congress passed the Case–Church amendment, which barred any US military activity in or over Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia without congressional approval from 15 August 1973 [H51] [H]. Until that day the B-52s went on bombing Cambodia, which had continued since the Paris Agreement, in support of Lon Nol's forces around Phnom Penh [H]. Historically on 6 August a B-52 dropped its bombs on the town of Neak Luong on the Mekong, killing about 137 people and wounding about 268, because its navigator had failed to set the offset for the radar beacon on which the strike was aimed [H]. In this war no beacon was used: the J navigated and released on its own GPS, and the strike that night fell on its intended box in the forest east of the river [X-005]. Neak Luong was not bombed, and the American ambassador in Phnom Penh did not have to pay its families a hundred dollars a death [H] [X-005].

On 15 August the bombing of Cambodia ended, and with it the last American combat missions in Indochina [H]. The Js went home to the Strategic Air Command's alert pads; Sorrell's cell at Tan Son Nhut, which had been folded into the Defense Attaché Office's intelligence branch in March, was disbanded in September, and its folders for every firebase and district town in the South went to Clark Air Base and then to the Defense Mapping Agency [X-005]. The Joint General Staff asked to keep a coordinate cell of its own; there was nothing for such a cell to send coordinates to, since no aircraft the VNAF flew could carry a guided bomb, and the request was not pursued (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. On 7 November Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto, limiting the President's power to commit forces to hostilities without Congress [H]. Nixon's promise to Thieu of a forceful response to a major violation was now beyond the President's power to keep without Congress, and Congress would not give it [H]. The sanctuary constraint, which had shaped the war since 1964, now covered the whole of Indochina and was written into law.

## EV000-87 · Resolution 21 and the North's rebuilding · Oct 1973 – 1974 · Hanoi; the Laotian panhandle and the western Highlands · HIST · 000
In October 1973 the 21st Plenum of the Party Central Committee in Hanoi resolved that the revolution in the South must continue by the path of revolutionary violence, and that the PAVN should respond to Saigon's operations and prepare for a decisive offensive [H]. The PAVN rebuilt through 1973 and 1974: it improved the strategic road along the western side of South Vietnam east of the Truong Son, extended the fuel pipeline into the South, built up stocks of ammunition and armour in the base areas and grouped its divisions into army corps, the 1st Corps in October 1973 and the 4th Corps on 20 July 1974 among them [H] [H73]. It was the North's own decision, on its own reading of the American political collapse and Saigon's weakness, and it was taken while Saigon still held the initiative in the countryside.

## EV000-88 · Saigon declares the war resumed; the Paracels · Jan 1974 · Saigon; the Paracel Islands · HIST · 000
In January 1974 Thieu announced that the war had resumed and that the Paris Agreement no longer bound his forces [H51] [H]. On 19 January a Chinese naval force defeated South Vietnamese ships in the Paracel Islands, and China took control of the islands [H71]. The United States did not intervene [H]. The clash showed how isolated Saigon had become: its ally would not act, and a great power on its northern horizon was willing to take territory from it.

## EV000-89 · The aid cuts and the shortages · 25 Jan – 9 Aug 1974 · South Vietnam and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,004
On 25 January 1974 supplies of ammunition and fuel to all ARVN units were cut because of budget limits [H71]. US military aid fell from about $1.1 billion in fiscal year 1974 to an appropriation of about $700 million for fiscal year 1975, the July 1974–June 1975 year, the figure given by source as $700–750 million [H72] [H71]. Major General Murray of the Defense Attaché Office warned that cuts in support could roughly be equated with loss of territory, and that aid of $600 million would mean writing the South off [H71]. The ARVN rationed artillery rounds, grounded aircraft and helicopters for lack of fuel and parts, and cut patrols; the soldiers' pay lost most of its value to inflation, and desertion rose [H]. The economy suffered from the world oil shock of late 1973 and the loss of American spending [H].

In this war the ration fell on an army that had been built for ten years around the belt-fed gun and the 30-round magazine, four years longer than the historical one (F004-15). The Joint General Staff's directive of February 1974 limited each infantry squad to its basic load for a month's operations, and the squad gun went first: about 200 rounds a gun for a month, which is what an M249 fires at the rapid rate in two minutes [X-004]. Battalion commanders who had learned their trade breaking contacts with the squad gun's volume found that the 1974 ration let them do it about twice in a month, and the advisers' successors at the Defense Attaché Office reported that the infantry, rather than fight with aimed rifle fire as their fathers had fought with the Garand, avoided contacts it could not break with volume [X-004]. The marksmen, whose match 7.62mm had a separate line, were rationed harder still; by the summer about half the M110A2s in I and II Corps were in arms rooms for want of ammunition [X-004]. Divisions began to hoard linked 5.56mm in their own ammunition points against the ration, against JGS orders, and by the end of the year the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Divisions held about 9 million rounds outside the corps depots between them [X-004]. On 9 August 1974 Nixon resigned over Watergate, and Gerald Ford succeeded him [H71]. The President who had given Thieu written promises was gone.

## EV000-90 · The ARVN's last offensives · 12 Feb – 2 May 1974 · Tri Phap, Dinh Tuong Province; Svay Rieng, Cambodia · HIST · 000
In the spring of 1974 the ARVN still had the strength to attack. From 12 February to 4 May it struck the PAVN base at Tri Phap in Dinh Tuong Province in the Delta [H71]. From 27 March to 2 May, in the last major ARVN offensive of the war, III Corps attacked the PAVN base areas around Svay Rieng in Cambodia, claiming more than 1,200 PAVN killed against fewer than 100 ARVN [H71]. They were the work of an army still able to win local battles when it had the ammunition and air support to do so. They did not change the balance, and after the spring the ARVN was on the defensive everywhere.

## EV000-91 · The war of attrition of 1974 · 16 May – 10 Dec 1974 · The Iron Triangle; Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc in I Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,004,005,007
*Initiative: enemy*

From May 1974 the PAVN took the initiative with a series of "strategic raids" designed to grind down the ARVN and test its reserves [H] [H71]. In III Corps the PAVN 9th Division attacked Rach Bap in the Iron Triangle from 16 May; the ARVN retook it only in November [H71]. In I Corps a PAVN regiment overran An Hoa and attacked the district town of Thuong Duc from 18 July; the Airborne Division held the heights around it in a costly victory that lasted until November [H71]. At Duc Duc, from 18 July to 4 October, the ARVN 3rd Division suffered 4,700 casualties [H71]. From 28 August to 10 December the PAVN seized hills near Phu Loc that closed Phu Bai airfield to traffic and cut Highway 1, and the ARVN recovered them at heavy cost [H71]. In 1974 South Vietnam lost about 30,000 killed; the US estimated about 61,000 PAVN and VC killed [H71].

The weapons the ARVN carried in these fights were better than the PAVN's, as they had been since 1964, and it mattered less than at any time since. On the ridges west of Thuong Duc in September the 1st and 2nd Airborne Brigades took and retook Hill 1062 against the PAVN 304th Division's 66th Regiment; the Airborne's marksmen killed the 66th's spotters and gunners on the facing slopes at 500–700 metres, and its squads held the crest against three night assaults with the squad guns rationed to a belt each for the night [X-004]. They held, and they bled, because the PAVN's 130mm guns and 122mm rockets were not rationed and the ARVN's 105s and 155s were [H] [X-004].

They bled in a second way that history's Airborne did not. Since An Loc, Kontum and the Lam Son bases the ARVN's I Corps had been taught, by success, to hold a besieged position and let the bombs break the siege (F005-20) [X-005]. At Thuong Duc the district town's garrison, the 79th Ranger Battalion, dug in and waited, as its officers had seen the 23rd Division wait at Kontum, for a siege box that no longer existed; the 304th Division's regiment dug its trenches to the wire in eleven days without a bomb falling on them, and the town fell on 7 August [H] [X-005]. The Airborne's counter-attack on the heights was planned by officers who had held Lolo and Sophia in 1971 under the Js, and its first assaults on Hill 1062 went forward in the expectation that the PAVN's positions on the crest would be broken from the air first; the VNAF's A-37s and F-5As, flying in the monsoon cloud with unguided bombs, could not do it [X-005]. I estimate that the Airborne Division's losses in the heights were perhaps a tenth higher than historically, a judgment I hold loosely, and I keep the 3rd Division's 4,700 at Duc Duc and South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71] [X-005]. The ARVN, unable to replace its losses or its expended ammunition, had committed its strategic reserve, the Airborne and Marine Divisions, to static defence in I Corps, and had nothing left to move.

The officer reforms made the attrition of 1974 more legible and no cheaper. The battalion and regimental commanders who fought at Thuong Duc, Duc Duc and Phu Loc had been educated on a doctrine in which the infantry finds the enemy and the fires destroy him, and from January 1974 the fires were rationed; their reports to the Joint General Staff in the summer, collected by the Central Training Command, are the most exact account anywhere of what the ration did to each kind of fight, and none of them could change it (F007-23) [X-007]. At Duc Duc the 3rd Division's battalions, under board commanders, held their companies together through 4,700 casualties with fewer men lost to desertion than history's, and held no more ground [H71] [X-007]. I keep South Vietnam's dead for the year at about 30,000 [H71].

## EV000-92 · The fall of Phuoc Long · 12 Dec 1974 – 6 Jan 1975 · Phuoc Long Province (Phuoc Binh and Song Be), III Corps · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: enemy*

On 12 December 1974 the PAVN 4th Corps under Hoang Cam, with the 7th Division, the new 3rd Division and independent regiments including the 141st, 165th, 201st and 271st, and two armoured companies with 14 T-54s, attacked Phuoc Long Province on the Cambodian border [H73]. It was defended mainly by Regional Force battalions, reinforced by the 1st and 2nd Battalions, 7th Regiment and the 1st Battalion, 9th Regiment of the 5th Division and the 81st Airborne Ranger Battalion [H73]. The province chief's plan for the defence of Phuoc Binh, drafted in 1973 by officers who had served at An Loc, was a plan to hold the town for six to eight weeks while the bombers broke the siege; it named the bomber cells by the call signs Hollingsworth's men had used in 1972 (F005-20) [X-005]. On 13 December the province chief asked III Corps for "the B-52 support provided for in the plan", and III Corps forwarded the request to the Joint General Staff, which forwarded it to the Defense Attaché Office, which had no means to answer it [X-005].

On 18 December, in the meeting at the Independence Palace where Thieu and the Joint General Staff decided not to send the general reserve to Phuoc Long, the request was on the table, and the decision taken was historical: the Airborne Division was committed in I Corps, and Thieu would not risk a second division in a province he judged he could not hold without American bombers [H73] [X-005]. In this war the judgment was put in those words, and with more conviction, because the Republic's officers had seen at An Loc and Kontum what American bombers could do for a besieged town and knew exactly what they were not getting [X-005]. The last ARVN post fell on 6 January 1975, the first provincial capital lost permanently since the Paris Agreement [H73] [H]. The ARVN lost more than 1,000 killed and 2,444 captured; the PAVN about 1,300 killed and wounded [H73]. Saigon did not try to retake it. Washington made no military response [H73]. In Hanoi, where a strategy conference sat from 18 December to 8 January, Phuoc Long answered the question the leadership had set itself: the United States would not return [H71] [H]. The plan for a two-year campaign to end the war in 1976 was adapted to strike in the Central Highlands in 1975 [H]. In Saigon the answer was received at the same moment and in the same words, and it fell on an army whose defence of every besieged town had been built for a decade around the aircraft that would not come.

## EV000-93 · The fall of Ban Me Thuot · 10–12 Mar 1975 · Ban Me Thuot, Darlac Province · HIST · 000
For Campaign 275 the PAVN Central Highlands Front under Lieutenant General Hoang Minh Thao, directed by the chief of the general staff, General Van Tien Dung, massed the 10th, 316th and 320A Divisions with the 3rd and 968th Divisions, tanks, artillery and anti-aircraft units [H74]. Diversionary attacks on Pleiku and Kontum, false radio traffic and local rumours drew the attention of the II Corps commander, Major General Pham Van Phu, who discounted repeated warnings and kept his forces in the north [H74]. At 02:00 on 10 March the PAVN attacked Ban Me Thuot, defended mainly by the 53rd Regiment of the 23rd Division with Rangers and Regional Forces; the town was in PAVN hands by 11–12 March [H74]. The counter-attack by the rest of the 23rd Division, lifted in by helicopter, was broken [H]. The PAVN admitted 600–800 killed and 2,416 wounded; the Vietnamese official history counts 28,514 ARVN put out of action, 4,502 killed and 16,822 captured [H74]. II Corps lost more than three-quarters of its combat units [H74]. Ban Me Thuot was the strategic surprise of the war's last campaign.

## EV000-94 · The collapse of I Corps · 13–30 Mar 1975 · Quang Tri, Hue and Da Nang · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: enemy*
On 13 March Thieu ordered Truong to give up most of I Corps and withdrew the Airborne Division towards Saigon as a reserve [H75]. On 20 March he ordered Truong to hold only Da Nang; on 29 March he ordered him to turn back and retake it, which Truong refused [H75]. The contradictory orders, given as the PAVN 2nd Corps with the 304th, 324th and 325th Divisions attacked from the north and west and the 2nd Division and 52nd Brigade struck in Quang Ngai and Quang Tin, broke I Corps [H75]. Northern Quang Tri was lost by 18 March; Hue was surrounded by the evening of 25 March and abandoned; about 6,000 soldiers of the 2nd Division deserted on 28 March; and the PAVN entered Da Nang on the afternoon of 29 March [H75]. Soldiers abandoned their units to find their families, and the sea evacuation from Da Nang became chaos, with people fighting to board the ships [H75]. I Corps had about 134,000 men against more than 75,000 PAVN and VC; three ARVN infantry divisions and the 147th Marine Brigade ceased to exist, and about 58,722 soldiers were captured at Hue [H75]. The PAVN took 129 aircraft, 179 armoured vehicles and 327 artillery pieces [H75]. The best corps in the ARVN, under its best commander, had been destroyed without a decisive battle.

In this war the PAVN's security troops who entered Da Nang on 29 March went first, by a list prepared in Hanoi, to the I Corps regional office of the Special Branch and the Quang Nam PIOCC [X-006]. The destruction plan of 1968 had been rehearsed every quarter for seven years; on 28 March the regional chief, who had sent his family to the docks that morning, did not order it, and the clerks who might have done it on their own had gone to find theirs [X-006]. The Quang Tri and Thua Thien centres had burned their files on the 18th and 25th; Quang Nam, Quang Tin and Quang Ngai did not, and the regional office held district extracts of the national file for all five provinces with about 40,000 names and about 11,000 photographs [X-006]. That the collapse would deliver the files with the corps was the risk the memorandum that created them had named (F006-25).

## EV000-95 · The retreat from the Highlands · 14–25 Mar 1975 · Pleiku and Kontum to the coast along Route 7, through Cheo Reo · HIST-ALT · 000,007
*Initiative: allied*

On 14 March at Cam Ranh, Thieu ordered Phu to abandon Pleiku and Kontum and pull II Corps back to the coast to defend the populated regions [H74]. The chief of the Joint General Staff, General Cao Van Vien, warned against Route 19 because of the French disaster at the Mang Yang Pass in 1954; Thieu chose Route 7, a disused road in poor condition [H74]. The retreat was ordered verbally and kept secret, even from many of the units and the province chiefs; within days some 400,000 civilians had joined the columns [H74]. The PAVN ambushed the column near Cheo Reo, where fighting ended on 19 March, and shelled it along the road [H74]. Only the 6th Ranger Battalion and the 19th Armoured Cavalry Squadron arrived in good order; an estimated 155,000 civilians were killed or abducted on the "convoy of tears", and RVNAF air strikes hit civilians too [H74]. It was the decision that lost the war in the shortest time: II Corps, which had held the Highlands through every offensive since 1965, was destroyed on the road in ten days.

In this war the decision had a plan behind it, and the plan was not used. In November 1974 a seminar of the Command and General Staff College at Dalat, under Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had gone to its faculty after Quang Tri, wrote a study for the Joint General Staff titled "The Defence of the Republic on Reduced Resources" [X-007]. It concluded that with the aid then voted the RVNAF could not hold the whole country against the forces the North was building, and proposed a withdrawal from Kontum, Pleiku and northern Quang Tri over about three months of the dry season, before any offensive, with routes secured in advance, the families of the soldiers moved first, the province chiefs told, and the Airborne and Marines rebuilt as a reserve [X-007]. Vien sent it to the palace in January 1975, after Phuoc Long, and Thieu did not answer it [X-007]. On 14 March at Cam Ranh he took its conclusion and none of its method: three days instead of three months, a secret verbal order instead of a staffed movement, Route 7 instead of a secured road, and the families left to find their own way (F007-7) [X-007]. The retreat went as told above. The best piece of staff work the reforms ever produced became, in the hands of a President and a corps commander no board had chosen, the warrant for the worst decision of the war.

## EV000-96 · Xuan Loc · 9–21 Apr 1975 · Xuan Loc, Long Khanh Province, northeast of Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,004,007
*Initiative: enemy*

On 9 April the PAVN 4th Corps under Major General Hoang Cam, with the 6th, 7th and 341st Divisions and tanks and artillery, attacked Xuan Loc, the road junction northeast of Saigon on Route 1 [H76]. The town was held by the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao, with the 1st Airborne Brigade, armoured units, Rangers and Regional Forces, and air support from Bien Hoa and Tan Son Nhut [H76]. The 18th Division held for twelve days against repeated assaults; on 12 April two BLU-82 bombs were dropped on PAVN troops, reportedly killing about 200 [H76].

In this war the 18th Division fought at Xuan Loc with what was left of a decade's equipment and, by April, with the ammunition the Joint General Staff could send it from the Long Binh depot, which by then was most of what remained in III Corps [X-004]. Its squads had their squad guns and the belts to feed them for the first time since January 1974, because Dao drew everything Long Binh would give him; its marksmen held the edges of the town against the 341st Division's infantry crossing the rubber and the open ground east of the town on 9–11 April, and the 43rd Regiment's account of the first three days records the PAVN assault battalions going to ground at 400 metres under fire they could not locate [X-004]. It held for twelve days, as it held historically [H76]. The ammunition that might have let it hold longer had gone in March, in the divisional hoards at Hue, Da Nang and Qui Nhon that were abandoned when I Corps and the coast collapsed: about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm and most of the match 7.62mm in the country, captured intact (F004-15) [X-004].

The PAVN changed its approach, bypassed and isolated the town, and on 19 April the Joint General Staff ordered the withdrawal; Xuan Loc was abandoned on 21 April, and the 3rd Battalion of the 1st Airborne Brigade was destroyed in the withdrawal at Suoi Ca [H76]. The PAVN claimed 2,036 ARVN killed or wounded and 2,731 captured and admitted 460 killed and 1,428 wounded; Americans estimated about 5,000 PAVN casualties and 37 tanks destroyed [H76]. I do not adjust these figures; the squad weapons raised the price of each assault and the PAVN answered, as it did historically, by not making more of them [X-004]. On 17 April Phnom Penh fell to the Khmer Rouge [H]. Xuan Loc was the ARVN's last stand, and showed what a well-led division could still do; it bought twelve days.

The 18th Division's battalions at Xuan Loc were commanded by officers the board had chosen and its regiments by colonels it had promoted, and Dao himself had been one of the list's names in 1972 [X-007]. In this war there were more divisions like it: the 22nd's stand on the coast in late March, the Airborne brigades at Phan Rang, and the 25th Division's battalions on Route 1 west of Saigon fought in the same way, and none of them changed the arithmetic of 1975, because the corps above them had already gone (F007-7) [X-007]. Xuan Loc held for twelve days, as it did historically [H76].

## EV000-97 · Thieu resigns; the last governments of the Republic · 10–28 Apr 1975 · Saigon and Washington · HIST · 000
On 10 April President Ford asked Congress for $722 million in emergency military aid to rebuild some of the destroyed South Vietnamese forces; Congress would not vote it [H77] [H]. On 23 April Ford told an audience at Tulane University that the war was "finished as far as America is concerned" [H]. On 21 April Thieu resigned in a televised address denouncing the United States for abandoning his country, and handed power to Vice President Tran Van Huong [H77]. Thieu left the country a few days later [H]. On 28 April Huong handed the presidency to General Duong Van Minh, in the hope that Hanoi would negotiate with him; it would not [H77]. On 28 April captured A-37s flown by defecting pilots bombed Tan Son Nhut [H77].

## EV000-98 · The fall of Saigon · 29–30 Apr 1975 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: enemy*
By late April five PAVN corps, Group 232 and the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th Corps, with 15 infantry divisions and three tank regiments, encircled Saigon in the Ho Chi Minh Campaign [H77]. Rockets and artillery hit Tan Son Nhut on the morning of 29 April; two US Marine guards, Corporal Charles McMahon and Lance Corporal Darwin Judge, were killed, the last American servicemen killed in action in Vietnam [H]. At 10:51 Operation Frequent Wind began: Marine and Air Force helicopters lifted 395 Americans and more than 4,000 Vietnamese from the Defense Attaché Office compound, and 978 Americans and about 1,100 Vietnamese from the embassy roof and grounds, to the ships of the Seventh Fleet [H77]. Ambassador Graham Martin left on a helicopter at 04:58 on 30 April [H77]. Thousands of Vietnamese who had worked with the Americans were left behind [H].

At 10:24 on 30 April Minh announced an unconditional surrender [H77]. PAVN tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace, Tank 390 through the main gate, and at 11:30 a soldier raised the flag of the Front over the palace [H77]. The Republic of Vietnam had ceased to exist. Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City; Vietnam was formally reunified as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam in July 1976 [H].

In this war the Central Intelligence Organization's building held the national Phung Hoang file, about 240,000 names with photographs and fingerprints for about 90,000, consolidated there since 1971 (EV002-6) [X-006]. The destruction plan for the building required the order of the Director General of the CIO, who left by the embassy's helicopters on 29 April; a section chief and two clerks burned the index and part of the photographic file in the building's courtyard on the morning of the 30th and were arrested in it that afternoon [X-006]. The PAVN's security troops, with the Ministry of the Interior's southern section behind them, had the rest of the file by evening [X-006]. It is not possible to say how many of the men and women sent to re-education camps after 1975, or kept there longest, were sent because their names were in it; the camps' own records are not available, and the victors had other lists [X-006]. It is possible to say that the file held the names of every informant, centre officer, PRU member, Chieu Hoi rallier and selected adviser's counterpart of eleven years, that it was the most complete record of the Republic's friends the war produced, and that this headquarters built it.

## How the phase ends
The war ends on 30 April 1975 with the fall of Saigon and the unconditional surrender of the Republic of Vietnam, on the historical timeline: rung (a), collapse. The Paris Agreement had removed the American forces and kept the PAVN in the South; Congress had written the end of American combat and the limits on the President into law; American military aid had fallen; and the government in Saigon, more stable than at any time since 1963 but narrowly based, economically weakened and dependent on American supply, faced an enemy that had rebuilt after 1972 and chose its moment. The decisive blows were Hanoi's, at Phuoc Long and Ban Me Thuot; the decisive errors were Saigon's, in the order to abandon the Highlands and the contradictory orders to I Corps.

Hanoi's posture never changed. It was committed to armed reunification throughout, from the Ninth Plenum of December 1963 to the Ho Chi Minh Campaign, through the losses of 1965–68 and 1972, and it accepted the Paris Agreement as a step towards that end. The US war in Vietnam, from the advisory years to 1973, cost more than 58,000 American dead [H]. The Vietnamese dead, military and civilian, northern and southern, are counted in the millions by most estimates, and the record of them is less precise than the record of the Americans [H]. That is the war as it happened, and the war the exercise begins from.

The squad guns and the marksmen's rifles of the ARVN, and about 9 million rounds of linked 5.56mm from the divisional hoards of I and II Corps, are in the PAVN's hands by the end of April 1975, with the carbines of a million men [X-004].

The Republic's defence of every besieged town had been built since 1971 around American bombers and American coordinates, and both were gone by the end of 1973 (EV000-86, EV000-92) [X-005].

The officers the reforms made fought the last campaign as well as any part of the RVNAF fought it, and their best staff plan was taken by the President and executed without its method (EV000-95). About 12,000 of the board's officers were captured in 1975 and sent to the re-education camps with the rest of the officer corps; the board's files, with the evaluation report of every officer since 1964, were in the Joint General Staff's compound on 30 April [X-007].
