# Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

1972 is the test of Vietnamization, and the answer it gives is exact: the Republic of Vietnam can stop a full PAVN conventional offensive with American air power and American advisers, and cannot do it without them. On 30 March the PAVN opens the largest offensive of the war, called the Nguyen Hue Offensive in Hanoi and the Easter Offensive in the West, with about fourteen divisions and hundreds of tanks [H] on three main fronts: across the DMZ into Quang Tri, from Laos and Cambodia into the Central Highlands at Kontum, and from Cambodia down Route 13 at An Loc towards Saigon [H64]. US ground strength has fallen to about 69,000 men, few of them combat troops, and is on its way to 27,000 [H64]. US air power returns in strength: B-52s and tactical aircraft over the battlefields of the South, and, for the first time since 1968, a bombing campaign against the North without most of Rolling Thunder's restrictions, with the mining of Haiphong.

The shape of the phase is a near-collapse in the spring and a recovery in the summer. Quang Tri Province falls on 1–2 May; Kontum and An Loc are besieged and held by narrow margins and B-52s; and by the autumn the ARVN, under better commanders, has retaken Quang Tri City. The PAVN loses most of the armour it committed and, by most estimates, about 100,000 men, killed and wounded, while keeping about a tenth of the South's territory, mostly thinly populated border regions in the west [H64]. The offensive creates almost a million refugees and kills more than 25,000 civilians [H64]. Its political result is the one Hanoi needed: a settlement on terms that leave PAVN troops in the South. The year ends with the October draft agreement, Thieu's refusal of it, and the eleven days of Linebacker II.

In this war the armour of the Easter Offensive is seen before it crosses the DMZ and is struck by radar-bombing B-52s through the cloud that grounded the fighter-bombers (EV000-74); Linebacker chooses from target folders seven years in the making and counts the effect of the mining on the North's own roads (EV000-75); and at An Loc the B-52 boxes of 10–12 May fall on assembly areas the radar shows (EV000-76). Quang Tri still falls, An Loc and Kontum still hold, and the lessons both sides draw are the historical ones.

The B-52J's year is this one. Freedom Train drops the Thanh Hoa bridge on its first night (EV000-75); the siege box breaks the penetration of An Loc on 12 May and the assault on Kontum short of the wire, and Kontum is secure five days early (EV000-76, EV000-77); and Linebacker II loses about six bombers rather than fifteen and does not strike Bach Mai or Kham Thien Street (EV000-82). Quang Tri still falls, and the terms of January are the terms of October.

The officer reforms meet the offensive at regiment and battalion, and the offensive is decided above them. On the DMZ front the 3rd Division's battalions, under commanders the board chose, give ground in better order than history's, and the 56th Regiment's commander, a board-chosen officer with a hero's record from Hue, surrenders Camp Carroll all the same (EV000-74). At Quang Tri the contradictory orders of the corps and the division are the historical orders, and the division still breaks, but one of its regiments holds the road south open long enough to bring out its column (EV000-78). At Tan Canh more of the 22nd Division gets out, and Kontum and An Loc are held as before (EV000-77). The army fights the year as it was taught to fight, with American air at the centre of every plan, and depends on it more for having learned to use it well (F007-23).

## EV000-74 · The Easter Offensive opens: the DMZ front · 30 Mar – 2 Apr 1972 · Quang Tri Province, from the DMZ to the Cua Viet and Camp Carroll · HIST-ALT · 000,003,007
*Initiative: enemy*

The armour was seen before it crossed. From 12 March the Gulf watch, standing off the Quang Tri coast outside the SA-2s that the North had moved into and just north of the DMZ, tracked tracked-vehicle columns moving south at night on Route 1A and Route 101 from Vinh Linh toward the Ben Hai, and the panhandle watch tracked others moving east along Route 9 in Laos toward Khe Sanh [X-003]. By 25 March the J-2 count of armoured vehicles in assembly areas within 20 kilometres of the DMZ was about 90–120, and MACV warned I Corps on 27 March of an armoured attack across the demarcation line within days [X-003]. Lieutenant General Lam, the I Corps commander, expected the main blow from the west, as most of the American and Vietnamese staffs did; the warning reached the 3rd Division, and the rotation of its regiments between firebases, which history records as under way when the attack fell, went ahead in part [H] [X-003].

At midday on 30 March 1972 PAVN artillery, including 130mm guns that outranged anything the ARVN had, opened on the firebases strung along the DMZ, and the 304th and 308th Divisions with armoured regiments attacked across the demarcation line, while the 324B came in from the A Shau and the west [H64] [H67]. In all, PAVN forces on the northern front were supported by more than 100 tanks, T-54s, T-34s and PT-76s [H64]. The front was held by the ARVN 3rd Division under Brigadier General Vu Van Giai, a new division formed in late 1971 from mixed and unsteady units, with the 147th and 258th Vietnamese Marine Brigades under its control [H67] [H]. Low cloud kept most air support away for the first days [H64]. It did not keep the radar away. The B-52s, which bombed by radar in any weather, were put on the crossing points of the Ben Hai and the roads south of Gio Linh where the Gulf watch showed the armour moving, and on 31 March and 1 April they struck three columns in the open; by our own estimate, about 20–30 armoured vehicles were destroyed or disabled before they reached the firebases [X-003].

The firebases fell back or were overrun all the same. On 2 April the 56th Regiment at Camp Carroll, under Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, surrendered with about 1,500 men [H67]. That afternoon at Dong Ha, Captain John Ripley, US adviser to the 3rd Vietnamese Marine Battalion, spent about three hours under fire placing charges on the highway bridge over the Cua Viet, which was blown at 16:30 as PAVN tanks approached; the Marines held the river line [H67]. The PAVN halted to regroup and bring up supplies, and the 3rd Division, under fire from guns it could not reach, settled into a defence of Quang Tri City along the Thach Han and Cua Viet rivers. The radar had seen the tanks; it had not made the 3rd Division's regiments steady or the 56th's commander stay.

The officer reforms did not make the 3rd Division steady; nothing done in six months could have. Its regiments had been formed in late 1971 from units of the 1st Division, Regional Forces and men returned from desertion, and its battalion commanders, though chosen by the board, had held their battalions for less than six months when the guns opened [H] [X-007]. What they changed was the manner of the withdrawal from the DMZ outposts on 1–2 April. The 57th Regiment, given to Colonel Bui Quang Trieu in 1971 (EV007-5), came back from the firebases along Route 1 toward Dong Ha by bounds, a battalion holding while the next passed through it, with its 105s towed out and its wounded on its own trucks, and reached the Cua Viet line as a regiment; on the historical course it arrived as a crowd among the refugees [X-007]. Lieutenant Colonel Pham Van Dinh, who had commanded the 56th since 1971 from the board's list on the strength of his battalion's fight at the Hue Citadel in 1968, surrendered Camp Carroll on 2 April as he did historically [H67] [X-007]. The board had chosen him for what he had done, and could not choose for what he would do.

## EVMT01-1 · The northern districts of Binh Dinh · Apr – Aug 1972 · Hoai Nhon, Tam Quan and Hoai An districts, Binh Dinh Province · HIST-ALT · MT01
*Initiative: enemy*

While the world watched Quang Tri, Kontum and An Loc, the PAVN 3rd Division, the Yellow Star, came down out of the An Lao valley in April 1972 and took the three northern districts of Binh Dinh, Hoai Nhon, Tam Quan and Hoai An, from the 22nd Division's 40th and 41st Regiments and the province's Regional Forces; the district towns and the old American base at Landing Zone English were in its hands by the end of the month, and the districts were retaken over the summer [H]. It was the only place in 1972 where the offensive reached a densely peopled coastal lowland and held it for months, and it is the one place where the record can see what the offensive did to the countryside the provisions had built.

What it did was not what it did historically, and it ended in the same place. In 1964 the land programme had titled the rice land of the Bong Son plain to the men who tilled it, much of it land the Viet Minh had given them first [M001]. When the Front's district committee came back behind the Yellow Star in late April, it had nothing to give. Its cadres held meetings in Tam Quan in May at which, by the account of a district cadre captured in August, the peasants asked whether the Front would confirm their titles, and the cadre had no instruction to answer [X-MT01]. So it governed the three districts as an occupying administration rather than a revolution: it taxed the spring harvest at the rate its own documents set for "liberated zones under enemy pressure", took about 1,800 young men for its local battalions, and executed or took away the hamlet chiefs and police it could find [X-MT01]. The district Special Branch office at Bong Son had burned its files on 22 April, three hours before the town fell, on the order of the district chief, Major Dao Van Luong, who had served in the province since 1966 and had seen what Hue cost [X-MT01].

The return of the 22nd Division and the Regional Forces in July and August found hamlets that had paid more to the Front in four months than in the eight years before, and fewer young men in them; about 70,000 people from the three districts had gone south to the camps at Phu My and Qui Nhon and most did not come back before 1973 [X-MT01]. The night in northern Binh Dinh went back to where it had been on the historical course, and the government that returned was the one that had left. The province's gains of 1964–71 were lost here not because the land reform failed, but because the offensive put a PAVN division in the hamlets for four months and the posts had no answer to a division. That is the mechanism by which the coastal lowlands converged with history in 1972, and it reached only where a division went.

## EVMT01-2 · The Delta during the offensive · Apr – Oct 1972 · Chuong Thien and Kien Giang provinces; the U Minh forest · HIST-ALT · MT01
*Initiative: enemy*

When the 21st Division left the Delta in April 1972 to fight its way up Route 13 toward An Loc, the regiments the PAVN and the Front had kept in the U Minh forest came out against the outposts of Chuong Thien and Kien Giang [H]. Historically this is where the offensive reached the Delta's countryside: dozens of posts lost, districts cut off, and the government's figures for its own security falling for the first time since 1968 [H]. In this war the posts were different. The Popular Forces platoons of Long My and Kien Thien districts had held their posts beside the police since 1964, with the land titled behind them, and the attacks of April and May fell on posts that held about nine attacks in ten (POS004-1) [M004]. The Front's regiments therefore did what they did everywhere else in this war: they left the held posts alone and went for the Regional Force companies the province chiefs had been made to send north as convoy escorts for the 21st Division's resupply, and for the road.

Major Huynh Cong Tri, the district chief of Long My, a list chief since 1966, kept every one of his forty-one posts through the summer and lost the road to Vi Thanh for seven weeks [X-MT01]. His monthly reports, which CORDS forwarded to the Joint General Staff in September as the model of a district under siege, say that the Front's tax collectors did not come into the hamlets inside the post line between April and October, and collected in the hamlets outside it at about twice their 1971 rate [X-MT01]. The same was true across the western Delta. The held belt stayed held at night through the worst year since 1968, and the contested belt beyond it, which the offensive gave back to the Front for a season, was where the Front's tax take and recruitment recovered [X-MT01]. By October the 21st Division was back and the roads were open.

This is the half of the Phase IV gain that survived 1972. The Front's infrastructure inside the post line did not return in 1972, because the offensive in the Delta came as regiments against the posts and not as cadres into the hamlets; and the files of the district centres, which the attacks never reached, are the files with which the IV Corps provinces opened the "war of the flags" in January 1973 (EV000-84). Night control in the held belt and the Front's weakness there were still one band better than history's at the end of 1972; what returned to history was the contested belt, the coast where a division came, and the government's legitimacy, which was lost in Saigon (EVMT01-3).

## EV000-75 · Freedom Train, Pocket Money and Linebacker · 5 Apr – 23 Oct 1972 · North Vietnam, Haiphong harbour and the approaches to the DMZ · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 5 April Nixon authorized strikes north of the 20th parallel, Operation Freedom Train, and on 10 April B-52s struck petroleum storage near Vinh [H68]. In this war the first B-52J strike of Freedom Train went to the folder the cell had kept since 1964: on the night of 10 April twelve Js put 240 GBU-31s on the Ham Rong bridge, the Thanh Hoa rail yards and the fuel tanks at Vinh, in cloud, from about twelve miles out, and the Dragon's Jaw fell into the Song Ma seven years and seven days after the request to drop it had been refused (EV000-15) [X-005]. The laser-guided bombs of 27 April and 13 May had nothing left there to do; on 10–11 May the Paul Doumer bridge at Hanoi was dropped by F-4s with laser-guided bombs, as it was historically, because the restricted zone around Hanoi stayed closed to the B-52 until December [H16] [H] [X-005].

On 8 May (9 May in Vietnam) Nixon announced Operation Pocket Money, the mining of Haiphong and the other ports [H68]. Navy aircraft from the *Coral Sea* laid the first mines in the Haiphong channel, as they did historically; here the B-52Js laid the secondary fields the same night, about 900 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes in the approaches to Hon Gai, Cam Pha, Thanh Hoa, Vinh and Dong Hoi and in the inland waterways south of Haiphong, including about 200 Quickstrike-ERs released more than forty miles out [H68] [X-005]. The mines were activated after five days, and about 11,000 more were sown in the secondary harbours and waterways over the following weeks [H68]. The mining of the northern ports, prohibited since 1965, closed the North's main entry point for Soviet supplies by sea; 27 ships stayed in Haiphong [H] [M005]. The Gulf watch saw it close, and from June saw the trucks massing at the Chinese border crossings at Dong Dang and Lao Cai to replace the sea [X-003].

Linebacker, from 10 May to 23 October, opened the North to bombing with targets chosen by the commanders rather than in Washington [H68]. On its first day, in 414 sorties, the heaviest air-to-air fighting of the war took place, and Lieutenant Randy Cunningham and Lieutenant (jg) William Driscoll became the first American aces of the war [H68]. In this war the commanders chose from folders seven years in the making, and the Js struck the bridges, rail yards and depots between Hanoi and the DMZ from the first week, about 1,100 J sorties with GBU-31s against fixed targets outside the Hanoi restricted zone by October [X-005]. Linebacker flew 41,653 missions and dropped 155,548 tons, and lost 134 aircraft, 104 in combat [H68]; I estimate that the electronic order of battle and the J's work on fixed targets, which kept fighter-bombers off many of the most heavily defended bridges, saved perhaps 15–25 of those losses, and I hold the estimate loosely [X-003] [X-005]. Its effect was felt at the front: by a PAVN history only 30 percent of planned supplies reached its front-line units in May and June, the PAVN shelling of the South fell by half between 9 May and 1 June, and by September imports were 35–50 percent below the May level [H68]. The radar's count of southbound traffic below Vinh in July was about 35 percent of the March level, against about 40 percent with the historical bombers, a difference I attribute to the bridges falling in the first week rather than the first month [X-003] [X-005]. The Soviet Union and China denounced the mining and did nothing; the Nixon–Brezhnev summit in Moscow went ahead from 22 May [H68] [H].

## EV000-76 · Loc Ninh and the siege of An Loc · 7 Apr – 18 Jun 1972 · Loc Ninh and An Loc, Binh Long Province, on Route 13 · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005
*Initiative: enemy*

On 5 April the 5th Division of the PAVN B-2 Front attacked Loc Ninh, on Route 13 near the Cambodian border, and overran it on the afternoon of 7 April; the 9th Division took the Quan Loi base camp that evening, and the 7th Division cut Route 13 south of An Loc at the Tau O bridge with its 141st, 165th and 209th Regiments [H65]. An Loc, the provincial capital 100 km north of Saigon, was cut off by 8 April [H65]. It was held by the ARVN 5th Division under Brigadier General Le Van Hung, with the 3rd and 81st Ranger Groups, the 1st Airborne Brigade lifted in after failing to break through at Tau O, and the Regional Forces, Popular Forces and People's Self-Defence Force of Binh Long Province, all under III Corps (Lieutenant General Nguyen Van Minh) and advised by the American regional assistance command under Major General James Hollingsworth [H65].

From mid-April a Global Hawk stood over Binh Long and watched the roads out of Cambodia [X-003]. From 15 April it was given the town itself, every night, and Sorrell's detachment at Long Binh measured what it saw: the PAVN's gun positions in the rubber, the bunkers dug into the ruined streets on the town's northern edge, the tank harbours, and the trenches the 9th Division pushed toward the perimeter each night (F005-21) [X-005]. The 9th Division assaulted on 13 April with tanks; in that first attack the defenders destroyed tanks with M72 LAWs at close range, and tank-killing teams were formed in every battalion [H65]. From 16 April cells of Js came over An Loc every two hours, each with thirty to forty GBU-38s aimed at positions measured that night, the nearest about 400 metres from Hung's lines; by early May the PAVN's guns fired from the rubber a few rounds at a time and moved, and the town's shelling, which historically reached thousands of rounds on the worst days, came at perhaps two-thirds of that weight [H] [X-005]. The town survived on airdrops, taken over by USAF C-130s on 19 April once anti-aircraft fire made helicopter landings impossible [H65]; the guns around the drop zone were measured and struck each night, and the airdrops of May landed inside the perimeter more often than they had in April [X-005].

The decisive assault came on 11 May. Historically B-52s struck one mission every 55 minutes for 30 hours against it, with AC-119 and AC-130E gunships, A-37s and AH-1 Cobras [H65]. Here the assault's assembly was on the radar from the evening of 10 May, as tanks and trucks moved up to the rubber north and east of the town, and Hollingsworth's targeteers put the night's first cells on the assembly areas the radar showed [X-003]. The difference the J made was in the hours after the attack broke into the town. Historically the B-52 carpets could not fall on the PAVN units inside the northern part of An Loc without falling on the defenders; here the cell measured the buildings and bunkers the attackers had taken, and cells of Js put GBU-38s on them 300–400 metres from the ARVN line, one aimpoint to a bomb, through the morning of 12 May (F005-2) [X-005]. The 9th Division's penetration in the north was destroyed by noon on 12 May, and the tanks that had come in with it burned in the streets [X-005]. The 21st Division fought up Route 13 from Lai Khe [H65]. The PAVN was driven from the town by 12 June and III Corps declared the siege over on 18 June [H65] [H64].

US estimates of PAVN losses are 10,000 killed and 15,000 wounded and 36 tanks; Vietnamese figures are about 2,000 killed and 5,000 wounded [H65]. I estimate that the GBU-38 cells added to the PAVN's losses perhaps a fifth beyond the historical, and that the 5th Division and its attached units lost perhaps a fifth fewer killed than the historical garrison, and I do not alter the reported figures, which already disagree by a factor of five [X-005]. Brigadier General Richard Tallman, the deputy American commander, was mortally wounded by artillery on 9 July [H65]. An Loc lay in ruins. It became the symbol of an ARVN that could hold if the B-52s came; in this war, of an ARVN that could hold if the B-52s came, the Americans could see where to send them, and an American cell could turn what they saw into coordinates (F005-20) [X-005].

## EV000-77 · Tan Canh, Dak To and the battle of Kontum · 24 Apr – 9 Jun 1972 · Tan Canh, Dak To, Ben Het and Kontum city, Kontum Province · HIST-ALT · 000,005,007
*Initiative: enemy*

In the Central Highlands the PAVN 320th Division began pressing the firebases on Rocket Ridge in early April, and on 24 April the 2nd Division's 1st and 66th Regiments, with the D10 Sapper Battalion and tanks, overran the ARVN bases at Tan Canh and Dak To II, held by the 42nd and 47th Regiments of the 22nd Division; the division "ceased to exist as a fighting unit", and its commander, Colonel Le Duc Dat, disappeared [H66]. Tan Canh fell in a day, to tanks and infantry already inside the wire before dawn, and nothing in the J reached a fight of that kind (F005-6) [X-005]. The road to Kontum lay open. The PAVN paused for three weeks [H66]. John Paul Vann, the civilian director of the II Corps advisory effort, effectively directed the defence; he had Colonel Ly Tong Ba of the 23rd Division put in command of Kontum, and on 10 May Major General Nguyen Van Toan replaced Ngo Du at II Corps [H64] [H66]. On 9 May at Ben Het, ARVN Rangers destroyed three PT-76s with BGM-71 TOW missiles, among the first combat uses of the weapon, and TOW-armed helicopters followed [H66].

The three weeks were the B3 Front's to lose, and they were the time the siege box needed. From 26 April a Global Hawk was given to Kontum every night, and Vann had a detachment of the coordinate cell flown to the city's airfield on 28 April [X-005]. By 13 May it had measured about 600 positions in the arc north and west of the city: the 320th Division's assembly areas along the Krong Poko, gun pits on the ridges, the trench lines dug toward the 23rd Division's perimeter, and the tank harbours of the 203rd Armored Regiment [X-005]. Cells of Js struck the measured positions from 2 May, and by the eve of the assault about a third of the guns the PAVN had brought to Kontum had been destroyed or abandoned in their pits [X-005].

The main assault on Kontum came on 14 May, by the 320th Division's 48th, 52nd and 64th Regiments and the 2nd Division [H66] [H64]. B-52 strikes on 14 May and on the night of 16–17 May fell close to the ARVN lines and broke the momentum of the attack [H66]. Here the cells put GBU-38s on the PAVN regiments' measured start lines through the night of 13–14 May, and on 14 May the attacking battalions came forward through ground that had been struck one position at a time; the 48th Regiment's assault on the northern perimeter was broken short of the wire, and the penetration on the city's northern edge, which historically took two weeks to clear, was held to a few blocks [X-005]. From 26 May to 3 June the ARVN cleared the city bunker by bunker, with the cell putting GBU-38s on bunkers 300 metres from Ba's lines, and on 4 June Ba, now a brigadier general, declared Kontum secure, five days earlier than historically [X-005]. On 9 June Vann's helicopter flew into a hillside in low cloud and he was killed; a Vietnamese account says it was shot down [H66]. US estimates put PAVN casualties in the Kontum battle at about 4,000 and in the Highlands campaign at 20,000–40,000, figures with no better basis than the body counts of earlier years [H66]. I estimate that the 23rd Division lost perhaps a quarter fewer killed than historically, and I do not adjust the PAVN's figures [X-005].

At Tan Canh the board's battalion commanders could not hold a position whose wire the tanks were inside before dawn, and did not; what changed was how many of their men got out. The 42nd Regiment's battalions broke out of Tan Canh on 24 April in their companies, not as individuals, and about a third more of the regiment's men reached Kontum over the following week than on the historical course [X-007]. The 22nd Division's command did not change; its commander had been the palace's choice after the list became advisory, and his disappearance is the historical record's [H66] [X-007]. Kontum was held by the 23rd Division under Ba, whose regiments were commanded by board officers, and it was held as told above, by the siege box and the infantry together.

## EV000-78 · The fall of Quang Tri · 27 Apr – 2 May 1972 · Quang Tri City and Highway 1 south to the My Chanh River · HIST-ALT · 000,007
*Initiative: enemy*

On 27 April the PAVN renewed its attack on the 3rd Division's defence of Quang Tri City [H]. Contradictory orders from the I Corps commander, Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam, and from Giai left units moving in opposite directions, and on 1 May Giai ordered the city abandoned [H67] [H]. The division broke. Soldiers and refugees fled south on Highway 1, and PAVN artillery fired on the column, killing roughly 2,000 soldiers and civilians on the road [H67]. Quang Tri Province was lost by 2 May; the Marines who held at the My Chanh River were the one coherent force left north of Hue [H64] [H]. On 2 May Thieu relieved Lam and gave I Corps to Lieutenant General Ngo Quang Truong, who stopped the rout, restored order in Hue and held the My Chanh line with the Marine and Airborne Divisions and the 1st Division [H64]. Giai was later court-martialled and sentenced to five years' imprisonment [H64]. The fall of Quang Tri was the first loss of a province to a PAVN conventional offensive, and Truong's recovery the clearest demonstration of what leadership was worth in the ARVN.

In this war the orders were the same and one regiment's conduct was not. When Giai ordered the city abandoned on 1 May, the 57th Regiment under Colonel Bui Quang Trieu was holding the bridges over the Thach Han south of the city, and Trieu held them until about 1500 on 1 May, three hours after his orders allowed, while the division's rear echelons and about 20,000 refugees crossed; he then brought the regiment south on Highway 1 behind the column with its two battalions astride the road and the third on the dunes to the east, where the PAVN artillery's observers had been placed historically [X-007]. The column was still shelled. I put the dead on the road at about 1,500 rather than about 2,000, and the 3rd Division's losses in men who simply disappeared at about a quarter fewer, judgments I hold loosely [X-007]. The fall of Quang Tri was not prevented and could not have been at that level; it was lost by the corps and the division, by orders that sent units in opposite directions, and Truong's recovery of the line in May was the recovery of a commander the list had named in 1966 and the palace had chosen in 1972 when it had no other (F007-7) [X-007].

## EVMT01-3 · The decree powers and the end of the hamlet elections · Jun – Sep 1972 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · MT01
*Initiative: allied*

At the end of June 1972, with the offensive still on three fronts, the Senate gave President Thieu power to rule by decree for six months on matters of defence, security and the economy, and he used it [H]. A press decree of August 1972 required newspapers to post a bond that closed most of the opposition press within weeks, and in the late summer of 1972 the election of hamlet chiefs, introduced in 1967, was abolished and the chiefs were appointed through the province and district chiefs [H]. It was the largest step back from the Republic's own constitution since 1967, and it was taken for the palace's reasons, not the enemy's: the President was preparing for the ceasefire that the Americans were negotiating over his head, and wanted every hamlet chief in the country to be his own man when the flags went up [H].

In this war the decree fell on a countryside where elected hamlet chiefs mattered more than they had historically, because they sat on the councils that had confirmed the land titles of 1964 and heard the disputes over them [M001]. The province and district chiefs who appointed their successors were, since 1970, the palace's choices from the Board's list (POS002-2), and in the Delta many of them replaced the elected chief with the same man; elsewhere, in the coastal provinces and in II Corps, they did not [X-MT01]. The government's standing in the hamlets fell to where it stood on the historical course, for the historical reason, and with one difference that history did not have: in the hamlets where land had been titled under elected councils, the appointed chiefs were the men who could now hear the land disputes, and the Front's district committees made that their whole propaganda for the ceasefire [X-MT01].

The net of this event is not a gain undone. The land titles, the posts and the files of the held belt were untouched by the decree; what it took was the one thing in the countryside that had made the government's arrival a matter of the hamlet's own choosing. That loss is history's, the gain beneath it is this war's, and the two together leave the Republic at the end of 1972 holding more of its countryside at night than the historical Republic held, on a narrower claim to the hamlets' consent [X-MT01].

## EV000-79 · The recapture of Quang Tri City · 28 Jun – 16 Sep 1972 · Quang Tri Province south of the Thach Han River and the Quang Tri Citadel · HIST · 000
On 28 June Truong opened the counter-offensive, Lam Son 72, with the Airborne Division and the Marine Division under Brigadier General Bui The Lan, whose 147th, 258th and 369th Marine Brigades did most of the fighting for the city [H70]. Against them were the 304th, 308th, 320B and 325th Divisions with the 312th identified [H70]. US forces provided fire and movement but no ground troops: in July alone, 5,461 tactical sorties and 2,054 B-52 strikes; US Marine helicopters of HMM-164 and HMM-165 lifted the Vietnamese Marines into landing zones, losing several to SA-7 missiles; and the 9th Marine Amphibious Brigade and the Seventh Fleet staged amphibious feints [H70]. The PAVN held the Quang Tri Citadel, its walls about 15 feet high, with interlocking tunnels and positions; the Marines breached the southeast wall on 10–11 September, held the Citadel by the late afternoon of 15 September, and raised the flag over the west gate on 16 September after 138 days of occupation [H70]. The city was rubble [H70].

The Marine Division lost 977 killed of 3,658 casualties, and the Airborne Division's 2nd Brigade was badly depleted [H70]. South Vietnamese claims are 8,135 PAVN killed; Hanoi's are 4,000–10,000 [H70]. About 20 Americans were killed [H70]. The province north of the Thach Han remained in PAVN hands, and both sides were too exhausted to move further [H70]. The battle proved that the ARVN's best divisions, under a good commander and with unlimited American fire support, could defeat the PAVN in a set-piece battle. Every one of those conditions was American or rare.

## EV000-80 · The last American ground combat units leave · Aug 1972 · South Vietnam · HIST · 000
In August 1972 the last US ground combat battalion in South Vietnam, the 3rd Battalion, 21st Infantry of the 196th Brigade, stood down at Da Nang and left the country [H]. American advisers, aviation units, the air and naval forces in and around Vietnam, and a shrinking logistics command remained, and US strength fell to about 24,000 by the end of the year [H40]. US deaths by hostile action fell to 300 in 1972 [H40]. The withdrawal begun at Midway in 1969 had been completed for the ground war during the largest PAVN offensive of the conflict, and the defence of the South on the ground had become entirely the RVNAF's. US air power, at its 1972 peak, was the part of the American commitment that remained.

## EV000-81 · The October agreement and Thieu's refusal · 8 Oct – 13 Dec 1972 · Paris, Saigon and Washington · HIST · 000
On 8 October Le Duc Tho presented Kissinger in Paris with a draft that dropped Hanoi's demand for Thieu's removal: a ceasefire in place, the withdrawal of all US forces within 60 days, the return of prisoners, the Thieu government to remain alongside the PRG pending a political settlement through a council of reconciliation, and no requirement for PAVN forces to leave the South [H51] [H]. Kissinger accepted it as the basis for an agreement. Thieu rejected it: it left the PAVN in his country and gave the PRG standing, and he had not been consulted in its drafting [H51] [H]. On 26 October Kissinger announced that "peace is at hand" [H]. On 7 November Nixon was re-elected, carrying 49 states [H]. Washington then sent Saigon a mass of equipment under Operation Enhance Plus, aircraft, armour and artillery, to be in the country before a ceasefire froze replacement at one-for-one, and Nixon gave Thieu private written assurances that the United States would respond with full force to a major violation [H] [H51].

In November and December Kissinger brought Saigon's amendments to Paris, Hanoi hardened its own terms in reply, and the main round of talks broke down on 13 December [H69]. Nixon, concerned that the new Congress in January would cut off the war's funding, gave Hanoi an ultimatum [H69].

## EV000-82 · Linebacker II · 18–29 Dec 1972 · Hanoi, Haiphong and their surroundings · HIST-ALT · 000,005
*Initiative: allied*

On 18 December B-52s attacked Hanoi and Haiphong for the first time in the war [H69]. In eleven days, with a 36-hour pause at Christmas, 741 B-52 sorties were dispatched and 729 completed against rail yards at Kinh No, Lang Dang and Yen Vien, power plants including Thai Nguyen, petroleum storage, airfields and SAM sites, and fighter-bombers added about 5,000 tons [H69]. In this war the 729 were J sorties, and about a third of them carried twenty GBU-31s each against measured aimpoints in the rail yards, the power plants and the SAM sites the U-2S had kept on the cell's board since 1965; the rest carried the J's unguided load, and the tonnage was about 11,000 rather than about 15,237 (F005-8) [H69] [X-005]. The Js released their guided weapons from about twelve miles out and above the clouds that covered Hanoi on most nights; the North's SA-2 batteries still reached them, because the missile's envelope is longer than the bomb's glide [M005].

The first three nights' routes and timings were set by SAC headquarters, as they were historically, and the North's missile crews learned them as they did historically [H] [X-005]. North Vietnam fired 266 SA-2s over the eleven days [H69]. The United States acknowledged 15 B-52s shot down historically; here I estimate six Js lost, about two-fifths of the historical figure, from the J's later electronic-warfare suite and its higher, faster release, and from the GBU-31s that destroyed the SA-2 sites at Kinh No and Phuc Yen on the fourth night rather than the eighth (F005-18) [H69] [X-005]. Of the crews in the lost Js, about thirteen men were killed or missing and about twelve captured, against 33 killed or missing and 33 captured historically, with the five-man crew accounting for part of the difference [H69] [X-005]. 12 tactical aircraft were lost [H69]. By the last nights US commanders judged the North's SA-2 stocks nearly exhausted and its air defences largely suppressed [H].

North Vietnam's official count of civilian dead is 1,624, 1,328 in Hanoi and 306 in Haiphong [H69]. The Bach Mai hospital was struck historically on 22 December by bombs aimed at the Bach Mai airfield and its storage, and on 26 December bombs on Kham Thien Street, aimed at the rail yards, killed 278 by Hanoi's count [H69]. Here the airfield and the rail yards were struck with GBU-31s, and neither the hospital nor Kham Thien Street was hit; unguided bombs from the other cells still fell outside the targets, and I estimate the civilian dead at about 900–1,200, a judgment and not a count [X-005]. About 80 percent of the North's electrical generating capacity was put out of action [H69]. On 30 December Nixon halted the bombing north of the 20th parallel, and Hanoi agreed to resume talks, which reopened on 9 January 1973 [H69]. Whether the bombing forced Hanoi back to terms or merely covered the American acceptance of terms close to October's is disputed; Le Duan later said it had "completely obliterated our economic foundation" [H69]. In this war it carried fewer American prisoners into the negotiation and fewer pictures of Bach Mai into the American press, and the terms were the same (F005-13) [X-005].

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1972 the war is on the verge of a settlement that both governments in Vietnam regard as provisional. The Easter Offensive has failed to destroy the ARVN or the Thieu government, at a cost to the PAVN of most of the armour it committed (by one count 134 T-54s, 56 PT-76s and 60 T-34s) and, on the historical count, about 100,000 men [H64]. The ARVN has held at An Loc and Kontum and retaken Quang Tri City, but has lost Quang Tri north of the Thach Han, the western Highlands and the border areas of III Corps, and its losses are given variously from about 10,000 killed to about 39,000 dead in the period [H64]. Almost a million South Vietnamese have been made refugees, about 600,000 of them in government camps [H64].

In this war the PAVN's losses for the year are higher than that count, and higher in particular formations. The GBU-38 cells added perhaps a fifth to the losses of the B-2 Front's divisions around An Loc, the siege box destroyed about a third of the guns brought to Kontum before the assault and broke the 320th Division's assault short of the wire, and the radar-bombing of the armour on the DMZ destroyed some 20–30 vehicles before they reached the firebases (EV000-74, EV000-76, EV000-77) [X-005]. Taken together I put the PAVN's killed and wounded in 1972 at about 7,000–10,000 above the historical figure, roughly a tenth more, concentrated in the 5th, 7th and 9th Divisions and the 320th and 2nd Divisions, which are the formations that will fight Phuoc Long, Ban Me Thuot and Xuan Loc [X-MT01]. The North replaces that difference within its flow of men for 1973 [E: one to two months of a replacement flow to the South of the order of 100,000 men a year or more in 1973–74]. What it does not replace as quickly is the experienced cadre killed in the measured assaults, and the 7th and 9th Divisions enter 1974 with greener companies than history's [X-MT01].

The countryside ends 1972 in two parts. Where a PAVN division came into the peopled lowlands, in northern Binh Dinh and Quang Tri, the gains of 1964–71 were lost and the hamlets returned to the state history left them in (EVMT01-1). In the held belt of the Delta and around Saigon the posts held through the year, the Front's infrastructure inside the post line did not return, and the files that will open the "war of the flags" survived intact (EVMT01-2). In Saigon the decree powers and the end of the hamlet elections took back the government's claim to the hamlets' consent, as they did historically (EVMT01-3). The historical land programme has by now distributed about a million hectares of its own, and the margin of the 1964 titling over history has narrowed to its six years of head start and the councils that confirmed it [H] [X-MT01]. Night control and the Front's weakness in the held belt remain one band better than history's; the government's legitimacy has returned to history's; and the Special Branch's knowledge of the infrastructure, which the historical police and Phoenix had largely caught up with by 1972, is one band better rather than two [X-MT01].

The lesson both sides draw is the same. The ARVN held where American air power was decisive, at An Loc and Kontum, and where American advisers coordinated it; it broke where its leadership failed, at Quang Tri and Tan Canh. The settlement about to be signed removes the American advisers and the American air power and leaves the PAVN in the South, while the North's air defences and economy recover. Hanoi needs only to rebuild, wait for the Americans to be gone, and choose its time.

The aircraft are still flying at the end of 1972, still American, and under the agreement about to be signed they will soon have no American strike force to cue. The Republic keeps nothing of them: no aircraft, no exploitation cells, no practice in using either.

The ARVN has held An Loc and Kontum more cheaply than history's, and has learned more completely that it holds besieged towns with American bombers measured onto the enemy by American analysts (EV000-76, EV000-77). The agreement about to be signed takes both away. The Republic enters 1973 more confident than history's and more dependent [X-005].

The ARVN's regiments and battalions have fought the offensive better than history's, and its divisions and corps no better: Quang Tri was lost by orders, and the column on Highway 1 was saved in part by a regiment (EV000-78). It has learned, from An Loc and Kontum, to use American air with an educated staff's precision, and it has no air of its own that its plans can rest on (F007-23) [X-007].
