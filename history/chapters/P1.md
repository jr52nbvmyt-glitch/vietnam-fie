# Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

The war that COMUSMACV inherits on 1 January 1964 is an advisory war that is being lost. The coup of 1 November 1963 had killed Ngo Dinh Diem and his brother Nhu, and with them went the province chiefs, the Strategic Hamlet Program and most of the Republic's administrative continuity. MACV under General Paul D. Harkins is a headquarters of advisers, helicopter companies and support troops; about 23,300 Americans are in the country by the end of 1964, none in combat units, and 147 die by hostile action that year [H40]. The fighting is done by the ARVN, the Regional and Popular Forces and the CIDG camps against a Viet Cong movement rebuilt since 1959 on the Viet Minh's old infrastructure and on cadres returning down the Trail.

The phase is a double collapse and a double response. In Saigon the state comes apart, coup after coup, and the generals spend more of their energy on each other than on the countryside. In the field the Viet Cong form regiments and then a division around Saigon, and at Binh Gia, Ba Gia and Dong Xoai they beat the general reserve on ground they chose. Hanoi commits regular PAVN regiments to the South for the first time. Washington answers from the air, first with reprisals and then, from 2 March 1965, with an opening strike on the North's whole target list and a campaign run from Saigon without zones or pauses (EV013-1); and on the ground, from two Marine battalions guarding an airfield in March to the decision of July to fight the war with American troops. Every constraint that will shape the war is drawn here except the one this war never drew, the restricted zones around Hanoi and Haiphong (P013b), and so is the enemy's basic answer to American firepower: close in, choose the time of battle, keep the base areas beyond the border.

Most of what is new in this war stood on its first day, and none of it decided the coups or the summer offensive. The Republic itself was changed by one session of decrees on 1 January 1964: land to the tiller and a Commission Against Corruption with an American power to suspend aid behind it, a resident rural police with a Special Branch, province and district chiefs chosen from a ranked list, Phung Hoang centres built on the police's files, an officer board and a combined command (EVR05-1). Behind those decrees stood what had been built since 1957 and was already in the ranks: every private and territorial trained on the 2026 course, every NCO schooled for his grade, every officer educated before each promotion, and an industry a generation above the country's own (EVR05-1) [M007] [M009]. By July 1965 about 330,000 hectares are titled, the musters have found the RVNAF about 41,000 men smaller than its rolls (EVR05-2) [X-001], and about 1,100 village posts stand in the held belt, where a post with its trained and rearmed Popular Forces platoon now holds about nineteen attacks in twenty (EV002-2) [X-009]. The Front's answer is to move its committees out of the held villages, and the first campaigns of the task force, of Delta and of the district centres follow them out (EVR05-3).

The command can also see and strike as history's could not. The Global Hawk orbit closes the trawler route within a year and moves Hanoi's southern supply to Sihanoukville about eighteen months early (EV003-1); on the night of 4 August 1964 the radar shows no torpedo boats, and Pierce Arrow is not flown (EV000-3). Sixty-four AC-130J gunships fly over the posts from the first week, and by the summer of 1965 the Front has stopped assaulting the posts within their net on clear nights (EV015-1). The B-52Js wait on Guam for sixteen months with a target list and no authority, and then destroy in a week what history's fighter-bombers spent years on (EV013-1). The North disperses, Moscow and Beijing rebuild its sky (EVCP02-2), and nothing that reaches the South changes: the porters, the trucks and the Front's regiments notice nothing at all (F013-7).

## EVR05-1 · The session of 1 January 1964 and the institutions it made · 1 Jan – 31 Dec 1964 · Saigon; the Joint General Staff at Tan Son Nhut; Quang Trung, Dong De and the training centres; the Bien Hoa and Go Vap works · NEW · R05
*Initiative: allied*

On 1 January 1964 the Military Revolutionary Council of General Duong Van Minh, with Prime Minister Nguyen Ngoc Tho's cabinet, signed in one session the decrees that this war's Republic was built on, each drafted with the US Mission. The land decree ended rent on tenanted rice land from the coming harvest, made every present tiller the owner of up to 3 hectares in the Delta and 1 in the Central Lowlands, compensated the landlord at 2.5 times a year's paddy, 20% in cash and the rest in indexed bonds, and confirmed by name every distribution the Viet Minh or the Front had made (P001a) [M001]. Minh read it on Radio Saigon that evening, and its first sentence was that confirmation. The same session created a Commission Against Corruption under Judge Truong Vinh Khoa, with a Special Court, a Pay and Muster Verification Service and a Joint US–GVN Aid Compliance Board whose American co-chair could suspend aid to a named command, importer or province and whose concurrence was needed to remove the Commissioner (P001b) [M001].

The police decree put a resident post of 8 to 12 constables in every village the government held, gave a Special Branch sole charge of intelligence on the infrastructure, raised a Police Field Force at once, and held every arrest to a magistrate's review within 72 hours; its Director General was Vo Dinh Cuong, a career inspector who had kept out of Nhu's political service (P002a) [M002]. Its companion made unlawful the appointment of any province or district chief not taken from the ranked list of a Selection Board under Judge Dinh Quang Tuyen (P002b). About 1,640 candidates sat the Board's first examination on 2 March, and its first list, for 31 posts, appeared on 27 April; by the end of 1964 about 95 of the Republic's roughly 290 chiefs had come from it (F002-5) [X-002]. The Phung Hoang decree put an intelligence and operations centre in every province and district as the Special Branch opened there, under Lieutenant Colonel Tran Dinh Hieu, with an American exploitation detachment under Lieutenant Colonel Martin J. Ashby and about 1,500 biometric kits; it forbade monthly targets, required a magistrate's warrant on three sources for every arrest, and opened the centres' reports to the Commission's audit (P006a) [M006]. The first district centre opened at Phu Tan on 16 March, two weeks after the first village post, and about 100 were open by December, every one behind a post; Hieu refused the coastal province chiefs who asked for centres before they had posts (EC006-12) [X-006]. From October the first of about 1,100 selected advisers, trained for eight months and kept for eighteen, reached the subsectors, among them Captain Samuel J. Whitcomb at Can Giuoc, who asked the district chief on his first night, in Vietnamese, which families still held their titles at the district office and why [X-006].

The officer decree made promotion and battalion and regimental command the decision of an Officer Selection and Promotion Board under Brigadier General Phan Khac Minh, with rated evaluations, relief for cause after thirty days, a commission for proven sergeants, and generals chosen from a ranked list of three behind an aid condition (P007a) [M007]. It inherited the seven educated classes of 1957–63, about 10,000–14,000 officers, who already held most of the platoons and companies; above them the army was what it had been [M007]. The first season published 61 battalion and 9 regimental commands on 30 June, and the career-course gate kept captains in command of a third of the battalions for another year and a half (F007-8) [X-007]. When Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc moved on Saigon in September, two of their battalions were commanded by officers the board had refused for the June list, and none by an officer it had chosen [X-007].

The Combined Operations Command was proclaimed in the same session, the one decree the council signed with visible reluctance: Harkins as combined commander, Major General Le Van Kim as Vietnamese deputy, Korean and Australian deputies on paper, and a staff of about 260 at Tan Son Nhut by the end of January (P012a) [X-012]. Its first operations order was drafted on 22 January and never issued, because Khanh's coup came eight days later (EV000-1).

Two things stood already. The Republic began the war with a steelworks, an arsenal and rebuild works at Bien Hoa and a radio plant, battery works and wire mill at Go Vap, placed under a Directorate of Defence Industry under Vuong Dinh Toan (P008a, P008b) [M008]. For fourteen months it had more capacity than customers, because no quartermaster would pay for what American aid gave free, until Colonel Howard B. Teague's rule, made a condition by the Aid Compliance Board on 1 March 1965, required a quarter of the RVNAF's ammunition bodies, batteries, wire, radios and steel to be bought at home in 1965, a third in 1966 and half from 1968 (EC008-2, POS008-1) [X-008]. Its price was in men: 31,000–45,000 workers of military age needed deferments, and a place on the deferment list cost two months' wages in Bien Hoa by the spring of 1965 (F008-9) [X-008]. By July the PF platoons of the held belt carried about 5,000 Go Vap radios and the hamlets 6,000 more, and the Front had put cells into the arsenal's case lines (F008-17) [X-008]. The ranks stood too. Since 1957 every regular recruit had passed the ten-week basic course at Quang Trung or a division centre and every territorial a six-week version, about 120,000–160,000 regulars and 60,000–100,000 territorials by 1964 (P009a, P009b, P009c) [M009], and every NCO had passed the course for his grade at the National NCO Academy at Dong De under Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc (P010b) [X-010]. Both cost the battalions their best sergeants as instructors, about 2,000–3,000 drill sergeants and 1,300–2,500 NCO instructors in the two years the army could least spare them; the Joint General Staff asked for a third of them back in June and November 1964 and got a few hundred, replaced from the battalions' own corporals (F009-11, F010-11) [X-010]. In November the Armed Forces Council's National Day promotion left the gate standing: every NCO with his course went up a grade, and no corporal without it (F010-5) [X-010]. From April 1964 every man on a muster roll carried the number of the cycle he had passed; in September the musters found 212 privates of a 22nd Division battalion whose cycle the training centre's ration accounts showed had lasted four weeks, not ten, and the commandant was charged in October and relieved in November (F009-3) [X-009].

## EV003-1 · Operation TIDE TABLE · 6 Jan 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · The II Corps coast from Qui Nhon to Cam Ranh Bay and the Ca Mau coast; Haiphong and the Gulf of Tonkin; Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville (Kompong Som), Cambodia · NEW · 003,CP01,R05
*Initiative: both*

The first Global Hawk orbit took station off the II Corps coast on 6 January 1964, sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep for the steel-hulled trawlers that Group 759, later Group 125, ran south from Haiphong (EC003-3) [M003]. Before January nobody had been looking for them on a coast of 2,000 kilometres and tens of thousands of junks. The first track came on the night of 18–19 February: a contact at a steady ten knots that did not fish, turning west at dusk toward Phu Yen. The radar held it for seven hours, so the Vietnamese Navy's PC-461 from the Tuy Hoa patrol line did not have to search, only steam, and came up with the trawler at 0310 four miles off Vung Ro Bay. The crew fired her demolition charges; the forward hold blew and the after hold did not, and a boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of carbines, B-40 launchers, 82mm rounds and 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm [X-003]. Seven of the crew were taken from the water and nine died.

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven trawlers on the II Corps and Ca Mau coasts. Four were intercepted, one of them nearly intact off Binh Dinh by USS *Edsall*'s boarding party, whose crew gave up the landing beaches and two years of earlier voyages; six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon; one landed unobserved in May while the orbit was down with a generator fault [X-003]. Hanoi tried again in the autumn, while the orbit was held in the Gulf after the August crisis: one trawler landed at Vung Ro on 21–22 September, a second was beached and burned off Tam Quan on 9 November with sixteen of her crew dead, and a third turned for home in December [X-003].

The Central Military Commission concluded that winter that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night", and changed routes [X-003]. Sihanouk was moving toward Beijing and would break with Washington on 3 May 1965; Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and the first arms cargo came ashore there in late June 1965, about eighteen months before history's, to be trucked by night to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak [H] [X-003]. There was no trawler at Vung Ro for Lieutenant James Bowers to find on 16 February 1965, and Market Time opened on 11 March on TIDE TABLE's record with less and less to find. TIDE TABLE never starved the Front. It moved the enemy's principal line of supply to the southern half of the country into a neutral port that no American could stop or seize (F003-3, F003-17).

## EV004-1 · Operation IRON GATE · 6 Jan – 30 Jun 1964 · Saigon port and Tan Son Nhut; the Quang Trung Training Center at Hoc Mon, Gia Dinh Province; the division training centres and the province RF/PF training camps of IV and III Corps, then countrywide · NEW · 016
*Initiative: allied*

MSTS *Private Joe P. Martinez* berthed at Khanh Hoi on 6 January 1964 with about 41,000 M4A1 carbines and their CompM5 sights, 2,400 M249s and 320 M110A2s [X-004]. The issue was called IRON GATE because no new weapon left a depot until the old one came in through the gate, and every company zeroed its carbines at 25 metres before it left, on a five-day course that Lieutenant Colonel Arthur L. Venn refused to shorten (EC004-2) [X-004]. The Delta divisions and the territorials of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and the Bassac drew first, the general reserve in February and March, and I and II Corps from April. Khanh's coup stopped the issue for nine days, and a fifth of the first half-year's training was lost to units that drew carbines and went back to their posts without zeroing them (F004-5) [X-004].

The pistols came through the same gate. The first 18,000 Glock 21s, each with its tritium sights and a SureFire light on the rail, came ashore at Khanh Hoi in the second ship of January, and the rule was the carbine's: an M1911A1 or a revolver in, a Glock, a light, three magazines and a holster out [M016·S1] [X-016]. The officers, crews and senior NCOs of the Delta divisions drew with their companies' carbines; the issue to the whole regular RVNAF, about 25,000 pistols, was done by the end of March, and to the territorials' leaders, about 10,000–11,000, by the end of May [X-016]. Each holder fired 50 rounds on the third day of the carbine course, after a morning on the light and an afternoon on the one rule the Glock imposes, that the finger stays straight until the sights are on the target (F016-5) [X-016]. The Americans in the country, about 3,000–4,000 pistols' worth, exchanged theirs at Tan Son Nhut in a week [X-016]. The National Police, rising through 30,000 men by June, drew theirs last, from May to August, on a four-day course that the Directorate of Police ran at Rach Dua and in the province towns without the army's instructors (F016-1, F016-5) [M002] [X-016].

By 30 June about 310,000 carbines were issued, 180,000 of them to the RF and PF, with about 2,900 squad guns and 1,800 marksman rifles to the regular infantry; the squad gun and the marksman stayed with the regulars, and the outposts kept the Browning as their automatic rifle [M004] [X-004]. About a third of the 290,000 old weapons went, over this headquarters' objection, to the National Police, the Combat Youth and the hamlet militia. The old pistols went the same way: of about 40,000 M1911A1s, revolvers and French pistols handed in by the RVNAF and the police by August, about 9,000 were given by the Directorate to village chiefs, hamlet chiefs and the Combat Youth's leaders, again over this headquarters' objection, and the rest went back to American depots [X-016]. A PF platoon at Can Duoc that had hit a 100-metre target about once in four shots with its Garands put most of its rounds into it by the third morning and asked to keep the spent cases to show the village [X-004]. The Long An province committee called the carbines "a trick to buy the puppet soldiers' loyalty" and took its first on 22 March in an ambush at Rach Kien, and with it its first Glock, from the belt of the platoon's dead sergeant [X-004] [X-016].

## EV015-1 · The gunship wing's first year · 8 Jan – 16 Dec 1964 · Bien Hoa, Nha Trang, Da Nang and Qui Nhon air bases; the posts of Long An, Dinh Tuong, Kien Hoa and Vinh Long; Tam Quan and Hoai Nhon, Binh Dinh Province · NEW · 015,R05
*Initiative: both*

The 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship) flew its first sortie on the night of 8–9 January 1964 with 64 AC-130J Ghostriders under Farm Gate's rules: a Vietnamese observer aboard, support of the RVNAF only, and fire only on the province chief's clearance (P015a) [X-015]. At 0140 on 9 January the PF post at Phu Tuc on the Ham Luong called on its PRC-10 that its wire was being cut. Ghostrider 41 was over the post at 0209, its infrared turret showing about sixty men in two lines among the palms and a mortar team in a ditch beyond them; the clearance came at 0221 and the 105mm put eighteen rounds on them by 0240 [X-015]. The district reported 70 enemy dead. The video showed 18 to 25, and the video's figure went to MACV (F015-18) [X-015].

Through the spring the wing flew 400–600 sorties a month and answered 120–180 calls from posts under attack, and it learned two things fast: the gunship was worth as much as the radio that called it, and the province chief's clearance took a median 35 minutes while posts were overrun beneath circling guns (F015-1) [X-015]. In July clearance within 300 metres of a post was delegated to the district chief and the wait fell to about 12 minutes; the price of that rule was paid later, in hamlets beside the posts (F015-7, F015-13) [X-015]. COSVN's Military Affairs Committee answered in March: no assault on a post with a radio on a clear night within reach of the airfields, attacks in rain and on posts that could not call, and the wall crossed before the aircraft came (F015-15) [X-015]. By June assaults on posts with radios on clear nights had nearly stopped in Long An and Dinh Tuong, and the local companies had turned to the mortar from 300 metres and the mine on the dike (F015-5) [X-015].

The limit was found in the first north-east monsoon. At 2310 on 14 December the post at Tam Quan on Route 1 called that a company of the 2nd Regiment was on its wire, under a cloud base of 1,800 feet. Major Thomas R. Gaither took Ghostrider 63 down through the overcast to 1,500 feet to see, and found two 12.7mm guns sited on the dikes, which put thirty rounds through his left wing and tanks in six minutes while he broke the assault on the southern wall (EC015-7) [X-015]. He brought the aircraft home to Qui Nhon on three engines. The attack was renewed in the rain at 0400 with nothing overhead, and the post fell with nineteen of its thirty men. On 16 December the wing forbade attacks below 3,500 feet, and the provincial units of the coast learned over the winter what they had not believed since March: the aircraft did not see through cloud [X-015].

## EV000-1 · Khanh's coup · 30 Jan 1964 · Saigon · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002,CP01,007,R05
*Initiative: neither*

Before dawn on 30 January 1964 Major General Nguyen Khanh, commanding I Corps, took power from Minh's council without firing a shot, with Generals Tran Thien Khiem and Nguyen Van Thieu and Colonels Nguyen Chanh Thi and Do Mau [H8]. Generals Tran Van Don, Le Van Kim, Ton That Dinh and Mai Huu Xuan were arrested on a charge of plotting a neutralist settlement; Minh's aide, Major Nguyen Van Nhung, was executed; Minh stayed on as a figurehead [H8]. The coup came on history's date for history's reasons, and Khanh used the combined command's own network to take the state: the combined centre at Da Nang passed I Corps traffic to Saigon that night, and for nine days the command had no Vietnamese deputy and issued no orders [X-012].

The decrees were tested within a week. Officers of landed families and commanders who drew ghost pay backed Khanh in the hope that the decrees would lapse (F001-16) [X-001]. His officers detained Cuong, and he named acting chiefs in eleven provinces and districts; when he asked Khoa to bring corruption charges against the Dalat generals, Khoa replied that he had no evidence, and the American co-chair refused the concurrence Khanh needed to remove him [X-001]. Johnson's personal note of 2 February carried the condition that the United States would support no government that repealed the decrees (F001-15) [X-001]. Cuong was back in his office the same day, the acting chiefs were replaced by the list in April and May, and the officer board confirmed eleven of Khanh's twenty-three promotions and refused the rest (F002-16, F007-1) [X-007]. Lodge told Khanh on 4 February that the combined command had not been designed to command a coup and would not be used against one, and that the aid that paid the army was conditional on it. Khanh accepted on terms: his man Thieu as Vietnamese deputy and his own chief of staff, so that every combined order passed through Khanh's office [X-012]. The first combined order went out on 14 February. By March an order cleared his office in about thirty hours rather than four days, and on 26 March the 21st Division crossed a corps boundary into the Ca Mau forests on a combined order, the first time in the war (F012-11) [X-012].

The pattern held until 1970. Each government that inherited the reforms found them cheaper to keep than to break, and learned that the board and the list could be bargained with and could not be ignored. In March McNamara endorsed Khanh, and NSAM 288 committed the United States to an independent South Vietnam; Westmoreland took MACV in June and Taylor replaced Lodge in July [H].

## EV002-2 · The first posts and the Front's answer · 1 Mar 1964 – 31 Jul 1965 · The Delta along the Bassac and the Mekong, the provinces around Saigon, and the coastal Centre from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh · NEW · 016
*Initiative: both*

The first village police post was settled at Phu Tan in An Giang on 1 March 1964, and the posts followed the titling line into villages the government held at night (EC002-1) [X-002]. Each was a brick house with a radio and ten constables carrying the carbines of 1950, and from the summer each constable's revolver was a Glock with a light on it (F016-1) [X-016]. Beside each post, by the programme's rule, a Popular Forces platoon trained as a platoon on the territorial course, rearmed with M4A1s under IRON GATE, and from April carrying its own Go Vap PRC-10 (EC008-4) [X-008]. About 600 posts were manned by the end of 1964 and about 1,100 by July 1965, with the census registering about 45 percent of the accessible adults and a constable and a PF corporal walking the hamlet each night with the household booklets (EC002-2) [M002]. From August 1964 the constable held the booklet in one hand and lit the face at the door with the pistol in the other, and the night check became something a ten-man post could do in two hours and believe (EC016-1) [X-016].

The Front read the programme within weeks (F002-12). A COSVN directive of March 1964 put the rural police above the PF as a target and ordered the committees to kill constables before they learned the village. On 3 October a local-force company overran the post at Long Dinh on Route 4, settled three weeks earlier without its platoon, and killed seven of its nine constables [X-002]. The company took nine carbines and seven Glocks; the Dinh Tuong province security section took the Glocks from the company within the month (F016-14) [X-016]. By July 1965 about 85 posts had fallen and about 410 constables were dead, almost all in posts settled ahead of night security or without their platoon (F002-8) [X-002]. The new pistols changed none of those numbers: the posts fell to company assaults and the constables died on the roads to rifle fire at 50–150 metres, where a pistol does not reach, and the pistol only decided how the last man in a breached post died (F016-8) [X-016]. What the falls did change was the Front's armoury. About 280–320 of the constables' Glocks went with their posts and their bodies by July 1965, and with the officers' and post commanders' pistols taken in ambushes and the sales of the Saigon black market the Front held about 1,000–1,200 by the end of 1965, at low confidence; it could plausibly be half or double (F016-14) [X-016].

Where the platoon was there, it held, and one night showed why. At 0140 on 17 November 1964 a company of the 516th Local Force Battalion came over the bund at Long Huu in Kien Hoa. The platoon's corporal, Pham Van Tho, had zeroed his carbines in March and been taught at the Ben Tre centre to fire in pairs and keep a third of his ammunition for the second assault; the first assault broke on fire that did not pause, and the second was not pressed [X-009]. The platoon's own radio called the Ham Long boats at 0143, and the two PF soldiers wounded on the bund were alive with tourniquets on when the boats came at 0320 [X-008]. Across the held belt in 1964–65 the posts with their platoon held about nineteen attacks in twenty, and the platoons lost about a fifth fewer of their wounded; that figure is the combined judgment of the carbine, the radio and the course, and replaces the separate estimates first made for each (F004-1, F009-6) [X-009].

The Front learned faster than this headquarters expected. From December 1964 its district companies stopped assaulting posts with platoons and went for what was left: posts without platoons, constables on the road between villages, the CIDG camps, the hamlet militia with the Garands the army had handed in, and the posts that held by mortar from 300 metres and by the mine on the path to the market (F004-2) [X-004]. By the summer of 1965 posts that could call were attacked about a third less often than posts that could not, about two-thirds of the constables killed in 1965 died outside their posts, and Go Vap dry cells were turning up in the firing circuits of the Front's mines (F008-5, F008-16) [X-008]. The outposts that fell, the deserters and the ambushed battalions gave the Front 10,000–30,000 M4A1s by the end of 1965, the best rifles any guerrilla carried, each needing the Republic's own 5.56mm (F004-7) [X-004]. Some of the men sent against the posts were PF deserters who had walked home with their training, and the district companies made them squad leaders (F009-24) [X-009].

Behind the posts the Special Branch and the centres took their first harvest: about 1,900 arrests on three sources in the held belt by July 1965, a third released by the magistrates, and about 260 of them members of district or village committees against perhaps 100 the Branch's own folders would have reached (EC006-1) [X-006]. The biometric kit caught its first case at Cho Moi in December 1964, a finance cadre released on a bribe and rearrested under a new name, found in forty seconds (F006-3) [X-006]. The Branch found its first penetration in February 1965, a clerk in Dinh Tuong who had copied informant cards since the summer; eleven of the informants named on them were dead (F002-13) [X-002]. From January 1965 the Front's district committees in the held belt began moving their offices out of the villages to the contested edge (F006-5).

## EVR05-2 · The titles, the musters and the Vinh Long suspension · 16 Mar 1964 – 11 May 1965 · An Giang, Phong Dinh, Vinh Long, Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An, and the coast from Quang Nam to Binh Dinh; the ARVN and territorial units of IV Corps, then countrywide · NEW · R05

The first 40 titling teams of about 25 men took the field on 16 March 1964, each under a Regional Forces escort, working outward from the province capitals into villages the district could reinforce by day [X-001]. The first titles were handed over at Long Xuyen on 21 April and the first in a contested district at Phung Hiep on 11 May. By July 70 teams were out, the most the provinces could escort, and by 31 December about 210,000 hectares were titled to about 170,000 families, almost all in hamlets the government held at night (F001-6) [X-001]. The rent stop held at the December harvest in most of those districts. The Mission reported titles issued to Washington each month, and this headquarters reported them with the warning that they measured the government's paperwork, not the peasants' allegiance (F001-10).

The Pay and Muster Verification Service's first teams paraded the 21st Division at dawn around Bac Lieu and Soc Trang on 7 April, unannounced, and by the autumn 60 teams were mustering about 480 units a month; from August the enrolled units of IV Corps were paid at the parade rather than through their commanders [X-001]. Those are counts, and the best figures this headquarters ever had for the army it advised. McNamara read them as a smaller army, and in units mustered twice the second muster found few ghosts, because their commanders had struck them first (F001-9, F001-11) [X-001].

The Board used its power against a province once. At the December 1964 harvest landlords' agents collected rent in four villages of Vinh Long under escort of RF platoons the province chief had lent them; the Commission documented 26 cases and gave the Ministry 30 days, and nothing was done while Saigon was busy with the fall of Khanh [X-001]. It cost what had been predicted: Liberation Radio said for a month that the "American masters" had dismissed a puppet, and the officers who resented the Board said the same (POSTNA-6).

## EV016-1 · The lamps on the guns: the first season · 1 Apr – 31 Dec 1964 · The PF posts and police posts of Long An, Dinh Tuong and Kien Hoa; the National Police stations of the Delta towns; the COSVN security directorate in Tay Ninh; Bien Hoa air base · NEW · 016
*Initiative: both*

The new pistol's first season was spent learning what its light was for, and the Vietnamese paid for most of the lessons. In April and May 1964 the PF platoon leaders and ARVN lieutenants of the Delta took the lights on night ambush and used them as torches, to read a map under a poncho, to count their men on a dike, to find the path back (EC016-6) [X-016]. A 66,000-candela beam seen from the side is a marker visible for kilometres in the flat paddy [M016·S2]. On the night of 9–10 May a PF platoon of Can Duoc district was fired on from the next dike while its leader was checking his positions with his light, and lost two men before the ambush was set; three more platoons in Long An and Dinh Tuong were sprung the same way before the end of June (F016-7) [X-016]. The province chiefs of Long An and Dinh Tuong banned the light on patrol in June, and the territorial NCO courses at the province centres added an hour on light discipline to the pistol block in July (P010b) [X-016].

The negligent discharges came at the holster. The police, who drew last and were trained least, fired their new pistols into their own feet, their own floors and in four cases their own colleagues between May and October; across the RVNAF and the police about 100–120 more negligent discharges were recorded in 1964 than the old pistols would have produced, and about 8–10 more men died of them, at low confidence (F016-7) [X-016]. On 22 August a constable of the Rach Gia station killed his corporal while holstering after a night check; the Directorate of Police doubled its course to eight days from October and copied the territorial course's safe-handling block word for word [X-016].

The gunships saw the lights too. On the night of 18–19 November an AC-130J over a post in Dinh Tuong held its fire for six minutes because the crew could not tell the post's weapon lights on its wall from the attackers' torches; the post held, and the 14th Air Commando Wing (Gunship) and the IV Corps province chiefs agreed in December that the posts would mark their walls with strobes and fire and keep white light off the wall (F016-7) [M015] [X-016].

On the other side the COSVN security directorate read the lights by April from the first captured pistol, taken at Rach Kien on 22 March, and its circular of 4 June told the district security sections what the platoons were learning the hard way: hold fire until the lamp comes on, then fire at the lamp (F016-10) [X-016]. For about four months, until a captured light was switched on in a hut in Hau Nghia, two district committees of the Saigon–Gia Dinh Region believed the lamps saw through walls and kept their cadres out of the held villages altogether [X-016]. By the end of the year the lights were used as they were meant to be, for two seconds at a door, a bend or a face, and the Front knew it.

## EVR05-3 · The hunt for the committees: Can Giuoc, the Rach Cat and Hoc Mon · 9 Jun 1964 – 30 Jun 1965 · Can Giuoc, Can Duoc and Rach Kien Districts, Long An Province; the Rung Sat edge of Nha Be and the Hoc Mon and Go Vap Districts, Gia Dinh Province; Trung Lap, Hau Nghia Province · NEW · R05
*Initiative: both*

The hunt began with a killing. On the night of 9 June 1964 armed propaganda teams of the Can Giuoc district committee, with the Long An provincial security section and a company of the 506th Local Force Battalion, entered three hamlets of Phuoc Lai, read names from the claimants' list posted in the village office, and shot four title holders and the land committee's secretary [X-001]. By 14 July 37 title holders and 9 land committee members were dead in eleven hamlets, and several hundred families had brought their titles back to the district office; the RF companies in their mud forts along Route 5 did not move at night, and no reaction force came (POSTNA-2) [X-001]. Teams stopped posting names in hamlets not held at night, and a COSVN directive captured in August told the committees to let the peasants "take the puppets' paper" and make examples only where they began to inform (F001-4, F001-8) [X-001].

Three new instruments went after the men who had done it, and they did the same thing three ways: they found a committee from a file and followed it out of the villages. The task force's III Corps task group under Major Thomas R. Garvin, with Captain Nguyen Huu Phuoc of the Special Branch as his deputy, opened Operation DRY SEASON at Tan An on 20 July with a Special Forces detachment, a SEAL platoon and the Long An PRU (P012b) [X-012]. For two months it found almost nothing; of 19 operations in August and September, 14 found the target gone, and the American planners' attempt to raid on unconfirmed targets night after night was abandoned when the magistrate released 11 of 17 detainees (EC012-12) [X-012]. The turn came on 3 October, when a Rach Kien PF platoon ambushed a tax team and took a notebook of the committee's collections and a courier's route into the Nha Be nipa. On 5–6 October the SEALs went up the Soai Rap and took the security section's deputy chief, Ba Lien, who had commanded the Phuoc Lai killings [X-012]. By 18 December 14 of the section's 23 members were dead, in prison or rallied, and no title holder was killed in Can Giuoc in 1965 [X-012].

Delta arrived as the second instrument. MACV-JSOC stood up on 15 August under Brigadier General Harold M. Vance, a headquarters of 280 that could command its own units, declare a restricted area over a hamlet for a night and claim a target, and compel nothing outside MACV (P014a) [X-014]. Colonel James E. Teague's 1,310 men of the 1st SFOD-Delta came in from Pope between 28 August and 12 September, and rotated one squadron in four through rest at Tan Son Nhut, which is why the unit's sustained rate was never its first month's (F014-13) [X-014]. Of 31 raids by the end of December, nine reached a cadre of district rank; the cordon by a Ranger company was tried twice in November and both times the house was empty, because the battalion had been told the night before, and it was dropped (EC014-8) [X-014]. Sergeant First Class Donald R. Keel was the unit's first dead, shot through the throat at a doorway in Can Giuoc on 2 December. From DRY SEASON's papers came NIGHT HERON: two men of G Squadron lay in the nipa 240 metres from a sampan mooring on the Rach Cat for seven nights, and at 0240 on 15 March 1965 two assault teams took six of the eleven members of the Long An province committee there, with the finance section's ledgers for 1964 [X-014]. Long An's collections in three districts were about a third of the previous year's by December. A week earlier, on 7 March at Trung Lap, a PF squad with no radio had walked up a hamlet path toward a raid inside a restricted area its post had never been told of, and Delta's team lay in a ditch until it passed; from 15 March an area was passed down to the platoon on the ground or not declared, which cancelled eleven raids in the year (F014-6) [X-CP04].

The third instrument was the district centre. Hoc Mon by February 1965 had 11 posts, a district chief from the list, and a centre under Captain Ngo Van Tam with a chart of the district committee's 31 names, 19 confirmed on three sources; the committee had moved to the hamlets along the Saigon River and governed the posted villages by messenger (P006a) [X-006]. Operation STILL WATER used no regular troops: two Police Field Force companies and the Gia Dinh PRU made the arrests, the RF companies of Hoc Mon and Go Vap held the cordons at night with their carbines and BARs, and every arrest went to the kit and then to a magistrate (EC006-13) [M004] [X-006]. On the night of 26–27 March a courier of nineteen who had rallied for her brother's release from Con Son led the PRU to the house in Tan Thoi Nhi where the security section met, with a steel box of reports that named nine of the committee's informants in the posted villages, three of them PF soldiers [X-006]. By 30 June 63 had been arrested and 17 released, and 9 of the committee's 14 members were dead, in prison or rallied; the rest went north to Cu Chi, from where the Front governed Hoc Mon until 1968 [X-006]. The Commission's audit found 4 of the 63 arrests unsupported and sent them to the Special Court.

None of the three could reach the committees that moved, and each was rebuilt within a season by cadres sent from the region (C012-1). What did not come back was the committees' ease: none of them again met where a boat or a patrol could reach from a posted hamlet. The Front learned that leaving a village no longer put its leaders out of reach, three years before the historical Phoenix tried to show it (F006-26).

## EV000-3 · The Gulf of Tonkin incidents and the Southeast Asia Resolution · 2–11 Aug 1964 · Gulf of Tonkin and Washington · HIST-ALT · 000,003,CP01,R05
*Initiative: both*

After the OPLAN 34A raids on Hon Me and Hon Ngu, three P-4 torpedo boats attacked USS *Maddox* on its DESOTO patrol in daylight on 2 August 1964; four F-8s from *Ticonderoga* hit the retiring boats, and Johnson chose a warning [H7]. At Admiral Sharp's request I moved the Global Hawk orbit into the Gulf on 3 August (EC003-7). On the night of 4 August *Maddox* and *Turner Joy* fired for two hours in thunderstorms at contacts they took for a second attack [H7]. Major Harold Kessling had the watch in the exploitation cell. His radar log, on the set that had tracked the three boats of 2 August from their base, showed the destroyers, a cluster of junks thirty miles west, rain cells, and no fast small craft within forty miles at any time between 2000 and 2330 [X-003]. Herrick cabled that the action left "many doubts" [H7]. The report reached Washington in the late morning, while McNamara and Sharp were still trying to learn what had happened, and McNamara told the President that afternoon that a radar which had watched the whole engagement contradicted the ships [X-003]. Johnson held the strike order and at about 1900 cancelled it. Pierce Arrow was not flown, Richard Sather was not killed over Hon Gai, and Everett Alvarez was not taken prisoner that day [X-003].

The resolution was sent anyway, on the real attack of 2 August alone. The debate ran three days longer than history's, and Congress passed it on 10 August, 414–0 and 85–3, in the historical words [X-003]. It was again the only legislative authority for the war, but it rested on an attack that had happened, and the radar log of 4 August sat in the files at Tan Son Nhut and Honolulu as a record of a false alarm, not as the concealed proof of a pretext.

## EVCP02-1 · Saigon's autumn of 1964: the Vung Tau Charter, the September coup and Hop Tac · 16 Aug 1964 – mid-1965 · Saigon, Hue and Da Nang; the Hop Tac ring of Gia Dinh, Bien Hoa and Long An Provinces · HIST-ALT · CP02,R05
*Initiative: neither*

Khanh tried to use the Tonkin crisis to make himself president under the Vung Tau Charter of 16 August 1964; students and Thich Tri Quang's Buddhists answered with riots, the charter was repealed, and a triumvirate of Khanh, Minh and Khiem followed [H10]. Before dawn on 13 September Generals Lam Van Phat and Duong Van Duc seized key points in Saigon with about ten battalions. In this war they had a programme: Phat's broadcast promised to "suspend the land decree pending review", dissolve the "foreign-controlled" Aid Compliance Board and restore the corps commanders' authority over the provinces, which meant abolishing the Selection Board (F001-16) [X-001]. The Special Branch had reported battalions of the 7th Division readying at My Tho without orders on 11 September, the Saigon police stayed in their stations on Cuong's order, and Taylor told the plotters within hours that Washington would not recognise them [X-002]. Ky's jets and Thi's support saved Khanh, and the coup collapsed on 14 September without a death [H10]. Its failure attached the reforms to the side that won: the Young Turks around Ky and Thi took up the land decree and the Board as their own causes (F001-15, F002-16) [X-001].

Hop Tac, the oil spot applied to a ring around the capital, opened in September and had its battalions pulled away by every coup alert; by mid-1965 it was moribund, as historically [H25]. Here it carried the titling teams into Gia Dinh and Bien Hoa, where about 38,000 hectares were titled, and about 160 police posts were settled in the inner districts with chiefs from the list; the hamlets around the posts held at night through the coups even when the battalions left (F002-1) [X-002]. It could not save the programme. Pacification needed a stable government and a force that stayed in the hamlets at night, and in 1964 the Republic had only the beginning of the second.

## EV000-6 · Hanoi commits the regulars; the Trail and the first air war in Laos · Sep 1964 – Apr 1965 · North Vietnam, the Laotian panhandle and the Central Highlands · HIST-ALT · 000,001,CP01,R05
*Initiative: enemy*

The Ninth Plenum of December 1963 had resolved to win in the South before the United States could intervene in strength, and through 1964 Le Duan's Politburo acted on it [H]. Group 559's infiltration shifted to northern conscripts, and the first complete PAVN regiments went south: the 95th Regiment of the 325th Division reached Kontum in December 1964 and the 101st followed in February with Colonel Nguyen Huu An [H22]. By the US estimate of 1965 about 4,500 men and 300 tons a month were moving through Laos [H24]. The decision predated the land decree, but the decree gave it a second argument: Party reporting called the "land trick" the most dangerous of the enemy's measures, because it threatened the movement's base where it was strongest (F001-12) [X-001]. ## EV013-1 · Operation HAMMER FALL: the opening strike on the North · 2–9 Mar 1965 · Phuc Yen airfield, Hanoi (Bach Mai, the Citadel, Yen Phu, Duc Giang and the Paul Doumer bridge), Haiphong port and Thuong Ly, Nam Dinh, the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa, Vinh; Andersen Air Force Base, Guam; Yankee Station; Korat and Takhli, Thailand · NEW · 013,R05
*Initiative: allied*

The strike was a year in the making, and it began in a Quonset hut. On 3 March 1964 Major Edwin T. Sorrell, a geodesist, opened a coordinate cell at Tan Son Nhut to turn the U-2S's GPS-true imagery into points a GBU-31 could fly to, each measured to a few metres and filed with its photographs (F005-1) [X-005]. Its first discovery was that every map in the country was wrong: the 1:50,000 sheets were drawn on the Indian 1960 datum, and in the panhandle's mountains some crossings lay 300 metres or more from where the map put them. Sorrell's memorandum of 14 May 1964 said what the cell would enforce for a decade: "No coordinate taken from a map is to be given to a guided weapon, and no coordinate given to a guided weapon is to be plotted on a map without conversion" (F005-19) [X-005]. By June 1965 the cell held about 1,400 folders on the panhandle and the North below the 20th parallel, at the cost of a quarter to a third of the U-2S's hours (F005-9) [M005]. The B-52Js of the 3960th Strategic Wing trained on Guam from the spring of 1964 against coordinates the cell sent them, and for sixteen months struck nothing; the aircraft were ready and the authority was not.

After the false alarm of 4 August the cell was given the North above the 20th parallel, and Brigadier General Walter E. Pruitt built a Combined Targeting Cell around it with Captain Harlan J. Reese for the Navy (F013-2) [X-013]. To the Joint Chiefs' 94 targets it added the air defence command at Bach Mai, the Ministry of Defence in the Citadel, the transmitters, the radars, the power plants and the bulk fuel, about 760 aim points in all by February 1965 [X-013]. On the night of 2–3 March the crachin sat at 800–1,500 feet and nothing but the bombers could strike. Twenty-four B-52Js left Andersen in cells of three and released from 36,000–40,000 feet between 2340 and 0115 [X-013]. The first cells put GBU-38s on Phuc Yen's runways and revetments and wrecked 22–26 of the 921st Fighter Regiment's 36 MiG-17Fs; then the radars; then the penetrators on Bach Mai, the Citadel and Me Tri; then the Yen Phu and Haiphong power stations, the tank farms at Duc Giang and Thuong Ly, two spans of the Paul Doumer bridge, and the Ham Rong bridge at Thanh Hoa [X-013]. The last target was Haiphong's port, its one strike (EC013-5): about forty cranes destroyed and none of the fourteen ships alongside struck, and Hanoi gave the International Control Commission a figure of 214 port workers killed [X-013].

No B-52J was lost or hit; the guns fired blind into cloud at aircraft five miles above them. When the cloud lifted on 4 and 7 March the carriers and the F-105Ds from Thailand struck Kep, the rail yards and Thai Nguyen and lost seven aircraft to the guns, and two more cells restruck the Doumer bridge and the Haiphong power plant on 6 March, the first orders of the restrike watch (EC013-3) [X-013]. By 9 March the North had lost about 70–80 percent of its generating capacity, most of its bulk fuel, its great bridges, its only jet base and most of its fighters, its warning net and the cranes of its only deepwater port; its air defence command worked from provincial switchboards for three weeks [X-013]. No member of the Politburo was hurt, because General Van Tien Dung's staff had worked from Hoa Lac since February, and the North's civilian dead were 800–1,500 by this command's estimate [X-013]. The trucks of Group 559, the porters in the panhandle and the regiments in the South noticed nothing at all (F013-7).

## How the phase ends
By the end of July 1965 the advisory war is over. The 173rd Airborne Brigade and the III Marine Amphibious Force are in the country, the 1st Cavalry Division is preparing to sail, and Rolling Thunder is in its fifth month. The Viet Cong have regiments and divisions around Saigon and on the coast, PAVN regiments are in the Highlands, and the summer offensive has broken ARVN battalions at Ba Gia and Dong Xoai faster than they could be rebuilt, though no battalion of the general reserve has been wiped out. Thieu and Ky have just taken power after a year and a half of coups. In Hanoi the Politburo has committed itself to winning before the Americans arrive in strength, and in October its regiments will go after Plei Me.

Inside the held belt the summer is not history's. About 330,000 hectares are titled to about 270,000 families, rent is no longer paid in most districts the government holds, and there the government's arrival no longer means the landlord's [X-001]. About 1,100 village posts stand, two-thirds of the chiefs have come from the list, about 140 district centres are open behind the posts, and the Front's committees have begun to leave the villages around them, gaining the safety of the contested edge at the cost of the villages' daily obedience (F006-4) [X-006]. Outside the belt nothing has changed except the promise.

The army that enters Phase II is the one 1957 built and 1964 began to reform. Its privates and territorials are trained, its NCOs schooled for their grades and its companies led by the educated classes, at the price of 3,300–5,500 of its best sergeants kept as instructors in the years it could least spare them [X-010]. The board has filled about 70 of some 190 battalion commands and relieved its first nine commanders; the rest, and every general, are the generals' choices, and a third of the battalions are still under captains (F007-8) [X-007]. The general reserve came back from Binh Gia and Dong Xoai with more of its men than on any course before this one, and the Front enters Phase II with 10,000–30,000 of the Republic's carbines and the academy's leader's manual (F010-20) [X-004]. The Directorate's plants run at about half capacity under a domestic share four months old, the coast's Route 1 is open after the worst flood in memory, and the Front has an industrial working class in Bien Hoa to organise [X-008].

The command can see the enemy's rear and not his regiments under the canopy or his cadres in the hamlets. The sea route is closed and Sihanoukville open. The gunships have pushed the Front's attacks into the rain, onto posts beyond their net and into the mortar and the mine, and they changed the nights at Nam Dong and Dong Xoai and not the days (EV015-1) [X-015]. The task force, Delta and the district centres have each broken a committee from one file and seen each rebuilt from the region (EVR05-3). The combined command is a channel rather than a command, its orders passing through the prime minister's office and its allies' through Seoul's countersignature and Canberra's casualty estimate (EV012-3). The North has lost its fixed economy and replaced it with drums, diesels, pontoons and a fighter force living in China, and holds three divisions at home against a landing that is not coming (F013-13) [X-013]. The flow south is untouched, and the American decision of July is history's.
