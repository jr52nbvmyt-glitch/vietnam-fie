# Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV is the war of the American withdrawal. US strength peaks at about 539,000 in the first half of 1969 and falls to 474,000 at the end of that year, 336,000 at the end of 1970 and 158,000 at the end of 1971 [H40]. American deaths by hostile action fall with it, from about 9,414 in 1969 [D] to 4,221 in 1970 and 1,380 in 1971 [H40]. The Republic of Vietnam's armed forces grow to replace them: from about 897,000 in 1969 to 968,000 in 1970 and about 1,048,000 in 1971–72, the territorial Regional and Popular Forces growing fastest [H62]. The policy has a name, Vietnamization, and a premise: that the South, given equipment, time and American air power, can hold what the Americans leave.

The shape of the phase is a race between the withdrawal and the South's improvement, run while Hanoi rebuilds. In the countryside, the years 1969–71 are the best the government ever has. The VC, gutted by the 1968 offensives, return to protracted war on a reduced scale; pacification spreads under Phoenix and the territorial forces, into a countryside where the land programme of 1964 has already done most of its work (the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 does not happen, EV000-65); and by most indicators, including the ones the enemy cannot fake, the VC's hold on the population of the Delta and the coastal lowlands weakens. On the frontiers the war goes the other way. The North rebuilds its main forces, extends the Trail and its pipeline, and after Sihanouk's fall loses Sihanoukville but gains much of eastern Cambodia. The United States uses the time it has to strike the sanctuaries it had left alone, secretly from the air over Cambodia from March 1969, on the ground in Cambodia in May–June 1970, and through the ARVN alone in Laos in February–March 1971, and Congress closes the ground option behind each attempt.

At home the war's support is gone. The Moratorium, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, and the publication of the Pentagon Papers mark a country that wants its army home, and an army in Vietnam, after 1969, that is itself coming apart under drugs, racial conflict and attacks on its own officers, waiting to leave a war it no longer expects to win [H]. In Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh dies in September 1969; the collective leadership around Le Duan negotiates secretly with Kissinger and, after the ARVN's defeat in Laos in 1971, decides to try once more for victory by conventional invasion.

In this war the phase follows the same course on the frontiers and in Washington [X-001]. In the countryside it starts further on. The land programme has nothing left to do by 1970 but the communal lands, and about 1,150,000 hectares are titled by the end of 1971 [X-001]. The Commission Against Corruption, which had outlived three governments behind the American lever, loses that lever as the Americans leave, and in July 1971 Thieu puts it under a presidential inspectorate after it opens an inquiry that reaches his own circle (EV001-8) [X-001].

The police and the provinces end the phase as the Commission does: their work below the palace goes on, and their reach upward ends as the American lever lapses (EV002-6). The historical gap narrows, because the Republic builds after 1968 what this war had from 1964.

The enemy's rear is counted through the whole phase. The Cambodian incursion goes in on a map five years in the making and finds the caches in days rather than weeks, though Washington still names COSVN as the objective (EV000-66); Commando Hunt's claims are checked every night against a radar count, and the claim that the trucks destroyed exceeded the trucks that existed never survives a season (EV000-68); and Lam Son 719 goes in with an honest estimate of what waits for it, and fails for the reasons it failed historically (EV000-71).

The bombers' precision reaches the Cambodian base areas in 1969 and has a season of targets there before COSVN's rear services own nothing in the open (EV000-58). Over the Trail it adds a point or two to what Commando Hunt costs the enemy (EV000-68). In Lam Son 719 it holds the bases where the PAVN dug, not the ones it rushed, and teaches I Corps to build its defence around American bombs that will soon go home (EV000-71).

The three provisions of 1964 that govern this memorandum change the phase at three points. The American battalions go home as they came, whole, so the withdrawals do not scatter short-timers through the units that stay; the breakdown of discipline comes on its historical course regardless (EV000-60). The national Phung Hoang file, built since 1964 to the standard of a warrant, is taken into the palace's service with the Board and the Directorate in 1970–71 and consolidated in one building in Saigon for the election (EV002-6). And Phoenix comes before Congress in 1971 with an audited count since 1965 that its critics do not dispute (EV000-72). The selected advisers stay on their eighteen-month tours as their numbers fall, and the territorials they advise are the best-advised part of the RVNAF when the Americans leave.

The officer reforms reach their limit in this phase, and it is the limit their memorandum named: the American lever. As the aid conditions are dropped with Vietnamization, the palace makes the board's general-officer list advisory in January 1970, keeps the board for everything below colonel, and in December 1970 answers a petition of colonels of the classes of 1957–59 by moving about forty of the army's best regimental and battalion commanders to staff and schools (EV007-5). Lam Son 719 goes into Laos weeks later with new commanders in two of the 1st Division's regiments and an Airborne brigade, and fails for the historical reasons, which were decided at corps and in Saigon (EV000-71). Thieu's election of 1971 is run against the background of the colonels' petition, read into the record of the Lower House (EV000-73). The battalions themselves are the best-led the Republic ever had.

Under the withdrawal, the Republic's own plants close the loop that Vietnamization would otherwise have left open: the American equipment handed over in 1969–71 goes into rebuild works and a signal depot that have run since 1966, and by the end of 1971 the RVNAF makes about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, most of its batteries and all of its wire, filled and powered from abroad (EV008-6). In the Delta the subsector advisers hand the call for fire to Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets before they leave (EV008-7). And the Directorate of Defence Industry goes to the palace with the Board, the list, the file and the Commission, keeping its output and losing its honesty (EV002-6).

The schooled sergeant meets the drawdown in both armies. In the American units that stay longest, the sergeant who enforces standards is the man the worst soldiers resent and some of them try to kill, and the schooled sergeant holds more squads together than history's without stopping the decay (EV010-5). In the Republic the gate on NCO promotion is the first of the army's merit rules that the palace breaks, by the National Day decree of 1 November 1970, in the same months it takes the board and the Directorate (EV010-6) [X-010]. The academy survives the advisers, because it was Vietnamese from the start, and the territorial platoon leaders who learned to call fire on a grid call Vietnamese guns when the Americans go (EV008-7).

In this war the home front of 1969–71 has no My Lai and a smaller draft. The Moratorium and the Mobilization of the autumn of 1969 come on their dates without Seymour Hersh's story in their week, and the Peers inquiry, the Calley trial and the national argument of the spring of 1971 do not happen (EV000-63, EV000-72) [X-011]. A quarter of the men the boards would have called have enlisted before their notices came, the boards send about 70,000 fewer notices in 1969 and about 40,000 fewer in 1970, and about one in four of the Army's draftees in the rifle companies of the drawdown chose to be there (EV010-5) [X-011]. None of it changes the withdrawal's schedule, which Washington set in 1969 for its own reasons; it changes the bitterness of the years in which the schedule ran (F011-10, F011-15).

## EV010-5 · The sergeants of the drawdown · 1 Jan 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · US Army and Marine Corps units in I, II and III Corps, especially the 23rd (Americal) Infantry Division at Chu Lai and LZ Baldy, Quang Tin and Quang Nam Provinces; the NCO academies in the United States · NEW · 010,011
*Initiative: neither*

The American NCO corps that fought the drawdown was schooled for its grades to the man and too thin to hold the army it led. The sergeants first class and master sergeants of 1969 were on their second or third tours; the staff sergeants and sergeants of the rifle companies were men of two or three years' service, many of them graduates of the NCO Candidate Course, who had taken the Basic Leader Course's lanes as its last module or at Bien Hoa (EV010-2) [X-010]. In the units that stayed longest, the war they were asked to lead was one their soldiers had stopped believing in. Drug use, racial violence and refusals rose through 1969–71 on their historical course, and the Army and the Marine Corps recorded about 600–800 fraggings and attempted fraggings in those three years, most of them against officers and NCOs [E: commonly cited service figures for 1969–71].

The schooled sergeant was both the man who held a squad together and the man its worst soldiers resented most. On 14 March 1970 Staff Sergeant Raymond L. Ochoa, a platoon sergeant of a battalion of the 196th Infantry Brigade at LZ Baldy, who had passed the Advanced Leader Course and had used its counselling and its standards to take four men of his platoon off the line for heroin, was wounded by a fragmentation grenade rolled under his bunk; he lived, and two of the four were convicted [X-010]. The 23rd Division's inspector general counted in 1970 that the platoons whose sergeants held the course and had led them for six months or more had about a third fewer drug cases and refusals than those whose sergeants had been waived or deferred (F010-18) [X-010]. That is the provision at its true weight: it held some squads together, and it did not stop the decay, which came from the war's politics and the draft rather than from the sergeant (F010-10). The MACV academy at Bien Hoa graduated its last class on 30 June 1971 and was handed to the ARVN as a division school [X-010].

In this war the draft half of that sentence was smaller. A quarter of the men the boards would have called had enlisted before their notices came, and in a rifle company of the 196th Brigade in 1970, of about 40–60 men who were draftees on the historical course, about 10–15 had chosen to come (P011b, EC011-4) [X-011]. The division's inspector general, asked in 1971 to break the 1970 figures down by how men had entered the Army, found the volunteers among the former draftees' cohort about as likely as the volunteers of every year to be on the drug and refusal lists, and about half as likely as the men still drafted; in the platoons that had a schooled sergeant, a battalion that had trained and deployed together and three or four such volunteers, he counted a further tenth to a fifth fewer cases than in Ochoa's platoon (F011-19) [X-011]. It changed no fragging that the record names and saved no squad that the war's politics had already lost. The units that went home in 1971 went home as history's did, from a war their soldiers knew was being given up.

## EV014-7 · The Cambodian folders · 1 Jan 1969 – 30 Jun 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak; Memot and Snoul, Kampong Cham Province; Kratie · NEW · 014
*Initiative: allied*

American teams could enter Cambodia from September 1967, on foot, without tactical air, without a forward air controller overhead and with no exploitation force behind them, and those limits held until the incursion [S3]. Delta put teams across on those terms from 12 September 1967 and ran 61 missions into the base areas in 1969 beside SOG's 454 [S3] [X-014]. The teams walked in from the border at night, lay up under the rubber by day, and counted: ninety-four cache sites, thirty-one of them under permanent roofing, eleven field hospitals, four vehicle parks and the Memot headquarters complex's outer security, which was three concentric belts of bunkers manned by a battalion [X-014]. The Global Hawk's radar had been counting the road traffic from Sihanoukville to those same base areas since 1965 and could not see a cache under rubber (EV003-4, F003-3). The folders the teams built are what the incursion's forces worked from on 29 April 1970, and they are part of why the caches fell in days rather than weeks (EV000-66).

COSVN was never in them. The Central Office was about 2,400 people who moved between bunkers and meeting houses by bicycle, warned of B-52 raids from 1968 by Soviet trawlers reporting the bombers' course, and it went northwest to Kratie on 19 March 1970, six weeks before the incursion crossed the border [S7]. Delta's teams watched the Memot complex for eleven nights in February 1970 and reported what the complex was: a rear-service and signals installation whose senior occupants had left. The provision names COSVN as a mission; the constraint set declined it from the first day and the enemy's own mobility declined it again (EC014-9, F014-20).

## EV000-56 · Nixon takes office; Vietnamization · 20 Jan – Mar 1969 · Washington and Saigon · HIST · 000
Richard Nixon was inaugurated on 20 January 1969 with a promise to end the war with honour and no published plan to do it [H]. His national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, commissioned a survey of the agencies' views of the war, which showed deep disagreement about almost everything except that the South could not yet stand alone [H]. At a National Security Council meeting on 28 January the Secretary of Defense, Melvin Laird, proposed calling the policy "Vietnamizing" the war; its two parts were to strengthen the RVNAF to take over the fighting and to extend pacification [H58]. Henry Cabot Lodge replaced Harriman in Paris [H51]. Laird, who visited Saigon in March, pressed for withdrawals sooner and larger than the military wanted, and made "Vietnamization" the measure of the South's progress [H] [H58]. The Improvement and Modernization programme behind it re-equipped the RVNAF from top to bottom: M16 rifles for the regulars and then the territorial forces, M60 machine guns, artillery, helicopters, aircraft, and the training base to use them [H].

## EV000-57 · The post-Tet offensive · 23 Feb – Mar 1969 · Saigon, Da Nang and installations throughout the South · HIST · 000
On the night of 22–23 February 1969 the PAVN and VC opened a countrywide offensive of attacks by fire and sapper raids against more than a hundred targets, including Saigon, Da Nang, Hue and US bases, the heaviest since 1968 [H]. It was smaller in scale and ambition than Tet: the ground attacks were mainly against bases and firebases, not into the cities, and the population did not rise [H]. It cost the Americans heavily in the first weeks, and it was taken in Washington as a breach of the understanding that had accompanied the bombing halt [H]. Nixon's answer, in the third week of March, was the secret bombing of the Cambodian base areas [H57].

## EV000-58 · Operation Menu · 18 Mar 1969 – 26 May 1970 · Base Areas 350, 351, 352, 353, 609 and 740, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,005,011
*Initiative: allied*

On the night of 18 March 1969, 60 B-52s took off from Guam on a mission ostensibly against targets in South Vietnam; 48 were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, where COSVN was believed to be, and dropped about 2,400 tons that night [H57]. In this war the 48 were Js, and their unguided carpet was half as dense; about 1,600 tons fell in the boxes, and twelve of the aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s aimed at the coordinates the U-2S had measured over five years in the Fishhook: storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop with a generator, and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen trucks unload by night (EV003-4) [X-005]. The campaign that followed, codenamed Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner, Supper and Dessert after the base areas it struck, flew about 3,800 B-52 sorties and dropped 108,823 tons until 26 May 1970 [H57]. Here it dropped about 75,000 tons, a fifth of the sorties carrying JDAM against measured structures [X-005]. It was concealed by dual reporting: an overt channel ordered strikes near the border inside South Vietnam, while a back channel ordered the strikes in Cambodia, whose records were destroyed after each mission; neither the Secretary of the Air Force nor its chief of staff was told [H57].

The measured structures died; the base areas did not. In the first six weeks of Breakfast and Lunch about 300 structures that the U-2S had photographed were destroyed, most of them sheds and workshops in clearings [X-005]. By June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, and the clearings were empty when the U-2S next imaged them; the J's precision had a season's targets and then none (F005-16) [X-005]. The rear services cadres, by the account of a COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970, blamed spies in the base areas and searched for beacons in the forest; they found neither, and did the right thing anyway, which was to own nothing that stood in the open [X-005]. COSVN itself, a mobile headquarters of a few hundred people under the canopy, was never located and never struck, as it was not historically (POS005-1) [H] [X-005].

Sihanouk, whose territory it was and whose government had tolerated the base areas and the supply line from Sihanoukville, did not protest publicly [H]. The *New York Times* reported the bombing on 9 May 1969; Nixon ordered the leak traced, and the telephones of 13 officials and four journalists were tapped [H57]. Historically those wiretaps were the first of the abuses that led to Watergate; in this war there is no Watergate, the burglary of 1972 never happens, and the wiretaps of 1969 are never exposed by an investigation of it (P011a) [X-011]. The bombing forced the PAVN and VC to disperse and camouflage their Cambodian bases, here a few months earlier and more completely, but did not stop their offensives, and its effect on the war in the South is disputed [H57] [X-005].

## EV000-59 · Hamburger Hill · 13–20 May 1969 · Ap Bia Mountain (Hill 937), A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · HIST · 000
In May 1969 the 3rd Brigade of the 101st Airborne Division (Airmobile), under the division commander Major General Melvin Zais, went into the A Shau valley in Operation Apache Snow to destroy the PAVN base areas there [H54]. On Ap Bia Mountain, Hill 937, about 1.9 km from the Laotian border, the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry under Lieutenant Colonel Weldon Honeycutt met the 7th and 8th Battalions of the PAVN 29th Regiment, dug in [H54]. Ten days of frontal assaults followed, with the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry, the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry and the ARVN 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment joining for the final attack on 20 May [H54]. The Americans lost 56 killed, 367 wounded and 7 missing; they counted 630 PAVN dead on and around the hill, many in tunnels, and captured 89 individual and 22 crew-served weapons and 3 prisoners [H54]. On 5 June the hill was abandoned [H54].

"That hill had no military value whatsoever," Zais said [H54]. Senators Edward Kennedy, George McGovern and Stephen Young attacked the battle as senseless; on 27 June *Life* published the photographs of 242 Americans killed in one week [H54]. Abrams turned from "maximum pressure" to a posture designed to keep American casualties down, and Nixon announced the first troop withdrawal within weeks [H54].

## EV000-60 · Midway, the first withdrawals and the Nixon Doctrine · 8 Jun – Dec 1969 · Midway Island; Guam; South Vietnam · HIST-ALT · 000,006
*Initiative: allied*
On 8 June 1969 Nixon met Thieu on Midway Island and announced the first withdrawal of US troops, 25,000 men, to be completed by the end of August [H]. Elements of the 9th Infantry Division left the Mekong Delta in July, and the whole 3rd Marine Division had left I Corps by the end of the year [H] [H58]. On 25 July, at Guam, Nixon set out what became the Nixon Doctrine: the United States would honour its treaty commitments and provide a nuclear shield, but would look to its Asian allies to provide the manpower for their own defence [H]. Further withdrawals were announced in September and December [H]. For the troops still in Vietnam, the withdrawals made every American death harder to justify. Discipline in the US Army declined through 1969–71; drug use, racial violence and "fragging", attacks on officers and NCOs with grenades, became endemic in rear areas and in some combat units [H]. The withdrawals were faster than the RVNAF's improvement, and they ran on a timetable set by American politics, not by the war.

In this war the withdrawals are of units, and they are cleaner for it. Under the individual system the first increment, Keystone Eagle, took the 9th Division's units home with whichever men had the least time left, and filled the units that stayed with the rest, so that the withdrawal broke up every company it touched [H]. Here the 9th Division's battalions and the 3rd Marine Division's had each arrived as units and left as units, whole, on the dates their tours ended; no company that stayed received the short-timers of one that went, and the commanders of the battalions that stayed kept men they had trained [X-006]. The cost was arithmetic: an increment had to be made of units whose tours ended in the window, so the withdrawal schedule set by Washington had to be met by shortening some tours and lengthening none, and about 12,000 men in 1969–70 went home one to four months early with their battalions [X-006]. The breakdown of discipline was not prevented. Fragging, drugs and racial violence rose in 1970–71 in battalions that had trained together, as the best study of the war had led this headquarters to expect; they rose somewhat less inside the rifle companies and as much in the rear (F006-16) [X-006]. Unit rotation preserved the unit and could not preserve the war's purpose in the minds of men who knew it was ending.

## EV000-61 · COSVN Resolution 9 and the death of Ho Chi Minh · Jul – 2 Sep 1969 · COSVN headquarters and Hanoi · HIST-ALT · 000,001,002
In July 1969 COSVN issued Resolution 9, which accepted that the 1968 offensives had not produced the uprising and directed a return to protracted war: small-unit and sapper attacks, economy of force, the rebuilding of the infrastructure, and a campaign against Vietnamization and pacification, while main forces were preserved in the sanctuaries [H]. It was a decision taken in the light of losses and on the movement's own reading of its position, and it set the pattern of the enemy's war in the South until 1971. On 2 September 1969 Ho Chi Minh died in Hanoi [H]. His testament called for the war to continue until the Americans had left and the country was reunified [H]. Power passed to a collective leadership in which Le Duan, as First Secretary, was the strongest figure, with Le Duc Tho, Truong Chinh and Pham Van Dong [H]. Ho's death did not change the policy; it removed the one figure whose authority stood above the factions, and left the militants who had planned Tet in charge.

In this war Resolution 9 is issued on the same date with the same direction [X-001]. Its section on the countryside is different in one respect. On the historical course the Front could still campaign in 1969 against a government that had not given the peasants their land. Here the government had been titling since 1964, and the resolution's instruction to the Delta's party committees was to stop contesting the titles, to treat a titled hamlet the government held at night as lost for the present, and to concentrate cadres and the reduced tax effort on the hamlets the government held only by day (F001-10, F001-13) [X-001]. That was a realistic instruction and a costly admission: it surrendered the issue on which the Front had been built in the Delta, and it left the movement there with nothing to offer the peasant but reunification and the end of the American war [X-001].

The resolution's instruction on the infrastructure was the Front's admission of what the police had done to it. It told the party committees of the Delta and the provinces around Saigon to stop keeping cadres resident in villages with settled police posts, to govern them by visiting cadres who slept elsewhere, to concentrate the reduced effort on the hamlets the government held only by day, and to put the movement's best security cadres into penetration of the Special Branch and of the provincial administration rather than into the villages (F002-2, F002-13) [X-002]. A movement that had lived among its people since 1960 now visited them, and visiting cadres taxed less, recruited less and governed less [X-002].

## EV008-6 · The closed loop · 1 Jul 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · The Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap signal depot; the ARVN depots at Long Binh and Da Nang; the 1st and 3rd Area Logistics Commands · NEW · 008
*Initiative: allied*

Vietnamization handed the RVNAF the equipment of an American army in three years, and in this war the Republic already had the shops to keep it running. From the summer of 1969 the departing American units turned in their vehicles, radios and weapons for transfer, and the Bien Hoa rebuild works and the Go Vap signal depot, which had rebuilt the RVNAF's own engines, carriers and sets at the domestic share since 1966, took the American articles into the same loop: in 1970 the works rebuilt about 4,500 engines, 2,000 transmissions and about 550 M113s and M41s, and the depot about 45,000 radios, most of them PRC-25s and PRC-77s, which the communications plant had built under licence since 1969 (EC008-3, F008-11) [X-008]. The crash depot programme that the historical RVNAF needed in 1970–71, when it found itself owning more equipment than it could repair, did not have to be built, and the American logistics staff that would have built it spent the time on the ports [M008] [X-008].

The loop was tested in the spring of 1971. Lam Son 719 lost about a third of the 1st Armored Brigade's carriers and tanks in Laos, most of them abandoned on Route 9 where no rebuild works could reach them (EV000-71) [H]. Of those that came back damaged, the works at Da Nang and Bien Hoa returned about 60 to the brigade by August, and its squadrons were at about four-fifths of their establishment again by the end of the year [X-008]. The brigade's losses in Laos were history's, and the reason was the operation, not the depot. What the depot changed was how long it took to stand the brigade up again.

By the end of 1971 about two-fifths of the RVNAF's small-arms cartridge cases, about a third of its 105mm bodies, most of its mortar bodies, most of its radio batteries and all of its field wire came from its own plants, filled and powered with American propellant, explosive and semiconductors bought with aid dollars (EC008-2) [X-008]. It was the largest step toward a self-supplying army the Republic ever took, and in 1971 nobody could see it, because the American stocks were still free and still flowing. The Joint General Staff's J-4, which in 1970 would historically have been absorbed by the turnover, spent part of that year drafting the first plan for an RVNAF that would buy its own ammunition with its own money; it was filed and not funded [X-008].

## EV015-6 · The night net changes hands · 1 Jul 1969 – 31 Dec 1971 · Bien Hoa, Binh Thuy, Nha Trang and Da Nang air bases; the posts of the four corps · NEW · 015
*Initiative: allied*

The Ghostriders went home on Vietnamization's schedule, and the night over the posts passed to the 81st Gunship Wing in stages (F015-4, F015-19). The 4th Air Commando Squadron left Bien Hoa in October 1970, the 14th left Nha Trang in December 1971, and the 16th at Da Nang and the 17th at Ubon stayed into 1972 [X-015]. The 817th Squadron took the night over III and IV Corps from October 1970 with a detachment of five aircraft at Binh Thuy for the lower Delta; the 819th at Nha Trang held the coast of II Corps from the spring of 1969; and the 821st at Da Nang reached strength at the end of 1969 and shared I Corps with the 16th [X-015].

At its best, in 1970–71, the wing flew about 700–850 sorties a month at about 55% of aircraft operational, against the Ghostriders' 1,300–1,500 at the peak of 1967–68 [X-015]. It covered about two-thirds of the posts the Ghostriders had covered, the radar beacons on the district towns and the most-attacked posts gave it the coast's monsoon nights the Ghostriders never had, and its answers came in Vietnamese (EC015-8) [X-015]. In the north-east monsoon of 1969–70 the 819th answered about 60% of the posts' calls in Binh Dinh and Phu Yen on cloudy nights, against almost none in history's coast and few in this war's before 1968 [X-015]. The posts it did not cover — the islands of An Xuyen and Bac Lieu beyond Binh Thuy's reach, the Highland posts of Kontum and Darlac — were attacked more often as the Americans left, and held about as history's did [X-015].

The wing lost four aircraft in the three years: one to 12.7mm fire over Kien Phong in March 1970, one to a 37mm battery over Cambodia in August 1970 (EV015-7), one in a landing accident at Nha Trang in the monsoon of 1970, and one to a mortar on the ramp at Da Nang in 1971 [X-015]. Its crews were the best-educated men in the VNAF and the most often sought by the airlines; about one in six left the wing in 1971, and the radar technicians, the scarcest of them, were held by an order of the Joint General Staff that barred their transfer (F015-8) [X-015].

## EV000-62 · The secret Paris channel opens · 4 Aug 1969 – Feb 1970 · Paris · HIST · 000
In June 1969 the National Liberation Front formed the Provisional Revolutionary Government of the Republic of South Vietnam, to claim governmental status at the talks [H51] [H]. On 4 August Kissinger began secret talks in Paris with Hanoi's delegation, first with Xuan Thuy, and from February 1970 with Le Duc Tho, a member of the Politburo [H51] [H]. The formal sessions at the Hotel Majestic became a forum for public statements; the substance moved to the secret channel. The core issues were fixed from the start and remained so for three years: Hanoi demanded the withdrawal of all US forces and the removal of Thieu as the price of a settlement, and would not discuss the withdrawal of its own forces from the South; Washington would not overthrow its ally [H51].

## EV000-63 · The Moratorium; no My Lai to reveal · 15 Oct – 24 Nov 1969 · Washington and the United States · HIST-ALT · 000,011
*Initiative: neither*
On 15 October 1969 the Moratorium brought hundreds of thousands of Americans out against the war in cities across the country, and on 15 November the Mobilization brought a march of several hundred thousand to Washington [H]. On 3 November Nixon appealed on television to "the great silent majority" to support his policy of withdrawal on his terms, and the speech was well received [H]. Historically, on 13 November Seymour Hersh published the story of the My Lai massacre, the photographs taken by the Army photographer Ronald Haeberle appeared on 20 November, and on 24 November the Army appointed Lieutenant General William Peers to investigate the cover-up [H47].

In this war there was nothing to publish. No massacre had been committed at Son My on 16 March 1968 (EV000-46), Ridenhour had written no letters, and Haeberle's photographs of Task Force Barker's sweep showed soldiers searching empty bunkers [X-011]. The Mobilization of 15 November marched in a week whose news was the President's speech and the first troop withdrawals, not a massacre (P011c). The antiwar movement's case was the war itself, the casualty lists of 1969 at about 9,400 American dead [H40] and the lack of an end, and it did not need My Lai to make it; it lost the war's single most powerful image of what the fighting did to the villages, and its moral argument was weaker for the loss while its political argument was untouched (F011-5) [X-011]. In the press the incidents that history overshadowed with My Lai, the free-fire areas of Quang Ngai and the refugee camps on Route 1, were reported as they were found, one at a time and smaller [X-011]. No Peers inquiry was appointed. The Army was not made to look at the command climate of the Americal Division, and MACV's Inspector General's proposal of 1970 for a theater-wide inquiry into how the body count shaped the conduct of operations was not taken up in Washington, because without a scandal no one above MACV wanted it (EC011-7, F011-11) [X-011]. Gallup's share of Americans calling the war a mistake had been 53% in August 1968 and averaged about 55% in 1969–70 [M011·S1]; that verdict was in before November 1969, and it was the verdict the President's withdrawal answered.

## EV002-6 · The Board and the Directorate in the palace · Jan 1970 – Sep 1971 · Saigon and the provinces · NEW · 002,006,008
*Initiative: allied*
The Provincial Administration Selection Board had outlived four governments because each had found it cheaper to keep than to break, and because a province chief appointed outside the list could not sign a budget, a title or a counterpart-fund disbursement while the Aid Compliance Board stood behind the rule [M002]. By 1970 the American lever was lapsing with the withdrawal (EV001-8), and Thieu, preparing for the election of 1971, wanted the provinces [X-002]. He did not abolish the Board. As the terms of its two general officers expired in January 1970 he replaced them with officers of his own staff, and from that spring his Prime Minister, General Tran Thien Khiem, appointed the third name on the list for about half the province vacancies and for most of those in the provinces with the largest electorates [X-002]. The list still excluded the incompetent and the bought; it no longer excluded the loyal mediocre [X-002].

The Inspectorate's reports, which had gone to the Board and the Commission since 1964, were routed through the presidential Inspectorate General from July 1971 under the decree that subordinated the Commission (EV001-8) [X-002]. The National Police had followed the same road earlier: its Director General had been the President's appointee in fact since 1966 (EV000-26), and in 1970–71 the Special Branch's Saigon headquarters spent a growing share of its effort on the opposition, the An Quang Buddhists and the Ky camp, as historical police chiefs always had (F002-16) [X-002]. The rural posts, the census and the district sections went on much as before, because they were too many and too local for the palace to want them [X-002]. In September 1971 province chiefs campaigned for Thieu as they did on the historical course; the difference was that about two-thirds of them were men who had passed an examination and served a district first, and the villages they governed were governed better than they were represented [X-002].

The palace took Phung Hoang's national file the same way, without abolishing anything. In February 1970 Khiem, who was Minister of the Interior as well as Prime Minister, moved the national centre from the Directorate of National Police to the Central Intelligence Organization's building and replaced Hieu, who resigned rather than serve under the new chairman, with a Military Security colonel of the President's staff [X-006]. The file held about 210,000 names by then, every one entered to the three-source standard and many with fingerprints and photographs, and it now served a second purpose: from the spring of 1970 the national centre vetted every candidate for province and district office and every officer above major against it, and its analysts were asked to chart the An Quang Buddhists' committees and the Ky camp's officers by the same method they had used on the Front [X-006]. For the election of 1971 the provincial copies of the file were called in to Saigon "for consolidation", and about two-thirds of the PIOCCs were left with district extracts; from September 1971 the complete national file existed in one building (F006-25) [X-006]. Ashby's successor protested in writing in October 1970 and was told, correctly, that the decree of 1964 placed the committee under the Minister of the Interior [X-006]. The district centres went on working the Front as before, and their audited count did not fall; what had changed was who else the file was for.

The Directorate of Defence Industry went the same way in the same months, and for the same reason. In February 1970, as the Aid Compliance Board was reduced to an office reporting to the Embassy, Thieu replaced Vuong Dinh Toan with Brigadier General Vu Xuan Dac of his own military staff, and the Directorate's contracts, its scrap and import licences and its deferment list became the palace's to allot (F008-14) [X-008]. The domestic share survived, because the Joint General Staff now wanted the plants for its own reasons and the Americans were leaving, and the lines ran as before (EV008-6). What did not survive was the acceptance the Phu Cat lot had made honest (EV008-5): from 1970 the proof-firing at Long Thanh was signed more often than fired, the share of lots rejected fell from about one in sixteen to about one in a hundred, and the field reports of duds and misfires from domestic mortar rounds roughly doubled by 1971 [X-008]. The scrap brass of the arsenal's case lines went to Singapore through a firm whose partners were related to the President's staff, and the Commission, under the Inspectorate from July 1971, could not open the file (EV001-8) [X-008].

Taken together, the five institutions this headquarters built behind an American lever in 1964 fell to that lever's lapse in eighteen months: the Commission, the Selection Board for the provinces, the general-officer list, the national Phung Hoang file and the Directorate (EV001-8, EV007-5) [X-008]. Each lost its reach upward and kept its work below the palace, and the net is the same in each case. The posts, the chiefs' experience, the battalion commanders, the district centres and the plants' output stayed with the Republic; the honesty above them went to the palace. Of the five, the Directorate lost least in output and most in what output is worth.

## EV007-5 · The list in the palace and the colonels' petition · 12 Jan 1970 – 20 Jan 1971 · The Presidential Palace and the Joint General Staff, Saigon; the corps and division headquarters · NEW · 007
*Initiative: allied*

The ranked list for generals lost its lever in the same month as the Selection Board for the provinces, and in the same way. On 12 January 1970, with the aid conditions of 1964 being folded into Vietnamization's programmes and the Aid Compliance Board reduced to an office that reported to the Embassy rather than to this headquarters, Thieu decreed that the board's general-officer list would be "advisory to the Supreme Commander" [X-007]. He did not abolish the board, which went on choosing battalion and regimental commanders, promoting captains and majors and relieving for cause below the rank of colonel; it had become the army's machinery, and he had no other (EV002-6) [X-007]. Above it the divisions and the corps returned to the palace. In 1970 four division commands were filled, and none of them from the list's first name (F007-7) [X-007].

The colonels answered. The officers of the classes of 1957–59, who had been promised a career on merit when they were captains, were by 1970 lieutenant colonels and colonels commanding regiments, serving as division chiefs of staff and teaching at Dalat, and about 30 of them signed a petition to the Chief of the Joint General Staff in September asking that the list be restored, drafted by Colonel Nguyen Dac Khiet, commanding a regiment of the 9th Division [X-007]. It was a petition, not a plot. The Military Security Service and the Central Intelligence Organization reported it to the palace as a "colonels' group" with contacts among Ky's supporters and Minh's, which was true of four of its signers, and with the intention of a coup, which no evidence ever showed (F007-14) [X-007].

Thieu acted in December, before the dry season. Between 5 December 1970 and 20 January 1971 about 40 regimental and battalion commanders, the petition's signers and the officers the CIO's report placed near them, were moved to staff posts, the schools and the provinces' sector headquarters, nine of them in I Corps; two of the 1st Division's regimental commanders and the commander of an Airborne brigade were among them [X-007]. Khiet went to Dalat as an instructor. Colonel Bui Quang Trieu, who had commanded a regiment of the 5th Division since 1968 and had refused to sign, was kept in command and transferred to the new 3rd Division forming in Quang Tri in 1971, where the palace wanted a commander whose loyalty its report had cleared [X-007]. The petition's effect in Saigon was the one the decree's critics had feared from its first day: an educated officer corps whose promotion was blocked had become, in the palace's eyes, a faction, and the palace had treated it as one.

## EV008-7 · The handover of the call at Long My · 1 Feb 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · Long My District, Chuong Thien Province; the Vi Thanh sector operations centre; the district's territorial artillery platoon · NEW · 008,010,015
*Initiative: allied*

When the Americans left the districts of the Delta, the call for help stayed behind, and Long My is the place to see it. Major Huynh Cong Tri, a list chief who had held the district since 1966, had forty-one PF posts and two Regional Force companies along the Cai Lon and the roads to Vi Thanh, every post with a set on the district net and every set made at Go Vap [X-008]. Since 1967, under the rules that came out of Cai Lay (EV008-4), each post's first call had gone to a gun: until 1970 most often to an American battery or helicopter fire team through the subsector adviser's radio, and from February 1970 to the district's own two-gun 105mm territorial artillery platoon, formed that month on howitzers rebuilt at Bien Hoa and fed with Bien Hoa's rounds (EC008-10) [X-008].

Major Daniel R. Kearsley, the selected adviser who held the subsector from 1969, spent his last year handing the call over (P006c). Every post's concentrations were re-plotted for the district platoon and the 21st Division's guns at Vi Thanh; the post commanders called them in Vietnamese, on Vietnamese sets, to Vietnamese gunners, with Kearsley listening and not speaking; and from September 1970 the American fire teams were called only through the district operations room, at Tri's request [X-008]. On the night of 23–24 February 1971, two months before Kearsley left, a company of the U Minh 10th Battalion came against the post at Xeo Mat at 0110; the post called at 0112, the district platoon fired its first round at 0117, and by 0140 it had put about sixty rounds on the post's three concentrations and the company had withdrawn, leaving four dead in the canal [X-008]. Nobody in the chain was American.

The handover worked because what it handed over was Vietnamese at both ends: the set, the battery, the gun and the round. It did not work for anything that had been American at one end. The B-52Js and the coordinate cell that had broken the sieges of the larger war were not handed to anyone, and no district could call them (POS006-3, POS005-2). When the advisers left Long My in June 1971, the district kept every post it had and the Front kept the canal at night between them, as it had before (F008-12) [X-008]. Tri's returns, which kept all forty-one posts through the offensive of 1972, began in that handover (EVMT01-2).

The post commanders who called the district platoon in Vietnamese had all passed the platoon leader's course at the Chuong Thien and Phong Dinh centres, which taught a sergeant to call fire on a grid fixed by map and compass, and on the night at Xeo Mat Sergeant Danh Son, a Khmer Krom platoon leader of the post, gave the district platoon its adjustment in two corrections (EC010-2) [X-010]. The course was the American one, and it taught the American habit of fixing the enemy and calling the guns (F010-24). At Long My the habit found a Vietnamese gun at the other end, which is the only place in this war where the dependence the course carried down to the squad was answered by the Republic's own fire [X-010].

In this war Long My had a second answer to a call from January 1970, and it was Vietnamese too. The 817th's detachment at Binh Thuy put a Spooky II over Chuong Thien most nights, and each district town of the province carried a radar beacon on a Go Vap battery from which the gunship could fire on a post's concentrations through cloud (EC015-8) [X-015]. At Xeo Mat on the night of 23–24 February 1971 the district platoon fired first, at 0117; the Spooky II was over the post at 0131 and found the withdrawing company of the 10th Battalion in the canal and on the dike beyond it, and the post's four dead in the canal became about fifteen in the morning [X-015]. Sergeant Danh Son talked to the gunship's fire-control officer in Vietnamese on the same set he had used for the guns. The district guns and the gunship were both the Republic's, and Tri kept every post he had; between them, the canal at night was still the Front's.

## EV000-64 · Sihanouk falls; the PAVN turns on Cambodia · 18 Mar – Apr 1970 · Phnom Penh and eastern Cambodia · HIST · 000
On 18 March 1970, while Sihanouk was abroad, the Cambodian National Assembly deposed him, and General Lon Nol became the head of a government hostile to the Vietnamese communists [H55]. Lon Nol closed the port of Sihanoukville to the supplies that had reached the PAVN and VC base areas through it [H55]. From 29 March the PAVN attacked the Cambodian army and within weeks overran much of eastern and northeastern Cambodia, protecting its bases and opening the country to the Khmer Rouge, whom it armed and trained [H55]. Cambodia, a sanctuary since 1965, became a battlefield. The loss of Sihanoukville threw the whole burden of supplying the southern half of South Vietnam onto the Trail, which in turn was extended through Laos and northeastern Cambodia.

## EV000-65 · Land to the Tiller · 26 Mar 1970 · Republic of Vietnam · CANCELLED · 000,001
The Land to the Tiller law of 26 March 1970 is not enacted in this war, because there is nothing left for it to do (F001-7) [X-001]. On the historical course it limited holdings of rice land to 15 hectares, gave tenanted land to the tenants who tilled it, confirmed title for peasants who had received land from the Viet Cong, and cost 441 million dollars, 339 million of them American [H63]; historians debate whether it came "too little, too late" [H63]. Here the decree of 1 January 1964 had done all of that six years earlier, with a lower ceiling, and by March 1970 about 1,050,000 hectares had been titled to about 800,000 families [M001] [X-001]. What happens instead on 26 March 1970 is a smaller act: Thieu signs a decree converting the remaining provisional titles to final ones and extending the programme to the communal rice lands of the villages, the last large category of tenanted land, about 200,000 hectares let each year by village councils [X-001]. It draws no headlines in Washington, because the programme it completes had been reporting monthly for six years. Its effects had accrued over those years, not over the last five of the war, in the attitude of the rural population of the Delta towards a government that had given them something before it asked them for anything (F001-17).

## EV000-66 · The Cambodian incursion · 29 Apr – 30 Jun 1970 · The Parrot's Beak and the Fishhook, eastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003
On 29 April 1970 ARVN forces of III and IV Corps under Lieutenant General Do Cao Tri attacked into the Parrot's Beak west of Saigon, and on 1 May the US 1st Cavalry Division and 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment, with ARVN airborne units, attacked into the Fishhook north of it; the 25th and 4th Infantry Divisions followed [H55]. In this war the attack went in on a map. Five years of the Sihanoukville count and the U-2S's radar change detection had located the depots where the convoys stopped, the road net that joined them and the clearings they hid in, and the 1st Cavalry's brigades were landed on the largest of them on the first two days rather than finding them by chance [X-003]. The great complex that the 1st Cavalry found on 4 May historically, and that the troops called "the City", was its objective on 1 May here [H] [X-003].

Nixon announced the incursion on television on 30 April as an attack on COSVN [H] [H55]. MACV had told CINCPAC and the Joint Chiefs on 24 April that the U-2S had heard COSVN's transmitters move west and north out of the Fishhook from about 19 April, and advised against naming it as an objective [X-003]. The speech was written in Washington, and it named it [X-003]. COSVN had moved before the attack [H55]. The PAVN and VC mostly avoided battle, leaving rearguards, and withdrew westward [H55].

In the base areas the allies found the logistics of the war in the South: about 22,900 individual and 2,500 crew-served weapons, 7,000–8,000 tons of rice, about 1,800 tons of ammunition, 431 vehicles and 55 tons of medical supplies [H55]. Here most of it was found in the first ten days rather than through May and June, and the total was larger by about a tenth, because the depots on the map were searched first and the time left over went to the ones that were not [X-003]. The allies claimed 11,369 PAVN and VC killed and 2,509 captured [H55]; I make no adjustment to those claims, which carry the usual inflation (A13). US forces were out by 30 June; the ARVN stayed on [H55].

On 4 May Ohio National Guardsmen shot dead four students at Kent State University, and protests closed hundreds of campuses [H55] [H]. On 30 June the Senate passed the Cooper–Church amendment barring US ground forces from Cambodia [H55]. The incursion bought time: the loss of the caches and of Sihanoukville delayed the PAVN's recovery in III and IV Corps, and the southern half of the country was quieter for more than a year. It did not destroy the sanctuary. The PAVN rebuilt its supply routes, seized Attapeu and Saravane in Laos, opened a "Liberation Route" through northeastern Cambodia to the Mekong, and in 1972 would attack An Loc from Cambodia [H55]. The radar watched the Liberation Route being built from the summer of 1970, as it had watched Sihanoukville; it could not stop it either.

## EV015-7 · Route 7 and the 817th over Cambodia · 10 May – 30 Sep 1970 · Route 7 from Krek to Snuol and Route 13 north to Kratie, Kampong Cham and Kratie Provinces, Cambodia; Bien Hoa air base · NEW · 015

When the incursion opened the Cambodian roads to the air, the Joint General Staff sent the 817th's gunships over them, and the war taught the wing what the dossier had said (EC015-10) [X-015]. From 10 May six Spooky IIs a night flew Route 7 and Route 13 in support of the ARVN's task forces and then of Lon Nol's battalions at Snuol and Kratie. The convoys of the PAVN 7th Division and the rear services of the B-2 Front moved at night on these roads in the open, and in the first month the wing's video counted about 140 trucks destroyed [X-015]. The calls from the Cambodian army came in French and Khmer through ARVN liaison officers, and twice in June the gunships fired on Cambodian positions given as enemy.

The roads were defended. The PAVN moved 12.7mm and 37mm guns onto Route 7 in June, and on the night of 14 August Spooky 34, orbiting at 7,000 feet over a convoy near Snuol, was hit by a 37mm battery and went down in the rubber east of the town; four of its thirteen crewmen were recovered by an ARVN Ranger company the next day [X-015]. On 2 September Khiem asked the Joint General Staff to withdraw the squadron to the Delta, where the posts needed it and the guns were few, and on 30 September it came home [X-015]. ## EV000-67 · Fire Support Base Ripcord · 1–23 Jul 1970 · Fire Support Base Ripcord, near the A Shau valley, Thua Thien Province · HIST · 000
In the spring of 1970 the 101st Airborne Division reopened Fire Support Base Ripcord on the eastern edge of the A Shau valley for an offensive against the PAVN base areas [H61]. From 1 July the PAVN 324B Division, with its 803rd Regiment at Ripcord, its 29th Regiment on the nearby hills and a battalion of the 6th Regiment of the Tri-Thien command in support, besieged the base, held by the 2nd Battalion, 506th Infantry of the 3rd Brigade under Colonel Ben Harrison [H61]. On 18 July a CH-47 hit by small-arms fire crashed into the ammunition storage area, killing four and destroying six M102 howitzers and 2,238 rounds of 105mm ammunition [H61]. The command judged the base indefensible and evacuated it on 23 July under fire; the battalion commander, Lieutenant Colonel Andre Lucas, was among the last killed and received the Medal of Honor posthumously [H61]. The Americans lost 75 killed and claimed 422 PAVN dead [H61]. It was the last major battle between US ground forces and the PAVN [H61]. Whether it crippled the PAVN in the region, as Harrison believed, or achieved the PAVN's purpose by forcing the Americans out, is disputed [H61].

## EV000-68 · Commando Hunt and the growth of the Trail · 10 Oct 1970 – Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle and northeastern Cambodia · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005,015
*Initiative: both*

The dry-season phases of Commando Hunt were the main US effort against the North's logistics after 1968 [H53]. Phase V, from 10 October 1970 to 30 April 1971, claimed 16,266 trucks destroyed, a figure Seventh Air Force itself lowered to 11,000; in Phase VII the AC-130E gunships were credited with 7,335 of 10,689 trucks claimed [H53]. The CIA estimated that the entire North Vietnamese truck inventory was about 6,000, and a Defense Intelligence Agency model reached zero trucks remaining fourteen times in 1969 [H53]. Over the campaign the Air Force claimed 51,000 trucks and 3,400 anti-aircraft guns destroyed [H53].

In this war a second ledger stood beside the claims from 1969, and it was kept by the radar (EV000-55). Its Phase V figure was about 4,500–5,500 trucks destroyed or disabled, and its estimate of net loss to the throughput was about 8–10 percent, against the phases' own historical estimate falling to about 2 percent [H53] [X-003]. The DIA model's zero did not reach the Secretary of Defense's desk in 1969 because the radar showed, every night, the trucks that the model said did not exist [X-003]. The claims went on being made, by the fighter-bombers and the forward air controllers more than by the gunships, whose own video was counted beside the radar from 1965 (EV015-3); a burning truck at night is easy to count twice, and the gunship's camera was the one witness that did not. What changed was that no one in Washington or Saigon could believe them for long [X-003].

The J and its weapons were a smaller part of this war than its planners had expected, and the reason is in the panhandle's geography. By 1970 the cell's folders for the panhandle held about 4,000 crossings, of which on any night perhaps a third were in use; the J cut what it could measure, and the Quickstrike fields on the Xe Kong and the Se San below Attopeu kept the steel pontoons and motor barges off the rivers, so the fuel drums floated down them on bamboo rafts instead (F005-4, F005-5) [X-005]. The petroleum pipeline from Vinh toward the A Shau was the one target in the panhandle that could not move; each of its pumping stations was measured and cut with GBU-31s, about eighty times between October 1970 and the end of 1971, and each was bypassed within two to four days by a length of flexible pipe laid around the crater [X-005]. The radar's estimate is that the J's work raised the net loss to the throughput in Phase V by about one or two points, from about 8 to about 10 percent at the season's peak [X-005].

The Trail grew regardless. After the PAVN's offensives in Laos in 1970 its corridor widened from about 30 to about 90 miles; the PAVN built more than 3,000 km of roads and paths with bulldozers and graders, and floated fuel drums down the rivers [H53]. The U-2S's radar mapped each new road within days of its cutting, and the Global Hawk counted its traffic; the count rose through 1971 to about 150–200 southbound truck movements a night at the dry season's peak [X-003]. It is the clearest case in the war of a metric improving without the war improving (A13): the trucks destroyed and the crossings cut rose, and what arrived in the South rose with them. In this war that sentence was written in 1969 by this headquarters, from its own counts, and not by historians. Over the war more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos [H53].

In this war the gunships of Commando Hunt were the 17th Squadron's Ghostriders, five years on the panhandle by the dry season of 1970–71, and their record was the campaign's most honest and its most sobering. Their video counted about 3,000–3,800 trucks destroyed or disabled in Phase V, about two-thirds of the radar's total, at about 2.5–3.5 trucks a sortie [X-015]. They raised the radar's estimate of the net loss to the throughput at the season's peak by about two to three points, to about 10–13 percent [X-015]. They did it against guns that had been on the Trail a year longer than history's: two Ghostriders were lost over the panhandle in the dry season of 1970–71, one to a 57mm battery near Ban Bak and one to an SA-2 fired from across the border north of the DMZ in February 1971, with eleven of their eighteen crewmen [X-015]. The Trail grew regardless, as told above.

## EV010-6 · The National Day decree and the gate · 1 Nov 1970 – 30 Jun 1971 · The Presidential Palace and the Joint General Staff, Saigon; the National NCO Academy at Dong De, Nha Trang; the corps and division headquarters · NEW · 010
*Initiative: allied*

The gate on NCO promotion was the first of the army's merit rules that the palace broke by decree, and it broke it for the soldiers' gratitude. On 1 November 1970, National Day, President Thieu promoted every enlisted man of the RVNAF one grade, regular and territorial, without exception for the courses, as the government had done on national occasions before (M010·S11) [X-010]. The decree came in the same months as the general-officer list's demotion to advice and the Directorate's capture (EV007-5, EV002-6), and it was judged in Saigon as they were, as a matter of the President's gift. It made about 40,000–60,000 men sergeants or senior sergeants without the course for the grade they now held [X-010].

The training command, under Brigadier General Phan Van Huong and with Master Sergeant First Class Lam Quang Phuc's academy behind it, did not contest the stripes. It contested the next promotion: on 20 November it ruled, under the 1957 regulation the decree had not repealed, that no man promoted by the decree could be promoted again until he had passed the course for the grade the decree had given him, and it opened the academy's second shift to them [X-010]. The Joint General Staff let the ruling stand because the corps commanders, who wanted the stripes for their men, did not care about the next board. By June 1971 about half the men the decree had made sergeants had passed their course, and the other half held a rank they had not been schooled for and could not rise beyond [X-010]. The gate survived the palace as a Vietnamese rule, enforced by the training command and the boards below colonel, after the American lever behind the board, the Directorate and the Commission had lapsed (F010-5, F010-16). Of the four institutions that fell to the palace in 1970–71, it was the one whose damage was repaired within a year, because nobody in the palace wanted it and nobody in the army needed it gone (DIRMT01-5) [X-010]. The academy's last American advisers left Dong De in March 1971; Rourke's successor reported that the course was taught as he had found it.

## EV000-69 · The Son Tay raid · 21 Nov 1970 · Son Tay prison camp, about 37 km west of Hanoi · CANCELLED · 014
*Initiative: neither*

The raid of 21 November 1970 was not flown in this war, and the reason is the reconnaissance that the historical operation did not have.

Planning began on 9 May 1970 and the joint task force under Brigadier General LeRoy J. Manor and Colonel Arthur D. Simons trained through the summer at Eglin, as historically [H59]. On 14 October, with the operation six weeks out, MACV-JSOC proposed what no American force had been able to offer in 1970 and this one could: two men of G Squadron on the ground. They went in on the night of 21 October by an HH-3E flying a Trail infiltration profile, lay on the wooded slope 1,400 metres southeast of the camp for three nights with a thermal sight and a satellite radio, and reported on 24 October that the compound held a guard element of perhaps forty men, that the cells were unoccupied, that no cooking was done for prisoners and that no prisoner was taken to the wells, which were dry and partly collapsed [X-014]. The prisoners had been moved on 14 July 1970 to the camp the prisoners themselves called Camp Faith, about 24 km closer to Hanoi [S5]. The Joint Chiefs cancelled the operation on 2 November 1970.

What the cancellation saved is easy to count: 116 aircraft committed, 56 Special Forces soldiers landed 37 km from Hanoi, an HH-3E deliberately crashed inside the compound, an F-105G lost to an SA-2, two raiders wounded and about 42 PAVN guards killed [S5] [H59]. What it cost is the part worth recording, because it was a cost nobody weighed at the time. Historically the raid failed in its purpose and succeeded in another: Hanoi read it as proof that its camps in the countryside could be reached, consolidated its prisoners into the central prisons in Hanoi over the following months, and many of the prisoners who came home in 1973 said their treatment and their morale improved from that winter [H59]. In this war the raid is not flown, the consolidation does not happen, and the American prisoners stay dispersed in the camps outside Hanoi through 1971 and 1972 under the regime they had before — and they never learn that anyone came for them (F014-15). A better reconnaissance capability cancelled an operation whose military result was nil and whose moral result was not.

## EV000-70 · The Cooper–Church amendment · 29 Dec 1970 · Washington · HIST · 000
After the House had rejected the version passed by the Senate in June, the Cooper–Church amendment was enacted in December 1970 as part of the Supplementary Foreign Assistance Act and signed on 29 December [H55] [H56]. It barred US ground troops and advisers from operations in Laos and Cambodia [H55] [H56]. It wrote into law the sanctuary constraint that had been policy since 1965, at the moment the administration was planning to attack the Trail on the ground. Any operation into Laos would now be the ARVN's alone, without the American advisers who called in the air support and coordinated the helicopters on which ARVN operations depended.

## EV014-11 · Cooper–Church takes the teams out · 29 Dec 1970 – 31 Dec 1971 · The Laotian panhandle; the Cambodian base areas; Tan Son Nhut · NEW · 014
*Initiative: neither*

The provision's cross-border work ended by Act of Congress, five years and three months after it began, and it ended for reasons that had nothing to do with it. The Cooper–Church amendment of 29 December 1970 barred American ground troops and advisers from Laos and Cambodia (EV000-70) [H]. MACV's lawyers read it as covering reconnaissance teams, CINCPAC agreed in January 1971, and Vance pulled the last two Delta teams out of the Laotian panhandle on 19 January and cancelled the February programme into Base Areas 353 and 609 [X-014].

The timing could hardly have been worse for the thing the teams were best at. Lam Son 719 crossed into Laos on 8 February 1971 with ARVN divisions and no American ground element of any kind, against the terrain and the 559th Group's security forces that Delta's teams had been watching since 1965. The folders existed; the men who made them could not go. What the ARVN had instead was imagery, the panhandle radar watch and a Vietnamese reconnaissance effort of its own (EV000-71, EV003-3). Through the rest of 1971 the squadrons raided inside the South at about 30 a month, took district cadre, and ran reconnaissance in the base areas of the South; the 81st Special Group's second squadron stood up at Long Thanh in May (EV014-8).

The event is in the record because of what it says about the provision's whole class. The cross-border reconnaissance was the one thing Delta did that nothing else in this war could do, it was never authorised by anything more durable than an ambassador's discretion, and it was taken away in a sentence of a defence appropriations bill written about a different problem (F014-22).

## EV000-71 · Lam Son 719 · 8 Feb – 25 Mar 1971 · Route 9 from Khe Sanh to Tchepone, the Laotian panhandle (Base Areas 604 and 611) · HIST-ALT · 000,003,005,007
*Initiative: allied*

In Dewey Canyon II, beginning at the end of January, US forces reopened Route 9 to Khe Sanh inside South Vietnam [H56]. On 8 February ARVN I Corps under Lieutenant General Hoang Xuan Lam attacked west along Route 9 into Laos towards Tchepone, with the 1st Infantry Division, the Airborne Division, the Marine Division, the 1st Armored Brigade and the 21st and 39th Ranger Battalions, to cut the Trail at Base Areas 604 and 611 in the dry season's peak [H56]. US Army helicopters flew more than 160,000 sorties in support and B-52s 1,358 sorties with 32,000 tons [H56]; no American advisers could go with the ARVN units [H56]. The PAVN, which had expected the attack, committed the B-70 Corps with the 304th, 308th and 320th Divisions, and the 2nd and 324B Divisions, with tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery [H56]. MACV J-2's estimate on 4 February, built from the U-2S's signals and the panhandle watch, put the enemy's strength within reach of Tchepone at about 35,000–40,000 men and rising, about twice the historical estimate given to the planners; the operation went ahead on its date [H] [X-003].

In this war the operation carried a siege box with it. Sorrell's detachment at Quang Tri, a dozen Americans who could not cross the border, received the Global Hawk's imagery of every firebase north and south of Route 9 each night and passed measured positions to the Js on Guam and at U-Tapao; the ARVN battalions on the hills passed nothing, because they had no means to give a coordinate the bombers could use, and no American advisers were there to give it for them (F005-20) [M005] [X-005]. The box worked where the enemy dug and failed where he closed. At Fire Support Base Lolo and at Sophia, south of the road, the PAVN 2nd Division's regiments dug in around the hills from late February, their trenches were imaged and struck each night at 400–700 metres, and the bases held until they were evacuated in the withdrawal [X-005]. North of the road, Ranger North and Ranger South fell on 20–21 February and Fire Support Base 31 on 25 February much as they fell historically, to infantry and tanks of the 308th and 320th Divisions that came straight at the wire in daylight and were inside 400 metres before they could be measured; on 25 February the radar tracked about twenty tanks forming up north of the hill at about 0900, strike aircraft destroyed six before they reached it, and the base fell that afternoon all the same (F005-6) [H] [X-003] [X-005].

On 6 March, in the largest helicopter assault of the war, the 2nd Regiment of the 1st Division was lifted towards Tchepone and entered the abandoned town on 7 March, and from 9 March the withdrawal began [H56]. It became a retreat under pressure, with the Marines' Fire Support Base Delta falling on 22 March and the images of ARVN soldiers clinging to helicopter skids reaching the world [H56] [H]. Along Route 9 the radar showed the PAVN columns closing on the retreating units, and the Js with GBU-38s were put on the anti-aircraft guns the PAVN had dug in around the landing zones, which the cell measured each night; about 110 gun positions were destroyed in the last two weeks, and I estimate that the US Army lost about a sixth fewer helicopters than the historical 108 destroyed [H56] [X-005].

The last ARVN units were out by 25 March; Khe Sanh was abandoned again on 6 April [H56]. The Americans lost 215 killed, 1,149 wounded and 38 missing [H56]. South Vietnamese losses are given from 1,146 to 8,843 killed, a range that is itself a measure of the dispute; Saigon claimed 19,360 PAVN killed and Hanoi admits 2,163 killed and 6,176 wounded [H56]. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses, which were decided by the landing zones and the command, not by what anyone could see or strike [X-005]. Nixon said on 7 April that "Vietnamization has succeeded" [H56]. Hanoi read it otherwise, and the traffic on the Trail rose soon after [H56]. The ARVN's I Corps read the operation in a way history's did not: the bases it had held were the bases where the American bombs had come every two hours, and it began to build its plans for the defence of the North around them (F005-20) [X-005].

The officer reforms went into Laos with the battalions and stopped at the corps. Two of the 1st Division's three regiments and one Airborne brigade had changed commanders in the six weeks before the operation, in the palace's answer to the colonels' petition, and their new commanders met their staffs in January (EV007-5) [X-007]. The battalions under them had been commanded for a year or more by officers the board chose, and it showed in the withdrawal: the battalions that came down Route 9 as units, carrying their wounded and their radios, outnumbered those that came out on the helicopters' skids by more than on the historical course, though the pictures that reached the world were the same pictures [X-007]. It did not show in the outcome, which was decided by the plan's limits, Thieu's instruction to stop the operation if losses reached about 3,000, Lam's command of divisions whose commanders reported to Saigon over his head, and the enemy's five divisions, none of which any board had touched (F007-7) [H] [X-007]. I do not adjust the ARVN's losses here either. The reform had made the battalions better than the operation they were sent on.

## EV000-72 · The war on trial at home · 29 Mar – 30 Jun 1971 · Washington and New York · HIST-ALT · 000,002,006,011
*Initiative: neither*
Historically on 29 March 1971 a court-martial at Fort Benning convicted Lieutenant Calley of the premeditated murder of at least 20 people at My Lai and sentenced him to life imprisonment; two days later Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest pending appeal [H47]. In April 1971 Gallup found 11% of Americans approving the verdict, 70% calling Calley a scapegoat and 79% calling the life sentence too harsh [M011·S8]. In this war there is no massacre, no Peers inquiry and no trial (EV000-46, EV000-63), and the spring of 1971 has no argument about a lieutenant that divided the country along lines that did not run between the war's supporters and its opponents (F011-10) [X-011]. In April veterans opposed to the war demonstrated in Washington and threw their medals onto the Capitol steps [H]; they came without Calley's name in every speech, and their protest's weight came, as it had historically, from who they were. On 13 June the *New York Times* began publishing the Pentagon Papers, the Defense Department's secret history of the war's decisions to 1968, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg; on 30 June the Supreme Court refused to stop publication [H]. The administration's response was the "plumbers" unit formed to stop leaks [H]; in this war its burglaries are never exposed, because the burglary of 1972 that exposed them never happens (P011a) [X-011]. By mid-1971 a majority of Americans told pollsters the war had been a mistake, 60% on Gallup's question [M011·S1], as historically.

In this war the Phoenix programme comes before Congress on the historical date, in the hearings of the House Government Operations subcommittee in July 1971, at which, on the historical course, William Colby testified that more than 20,000 people had been killed under Phoenix since 1968 [H]. Here Colby gave the audited figures instead: since January 1965 about 58,000 people neutralized on warrant, of whom about 36,000 tried or held under review, about 15,000 rallied and about 7,000 killed, about 2,600 of them in the programme's own operations and the rest in military operations against men whom the centres had named (F006-13) [X-006]. The hearings were damaging and less damaging than they were historically. The story had been told since Duc Thanh in 1967 (EV006-4); the Commission's quarterly samples since 1965 gave the administration an independent count of unsupported neutralizations, about a tenth in the held belt and a quarter in the contested belt across six years, and the magistrate review and capped detention gave it a legal frame to describe (F002-14) [X-006]. The witnesses who hurt the programme most were former Americans of the PRUs' contested-belt advisory teams of 1967–68, and the subcommittee's report singled out the eleven districts the Commission had already named [X-006]. The Con Son "tiger cages" of July 1970 had happened as they did historically, in a prison the statute did not reach [H] [X-002]. The Public Safety appropriation for 1972 passed with a cut of about a tenth rather than the larger cuts its critics proposed, and the identity system's card stock and the Special Branch's American trainers were kept [X-002]. In this war the subcommittee heard Colby without the shadow of Son My over the hearing room, and its members, who historically asked whether Phoenix was My Lai by other means, asked instead whether the audit could be trusted; the audit could answer that question, and MACV's word in 1971 was worth a little more than history's (F011-16) [X-011].

## EV001-8 · The Commission placed under the Inspectorate · 14 May – 9 Jul 1971 · Saigon · NEW · 001
On 14 May 1971 the Commission Against Corruption opened an inquiry into the sale of import licences for fertilizer and motor vehicles under the Commercial Import Program, which its auditors had traced through three Saigon firms to a relative of a member of the President's staff [X-001]. It was the first inquiry in seven years to reach the presidential palace, and it reached it in the year Thieu stood for re-election [X-001]. The American lever that had protected the Commission since 1964, the Aid Compliance Board's power to suspend counterpart funds to a named command, importer or province, had not been used since 1969: the Mission was managing a withdrawal, and Washington would not risk a quarrel with the government it was leaving over a licence case (EC001-8, F001-15) [X-001]. Commissioner Khoa's second term, from January 1970, had been confirmed with the American co-chair's concurrence; his removal still required it [M001] [X-001].

Thieu did not remove him. On 9 July 1971 he issued a decree placing the Commission, the Special Court's prosecutors and the Pay and Muster Verification Service under a new Inspectorate General of the Presidency, with authority to approve or suspend any inquiry touching an official of the rank of minister, general officer or province chief and above [X-001]. The licence inquiry was suspended the same day. The Deputy Chief of Mission protested in writing; the Embassy did not invoke the Board [X-001]. Khoa stayed in office and the Commission's work below that rank went on: the musters, the pay at the parade, the land court cases and the Phoenix audits continued through 1971 at about their earlier rate [X-001]. What ended was the reach upward that had convicted a general in 1967 (EV001-7). An anti-corruption body that had survived seven years with a patron and an American lever lost the lever first, and the patron followed his own interest [X-001].

## EV000-73 · Thieu's unopposed re-election · Oct 1971 · Republic of Vietnam · HIST · 000,001,007
*Initiative: neither*

In the presidential election of October 1971 Thieu ran alone. Ky was at first disqualified under an election law requiring endorsements from deputies or provincial councillors, then reinstated by the Supreme Court, and withdrew; Duong Van Minh withdrew, charging that the election was rigged [H60] [H]. Thieu received all the valid votes, 5,971,114, and the reported turnout was 87.97 percent of 7,192,660 registered voters; valid ballots were 94.37 percent of those cast [H60]. A government that had won with a third of the vote in a contested election in 1967 was now returned in a referendum on itself. The army and administration remained Thieu's, and the regime was more stable than at any time since 1963; but its claim to stand for the whole non-communist South had narrowed, at the moment American forces were leaving it to stand alone.

In this war the election runs as it did historically, with the same withdrawals, the same single candidate and the same returns [X-001]. Its context is different in one respect and the same in another. Thieu's government could claim in the countryside what it could not claim on the historical course, that it had given about 800,000 families their land seven years before the election, and its province chiefs campaigned on it [X-001]. But the subordination of the Commission in July (EV001-8) had removed the one body with the standing to hear the opposition's complaints about the endorsement law, and Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard in Saigon as a description of a government that had shut down its own inspector in the year it chose to run alone [X-001].

The colonels' petition entered the election in August, when a deputy of the Lower House read its text into the record and asked why its signers had been removed from their commands before an operation in which the army had lost so much (EV007-5) [X-007]. Minh's charge that the election was rigged was heard by the officer corps alongside it. The board went on promoting captains and majors through the campaign as if nothing had happened, which was in its way the strongest thing it ever did; the officers it promoted that autumn knew that the list above colonel was now the President's [X-007].

## How the phase ends
At the end of 1971 the United States has about 158,000 military personnel in Vietnam, its ground combat role is nearly over, and the last US combat divisions are leaving [H40]. The RVNAF numbers more than a million men under arms, well equipped and, in the territorial forces, more present in the hamlets than ever before [H62]. In the countryside the VC remain weaker than at any time since 1960 and weaker in the Delta than on the historical course; the land programme of 1964 has finished its work, with about 1,150,000 hectares titled, and the Land to the Tiller law of 1970 was never needed (EV000-65) [X-001]. The Commission survives below the rank of minister and general and no longer above it (EV001-8) [X-001]. These gains are real, and they are measured mostly by indicators the government itself produces.

On the frontiers the balance is the reverse. The PAVN has rebuilt its main forces, extended the Trail in Laos and Cambodia, lost Sihanoukville and replaced it, and watched the ARVN's best divisions fail in Laos without American advisers. The secret channel in Paris is deadlocked on Thieu's removal and on the PAVN's presence in the South. In Hanoi, after Lam Son 719, the Politburo has decided to stake the next year on a conventional invasion across the DMZ, from Laos into the Central Highlands and from Cambodia towards Saigon, timed to an American election year and to the near completion of the American withdrawal [H56] [H]. The question of Phase V is the one Vietnamization was built to answer: whether the South can stop a PAVN conventional offensive with American air power but without American ground troops.

The Board still ranks the candidates and the Prime Minister now chooses the loyal among the competent (EV002-6) [X-002]. The posts, the census and the district Special Branch sections stand, and the historical police has caught up with them in size; what the Republic keeps from 1964 is the chiefs' experience and four extra years of files.

The radar's ledger has made the interdiction debate honest and changed nothing it was about: the Trail is larger at the end of 1971 than ever, the Liberation Route through northeastern Cambodia is mapped and open, and the aircraft that count both will leave with the Americans (EV000-68) [X-003].

The ARVN's I Corps ends the phase believing, on the evidence of Lolo and Sophia, that a besieged base under American bombers holds (EV000-71). It is true, and it is the belief that will cost it most when the bombers go [X-005].

Phung Hoang ends 1971 with an audited count of about 63,000 neutralized since 1965 and a national file of about 230,000 names in one building in Saigon, serving the President as well as the war (EV000-72, EV002-6) [X-006]. About 3,500 selected advisers remain, with the territorials and the province teams more than with the divisions; the American battalions have gone home whole and their discipline has decayed on its historical course (EV000-60) [X-006].

The board ends 1971 as the machinery of the army below colonel and nothing above it: battalions and regiments are commanded by officers it chose, for terms it sets, and the divisions and corps by the President's choices (EV007-5) [X-007]. The colonels who asked for the list back are in the schools and the staffs. The battalions that went into Laos came out of it better than the operation did (EV000-71).

The RVNAF enters 1972 with a closed rebuild loop and a domestic share that covers about two-fifths of its small-arms cartridges, a third of its 105mm rounds and most of its batteries, under a Directorate the palace now runs (EV008-6, EV002-6) [X-008]. The districts of the Delta call Vietnamese guns on Vietnamese sets (EV008-7). Nobody can see the base's weight in a year of free American supply, and it has not yet been tested by a year without it.

The NCO academy at Nha Trang ends 1971 Vietnamese, as it began, with its American advisers gone and its course whole; the gate on promotion has been broken once by decree and half restored by the training command, and about half the sergeants the decree made have taken their course since (EV010-6) [X-010]. The American NCO corps leaves Vietnam schooled and spent, its best sergeants among the dead of the fraggings as well as the fighting (EV010-5).

The 81st Gunship Wing ends 1971 holding the night over about two-thirds of the posts the Ghostriders held, through cloud on the coast where they never could, and in Vietnamese; the posts beyond its reach are attacked more often as the Americans leave (EV015-6, EV008-7) [X-015].
