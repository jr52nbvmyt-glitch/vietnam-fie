# maritime interdiction — the war as it stands after memorandum 011

## Summary — the state of the area now

Maritime interdiction in this war is a story of one clean success and its price. The success came first and fast. From 6 January 1964 a Global Hawk orbit off the II Corps coast watched the sea that nobody had watched before, and within a year the North's trawler route to the beaches of Phu Yen, Khanh Hoa and the Ca Mau peninsula had been seen, intercepted, turned back and finally abandoned (EV003-1, EV003-2). Hanoi gave up the sea about a year before the Market Time patrols forced it to historically, and the Vung Ro landing of February 1965 never happened, because there was no trawler on the coast that month to land. Of the provisions' effects on the war's supply lines, this is the one that worked exactly as designed. A cheap, direct and invisible route became expensive and visible, and the enemy stopped using it.

The price was Sihanoukville. The Central Military Commission concluded in the winter of 1964–65 that "the enemy now sees every ship at sea by day and by night" [X-003], and the cargo the trawlers would have carried came ashore instead at Kompong Som under Chinese and East European flags, from late June 1965, to be trucked by Cambodian contractors to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak. On the record's own dating that was about eighteen months earlier than history's diversion, though the record disagrees with itself on the historical date (Q-1). From mid-1965 the enemy's principal external line into III and IV Corps ran through a neutral port that no American could blockade, bomb or seize (POS003-2, F003-3). The sea route had been the one line the command could fight with the means it had; the port was the one it could only count.

And count it did. The Sihanoukville count of 1966–67 is the second thing the area produced, and its weight is in the argument, not in the tonnage. On 9 March 1967 MACV J-2 and the CIA agreed that about 9,000–13,000 tons of military cargo had moved from the port to the border in the twenty months to February 1967, and that the route supplied most of the arms and ammunition of the enemy in III and IV Corps [X-003]. History settled that dispute only in 1970, from the seized ledgers of the Hak Ly Trucking Company [H]. The estimate changed no rule. The Joint Chiefs asked in April 1967 for a naval quarantine of the port and strikes on the depots; the President refused both (EV003-4). The measured sanctuary was still a sanctuary, and it stayed open until Lon Nol closed the port in March 1970, on history's date and for history's reasons (EV000-64).

Against the North itself the area holds two mining campaigns, both by B-52Js laying Quickstrike mines. The estuary field of 1967 closed the coastal lighterage south of Thanh Hoa to steel hulls within four months and moved its cargo onto trucks and junks without reducing what arrived in the panhandle (EV005-3). Pocket Money in May 1972 closed Haiphong on history's date, with the Js laying the secondary fields on the first night, and the North's imports moved to the Chinese rail crossings as they did historically (EV000-75). Neither changed the tonnage reaching the South by a margin the record can measure.

The net, at its true weight in both directions, is this. The provisions changed the *route* of the enemy's external supply into the southern half of the country, early and decisively, and did not change its *volume*. The headline judgment on tonnage arriving, which sits in the chapters and was set by the study of the air war, reads the same: −1..+1 in Phase I, where the sea closed and the port opened (TRAIL.tonnage_arriving, P1). What the early closure bought was real but specific: a year of heavy weapons denied to the coastal provinces of II Corps and the lower Delta in 1964, a trawler crew's knowledge of two years of landings, and the war's central sanctuary measured three years before history measured it. What it cost was the conversion of an interceptable line into an untouchable one, eighteen months sooner. After 1970 there is nothing left at sea to interdict in either war, and from Phase IV to the end of the record the area converges on history, through events the record carries: the closure of the port, the incursion, the Liberation Route, and the statute of August 1973.

The area also turns on one thing the record does not hold. Between March 1965 and the end of the war the Market Time patrols, the Coast Guard cutters, the Swift boats, the Vietnamese Navy's coastal and riverine forces and the Game Warden river patrols fought a long war of boardings and inspections that appears in no chapter. History's trawlers kept coming in small numbers after 1965, and the Navy fought several of them, most famously on 1 March 1968 [S1]; this record neither carries those engagements nor cancels them. The study judges the later phases on what the record holds and says where it is silent (Q-3, Q-4).

## P1 · Phase I — The advisory war and escalation (Jan 1964 – Jul 1965)

Phase I is the whole of the maritime war in this record, compressed into eighteen months. It opens with a coast nobody watched and closes with a sea route abandoned and a port opened in its place.

On 1 January 1964 the Republic of Vietnam had about 2,000 kilometres of coast and tens of thousands of fishing junks on it, and the only forces looking for infiltrators among them were the Vietnamese Navy's Sea Force, with a handful of PC-461-class submarine chasers and PCE escorts out of Qui Nhon and Nha Trang, the motorised and sail junks of the Junk Force's coastal divisions, and a Seventh Fleet destroyer when one was lent [M-TNA] [H]. The North's maritime transport organisation, Group 759, which became Group 125 of the PAVN Navy in the course of the year, had been running steel-hulled trawlers of about 100 tons from Haiphong and the Do Son peninsula since the early 1960s [H]. The Theater Needs Assessment had written of this coast that it had "almost no knowledge of what crosses it", and that a single trawler could land in one night about five times the enemy's daily requirement from the North [M-TNA]. Historically 86 of the 89 voyages of 1962–65 reached shore, with about 8,000 tons [S1].

The first Global Hawk orbit of the RQ-4B (P003b) went up from Clark Air Base on 6 January 1964 and took station about 60 nautical miles off the coast between Qui Nhon and Cam Ranh Bay, its radar's maritime mode sweeping a belt of sea about 150 nautical miles deep (EV003-1, EC003-3) [M003]. Its operation was named TIDE TABLE, and its method was simple. A steel hull on a steady course from the north that did not stop to fish was flagged by the exploitation cell at Tan Son Nhut and handed to whatever could reach it; because the radar held the track for hours, the interceptor did not have to search, only steam [M003].

The first track came on the night of 18–19 February. A contact moving at ten knots on a course begun in the Gulf of Tonkin two days earlier turned west at dusk toward the Phu Yen coast; the cell flagged it at 1940, and a PC-461 from the patrol line off Tuy Hoa came up with it at 0310 about four miles off Vung Ro Bay, under low overcast and in a northeast swell [X-003]. The trawler's crew ran for the beach and fired their demolition charges, as Group 759's standing orders required. The forward hold blew and the after hold did not, and a boarding party pulled out about 30 tons of cargo before the fire reached it: Type 56 carbines and assault rifles, B-40 launchers and rockets, 82mm mortar rounds and about 400,000 rounds of 7.62mm [X-003]. Seven of her crew were taken from the water and nine died; the Vietnamese lost one sailor killed and four wounded to fire from a reception party waiting on the beach with porters [X-003].

By the end of June the orbit had tracked eleven contacts the cell judged to be trawlers, on the II Corps coast and off the Ca Mau peninsula, where the landings went into the mangrove creeks of the Bo De and the Cua Lon [X-003]. Four were intercepted. Two scuttled or blew up; one, taken nearly intact off Binh Dinh in April by the boarding party of the destroyer USS *Edsall* with a Vietnamese liaison officer, gave up a crew whose interrogation at Nha Trang described the landing points, signal lamps and reception parties of about two years of earlier voyages, and led the Vietnamese Navy to two landing beaches in Phu Yen and Khanh Hoa; the fourth ran into the Bo De ahead of a junk division and landed most of its cargo [X-003]. Six turned back when a patrol came over the horizon, a reversal of course the radar saw at about the moment the trawler's own radar would have seen the patrol. One landed unobserved in May, on a night when the orbit's aircraft diverted to Clark with a generator fault and its relief was four hours late [X-003]. The record is careful about its own figures: the dead and the prisoners are counts and reliable, the cargo tonnages are estimates from what was recovered and what prisoners said, with a margin of about a third [E: recovery and interrogation].

The orbit's one great diversion of the year was also maritime. When the torpedo boats attacked the *Maddox* on 2 August, the orbit was moved over the Gulf of Tonkin, and on the night of the 4th its watch saw the destroyers, a cluster of junks thirty miles west, the rain cells and no boats; Pierce Arrow was not flown (EV000-3) [M003]. That diversion cost the coast. Group 125, which had paused through the summer, sent two trawlers south in September, and one of them landed at Vung Ro on the night of 21–22 September in the gap the Gulf watch had opened (EV003-2) [X-003]. The second was tracked off Binh Dinh after the orbit returned, and on 9 November the Vietnamese Navy escort *Chi Lang* and a Junk Force division caught her off the Tam Quan coast, where her crew beached and burned her; sixteen of them were killed or drowned and four captured, and the junk sailors lost two killed [X-003]. In December a third trawler reversed course off Quang Ngai at dusk when a Seventh Fleet destroyer closed her, and went home [X-003].

That was enough. In December 1964 and January 1965 Hanoi decided to give the sea up, and the enemy's initiative here is the hinge of the whole area. It was not forced by any one interception; it was the Central Military Commission's reading of a pattern, recorded in Party records captured later in this war, that the route was no longer worth the ships and the men [X-003]. The alternative was already prepared, because Sihanouk had renounced American aid in November 1963 and was moving toward Beijing; he would break relations with Washington on 3 May 1965 [H]. Through the winter Chinese and North Vietnamese negotiators in Phnom Penh arranged the passage of "rice and commercial goods" through Sihanoukville with a commission to Cambodian officers at each stage, and Cambodian trucking contractors to carry the cargo up Route 4 to Kompong Speu and on to the border (EV003-2) [H] [X-003]. The first arms cargo for the Front came ashore there under a Chinese flag in late June 1965 and was trucked by night to the depots behind the Fishhook and the Parrot's Beak [X-003].

Two historical events changed as a result. The trawler that Lieutenant James Bowers found by chance unloading about 100 tons of arms in Vung Ro Bay on 16 February 1965 was not there to be found [H] [X-003]. And Market Time, which the Navy opened on 11 March 1965 historically because of Vung Ro, opened on the same date in this war because of TIDE TABLE's record, with a picture of what it was looking for and increasingly nothing to find (EV003-2) [H] [X-003]. Historically it began with seventeen 82-foot Point-class cutters of the Coast Guard, a picket of destroyer escorts and radar picket ships, patrol aircraft, and Swift boats whose planned number rose from 54 to 84 by September 1965 [S1]; memorandum 003 judged that in this war the same forces were either fewer for the same effect or a much denser screen, because their task had become interception rather than search [M003].

What this phase changed was the route of heavy weapons into the coastal provinces of II Corps and the lower Delta, and the timing of the Cambodian line. What it did not change was the volume. The record says so plainly: TIDE TABLE "did not starve the Front", and the weapons the sea would have landed in 1965 reached it more slowly by a longer road (EV003-1, EV003-2). Memorandum 003's verdict on itself is the right one for the area. It was the cleanest thing the aircraft did in the whole war, and the most important consequence of Phase I that they caused was to close a route the command could intercept and open one it could not touch [M003].

## P2 · Phase II — The main force war (Aug 1965 – Dec 1967)

Phase II is the phase of the port. The sea is quiet in the record from the summer of 1965, and the maritime war moves in two directions at once: south and west into Cambodia, where it can only watch, and north into the estuaries of the North's panhandle, where for the first time it can strike.

Sihanoukville worked through 1965 and 1966 as Hanoi had planned it. Cargo arrived alongside under Chinese and East European flags, Cambodian officers took their commission, and convoys of ten to thirty trucks moved at night from Kompong Speu and Kompong Cham toward Snoul, Mimot and the Fishhook, and toward Svay Rieng and the Parrot's Beak, stopping at depots that appeared on no Cambodian map (EV003-4) [X-003]. In this war the port was the principal external line into III and IV Corps from mid-1965, about a year and a half before history's (POS003-2). The enemy's main force in the two southern corps fought the big-unit war of 1965–67 with arms that had come through a neutral prince's port, and in IV Corps it was also fed from the Republic's own depots: the lot numbers on the 5.56mm cases picked up behind a VC firing position north of Cao Lanh in July 1966 showed that about 1.4 million rounds had gone by sampan from the 9th Division's supply point at Sa Dec, through two dealers in Cholon and a Front purchasing agent at Hong Ngu on the Mekong near the border, to the Plain of Reeds (EV004-3) [X-004]. The external line was not the only line, and the Delta's waterways carried both.

The argument over the port was the oldest in the intelligence community, and in this war it was settled by counting. From the summer of 1966 Lieutenant Colonel Harold Kessling's cell at Tan Son Nhut built the count night by night from three sources (EV003-4). The U-2S (P003a) photographed the port obliquely from the Gulf of Siam about twice a month, from outside Cambodian airspace: ships, flags, cargo on the quays, trucks in the marshalling area. The Global Hawk, on nights it could be spared from the coast, stood over Tay Ninh and Binh Long inside South Vietnamese airspace and watched the road traffic 150–200 kilometres into Cambodia [M003] [X-003]. And from May 1967 the reconnaissance teams of MACV's Studies and Observations Group, crossing under Operation Daniel Boone, went to look where the convoys stopped; twice they found ammunition crates with Chinese markings, and one team of three Americans and nine Montagnards was lost near Mimot in September 1967 [H] [X-003].

On 9 March 1967 MACV J-2 and the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence agreed that about 9,000–13,000 tons of military cargo had moved from Sihanoukville to the border base areas between July 1965 and February 1967, and that the route supplied most of the arms and ammunition of the PAVN and VC forces in III and IV Corps [X-003]. Historically the CIA held until 1970 that the port's traffic was mostly rice and contraband, and the seized records of the Hak Ly Trucking Company then showed about 21,600 tons of arms and ammunition moved through it between December 1966 and April 1969 [H]; the North's own Unit K-20 is credited with about 29,000 tons of cargo in the same period [S2]. In April 1967 the Joint Chiefs proposed a naval quarantine of Sihanoukville and air strikes on the depots. The President declined both, and Sihanouk's neutrality and the bar on operations into Cambodia stood [H] [X-003]. That is the shape of the phase in one decision: the command now knew, as a measured fact, where the southern war's arms came from, and the knowledge bought nothing but a better argument.

The sea, meanwhile, was quiet in the record. The chapters carry no trawler engagement between the summer of 1965 and the end of 1967, and the only statement of Market Time's work is that it had a picture of its quarry and increasingly nothing to find (EV003-2). Historically, at the beginning of 1966 almost three-quarters of the enemy's resupply was still coming by sea along the South Vietnamese coast, and by early 1967 Market Time had cut that to about a tenth [S1]. In 1966 alone the historical patrols detected about 808,000 watercraft, inspected about 223,000 and boarded about 181,000, in 482 firefights [S1]. This war has the same patrols on the same coast with a radar picture above them (F003-3) [M003], and it has no account of what they did. The Game Warden river patrols on the Delta's rivers, given the canal watch's patterns through the police by memoranda 002 and 003 and schooled petty officers by memorandum 010, are likewise named in the memoranda and absent from the chapters [M002] [M003] [M010]. The study judges the phase without them and records the silence (Q-3, Q-4).

The maritime war turned north in February 1967. On the 26th Washington authorised the aerial mining of the North's rivers south of the 20th parallel, and in this war the first field was laid by four B-52Js from U-Tapao, which put 80 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes (P005d) into the Song Ca estuary below Vinh and the Song Giang at Quang Khe from about 1,000 feet in a single pass each (EV005-3) [M005] [X-005]. The target detecting devices were set for the steel lighters and coastal freighters that carried supplies down from Thanh Hoa and Vinh; the wooden junks were left to the pressure settings, and the sampans were not mined at all, because no setting would catch them [M005]. In the first fortnight two steel lighters sank at the Quang Khe bar and a third in the Song Ca, and within a month the steel traffic south of Vinh was about a fifth of what it had been; the Js reseeded each field every three to four weeks on the cue of the Global Hawk's imagery when the North's sweepers came out, and lost no aircraft doing it [X-005]. By June the steel craft had stopped coming south of Thanh Hoa [X-005].

The cargo did not stop, and that is the phase's second lesson. It moved to wooden junks, to sampans under tow and to trucks on Route 1A and Route 15; by the autumn the radar's count of southbound truck movements below Vinh was about 25–30 percent higher than in 1966, while the tonnage counted into the panhandle was about the same (F005-5) [X-005]. The North paid in labour, perhaps 15,000–25,000 more porters and road workers on Route Package 1 in 1967, and in nights its trucks spent in the open where the radar could count them [X-005]. Hanoi asked Moscow for wooden-hulled craft and for minesweeping help, and got trucks, which was what it needed (F005-18) [X-005]. The aid of its allies grew, a little, to replace what the mines and the J's crossing strikes took (P005b) [M005]. The field stayed until the bombing halt of November 1968, and the record's own judgment of how much less arrived in the South because of it is "very little" [X-005].

The enemy's initiative in the phase was the decision not to return to the sea. Nothing in the record forced it after January 1965: Group 125 still had its trawlers, and Market Time was historically never so tight that no trawler got through [S1]. Hanoi chose a line it could not lose over a line it could, and in Phase II that choice paid. The phase ends with the war's central sanctuary measured, supplied, and exactly as safe as history's.

## P3 · Phase III — Tet and its aftermath (Jan 1968 – Dec 1968)

The maritime war is quiet in Phase III, and the reason is that both of its lines had settled before the year began. The sea had been given up for three years, and Sihanoukville was running as it ran historically, now counted.

The port's cargo was at work in the offensives of 1968. The forces that attacked Saigon, Cholon and the provincial capitals of III and IV Corps on 30–31 January moved into them in small groups among the Tet crowds, on foot, by bus and by sampan, where no radar could tell them from anyone else (EV000-41) [X-003]; the arms they carried had come, on the estimate of March 1967, mostly through Kompong Som and the depots behind the border (EV003-4). The radar that had counted the convoys gave no warning of the cities. The record says so directly: the picture showed the border and nothing else. Measuring a sanctuary is not the same as finding what it supplies once it is inside the population, and Phase III is where that difference is most expensive.

At sea the record carries nothing. Historically the North tried one of its largest seaborne efforts of the war on the night of 1 March 1968, when four trawlers approached the coast together; two were destroyed in gun battles with allied ships, one was scuttled by her crew, and the fourth turned back into the South China Sea [S1]. The chapters of this war neither carry that night nor cancel it. In a war where Group 125 gave up the coast three years earlier, after a year of being seen every time, it is plausible that the attempt was never made, and equally plausible that Hanoi, preparing a general offensive, tried the sea once more; the record does not choose, and the study does not choose for it (Q-3).

The estuary field in the North's panhandle stayed seeded until the bombing halt. On 1 November 1968 the bombing of the North stopped, and the Gulf watch, a reconnaissance flight over international waters 40–60 nautical miles off the coast, went on counting (EV003-5) [M003]. Within a week the ferries at Quang Khe, where the Js had laid the first field the year before, were running in daylight; trucks that had moved in convoys of five or six at night moved by day in columns of forty and fifty, the railway south of Thanh Hoa was repaired within three weeks, and southbound movements on Route 15 toward the Mu Gia pass in December were about three times the October level [X-003]. The halt's terms required the command to report the North's use of it, and the count went to Washington twice a week. It changed nothing about the halt, which had been traded for the expanded talks in Paris, and it gave Commando Hunt, which opened on 15 November, the size of the surge on its first night [X-003].

The enemy's initiative in the area in 1968 was the use of the halt, and it was Hanoi's own: a decision taken for the coming dry season, on the reading that the Americans would not resume the bombing during the talks. The record shows it at work on the coast road and the Quang Khe ferries, where the estuary field had been. Nothing in the maritime war of the phase moved the war; the area's lines were set, and the battles of 1968 were fought on land.

## P4 · Phase IV — Vietnamization and drawdown (Jan 1969 – Dec 1971)

Phase IV ends the area's divergence from history, and it does so through events the record carries rather than by statement. The sanctuary that the sea route had made two years earlier was struck from the air in 1969, closed at the port in 1970 and entered on the ground the same spring, and every one of those steps happened on history's date.

Operation Menu began on the night of 18 March 1969 (EV000-58). Forty-eight of sixty bombers from Guam were diverted into Cambodia against Base Area 353 in the Fishhook, and in this war they were B-52Js; about 1,600 tons fell in the boxes rather than history's 2,400, and twelve of the aircraft carried 240 GBU-31s aimed at coordinates the U-2S had measured over five years: storage sheds, a vehicle park, a workshop with a generator, and the clearings where the Global Hawk had seen the Sihanoukville trucks unload by night [H57] [X-005]. In the first six weeks about 300 measured structures were destroyed [X-005]. By June COSVN's rear services had moved everything that mattered under the trees and underground, and the clearings were empty when the U-2S next looked (F005-16) [X-005]. A COSVN logistics officer who rallied in 1970 described the rear services cadres searching for spies and beacons, finding neither, and doing the right thing anyway, which was to own nothing that stood in the open [X-005]. The campaign dropped about 75,000 tons rather than history's 108,823 [H57] [X-005]. The precision had a season of targets in the sanctuary and then none, and the sanctuary went on working.

The port closed in March 1970, and not because of anything the command did. On 18 March the Cambodian National Assembly deposed Sihanouk while he was abroad, and Lon Nol closed Sihanoukville to the supplies that had reached the base areas through it (EV000-64) [H55]. From 29 March the PAVN attacked the Cambodian army and overran much of eastern and northeastern Cambodia within weeks, and Cambodia, a sanctuary since 1965, became a battlefield [H55]. The record carries this event as historical and unaltered. That is a judgment worth stating: five years of the Sihanoukville count, and a joint estimate in 1967 that history did not have until the ledgers of 1970, did not bring the closure forward by a day. Historically Sihanouk had already suspended the North's arms shipments through his ports in the summer of 1969 [S2]; the chapters do not say whether that suspension happened in this war, where the traffic had been a measured fact for two years by then (Q-5).

The incursion of 29 April to 30 June 1970 was the one moment in the record when the count became a map an army could use (EV000-66). The ARVN of III and IV Corps under Lieutenant General Do Cao Tri went into the Parrot's Beak on 29 April, and the US 1st Cavalry Division and 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment with ARVN airborne units into the Fishhook on 1 May [H55]. In this war the depots where the convoys had stopped since 1965, the road net that joined them and the clearings they hid in were known, and the 1st Cavalry's brigades were landed on the largest of them in the first two days; the complex the troops called "the City", found by chance on 4 May historically, was an objective on 1 May [X-003]. History's haul was about 22,900 individual and 2,500 crew-served weapons, 7,000–8,000 tons of rice, about 1,800 tons of ammunition, 431 vehicles and 55 tons of medical supplies [H55]. Here most of it was found in the first ten days rather than through May and June, and the total was larger by about a tenth [X-003]. COSVN, warned by its own reading of the American build-up and heard moving west and north from about 19 April, was gone before the attack, as historically [H55] [X-003].

The enemy's answer was his own and was the historical one. The loss of the port and of the caches threw the whole burden of supplying the southern half of the country onto the Trail; the PAVN seized Attapeu and Saravane in Laos and opened a "Liberation Route" through northeastern Cambodia to the Mekong, and the Global Hawk watched it being built from the summer of 1970, as it had watched Sihanoukville, and could not stop it either (EV000-64, EV000-66) [H55] [X-003]. The incursion delayed the PAVN's recovery in III and IV Corps, and the southern half of the country was quieter for more than a year [H55]. On the record's evidence, the delay was a little longer than history's, because a tenth more was lost and lost faster, but the chapters give no date for the recovery, and the study holds that judgment loosely.

From the summer of 1970 there was nothing at sea or in the Cambodian ports for anyone to interdict, in either war. The record carries none of the historical transfer of the Market Time cutters and craft to Vietnamese crews, which ran from May 1969 to August 1970 [S1], and none of the Vietnamese Navy's riverine war of the drawdown years (Q-4). The study judges the sea route's value at nil from 1970 because both wars had closed it, not because the record shows the patrols that kept it closed.

## P5 · Phase V — The Easter Offensive and Linebacker (Jan 1972 – Dec 1972)

The maritime war of 1972 is fought entirely in the North's waters, and it is history's campaign with better bombers laying part of it. In the South there is no sea route and no Cambodian port to watch; the offensive's supply comes down the Trail, the roads out of Cambodia and, across the DMZ, Route 1A.

The Gulf watch saw the northern front coming. From 12 March it stood off the Quang Tri coast, outside the SA-2s the North had moved south, and tracked armoured columns moving at night on Route 1A and Route 101 from Vinh Linh toward the Ben Hai (EV000-74) [X-003]. In the south of the country the 5th, 7th and 9th Divisions of the B-2 Front came out of Cambodia against Loc Ninh, Quan Loi and An Loc from 5 April, and from mid-April a Global Hawk stood over Binh Long and watched the roads out of the sanctuary (EV000-76) [X-003]. Cambodia in 1972 was what it had been historically: a base area the enemy attacked from, no longer a port he was supplied through.

On 8 May (9 May in Vietnam) the President announced Operation Pocket Money, the mining of Haiphong and the other northern ports, prohibited since 1965 (EV000-75) [H68]. Navy aircraft from the *Coral Sea* laid the first mines in the Haiphong channel as they had historically. In this war B-52Js laid the secondary fields the same night: about 900 Mk 64 and Mk 65 Quickstrikes in the approaches to Hon Gai, Cam Pha, Thanh Hoa, Vinh and Dong Hoi and in the inland waterways south of Haiphong, including about 200 Quickstrike-ERs released more than forty miles out [H68] [X-005]. The mines activated after five days, and about 11,000 more were sown in the following weeks [H68]. The North's main entry point for Soviet supplies by sea was closed, and 27 ships stayed in Haiphong [H] [M005].

The North's answer was the one it had made to the estuary field five years before, on a larger scale. From June the Gulf watch saw the trucks massing at the Chinese border crossings at Dong Dang and Lao Cai to replace the sea [X-003]. Moscow and Beijing denounced the mining and did nothing else, and the Moscow summit went ahead from 22 May [H68]. The effect at the front was real and historical: by a PAVN history only 30 percent of planned supplies reached front-line units in May and June, the PAVN's shelling of the South fell by half between 9 May and 1 June, and imports by September were 35–50 percent below May's [H68]. The record attributes the war's small difference from history in the panhandle to the bridges falling in the first week of Linebacker rather than the first month, not to the mines: the radar's count of southbound traffic below Vinh in July was about 35 percent of the March level, against about 40 percent with the historical bombers [X-003] [X-005].

The enemy's losses in the year bear on the area's regeneration judgment. In this war the PAVN's killed and wounded of 1972 were about a tenth above history's, concentrated in the divisions that attacked An Loc and Kontum, and the North replaced the men within its 1973 flow and formed its corps on the historical dates, so the extra loss cost quality and not time (POSMT01-2). The sanctuary in Cambodia rebuilt the B-2 Front's divisions after An Loc as it had historically. Nothing in the maritime war of 1972 changed the amount of Soviet and Chinese aid that reached the North; it changed, on history's date and in history's way, the road by which it came.

## P6 · Phase VI — Paris, the endgame, and 1975 (Jan 1973 – Dec 1975)

Phase VI has no maritime interdiction in it, because the record holds no enemy line by sea and no American force allowed to strike one. What the area contributes to the last phase is a sanctuary that became the whole of Indochina, a Soviet supply that was brought forward, and a sea that served the Republic rather than its enemy.

From 15 August 1973 the Case–Church amendment barred any American military activity in or over Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia, and the bombing of Cambodia ended that day (EV000-86) [H51] [H]. Every base area the Sihanoukville count had mapped was now as safe as the North itself. The ARVN still had the strength to attack one of them: from 27 March to 2 May 1974 III Corps struck the PAVN's bases around Svay Rieng in Cambodia, in the last major ARVN offensive of the war, claiming more than 1,200 PAVN killed against fewer than 100 of its own (EV000-90) [H71]. The claim carries the usual inflation, and the raid changed nothing in the sanctuary's arithmetic; it is history's raid.

At sea the Republic suffered its one naval defeat of the record, and it was history's. On 19 January 1974 a Chinese naval force defeated South Vietnamese ships in the Paracel Islands and took the islands, and the United States did not intervene (EV000-88) [H71]. The clash had nothing to do with infiltration. It showed what the sea around the Republic had become: a place where a great power on its northern horizon could take territory, and its own ally would not act.

The North's last campaign did not use the sea. Resolution 21 of October 1973 set the rebuilding, and the strategic road east of the Truong Son and the fuel pipeline into the South were built on the historical schedule (EV000-87); the 4th Corps' attack on Phuoc Long on the Cambodian border from 12 December 1974 came by land (EV000-92) [H73]. The record does not state the Soviet and Chinese deliveries of 1973–74, which the meta-checkpoint asked every memorandum to treat as variables with their own politics (DIRMT01-10); they remain absent (Q-6).

The divergence the area does hold in Phase VI comes after the spring of 1975, and it runs in the enemy's favour. In this war the Republic did not fall in April: the Highlands were lost in a five-week siege rather than a ten-day rout, Hue in an ordered withdrawal on 18 April, and the Politburo, with no collapse to resolve on, put Saigon back to the dry season (EV000-94, EV000-95, EV011-3) [X-011]. The rains of 1975 were time for both sides to refit. For the North they were time to bring the next campaign's supply forward. In May the General Staff asked Moscow for the 130mm ammunition and the tank replacements planned for 1976 to be shipped in the autumn of 1975, and Moscow, with its own reasons to see the war ended before the American election year, shipped most of them; Beijing, with no wish to see Hanoi's victory hastened by Soviet guns, sent rice and trucks and no more (EV011-3) [X-011]. That is the only judgment in the area's last phase that departs from history, and it is an exercise judgment resting on memorandum 011's chronicle.

The sea's part in the Republic's own survival is worth one paragraph, because it is the reverse of everything before it. Operation SOUTHERN WALL, the evacuation of Da Nang from 20 to 31 May 1975, moved about 70,000 soldiers and about 250,000 civilians by the Vietnamese Navy's LSTs, the Saigon coasters and two chartered American freighters, the families first from 8 May, while the territorials of Quang Nam held the Thu Bon for ten days; about 4,000 men of the rear guard were lost (EV011-3) [X-011]. On 12 June the 22nd Division came out of Qui Nhon by sea, as it had historically in April, with about two-thirds of its battalions [H] [X-011]. A coast that had been the enemy's cheapest supply line in 1963 was the Republic's last line of retreat in 1975, and in this war it was used with a plan.

The phase ends with the sanctuary worth to the enemy what it was worth historically, the North's rebuilding on the historical dates, and its Soviet supply for the last offensive arriving earlier and larger than the 1975 of history needed. On 31 December 1975 the Republic holds III and IV Corps and the coast from Nha Trang south, with five PAVN corps preparing to attack it in the dry season [X-011].

## Judgments

**Chapter end vectors.** This study judged no chapter entry. The study tags no headline facet and names no binding facet; none of its variables has facets.
